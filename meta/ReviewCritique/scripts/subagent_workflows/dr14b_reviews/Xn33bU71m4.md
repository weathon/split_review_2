### Summary

This paper investigates the effectiveness of Large Language Models (LLMs) in reverse engineering tasks, specifically focusing on function name recovery and type inference. The authors systematically evaluate nine widely used open-source LLMs, including CodeLlama, Llama 2, and DeepSeek-R1, across different architectures and optimization levels. The study reveals that, without fine-tuning, these models perform poorly in both tasks, highlighting the need for more effective fine-tuning strategies and higher-quality training data. The paper also explores the impact of model size, training data quality, and the use of auxiliary tools like CodeWordNet on the performance of LLMs in these tasks.

### Strengths

1. The paper presents a systematic evaluation of multiple LLMs on reverse engineering tasks, which is a novel contribution to the field. It addresses a gap in understanding the practical utility of LLMs for binary analysis, particularly in name recovery and type inference.

2. The use of an automated framework for evaluation ensures a fair and consistent comparison across different LLMs. The paper also employs rigorous metrics, such as F1 scores, to quantify the performance of the models.

3. The study provides insights into the limitations of LLMs in reverse engineering, highlighting the need for high-quality training data and effective fine-tuning strategies. It also explores the impact of model size and training data quality on performance, offering valuable findings for future research.

4. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for fine-tuning and deploying these LLMs, which is crucial for practical applications.

5. The paper does not explore the potential of combining LLMs with other AI techniques, such as reinforcement learning or graph neural networks, which could enhance their performance in reverse engineering tasks.

6. The paper does not address the security implications of using LLMs in reverse engineering, such as the potential for adversarial attacks or the generation of incorrect or misleading information.

### Suggestions

The study would benefit from a more comprehensive exploration of the potential applications of LLMs in reverse engineering beyond name recovery and type inference. Specifically, the authors should consider investigating tasks such as vulnerability detection, code obfuscation analysis, and malware classification. These tasks present unique challenges and would provide a more complete picture of the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in this domain. For instance, evaluating the LLMs' ability to identify common vulnerability patterns or to understand obfuscated code would be valuable contributions. Furthermore, the paper should include a more detailed analysis of how different architectural features impact the performance of LLMs. This could involve examining the effects of specific instruction set architectures (ISAs), such as x86, ARM, or MIPS, and their associated complexities on the LLMs' ability to understand and reverse engineer code. Additionally, the study should investigate how different optimization levels (e.g., O0, O1, O2, O3) affect the performance of LLMs, as these optimizations can significantly alter the structure and semantics of the code. A more granular analysis of these factors would enhance the generalizability of the findings and provide a more nuanced understanding of the challenges involved in applying LLMs to reverse engineering tasks across diverse architectures.

To address the concerns regarding the use of decompiled code, the authors should incorporate a more robust analysis of the impact of decompilation artifacts on the evaluation results. This could involve comparing the performance of LLMs on both decompiled code and assembly code, and quantifying the differences in performance. Furthermore, the authors should consider using multiple decompilers and analyzing the variance in the results to assess the robustness of their findings. This would help to determine whether the observed performance is due to the inherent limitations of the LLMs or the inaccuracies introduced by the decompilation process. Additionally, the paper should include a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for fine-tuning and deploying the LLMs. This should include information on the hardware requirements, training time, and inference time. This information is crucial for practical applications and would help to determine the feasibility of using LLMs in real-world reverse engineering scenarios. The authors should also explore the potential of combining LLMs with other AI techniques, such as reinforcement learning or graph neural networks, to enhance their performance in reverse engineering tasks. For example, reinforcement learning could be used to train LLMs to explore the program structure more effectively, while graph neural networks could be used to model the control flow and data dependencies in the code. These hybrid approaches could potentially lead to significant improvements in the performance of LLMs in reverse engineering.

Finally, the paper should address the security implications of using LLMs in reverse engineering. This should include an analysis of the potential for adversarial attacks, such as the injection of malicious code into the training data, and the generation of incorrect or misleading information. The authors should also discuss the potential for LLMs to be used for malicious purposes, such as the automated generation of malware. This analysis is crucial for ensuring the responsible development and deployment of LLMs in reverse engineering. The authors should also consider the ethical implications of using LLMs in this context, such as the potential for bias in the training data and the impact on the job market for reverse engineers. A more thorough discussion of these issues would enhance the overall impact and relevance of the paper.
