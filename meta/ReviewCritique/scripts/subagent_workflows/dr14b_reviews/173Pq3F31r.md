### Summary

This paper presents a unified framework for expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT). The proposed method disentangles note-level score content and global performance style representations from both paired and unpaired data, and is built on a transformer-based sequence-to-sequence architecture. A diffusion-based performance style recommendation (PSR) module is introduced to generate style embeddings directly from score content, supporting style transfer and flexible rendering across a range of expressive styles. The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

1. The proposed method is only evaluated on the ASAP dataset. It would be better to evaluate the proposed method on more datasets to verify its generalization ability. Specifically, the method should be tested on datasets with varying styles, tempos, and dynamics to ensure robustness. The current evaluation lacks a comprehensive analysis of how the model performs under different musical conditions.
2. The proposed method is only for piano. It would be interesting to extend the proposed method to other instruments. The current approach relies on piano-specific features and representations, which limits its applicability to other instruments with different articulation and timbral characteristics. The paper should discuss the challenges and potential solutions for adapting the method to other instruments.

### Suggestions

To address the limited evaluation, the authors should consider including datasets that cover a wider range of musical styles and complexities. For example, datasets with different genres (e.g., classical, jazz, pop) or varying levels of expressiveness would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model's generalization capabilities. Furthermore, the evaluation should include a detailed analysis of the model's performance on different types of musical passages, such as those with fast runs, slow melodies, or complex harmonies. This would help identify potential weaknesses and areas for improvement. The authors could also explore using data augmentation techniques to artificially increase the diversity of the training data, which could improve the model's robustness.

To extend the method to other instruments, the authors should investigate how to adapt the input representation and the model architecture to accommodate the specific characteristics of different instruments. For example, the input representation could be modified to include instrument-specific features, such as bowing techniques for string instruments or breath control for wind instruments. The model architecture could also be modified to handle the different articulation and timbral characteristics of different instruments. The authors should also consider how to handle the different ranges and capabilities of different instruments. For example, some instruments may have a wider range of pitches or more complex articulation possibilities than the piano. The paper should discuss the challenges and potential solutions for adapting the method to other instruments, including the need for new datasets and evaluation metrics.

Finally, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the disentanglement of score content and performance style. While the authors claim that the model learns disentangled representations, the paper lacks a thorough analysis of how these representations are actually separated and how they contribute to the final performance. The authors could explore techniques such as visualization or ablation studies to better understand the learned representations. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the limitations of the proposed approach and potential avenues for future research. This would provide a more balanced and comprehensive assessment of the proposed method.
