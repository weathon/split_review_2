

How Should Pre-Trained Language Models Be Fine-Tuned Towards Adversarial Robustness?

 Anonymous Author(s) AfﬁliationAddress email

Abstract The ﬁne-tuning of pre-trained language models has a great success in many NLP 1 ﬁelds. Yet, it is strikingly vulnerable to adversarial examples, e.g. , word substitution 2 attacks using only synonyms can easily fool a BERT-based sentiment analysis 3 model. In this paper, we demonstrate that adversarial training, the prevalent defense 4 technique, does not directly ﬁt a conventional ﬁne-tuning scenario, because it 5 suffers severely from catastrophic forgetting: failing to retain the generic and robust 6 linguistic features that have already been captured by the pre-trained model. In 7 this light, we propose R obust I nformative F ine- T uning (RIFT), a novel adversarial 8 ﬁne-tuning method from an information-theoretical perspective. In particular, RIFT 9 encourages an objective model to retain the features learned from the pre-trained 10 model throughout the entire ﬁne-tuning process, whereas a conventional one only 11 uses the pre-trained weights for initialization. Experimental results show that RIFT 12 consistently outperforms the state-of-the-arts on two popular NLP tasks: sentiment 13 analysis and natural language inference, under different attacks across various 14 pre-trained language models. Our codes are available in Appendix. 15

 Deep models are well-known to be vulnerable to adversarial examples [62, 18, 48, 33]. For instance,

 ﬁne-tuned models pre-trained on very large corpora can be easily fooled by word substitution attacks 18 using only synonyms [2, 56, 30, 12]. This has raised grand security challenges to modern NLP 19 systems, such as spam ﬁltering and malware detection, where pre-trained language models like BERT 20 [11] are widely deployed. 21 Attack algorithms [18, 7, 74, 38, 2] aim to maliciously generate adversarial examples to fool a 22 victim model, while adversarial defense aims at building robustness against them. Among the 23 defense methods, adversarial training [62, 18, 46, 43] is the most effective one [3]. It updates model 24 parameters using perturbed adversarial samples generated on-the-ﬂy and yields consistently robust 25 performance even against the challenging adaptive attacks [3, 66]. 26 However, despite its effectiveness in training from scratch, adversarial training may not directly 27 ﬁt the current NLP paradigm, the ﬁne-tuning of pre-trained language models. First, ﬁne-tuning 28 per se suffers from catastrophic forgetting [44, 17, 32], i.e. , the resultant model tends to over-ﬁt 29 to a small ﬁne-tuning data set, which may deviate too far from the pre-trained knowledge [24, 79]. 30 Second, adversarially ﬁne-tuned models are more likely to forget: adversarial samples are usually 31 out-of-distribution [36, 61], thus they are generally inconsistent with the pre-trained model. As a 32 result, adversarial ﬁne-tuning fails to memorize all the robust and generic linguistic features already 33 learned during pre-training [63, 55], which are however very beneﬁcial for a robust objective model. 34 Addressing the forgetting problem is essential for achieving more robust objective models. In this paper, we follow an information-theoretical lens to look into the forgetting problem: we 42 employ mutual information to measure how well an objective model memorizes the useful features 43 captured before. This motivates our novel adversarial ﬁne-tuning method, Robust Informative Fine- 44 Tuning (RIFT). In addition to ﬁtting a down-stream task as conventions, RIFT maximizes the mutual 45 information between the output of an objective model and that of the pre-trained model conditioned 46 on the class label. 2.1 Notations and Problem Setting 61

 In this paper, we focus on the text classiﬁcation task to introduce our method, while it can be easily 62 extended to other NLP tasks. We suppose random variables X,Y ∼ p D ( x,y ) , where X represents 63 the textual input, Y represents the class label, p D is the data distribution, and x,y are the observed 64 values. Our goal is to build a classiﬁer q ( y ∣ F s ( x )) , where F s (⋅) , referred to as our objective model 65 in the rest of this paper, is a feature extractor ﬁne-tuned from the encoder of a pre-trained language 66 model, e.g., Transformer [70], and q (⋅∣⋅) is parameterized by using an MLP with softmaxed output. 67 We favor a classiﬁer that is robust against adversarial attacks [62, 2], i.e., maintaining high accuracy 68 even given adversarial examples as inputs. 69 2

To begin with, ﬁne-tuning itself suffers from catastrophic 80 forgetting [44, 17, 32]. During ﬁne-tuning, an objective model F s (⋅) tends to continuously deviate 81 away from the pre-trained one to ﬁt a down-stream task [24, 79], and thus the useful information 82 already captured before is less utilized as the ﬁne-tuning proceeds. Further, adversarial ﬁne-tuning 83 suffers even more from the forgetting problem, the reasons of which are given as what follows. 84 (i) Adversarial Fine-Tuning Tends to Forget: Adversarial ﬁne-tuning targets at tackling adversarial 85 examples, which are generally out of the manifold [36, 61] of the pre-training corpora. To additionally 86 handle them, an objective model would be ﬁne-tuned towards a solution that is far away from the 87 optimization starting point, i.e. , the pre-trained model. Figure 2 (b) empirically shows this point: by 88 increasing β in Eq. 2, we emphasize more on robustness instead of vanilla accuracy, and consequently, 89 at the last epoch the distance between models also increases. Besides, adversarial ﬁne-tuning often 90 entails more iterations to converge (several times of normal ﬁne-tuning), which further intensiﬁes the 91 forgetting problem, as the objective model is continuously deviating away as shown in Figure 2 (a). 92 (ii) Adversarial Fine-Tuning Needs to Memorize: Overﬁtting to training data is a dominant phe- 93 nomenon throughout the adversarial training / ﬁne-tuning process [58] . To mitigate overﬁtting and 94 generalize better, adversarial ﬁne-tuning should retain all the generalizable features already captured 95 by the pre-trained model [11, 39]. In addition, adversarial ﬁne -tuning favors an objective model that 96 extracts features invariant to perturbations [68, 26] for robustness . As such, all those generalizable 97 and robust linguistic information captured during pre-training [11, 63] are particularly beneﬁcial and 98 should be memorized. We empirically validates that encouraging memorization does improve both 99 generalization and robustness in Sec. 4.5. 100 To address forgetting, previous methods such as pre-trained weight decay [8, 10] and random mixout 101 [35] have shown their effectiveness in stabilizing ﬁne-tuning [79]. However, they focus only on 102 the parameter space, in which they encourage an object model to be similar to the pre-trained one. 103 3 Distance in the parameter space can only approximately characterize the change in the function space, 104 and fails to take the data distribution into consideration. A more natural way to capture the extent to 105 which a model memorizes or forgets, should be using the mutual information between outputs of the 106 two models, and we provide our solution as follows. 107

2.4 Conditional Mutual Information Better Fits A Down-Stream Task

 147 Above we have introduced the training objective of informative ﬁne-tuning and decomposed it into 148 I ( S ; Y ) and I ( S ; T ∣ Y ) for optimization. However, one may wonder why not maximize I ( S ; T ) 149 directly instead of I ( S ; T ∣ Y ) . From an information-theoretical perspective, if we maximize I ( S ; Y ) 150 and I ( S ; T ) , the intersection of three circles in Figure 1, i.e., the interaction information of them, 151 is repeatedly optimized and might induce conﬂiction. Such a view provides some intuitions, but it 152 is still vague in the sense of what is actually going on. Therefore, we provide a view of the actual 153 implementation, i.e. , contrastive loss. 154 We consider contrastive learning by decomposing it into the encouragement of alignment and unifor- 155 mity following [72]. For example, maximizing I ( S ; T ) = H ( S ) − H ( S ∣ T ) is to decrease H ( S ∣ T ) 156 and increase H ( S ) . Decreasing H ( S ∣ T ) corresponds to encouraging alignment in contrastive learn- 157 ing, which aims to align s i and t i in the sense of inner product when they are projected into a 158 hyper-sphere. In the meanwhile, increasing H ( S ) corresponds to uniformity in contrastive learning, 159 which encourages s i and s j , where i ≠ j , to diffuse over the whole sub-space as uniformly as possible. 160 It can be seen in Figure 3 (c) that, in the space of S 1 , alignment is achieved in that points from two 161 different circles but with the same color are aligned, and uniformity is achieved in that all points 162 diffuse over the whole S 1 . 163 However, when maximizing I ( S ; T ) , the uniformity in the contrastive loss is encouraged over the 164 whole data manifold. Diffusing uniformly over the whole data manifold can be against the objective 165 of maximizing I ( S ; Y ) , which aims to separate the whole data manifold into class-speciﬁc parts as 166 shown in Figure 3 (b). In contrast, maximizing I ( S ; T ∣ Y ) only encourages uniformity inside each 167 class-speciﬁc data manifold, which is complementary to maximizing I ( S ; Y ) , as show in Figure 3 168 (d). In the meanwhile, the alignment is still enforced. More empirical support of this section can be 169 found in Sec. 4.4. 170

3 Related Work

 211 Pre-Trained Language Models: Language modeling aims at estimating the probabilistic density of 212 the textual data distribution. Classicial language modeling methods vary from CBOW, Skip-Gram 213 [45], to GloVe [49] and ELMo [50]. Recently, Transformer [70] has demonstrated its power in 214 language modeling, e.g., GPT [53] and BERT [11]. The representations learned by deep pre-trained 215 language models are shown to be universal and highly generalizable [75] [39], and ﬁne-tuning 216 pre-trained language models becomes a paradigm in many NLP tasks [24, 11]. 217 Adversarial Robustness: DNNs have achieved success in many ﬁelds, but they are susceptible 218 to adversarial examples [62]. In NLP, attacks algorithms include char-level modiﬁcations [23, 13, 219 4, 16, 15, 52], sequence-level manipulations [27, 57, 28, 80], and adversarial word substitutions 220 [2, 38, 56, 30, 78, 76]. For defenses, adversarial training [62, 18, 43] generates adversarial examples 221 on-the-ﬂy and is currently the most effective. Miyato et al. [46] ﬁrst introduce adversarial training to 222 NLP using L 2 -ball to model perturbations, and later more geometry-aligned methods like ﬁrst-order 223 approximation[13], axis-aligned bound [29, 25], and convex hull [12], become favorable. 224 Fine-Tuning and Catastrophic Forgetting: The ﬁne-tuning of pre-trained language models [24, 11] 225 can be very unstable [11], as during ﬁne-tuning the objective model can deviate too much from the 226 pre-trained one, and easily over-ﬁts to small ﬁne-tuning sets [24]. Such phenomenon is referred as 227 catastrophic forgetting [44, 17] in ﬁne-tuning [24, 35, 79]. Methods to address catastrophic forgetting 228 include pre-trained weight decay [8, 10, 79], learning rate decreasing [24], and Mixout regularization 229 [35]. These methods focus on parameter space to constrain the distance between two models while 230 our method addresses forgetting following an information-theoretical perspective. In continuous 231 learning, method like rehearsal-based [40, 54, 37, 1] and regularization-based [32, 14] also aims at the 232 forgetting problem, but under a different setting: they focus on balanced performance on tasks with 233 previous data, while in our setting the pre-training corpora are not available and language modeling 234 is not our concern. 235 Contrastive Learning and Mutual Information: Contrastive learning [47, 22, 59] has demon- 236 strated its power in learning self-supervised representations [9, 20]. The contrastive loss such as 237 InfoNCE[47], InfoMax [22], is proposed as maximizing the mutual information of features in dif- 238 ferent views, and further discussed in [51, 65, 67, 64, 72]. This paper shares the same perspective 239 of information theory, but looks into a different problem, addressing forgetting for ﬁne-tuning. 240 Addressing forgetting motivates our objective of maximizing the mutual information between the 241 outputs of two models, which is different from conventional contrastive learning, and cooperation 242 with ﬁne-tuning motivates the use of conditional mutual information, which is quite different also. 243

4.1 Experimental Setting

 245 Tasks and Datasets: We evaluate the robust accuracy and compare our method with the state-of-the- 246 arts on: (i) Sentiment analysis using the IMDB dataset [42]. (ii) Natural language inference using 247 the SNLI dataset [6]. We mainly focus on robustness against adversarial word substitutions [2, 56], 248 as such attacks preserve the syntactic and semantics very well [29, 76, 12], and are very hard to 249 detect even by humans. Under this attack setting, any word in the input sequence can be substituted 250 by a semantically similar word of it (often its synonym). We evaluate robustness on 1000 random 251 examples from the testset of IMDB and SNLI respectively following [29, 12]. 252 Model Architectures: We examine our methods and compare with state-of-the-arts on the following 253 two prevailing pre-trained language models: (i) BERT-base-uncased [11]. (ii) RoBERTa-base [39]. 254 Attack Algorithms: Two powerful attacks are employed: (i) Genetic [2] based on population 255 algorithm. Aligned with [29, 12], the population size and iterations are set as 60 and 40 respectively. 256 (ii) PWWS [56] based on word saliency. We only attack hypothesis on SNLI aligned with [29, 12]. 257 Substitution Set: We follow [29, 12] to use the substitution set from [2], and the same language 258 model constraint is applied to Genetic attacks and not to PWWS attacks. 259

4.3 Main Result

 282 In this section we compare our method with state-of-the-arts by robustness under attacks. As shown 283 in Tables 1 and 2., RIFT consistently achieves the best robust performance among state-of-the-arts in 284 all datasets across different pre-trained language models under all attacks. For instance, on IMDB our 285 method outperforms the RoBERTa-based runner-up method by 2 . 9% under Genetic attacks and 1 . 9% 286 under PWWS attacks. On SNLI based on BERT, we surpass the runner-up method by 1 . 2% under 287 Genetic attacks and 1 . 1% under PWWS attacks. In addition, RIFT consistently improves robustness 288 upon the adversarial ﬁne-tuning baseline, while the improvements by other methods are not stable. 289 We observe that on SNLI, we surpass the runner-up by a relatively small margin compared to IMDB. 290 This may relate to the dataset property: input from SNLI has a smaller attack space (on average 6 . 5 4 291 combinations of substitutions on SNLI compared to 6 108 on IMDB), and thus smaller absolute space 292 left for improvement. 4.5 Ablation Study and Tradeoff Curve Between Robustness and Vanilla Accuracy 301

 In this section we conduct ablation study on α , which is the weight of L r-info in Eq. 11. It aims 302 to control to what extent the objective model absorbs information from the pre-trained one. As 303 shown in Table 4, a good value of α improves both vanilla accuracy and robust accuracy; e.g. , on 304 IMDB increasing α from 0 to 0 . 1 results in increased vanilla accuracy by 0 . 2% and increased robust 305 accuracy under Genetic attacks by 2 . 4% . This demonstrates that RIFT does motivate the objective 306 model to retain robust and generalizable features that are beneﬁcial to both robustness and vanilla 307 accuracy. When α goes too large, the objective of ﬁne-tuning would focus too much on preserving as 308 much information from the pre-trained model as possible, and thus ignores the down-stream task. 309 One may wonder whether informative ﬁne-tuning itself can improve the clean accuracy upon normal 310 ﬁne-tuning. The answer is yes; e.g. , on IMDB using RoBERTa, informative ﬁne-tuning improves 311 about 0 . 3% clean accuracy upon normal ﬁne-tuning baseline. The improvement is not very signiﬁcant 312 as normal ﬁne-tuning targets at vanilla input only and thus suffers less from the forgetting problem. 313 One common problem in contrastive loss is that the complexity grows quadratically with N , but 314 larger N contributes to tighter bound on the mutual information [47, 51]. One potential improvement 315 is to maintain a dictionary updated on-the-ﬂy like [20] and we will leave it for future exploration. 316 We ﬁnally show the trade-off curve between robustness and vanilla accuracy in Figure 4. As shown, 317 RIFT outperforms the state-of-the-arts in terms of both robust accuracy and vanilla accuracy. It again 318 validates that RIFT indeed helps the objective model capture robust and generalizable information to 319 improve both robustness and vanilla accuracy, rather than trivially trading off one against another. 320

5 Discussion and Conclusion

 321 In this paper, we proposed RIFT to ﬁne-tune a pre-trained language model towards robust down-stream 322 performance. RIFT addresses the forgetting problem from an information-theoretical perspective and 323 consistently outperforms state-of-the-arts in our experiments. We hope that this work can contribute 324 to the NLP robustness in general and thus to more reliable NLP systems in real-life applications. 325 9
