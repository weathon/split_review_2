

COCO-LM: Correcting and Contrasting Text Sequences for Language Model Pretraining

 Anonymous Author(s) AfﬁliationAddress email

Abstract We present a self-supervised learning framework, COCO-LM, that pretrains Lan- 1 guage Models by COrrecting and COntrasting corrupted text sequences. Following 2 ELECTRA-style pretraining, COCO-LM employs an auxiliary language model 3 to corrupt text sequences, upon which it constructs two new tasks for pretraining 4 the main model. The ﬁrst token-level task, Corrective Language Modeling, is 5 to detect and correct tokens replaced by the auxiliary model, in order to better 6 capture token-level semantics. The second sequence-level task, Sequence Con- 7 trastive Learning, is to align text sequences originated from the same source input 8 while ensuring uniformity in the representation space. Experiments on GLUE and 9 SQuAD demonstrate that COCO-LM not only outperforms recent state-of-the-art 10 pretrained models in accuracy, but also improves pretraining efﬁciency. It achieves 11 the MNLI accuracy of ELECTRA with 50% of its pretraining GPU hours. With 12 367 million parameters, it matches the MNLI average accuracy of Megatron with 13 3 . 9 billion parameters. Our code and pretrained models will be open-sourced. 14



1 Introduction 15

 Pretrained language models (PLMs) have reshaped the way AI systems process natural language [11, 16 36, 39, 40]. Before task-speciﬁc ﬁne-tuning, it is now a common practice to ﬁrst pretrain the deep 17 neural networks, often Transformers [53], via a self-supervised token-level language modeling 18 framework [29, 32, 40]. Whether it is autoregressive [39], permutational [63]

,

 or masked language 19 modeling (MLM) [11], the Transformer networks are pretrained to recover some omitted tokens 20 using the rest of input texts. Then the language semantics captured during pretraining are conveyed 21 to downstream tasks via the pretrained Transformer parameters [5, 8, 44]. 22 Recent research [15, 17, 26, 43] observed several challenges in this self-supervised learning frame- 23 work. One challenge is its efﬁciency. After pretrained for a while with the standard token-level 24 language modeling, the networks have already captured the basic language patterns, making a large 25 fraction of pretraining signals no longer informative. Linear improvement in the model effectiveness 26 often requires exponentially more pretraining compute and parameters [26], which is unsustainable. 27 Another challenge is the anisotropy of text representations from pretrained models. The sequence 28 representations from many pretrained models are quite irregular [30, 43] and require dedicated 29 ﬁne-tuning approaches to be useful in sequence-level applications [33, 61]. 30 Clark et al. [7] proposed a new pretraining strategy, ELECTRA, that uses an auxiliary language model 31 (“generator”) to replace tokens in input texts and pretrains the main Transformer (“discriminator”) to 32 detect replaced tokens. This improves the pretraining efﬁciency and effectiveness, but at the same 33 time, pretraining only via binary classiﬁcation hinders the model’s usage on applications that require 34 language modeling capability [6]. It could further distort the representation space as the Transformers 35 are pretrained to output the same “non-replacement” label for all actual tokens. 36 Submitted to 35th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2021). Do not distribute. In this paper, we present a new self-supervised learning approach, COCO-LM, that pretrains Lan- 37 guage Models by COrrecting and COntrasting corrupted text sequences. Following ELECTRA-style 38 pretraining, COCO-LM employs an auxiliary model to corrupt the input texts, upon which it intro- 39 duces two new pretraining tasks for the main Transformer, one at token level and one at sequence 40 level. The token-level task, corrective language modeling (CLM), pretrains the main Transformer 41 to detect and correct the tokens in the corrupted sequences. It uses a multi-task setup to combine 42 the beneﬁts of replaced token detection and language modeling. The sequence-level task, sequence 43 contrastive learning (SCL), pretrains the model to align text sequences originated from the same 44 source sequence and enforce uniformity of the representation space. 45 In our experiments on GLUE [54] and SQuAD [41] benchmarks, COCO-LM not only outperforms 46 recent state-of-the-art pretraining approaches in effectiveness, but also signiﬁcantly improves the 47 pretraining efﬁciency. Under the same setting, COCO-LM matches the MNLI accuracy of RoBERTa 48 and ELECTRA with 60% and 50% of their GPU hours in pretraining, respectively. With 367 million 49 parameters, COCO-LM Large matches the MNLI average accuracy of Megatron 3.9B [49], one of the 50 largest BERT-style model with 3 . 9 billion parameters. Our analyses provide further insights on 51 the beneﬁt of SCL in ensuring alignment and uniformity in the representation space for robust 52 generalization, as well as the advantage of CLM in learning token representations and improving the 53 accuracy on token-level tasks. 54

2 Related Work 55

 Various token-level tasks have been used to pretrain language models. The most classic auto-regressive 56 language modeling is to predict a token given all the previous tokens, or subsequent ones [36, 39]. 57 BERT uses masked language modeling (MLM) that recovers randomly masked tokens using the rest 58 input. XLNet proposes permutation language modeling that conducts MLM in an autoregressive 59 manner [63]; UniLM uses pseudo MLM which uniﬁes autoregressive and MLM tasks [1, 13]. 60 Sequence-level tasks are also explored in pretraining. They often pretrain the model to predict certain 61 co-occurrences of sequence pairs in the corpus. For example, next sentence prediction [11], sentence 62 ordering [28] and previous sentence prediction [56] concatenate two sentences (either correlated or 63 random), feed them to the Transformer, and train the model to classify the pair. 64 Empirically, MLM is still among the most effective tasks to pretrain encoders [29, 32, 40]. 65 RoBERTa [32] found the sentence-level task in BERT not beneﬁtial and discarded it. BART [29] and 66 T5 [40] both observed that MLM is often the most effective task. The empirical advantages of other 67 pretraining tasks are more task-speciﬁc, for example, entity related masks for knowledge intensive 68 applications [21, 25], and sequence-level tasks for long form text modeling [42]. 69 Instead of randomly altering texts, ELECTRA [7] uses a smaller auxiliary Transformer pretrained 70 by MLM to replace some tokens in the text sequences using its language modeling probability, and 71 pretrains the main Transformer to detect the replaced tokens. ELECTRA achieves state-of-the-art 72 accuracy in many language tasks [7]. Later, Clark et el. [6] developed ELECTRIC, which pretrains 73 encoders by contrasting original tokens against negatives sampled from a cloze model. ELECTRIC 74 re-enables the language modeling capability but underperforms ELECTRA in downstream tasks. 75 Our work is also related to contrastive learning which has shown great success in visual representation 76 learning [4, 23, 34]. Its effectiveness of in language is more observed in the ﬁne-tuning stage, for 77 example, in sentence representation [17], dense retrieval [61], and GLUE ﬁne-tuning [20]. 78

 79 In this section, we present the preliminaries of pretraining language models, their challenges, and the 80 self-supervised learning framework of COCO-LM. 81

3.1 Preliminary on Language Model Pretraining 82

 In this work we focus on pretraining BERT-style bidirectional Transformer encoders [11] widely 83 used in language representation tasks. We ﬁrst recap the masked language modeling (MLM) task 84 introduced by BERT [11] and then discuss the pretraining framework from ELECTRA [7]. 85 2 BERT Pretraining uses the masked language modeling task (MLM) [11], which is to take an input 86 sequence X orig = [ x orig 1 , . . . , x orig i , . . . , x orig n ] , with 15% random tokens replaced by [MASK] symbols 87 ( e.g. , the i -th token), and train the model to predict the original tokens at the masked positions: 88 orig where the Transformer generates contextualized representations H = { h i } ni =1 . The MLM Head 89 predicts the masked token from the vocabulary V using the hidden representation h i and token em- 90 beddings x . The pretraining minimizes the MLM loss on the set of masked positions M . Speciﬁcally, 91 ELECTRA Pretraining uses two Transformers, a “generator” pretrained by MLM, and a “discrimi- 92 nator” pretrained using the generator’s outputs. We refer them as auxiliary and main Transformers, 93 as the former is discarded after pretraining. 94 The auxiliary model outputs a corrupted sequence X MLM by sampling from its predicted probability: 95 The masked positions are replaced by sampled tokens considered plausible in context by the auxiliary 96 Transformer, which are more deceiving than random replacements. ELECTRA uses a skinnier 97 auxiliary network ( e.g. , hidden dimension is 1 / 3 of the main model) to control the signal difﬁculty. 98 The main Transformer takes X MLM and classiﬁes the replaced tokens: where 1 ( · ) is the indicator function. The Replaced Token Detection (RTD) head uses a sigmoid linear 100 layer to output the binary probability, and the main Transformer is trained with binary cross entropy loss. The RTD task is trained on all tokens instead of masked ones and improves efﬁciency. The two Transformers are pretrained jointly. The auxiliary model gradually generates more realistic replacement tokens and the main model learns to better detect them. This forms a natural learning curriculum and signiﬁcantly improves ELECTRA’s accuracy in downstream tasks [7].

3.2 Challenges of ELECTRA-Style Pretraining

 Missing Language Modeling Beneﬁts. The classiﬁcation task in ELECTRA is easier and more stable [62], but raises two challenges. The ﬁrst is the lack of language modeling capabil- 110 ity which is a necessity in some tasks [6]. For 111 example, prompt-based learning requires a lan- 112 guage model to generate labels [16, 45, 46]. The 113 second is that the binary classiﬁcation task may 114 not be sufﬁcient to capture certain word-level 115 semantics that are critical for token-level tasks. 116 Squeezing Representation Space. Another 117 challenge is that the representations from 118 Transformer-based language models often reside 119 in a narrow cone, where two random sentences 120 have high similarity scores (lack of uniformity), 121 and closely related sentences may have more 122 different representations (lack of alignment) [15, 17, 31]. 3.3 COCO-LM Pretraining

 COCO-LM also employs an auxiliary Transformer to construct the corrupted text sequence, as in 133 Eqn. (1), but it introduces two new pretraining tasks upon the corrupted sequences, in order to address 134 the challenges previously described. In the rest of this section, we present these two tasks and then 135 the detailed conﬁgurations of COCO-LM. Its framework is illustrated in Figure 2. 136 Corrective Language Modeling (CLM) trains the main Transformer to recover the original tokens, 137 given the corrupted text sequence X MLM : 138 The CLM Head uses the hidden representations H to output a language modeling probability, instead 139 of a binary classiﬁcation score. The forward pass of the CLM Head is the same as All-Token 140 MLM, an ablation version of ELECTRA [7] that consists of a language modeling layer and a binary 141 classiﬁcation layer for the copy mechanism: 142 where w copy is a learnable weight and p copy ( y i | h i ) is the copy mechanism ( y i = 1 when the input 143 token is original and can be directly copied to the output; y i = 0 when the input token needs to be 144 corrected by generating another token from the vocabulary). 145 In ELECTRA, All-Token MLM performs worse than RTD [7]. Language modeling on the corrupted 146 text sequence X MLM is hard as the replaced tokens from the auxiliary model are more deceiving than 147 [MASK] . To improve the language model learning, different from All-Token MLM, CLM employs a 148 multi-task setup that combines the RTD task to explicitly train the copy mechanism p copy ( · ) : 149 L CLM = λ copy L copy + L LM 4 The hyperparameter λ copy balances the weights of the two tasks. The binary cross entropy loss in 150 Eqn. (2) explicitly trains the copy probability. We also use stop gradient ( sg ) to decouple the gradient 151 backpropagation to p copy ( · ) from the LM task. This way, the main Transformer ﬁrst learns the easier 152 classiﬁcation task and then uses it to help learn the harder LM task. 153 CLM combines the advantages of MLM and ELECTRA: The main Transformer is trained with a 154 language modeling objective on all tokens with the help of the binary classiﬁcation task, thus enjoying 155 the efﬁciency beneﬁts of ELECTRA and preserving the language modeling beneﬁts. 156 Sequence Contrastive Learning (SCL) forms a contrastive learning objective upon the sequence 157 encodings to learn more robust representations. Broadly, contrastive learning is to align a positive pair 158 of instances, often different views of the same information [4, 34], in contrast to unrelated negative 159 instances [23, 61]. The different views are often obtained by applying data augmentations on the 160 same input, for example, rotation, cropping, and blurring on visual representations [4, 34], so that the 161 neural networks can learn representations robust to these data alterations. 162 In COCO-LM, the corrupted sequence X MLM already provides a form of data augmentation. We pair 163 it with another argumentation, X crop , a randomly cropped contiguous span of X orig (the length of 164 X crop is 90% of X orig so that the major sequence meaning is preserved), to construct the positive pair 165 and to contrast with random negatives. 166 Speciﬁcally, a training batch B in SCL includes a random set of corrupted and cropped sequences: 167 B = { ( X MLM 1 , X crop 1 ) , . . . , ( X MLM N , X crop N ) } , with X MLM k and X crop k originated from X orig k . A positive 168 contrastive pair ( X, X + ) consists of either ( X MLM k , X crop k ) or ( X crop k , X MLM k ) (symmetrical contrast). 169 The negative instances are all the remaining sequences in the batch B − = B \ { ( X, X + ) } . The 170 contrastive loss is formulated as: 171 where s , s + , s − are the representations of X, X + , X − , respectively, from the main Transformer 172 ( i.e. , h [CLS] ). The similarity metric is cosine similarity (cos) and the temperature τ is set to 1 . 173 As shown in Wang et al. [55], the ﬁrst term in Eqn. (3) (cos ( s , s + ) ) improves alignment of the 174 space. It encourages representations to be robust to the corruptions and the alterations on the original 175 text. The second term in Eqn. (3) promotes uniformity . It pushes unrelated sequences apart in the 176 representation space and ensures low cosine similarity between random data points. Several studies 177 have observed improved generalization ability from better alignment and uniformity [17, 37, 55]. 178 Aligning X MLM with X crop requires the main Transformer to produce sequence representations robust 179 to both token-level ( i.e. , MLM replacements) and sequence-level ( i.e. , cropping) alterations. The 180 model is thus encouraged to reason more using partially altered sequences to recover the original 181 information. 182 Overall Training. The main Transformer is pretrained to correct the corruption (CLM) and to contrast 185 the corrupted sequences with the cropped sequences (SCL). The two Transformers are pretrained 186 jointly with the loss in Eqn. (4). The main Transformer is used in downstream applications. 187 Network Conﬁgurations. Similar to ELECTRA, the auxiliary Transformer is smaller than the main 188 model, but we use different conﬁgurations in the auxiliary model: (1) We reduce the number of layers 189 to 1 / 3 or 1 / 4 but keep its hidden dimension the same with the main model, instead of shrinking its 190 hidden dimensions; (2) We disable the dropouts in it when sampling replacement tokens. We ﬁnd 191 such conﬁgurations empirically more effective and use them as the backbone of COCO-LM. The 192 main Transformer follows the standard architecture of BERT/ELECTRA and can be easily adopted 193 by pipelines in downstream applications with almost no changes. 194 5

4 Experimental Setup

 195 Pretraining Settings. We employ three standard settings, base , base++ , and large++ . Base is the 196 BERT Base training conﬁguration [11]: Pretraining on Wikipedia and BookCorpus [64] ( 16 GB of 197 texts) for 256 million samples on 512 token sequences ( 125 K batches with 2048 batch size). We use 198 the same corpus and 32 , 768 uncased BPE vocabulary [47] as with TUPE [27]. 199 Base++ trains the base size model with larger corpora and/or more training steps. Following recent 200 research [1, 32, 63], we add in OpenWebText [19], CC-News [32] and STORIES [52], to a total of 160 201 GB texts, and train for 4 . 0 billion (with 2048 batch size) samples [32]. We follow the prepossessing 202 of UniLMV2 [1] and use 64 , 000 cased BPE vocabulary. 203 Large++ uses the same training corpora as base++ but pretrains for 2.7 billion samples (2048 batch 204 size) , about 68% of RoBERTa Large . Its Transformer conﬁguration is the same with BERT Large [11]. 205 Model Architecture. Our base / base++ model uses the BERT Base architecture [11]: 12 layer Trans- 206 former, 768 hidden size, plus T5 relative position encoding [40]. Our large++ model is the same 207 with BERT Large , 24 layer and 1024 hidden size, plus T5 relative position encoding [40]. Our auxiliary 208 network uses the same hidden size but a shallow 4 -layer Transformer in base / base++ and a 6 -layer 209 one in large++ . When generating X MLM we disable the dropout in the auxiliary model. 210 Downstream Tasks. We use the tasks included in GLUE [54] and SQuAD 2.0 reading compres- 211 sion [41]. The ﬁne-tuning protocols use the open-source implementation from TUPE [27] on GLUE 212 tasks and huggingface [59] on SQuAD. All our models are evaluated with the same ﬁne-tuning 213 protocols. The reported results are the median/average of ﬁve/ten random seeds in GLUE/SQuAD, to 214 be consistent with recent research [1, 32]. Please refer to Appendix for more details. 215 Baselines. We compare with various pretrained models in each setting. To reduce the variances in 216 data processing/environments, we also pretrain and ﬁne-tune RoBERTa and ELECTRA under exact 217 the same setting with COCO-LM, marked with “(Ours)”. 6 Conclusions and Future Work

 307 In this paper, we present COCO-LM, which pretrains language models using Corrective Language 308 Modeling and Sequence Contrastive Learning upon corrupted text sequences. With standard pre- 309 training settings and Transformer architectures, COCO-LM improves the accuracy on the GLUE and 310 SQuAD benchmarks, while also being more efﬁcient in utilizing pretraining computing resources 311 and Transformer parameters. 312 One limitation of this work is that the contrastive pairs are constructed by simple cropping and 313 MLM replacements. Recent research have shown the effectiveness of advanced data argumentation 314 techniques in ﬁne-tuning language models [17, 38, 51]. A future research direction is to explore 315 better ways to construct contrast pairs in language model pretraining. 316 Despite the empirical advantage of this auxiliary-main dual model framework, the auxiliary Trans- 317 former training is not inﬂuenced by the main Transformer, thus the training signals from the auxiliary 318 model may not be optimal for the main model. To better understand and automate the training of the 319 auxiliary model is another important future research direction. 320 9
