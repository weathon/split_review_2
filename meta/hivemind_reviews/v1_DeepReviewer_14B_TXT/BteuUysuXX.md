## Summary
This paper introduces a novel attack method targeting large vision-language models (VLMs), such as GPT-4, with the aim of inducing high energy consumption and latency during inference. The core idea revolves around crafting 'verbose images' by adding imperceptible perturbations to input images. These perturbations are designed to manipulate the VLM's generation process, causing it to produce longer text sequences, which in turn increases the computational cost and latency. The authors propose three loss functions to optimize the creation of these verbose images: a delayed end-of-sequence (EOS) loss to encourage the model to generate more tokens, an uncertainty loss to increase the randomness of each generated token, and a token diversity loss to enhance the variety of tokens in the generated sequence. These loss functions are balanced using a temporal weight adjustment algorithm. The experimental results, conducted on various VLMs and datasets, demonstrate that the proposed verbose images can significantly increase the length of generated sequences, leading to higher energy consumption and latency compared to original images and other baseline methods. The paper's primary contribution lies in identifying a new attack surface for VLMs by exploiting the relationship between sequence length and computational cost, and in proposing a method to generate adversarial images that effectively trigger this vulnerability. The authors present a comprehensive evaluation of their method, showing its effectiveness across multiple models and datasets, which underscores the potential challenges for real-world applications of VLMs. Overall, the paper highlights a significant security concern related to the availability of VLMs and provides a valuable starting point for further research in this area.

## Strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the introduction of 'verbose images' as a method to induce high energy-latency costs in VLMs is a novel and unique approach. This concept explores a previously underexplored attack surface of these models, shifting the focus from traditional adversarial attacks that aim to mislead the model's output to attacks that target the computational cost of inference. This is a significant contribution, as it highlights a new dimension of vulnerability in VLMs. Second, the authors provide a comprehensive experimental evaluation of their method across multiple VLMs and datasets. This thorough evaluation demonstrates the effectiveness of verbose images in increasing the length of generated sequences and the associated energy-latency costs. The experiments are well-designed and the results are clearly presented, which strengthens the credibility of the findings. The use of multiple models and datasets also ensures that the results are not specific to a particular architecture or dataset, increasing the generalizability of the findings. Third, the paper is well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to follow the methodology and understand the experimental results. The authors provide a detailed explanation of the proposed method, including the loss functions and the optimization process. The use of figures and tables further enhances the clarity of the presentation. Finally, the paper's focus on the practical implications of the attack is commendable. By demonstrating the potential for resource exhaustion, the authors highlight the importance of addressing this vulnerability in real-world deployments of VLMs. This practical perspective adds to the significance of the paper and makes it relevant to both researchers and practitioners.

## Weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling approach to attacking VLMs, I have identified several limitations that warrant further discussion. First, the paper's evaluation is primarily focused on the image captioning task. While the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of verbose images in increasing sequence length and energy consumption for this task, the generalizability of the proposed method to other multi-modal tasks, such as visual question answering (VQA) and visual reasoning, remains unclear. As the paper itself states, 'These models perform the captioning task for the image under their default prompt template.' This narrow focus limits the scope of the findings and raises questions about the method's applicability in more complex scenarios. The paper does not explore how the proposed loss functions and optimization strategies would need to be adapted for tasks that involve more intricate interactions between visual and textual inputs. This is a significant limitation, as VLMs are often used in diverse applications beyond image captioning. Second, the paper lacks a discussion on potential defenses against verbose image attacks. The authors do not explore techniques such as input sanitization, which could involve filtering or modifying the perturbed images before they are fed into the VLM, or model hardening techniques, which could involve modifying the VLM itself to be more resistant to verbose images. This absence of a discussion on countermeasures leaves a gap in understanding the real-world implications of this attack. It is crucial to investigate how these attacks can be mitigated to ensure the robustness of VLMs in practical settings. The paper does not address the trade-offs between the effectiveness of these defenses and their impact on the model's performance on benign inputs. Third, while the paper analyzes the impact of different perturbation magnitudes on the energy-latency cost and provides the LIPIS score as a measure of perceptual difference, the analysis could be more granular. The paper shows that increasing the perturbation magnitude leads to longer generated sequences and higher energy-latency costs, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the method to different perturbation levels. It is important to understand the minimum perturbation required to achieve a significant increase in energy consumption and latency, as this would determine the attack's stealthiness. The paper does not provide a qualitative assessment of the perceptual quality of the images at different perturbation levels. This lack of a detailed analysis limits the practical applicability of the method. Fourth, the paper does not include experiments or discussion on the impact of fine-tuning on the effectiveness of verbose images. It is possible that VLMs could be fine-tuned to mitigate the effects of these attacks, and the paper does not address this scenario. This is a significant limitation, as it raises questions about the long-term viability of the proposed attack. Finally, the paper does not explicitly define or measure an 'attack success rate.' While the paper demonstrates an increase in sequence length, energy consumption, and latency, it does not provide a metric for determining whether the attack is successful or not. This lack of a clear success metric makes it difficult to evaluate the effectiveness of the method. The paper also does not explore how the success rate changes with different perturbation budgets. These limitations, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the conclusions and practical implications of the research. I have high confidence in these identified weaknesses, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and lack of discussion on these critical aspects.

## Suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for future research. First, the authors should expand the evaluation of the proposed method to include a broader range of multi-modal tasks, such as visual question answering (VQA) and visual reasoning. This would involve adapting the loss functions and optimization strategies to the specific requirements of these tasks. For instance, in VQA, the goal would be to generate longer, more complex answers, while in visual reasoning, the focus would be on extending the reasoning chain. Furthermore, the evaluation should include a diverse set of datasets for each task to ensure the generalizability of the findings. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the attack's capabilities and limitations across different task scenarios. Second, the authors should investigate potential defenses against verbose image attacks. This could include techniques such as input sanitization, which could involve filtering or modifying the perturbed images before they are fed into the VLM. This could include methods like denoising or adversarial training to make the model more robust to perturbations. Another avenue to explore is model hardening, which involves modifying the VLM itself to be more resistant to verbose images. This could involve techniques like adversarial training or regularization methods that penalize the model for generating overly long sequences. A thorough analysis of these defenses would provide a more complete picture of the practical implications of the proposed attack and guide the development of more robust VLMs. The paper should also discuss the trade-offs between the effectiveness of these defenses and their impact on the model's performance on benign inputs. Third, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the trade-off between the magnitude of the perturbation and the resulting increase in energy-latency cost. This analysis should include both quantitative metrics, such as the LPIPS score, and qualitative assessments of the perturbed images. The authors should explore the sensitivity of the method to different perturbation levels and determine the minimum perturbation required to achieve a significant increase in energy consumption and latency. This would help to understand the stealthiness of the attack. Fourth, the authors should investigate the impact of fine-tuning on the effectiveness of verbose images. This would involve fine-tuning VLMs on datasets that include verbose images and evaluating the resulting changes in energy consumption and latency. This would provide insights into the long-term viability of the proposed attack and the potential for models to adapt to these perturbations. Finally, the authors should define and measure an 'attack success rate.' This could involve setting a threshold for the increase in sequence length, energy consumption, or latency and then evaluating the percentage of attacks that meet this threshold. The authors should also explore how the success rate changes with different perturbation budgets. These suggestions, which are directly linked to the identified weaknesses, would significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its practical relevance.
