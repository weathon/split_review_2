## summary
This paper introduces a novel data selection method, termed GOT-D (Gradients of Optimal Transport for Data Selection), designed to enhance the pre-fine-tuning of large language models (LLMs). The core idea behind GOT-D is to select a subset of unlabeled data that effectively bridges the distributional gap between the pre-training data and the target task data. Unlike traditional methods that focus on aligning the selected data with the target distribution, GOT-D prioritizes data that, when used for pre-fine-tuning, shifts the model's representation closer to the target task distribution. This is achieved by leveraging optimal transport (OT) to measure the distance between the pre-training and target distributions and then selecting data that minimizes this distance. The authors theoretically justify their approach, demonstrating its optimality under certain conditions, and show that it can be efficiently computed using the dual formulation of OT. The method is designed to be computationally efficient, scaling to millions of samples within a single GPU hour. Empirically, the authors evaluate GOT-D on a variety of tasks, including model detoxification, domain adaptation, and general language understanding, demonstrating its superiority over existing data selection methods in terms of both effectiveness and efficiency. The paper's key contributions lie in its novel perspective on data selection for pre-fine-tuning, its theoretical grounding in optimal transport, and its practical implementation that allows for scalable data selection. By focusing on the shift from the pre-training distribution rather than solely aligning with the target distribution, GOT-D offers a more nuanced approach to data selection for LLMs. The authors' work is significant because it addresses the challenge of selecting relevant data for pre-fine-tuning, which is crucial for improving the performance of LLMs on downstream tasks, particularly when labeled data is scarce. The paper's findings suggest that carefully selecting data for pre-fine-tuning can lead to substantial improvements in model performance, and the proposed method provides a practical and efficient way to achieve this. The authors also open-source their code, making the benefits of cost-effective fine-tuning more accessible to the broader research community. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, theoretically sound, and empirically validated approach to data selection for pre-fine-tuning of LLMs, making a valuable contribution to the field.

## strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper is exceptionally well-written and organized, making it easy to follow the authors' line of reasoning. The motivation for the work is clearly articulated, and the problem definition is precise. The authors effectively highlight the limitations of existing data selection methods, which often focus on aligning the selected data with the target distribution without considering the pre-training distribution. This clear problem statement sets the stage for the introduction of GOT-D. The proposed method itself is a significant strength. The use of optimal transport to measure the distance between distributions is a well-established technique, and the authors provide a solid theoretical justification for their approach. They demonstrate that minimizing the OT distance between the pre-training and target distributions is optimal under certain conditions, which adds a strong theoretical foundation to their work. Furthermore, the authors show that this optimization can be efficiently computed using the dual formulation of OT, which is crucial for practical applications. The practical implementation of GOT-D is another notable strength. The authors claim that their method is significantly faster than existing techniques, scaling to millions of samples within a single GPU hour. While I noted a lack of detailed quantitative comparisons of computational cost, the paper does provide evidence of its scalability. The empirical evaluation of GOT-D is comprehensive, covering a diverse range of tasks, including model detoxification, domain adaptation, and general language understanding. The results consistently show that GOT-D outperforms existing data selection methods, demonstrating its effectiveness across different scenarios. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of their results, highlighting the strengths and limitations of their approach. Finally, the open-sourcing of the code is a commendable practice that enhances the reproducibility and accessibility of the work. This allows other researchers to build upon their findings and further explore the potential of GOT-D. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, theoretically sound, and empirically validated approach to data selection for pre-fine-tuning of LLMs, making a valuable contribution to the field.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant attention. First, the paper lacks a detailed discussion and empirical evaluation of the sensitivity of GOT-D to various hyperparameters and design choices within the optimal transport framework. Specifically, the authors do not explore the impact of different cost functions, such as the squared Euclidean distance or cosine distance, on the performance of the method. The paper mentions using a symmetric positive-definite cost function but does not justify this choice or explore alternatives. Furthermore, the paper does not analyze how the choice of regularization parameters affects the stability and convergence of the OT computation. While the authors mention using entropy regularization, they do not provide any empirical evidence to support this choice or explore the impact of different regularization strengths. The paper also lacks an investigation into the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency when using approximation methods for OT. The authors mention using GPU implementation for efficiency, but they do not provide a detailed analysis of the accuracy and efficiency of the approximation methods used. This lack of sensitivity analysis is a significant limitation, as it leaves open questions about the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly lacks these discussions and experiments. Second, the paper does not compare GOT-D with other data selection methods that are based on different criteria, such as diversity, representativeness, or uncertainty. The paper focuses on comparing GOT-D with methods that also aim to align distributions, such as Random selection, RTP, and DSIR, but it does not explore the landscape of other data selection strategies. For instance, the paper does not compare GOT-D with methods that select data based on the entropy of the model's predictions or the distance of the data points from the decision boundary. Similarly, the paper does not compare GOT-D with methods that select data based on the diversity of the selected subset or the representativeness of the selected subset with respect to the target distribution. This lack of comparison with diverse data selection strategies limits the understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of GOT-D relative to other approaches. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's experimental section clearly lacks these comparisons. Third, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of how the selected data is used for fine-tuning. While the paper mentions that the model is fine-tuned using Masked Language Modeling (MLM) with a 15% masking rate, it does not specify other crucial hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and the number of epochs. The paper also does not analyze how the fine-tuning performance varies with different fine-tuning settings or different sizes of the selected subset. While the paper does show results with different sizes of the selected subset, it does not provide a detailed analysis of how the fine-tuning performance varies with different fine-tuning settings. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results and understand the practical implications of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly omits these details. Fourth, the paper acknowledges that GOT-D is not intended for tasks requiring domain knowledge that are very different from the scope of pre-training data, but it does not provide any empirical evidence to support this claim. The authors state that adapting LLMs pre-trained only on English literature to tasks requiring expertise in a programming language would not be effective, but they do not include any experiments to demonstrate this limitation. This lack of empirical validation weakens the claim and leaves open questions about the applicability of GOT-D in different scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the limitation but does not provide any empirical evidence. Fifth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of GOT-D compared to other data selection methods. While the authors claim that their method is significantly faster than existing techniques, they do not provide a quantitative comparison of the computational cost with the baseline methods. The paper mentions that the selection process takes a few minutes for millions of samples, but it does not provide a detailed benchmark of the time required for data selection and the memory usage. This lack of detailed computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a detailed quantitative comparison of computational cost. Finally, the paper does not discuss the potential impact of the choice of the pre-training distribution on the performance of GOT-D. The authors assume that the candidate dataset is a proxy for the pre-training distribution, but they do not explore how the performance of GOT-D varies when using different pre-training distributions. This lack of analysis limits the understanding of the robustness of the method to variations in the pre-training data. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper assumes a proxy for the pre-training distribution and does not include experiments with varying pre-training distributions.

## suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for this paper. First, the authors should conduct a thorough sensitivity analysis of GOT-D to various hyperparameters and design choices within the optimal transport framework. This analysis should include experiments with different cost functions, such as the squared Euclidean distance and cosine distance, and a detailed discussion of the rationale behind the chosen cost function. The authors should also explore the impact of different regularization parameters on the stability and convergence of the OT computation, providing empirical evidence to support their choice of regularization strength. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency when using approximation methods for OT, and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate approximation techniques based on the size of the dataset and the desired level of accuracy. This analysis should include empirical results demonstrating the effect of these choices on the final performance of the fine-tuned model. Second, the authors should include a comparison of GOT-D with other data selection methods that are based on different criteria, such as diversity, representativeness, or uncertainty. This comparison should include methods that select data based on the entropy of the model's predictions, the distance of the data points from the decision boundary, the diversity of the selected subset, and the representativeness of the selected subset with respect to the target distribution. This comparison should not only focus on the final performance of the fine-tuned model but also on the computational cost and the interpretability of the selected data. A more comprehensive comparison would provide a better understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of GOT-D and its position relative to existing techniques. Third, the authors should provide more details on the fine-tuning process. This includes specifying the fine-tuning objective, the learning rate, the number of epochs, and any other relevant hyperparameters. It is also important to analyze how the fine-tuning performance varies with different fine-tuning settings and different sizes of the selected subset. This analysis should include a discussion of the potential limitations of the proposed method, such as the possibility of overfitting to the selected subset or the sensitivity to the choice of the fine-tuning parameters. A more detailed explanation of the fine-tuning process would make the paper more reproducible and provide a better understanding of the practical implications of the proposed method. Fourth, the authors should provide empirical evidence to support their claim that GOT-D is not intended for tasks requiring domain knowledge that are very different from the scope of pre-training data. This could be achieved by evaluating the method on a dataset that requires specialized knowledge, such as a dataset related to medical or legal text. This would help to clarify the scope of the method and identify scenarios where it might not be applicable. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to compare the performance of GOT-D with other data selection techniques in these challenging scenarios. Fifth, the authors should provide a detailed quantitative comparison of the computational cost of GOT-D with other data selection methods. This comparison should include the time required for data selection, the memory usage, and the scalability of the method with respect to the size of the dataset. This analysis would provide a better understanding of the practical applicability of the method. Finally, the authors should investigate the impact of the choice of the pre-training distribution on the performance of GOT-D. This could be achieved by evaluating the method with different pre-training distributions and analyzing how the performance varies. This analysis would help to identify the optimal pre-training distribution for the proposed method and provide a better understanding of its robustness. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and provide a more comprehensive and robust analysis of their proposed method.
