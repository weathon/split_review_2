## summary
This paper introduces a novel EEG dataset designed to capture brain responses at the word level during reading, with a specific focus on semantic relevance. The authors collected EEG data from 15 participants as they read sentences extracted from Wikipedia articles. The task involved participants selecting a topic of interest, and then reading sentences from documents either related or unrelated to that topic. This setup allowed for the creation of a dataset where word-level EEG signals are time-locked to the presentation of semantically relevant or irrelevant information. The core methodological approach involves a controlled experimental design where participants read sentences word-by-word on a screen, with each word presented for a fixed duration of 700ms. The EEG data was then preprocessed and segmented into epochs corresponding to each word. The authors also conducted a relevance assessment at the word level, where three external annotators labeled each word as either semantically relevant (1) or irrelevant (0) with respect to the topic of the document. The primary outcome of this work is the creation and release of this new EEG dataset, which is intended to facilitate research in psycholinguistics, brain-computer interfaces, and information retrieval systems. The authors also provide benchmark experiments using several machine learning models to classify words and sentences based on their relevance. These experiments serve as a baseline for future research using the dataset. The dataset contains 23,270 EEG recordings, with each participant reading eight different topics and 16 documents, resulting in a substantial amount of data for analysis. The authors emphasize the dataset's potential for advancing relevance-based systems in human-computer interaction, particularly for real-time language relevance detection. The benchmark experiments, while useful, are primarily intended to establish a baseline for future work and demonstrate the dataset's potential for semantic relevance detection. The authors also provide a detailed description of the experimental setup and data collection process, which is crucial for ensuring the reproducibility and usability of the dataset by the broader research community. The paper also includes an analysis of the EEG data, showing differences in brain activity between relevant and irrelevant words, which further highlights the potential of this dataset for studying semantic processing. The authors acknowledge the limitations of the study, such as the relatively small sample size and the homogeneity of the participant pool, and suggest directions for future research to address these limitations. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution by providing a new resource for the research community, which can be used to further our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying language processing and semantic relevance detection.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in the introduction of a novel EEG dataset focused on semantic text relevance at the word level. This is a significant contribution, as existing EEG datasets often lack the fine-grained temporal resolution and explicit relevance annotations that this dataset provides. The controlled experimental design, where participants read sentences word-by-word with a fixed presentation time, is a methodological strength. This approach minimizes confounding factors and ensures that the EEG signals are time-locked to the presentation of individual words, which is crucial for accurate analysis. The authors' decision to collect data from participants who were reading text that was either semantically relevant or irrelevant to their self-selected topics is also a strength. This approach makes the task more ecologically valid than using pre-defined topics, and it allows for the study of relevance in a more naturalistic context. The authors also provide a clear and detailed description of the data collection and processing procedures, which is essential for ensuring the reproducibility and usability of the dataset. The inclusion of benchmark experiments using multiple machine learning models is another strength. These experiments provide a useful baseline for future research and demonstrate the dataset's potential for semantic relevance detection. The authors' decision to release the dataset and code openly is commendable, as it will facilitate further research in this area. The dataset's potential for advancing research in psycholinguistics, brain-computer interfaces, and information retrieval systems is also a significant strength. The authors have clearly articulated the potential applications of the dataset, which will likely motivate other researchers to use it. Finally, the authors' analysis of the EEG data, showing differences in brain activity between relevant and irrelevant words, is a valuable contribution. This analysis provides initial evidence for the dataset's validity and highlights its potential for studying semantic processing. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of the study, which is a sign of intellectual honesty and rigor. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution by providing a new resource for the research community, which can be used to further our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying language processing and semantic relevance detection.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a valuable contribution, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the dataset's limited size, with only 15 participants, raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings. While the authors mention that this sample size is consistent with other EEG studies, the relatively small number of participants may limit the statistical power of the study and the robustness of the conclusions. This is particularly relevant when considering the high variability inherent in EEG data. The authors themselves acknowledge this limitation in Section 6, stating that the participant pool is relatively homogeneous and that collecting data from more diverse populations could improve generalizability. This limitation is not just about the number of participants, but also about the diversity of the participant pool, which is primarily composed of students from a single institution. This lack of diversity could introduce biases that affect the performance of models trained on this dataset when applied to other populations. Second, the tasks used in the study, which are limited to binary word and sentence relevance classification, may not fully capture the complexity of semantic text relevance. The relevance classification is a binary task (relevant or irrelevant), which oversimplifies the nuanced nature of semantic relevance. The dataset does not account for degrees of relevance, which could be important for a more comprehensive understanding of semantic processing. This binary classification may not reflect real-world scenarios where relevance is often a continuous variable. The authors do not explore more complex tasks that could capture the nuances of semantic relevance, such as ranking sentences based on their relevance to a given topic. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of potential confounding factors, such as individual differences in reading speed, working memory, or prior knowledge. These factors could influence the EEG data and introduce noise or bias into the relevance classification tasks. For example, participants with faster reading speeds might process words differently than those with slower reading speeds, which could affect the EEG signals. The authors also do not discuss the potential impact of task-related cognitive load on the EEG recordings. The cognitive load associated with the reading task itself could vary across participants and influence the EEG signals, making it difficult to isolate the neural correlates of semantic relevance. Fourth, the study uses a fixed 700ms window for word presentation, which may not account for variations in cognitive processing time for different words or sentence structures. This fixed window could truncate the EEG signal for words that elicit later cognitive responses, potentially leading to a loss of relevant information and affecting the accuracy of relevance classification, especially for complex or ambiguous words. The authors justify the 250-950ms analysis window based on existing research, but the fixed presentation time might not align with the natural processing time for all words. Fifth, while the authors report inter-annotator agreement using Fleiss' Kappa, the annotation process is still subjective and does not account for nuanced or context-specific interpretations. The annotation process treats all relevant words as equally relevant, which is a simplification that may not reflect real-world semantic processing. Although the authors report a substantial agreement between annotators, the lack of a more nuanced annotation scheme is a limitation. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the potential confounding effects of word frequency or predictability on the EEG signals. High-frequency or predictable words might elicit different brain responses compared to low-frequency or unpredictable words, which could confound the relevance effects. The absence of a control for these factors makes it difficult to isolate the neural correlates of semantic relevance. The authors do not mention controlling for these factors in the data collection or analysis, which is a significant oversight. In summary, these weaknesses, particularly the limited dataset size, the oversimplified relevance task, the lack of detailed limitations analysis, the fixed word presentation window, the uncontrolled confounding factors, and the lack of a thorough gap analysis, significantly impact the conclusions that can be drawn from this study. These limitations should be addressed in future research to improve the validity and generalizability of the findings.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, the authors should prioritize expanding the dataset to include more participants and a greater variety of texts. Increasing the number of participants would improve the statistical power of the study and enhance the generalizability of the findings. A more diverse participant pool, including individuals from different age groups, educational backgrounds, and cultural contexts, would also help to mitigate potential biases. Furthermore, including a wider range of text types, such as different genres, lengths, and complexities, would make the dataset more representative of real-world scenarios. This would also allow for the development of more robust models that can generalize across different text types. Second, the authors should explore more complex tasks that involve semantic text relevance. Instead of relying solely on binary classification, they could incorporate tasks that require participants to identify the most relevant sentence in a paragraph or to rank sentences based on their relevance to a given topic. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the dataset and its potential for various applications. Additionally, the authors could investigate the use of more sophisticated machine learning models that can capture the nuances of semantic relevance, such as models that incorporate contextual information or models that can handle hierarchical structures in text. Third, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the limitations of the dataset, including potential biases and confounding factors. This could involve collecting additional data on participant characteristics, such as reading speed, working memory capacity, and prior knowledge of the topics. The authors should also investigate the impact of different instructions on the EEG data and explore methods for mitigating any potential biases. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to analyze the EEG data for artifacts and outliers, and to develop methods for handling these issues. A detailed discussion of these limitations would provide a more complete picture of the dataset and its potential applications. Fourth, the authors should address the limitations of using a fixed 700ms window for word presentation by exploring alternative methods for handling variable word presentation times. One approach could involve using a dynamic windowing technique that adapts to the length of each word, ensuring that the entire EEG signal is captured. Another approach could be to investigate the use of recurrent neural networks or other time-series models that can handle variable-length input sequences. Additionally, the study should consider the potential impact of word length on the EEG signal and control for this factor in the analysis. This could involve including word length as a covariate in the model or using a word length-matched control condition. Fifth, to improve the reliability and validity of the relevance annotations, the authors should implement a more rigorous annotation process. This could involve calculating inter-annotator agreement metrics such as Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha to assess the consistency of the annotations. The study should also consider using a multi-label annotation scheme that allows for nuanced interpretations of relevance, rather than a simple binary relevant/irrelevant classification. This would enable the study to capture the degree of relevance, which could be important for understanding the neural correlates of semantic processing. Furthermore, the study should provide clear guidelines for annotators to ensure that the annotations are consistent and reliable. Finally, the authors should address the potential confounding effects of word frequency and predictability on the EEG signals. This could involve collecting word frequency data from a large corpus and including it as a covariate in the analysis. The study should also consider using a word predictability measure, such as surprisal, to control for the effects of word predictability on the EEG signals. Additionally, the study should explore the use of a control condition that involves reading non-relevant texts with similar word frequency and predictability profiles. This would enable the study to isolate the neural correlates of semantic relevance from the effects of word frequency and predictability. Furthermore, the study should investigate the potential impact of task-related cognitive load on the EEG recordings. This could involve measuring cognitive load using a secondary task or a subjective rating scale. The study should also consider using a control condition that involves reading non-relevant texts under the same cognitive load conditions. By addressing these issues, the study can ensure that the EEG signals are not confounded by word frequency, predictability, or task-related cognitive load. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly improve the quality and usability of the dataset and would address the major limitations identified in this review.

First, how does the fixed 700ms window for word presentation affect the EEG signal for words that might require longer cognitive processing times? Could this impact the accuracy of relevance classification, especially for complex or ambiguous words? I am curious about the rationale behind this specific time window and whether the authors considered alternative approaches. Second, how did the authors ensure consistency in relevance annotation across different annotators? While they report Fleiss' Kappa, could they provide more details on the annotation process and any specific guidelines given to the annotators? I am also interested in whether the authors considered a more nuanced annotation scheme that could capture degrees of relevance, rather than a simple binary classification. Third, what measures were taken to control for confounding factors such as word frequency or predictability, which could influence brain responses independently of semantic relevance? The paper does not mention any controls for these factors, and I am curious about the potential impact of these variables on the results. Fourth, how does the dataset account for individual differences in reading speed or cognitive processing, and how might these differences affect the results? The paper does not provide any information on participant reading speeds or cognitive abilities, and I am concerned about the potential impact of these individual differences on the EEG data. Fifth, could the authors elaborate on the potential impact of task-related cognitive load on the EEG recordings? Were there any measures in place to mitigate this effect? I am interested in whether the authors considered the cognitive demands of the task and how these demands might influence the EEG signals. Sixth, how well does the dataset generalize to diverse populations, given that the participants are primarily students from a limited demographic? Could the authors discuss any plans for expanding the dataset to include more diverse participants? I am concerned about the potential biases introduced by the limited demographic scope of the participant pool. Finally, how does this dataset compare to other publicly available EEG datasets in terms of size, quality, and applicability to semantic text relevance detection? A more detailed comparison with existing datasets would help to contextualize the contributions of this work and highlight its unique features. These questions are intended to clarify some of the methodological choices and potential limitations of the study, and I believe that addressing these questions would significantly improve the paper.
