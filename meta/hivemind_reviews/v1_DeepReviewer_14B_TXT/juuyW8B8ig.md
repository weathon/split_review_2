## Summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for learning language-informed visual concept representations by distilling knowledge from pre-trained vision-language models. The core idea revolves around training a set of concept encoders, each dedicated to extracting disentangled concept embeddings from images along specific, language-informed concept axes. These axes, such as color, category, material, style, and season, are pre-defined for each dataset. The training process leverages synthetic images generated by a pre-trained Text-to-Image (T2I) model, DeepFloyd, and employs a combination of a reconstruction loss and an anchoring loss. The reconstruction loss encourages the generated image, conditioned on the extracted concept embeddings, to match the original input image. The anchoring loss, a key innovation, anchors the extracted concept embeddings to text embeddings obtained from a pre-trained Visual Question Answering (VQA) model, BLIP. This anchoring mechanism aims to improve the disentanglement of the concept embeddings by leveraging the semantic structure of language. At inference time, the trained concept encoders can extract concept embeddings from a test image, which can then be remixed or edited to generate novel images with different concept compositions. Furthermore, the authors propose a lightweight test-time finetuning procedure to adapt the encoders to novel concepts not seen during training. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through qualitative and quantitative experiments, showing that their method achieves better disentanglement and compositionality compared to text-based prompting baselines. The paper's main contribution lies in its ability to learn disentangled visual concept representations that can be manipulated and composed to generate novel images, offering a flexible and controllable approach to image synthesis. The use of a pre-trained T2I model for data generation and a pre-trained VQA model for text anchoring allows the method to learn effectively from synthetic data, avoiding the need for extensive human annotations. The test-time finetuning procedure further enhances the method's adaptability to new concepts, making it a versatile tool for image editing and generation.

## Strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The core idea of learning language-informed visual concept representations by distilling knowledge from pre-trained vision-language models is both innovative and promising. The approach of using concept encoders to extract disentangled concept embeddings along predefined concept axes is a clever way to enable more flexible and controllable image generation. The use of a pre-trained T2I model, DeepFloyd, to generate synthetic training data is a practical solution that avoids the need for extensive human annotations, which is a significant advantage. The introduction of the anchoring loss, which leverages a pre-trained VQA model, BLIP, to anchor the concept embeddings to text embeddings, is a novel and effective way to improve the disentanglement of the learned representations. This approach cleverly reuses the disentangled nature of linguistic concepts to guide the learning of visual concepts. The method's ability to extract disentangled concept embeddings from images, which can then be remixed or edited to generate novel images with different concept compositions, is a significant achievement. The qualitative results presented in the paper demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in achieving disentanglement and compositionality. The test-time finetuning procedure, while not without its limitations, is a valuable addition that allows the method to adapt to novel concepts not seen during training. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a broader audience. The authors provide a clear explanation of their method and the underlying concepts, which is commendable. The quantitative results, while not as extensive as I would have liked, do provide some evidence of the method's effectiveness, particularly in terms of CLIP alignment score. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and technically sound approach to learning visual concept representations, with several innovative contributions that have the potential to advance the field of image synthesis and editing.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the training dataset is limited in scope, consisting of only five domains: fruits, figurines, furniture, art, and clothing. The paper explicitly states this in Section 4.1, where it mentions, "We train the concept encoders only using synthetic images generated by DeepFloyd from 5 different domains, including \emph{fruits}, \emph{figurines}, \emph{furniture}, \emph{art}, and \emph{clothing}." This limited number of domains raises concerns about the generalization ability of the proposed method to unseen concepts and compositions outside of these specific categories. The paper does not provide sufficient detail on the diversity of the generated synthetic data used for training, making it difficult to assess the model's ability to generalize to more complex or abstract concepts not represented in the training data. This lack of diversity in the training data is a significant limitation that could impact the method's practical applicability. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's description of the training data. Second, the experimental evaluation is limited in several ways. The paper primarily compares its method with two text-based prompting baselines, Null-text Inversion and InstructPix2Pix, which were published in 2022. As stated in Section 4.3, "We compare to two existing methods. Null-text Inversion performs concept editing by first inverting the input image to token embeddings of Stable-Diffusion and then applying Prompt-to-Prompt, which modifies cross attention maps for editing... InstructPix2Pix is a conditional diffusion model for text-based image editing." While these are relevant baselines, the field of controllable generation and editing has advanced since then, and the paper does not adequately justify why more recent and relevant methods were not included. This lack of comparison with more recent state-of-the-art methods makes it difficult to assess the true performance and novelty of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the evaluation lacks a comprehensive analysis of the disentanglement quality, relying primarily on qualitative examples rather than quantitative metrics. While the paper uses CLIP alignment score as a quantitative metric (Table 1), this metric does not directly measure the degree of disentanglement. The paper relies heavily on qualitative examples in figures to demonstrate disentanglement, which is not sufficient to validate the claim. There is no metric to measure the degree of disentanglement, making it difficult to assess the effectiveness of the proposed method in achieving this goal. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is evident from the paper's experimental setup and the lack of standard disentanglement metrics. Third, the paper claims that the proposed method can generalize to unseen concepts via test-time finetuning, but this claim is not sufficiently supported by empirical evidence. The paper mentions in Section 4.2, "While the encoders have only seen a limited range of concepts during training due to the small size of the training dataset, it can be quickly adapted to unseen concepts with the lightweight test-time finetuning procedure in \cref{subsect:method_training_inference}, as shown in \Cref{fig:exp_generalization}." While Figure 7 shows qualitative examples of generalization after test-time finetuning, there is a lack of rigorous quantitative evaluation of this process. The paper does not provide metrics on how well the model adapts to unseen concepts after finetuning, compared to its performance without finetuning or compared to other adaptation methods. The paper mentions in Section 3.3, "After training, the encoders have learned to generate outputs within a relatively narrow region of the embedding space, which allows the model to adapt to the test images shown in \Cref{fig:exp_generalization} within around 600 iterations while maintaining disentanglement and compositional capability." However, this is not a quantitative evaluation of the adaptation process. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks quantitative evidence to support the claim of effective generalization through test-time finetuning. Fourth, the method relies on the quality of the pre-trained VQA model, BLIP, for obtaining text anchors. The paper states in the Introduction, "To do so, we reuse the disentangled nature of linguistic concepts and ground the predictions to a set of discrete text anchors in the concept embeddings space, which can be obtained by querying a pre-trained generic Visual Question Answering (VQA) model, \blip..." and in Section 3.2, "Specifically, for each training image $\mathbf{x}$ and for each concept axis of interest (\eg, \texttt{color}) indexed by $k$, we query the \blip model $\Psi$ with the image $\mathbf{x}$ and a question $q_k$ in natural language that is specific to this concept axis, \eg, ``what is the \texttt{color} of the object in the image'." However, BLIP is not perfect and may make mistakes in some cases. The paper does not analyze how sensitive the method is to the quality of these text anchors, and whether errors in BLIP's predictions lead to significant degradation in the disentanglement or compositionality of the learned concept embeddings. For example, if BLIP incorrectly identifies the color of an object, how does this impact the learned color embedding and its ability to be composed with other concepts? My confidence in this weakness is high, as the method's reliance on BLIP is explicitly stated, and the paper lacks any analysis of the impact of BLIP's potential errors. Finally, the method requires training a separate set of concept encoders for each dataset, which could be computationally expensive and time-consuming, especially for large datasets. The paper mentions in Section 4.1, "Training on one dataset takes approximately $12$ hours on one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU." This indicates that the training process is not trivial, and the computational cost could be a limiting factor for practical applications. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the training time for one dataset, and it is reasonable to assume that the cost would increase for larger datasets.

## Suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for this paper. First, the authors should significantly expand the training dataset to include a wider range of domains and more complex concepts. This would involve generating synthetic data using the pre-trained T2I model for a more diverse set of categories, including more abstract concepts and complex scenes. It would also be beneficial to include a detailed analysis of the synthetic data generation process, including the diversity of the generated images and the range of concepts covered. Furthermore, the authors should conduct experiments on held-out datasets that contain concepts and compositions not seen during training. This would provide a more realistic assessment of the model's ability to generalize to new scenarios. The evaluation should also include a quantitative analysis of the model's performance on these held-out datasets, rather than relying solely on qualitative examples. Second, the authors should include more recent and relevant methods in their experimental evaluation. This would provide a more accurate assessment of the proposed method's performance relative to the current state-of-the-art. The authors should consider using more comprehensive metrics to evaluate the disentanglement quality, such as those used in the disentanglement learning literature. This would provide a more objective and quantitative measure of the model's ability to disentangle different concept axes. The paper should also include a more detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed method, including scenarios where it may fail to generalize or disentangle concepts effectively. This would provide a more balanced and realistic assessment of the method's capabilities. Third, the authors need to provide more substantial evidence to support their claim of generalizing to unseen concepts via test-time fine-tuning. This should include a rigorous evaluation of the fine-tuning process, including the number of samples required for effective adaptation and the sensitivity of the process to the choice of fine-tuning data. The authors should also provide a quantitative analysis of the model's performance on unseen concepts after fine-tuning, comparing it to the performance without fine-tuning. This would provide a more convincing demonstration of the method's ability to adapt to new concepts. The paper should also include a discussion of the limitations of the fine-tuning process, including scenarios where it may fail to adapt effectively. Fourth, the authors should investigate the sensitivity of their method to the quality of the text anchors obtained from BLIP. This could involve conducting experiments where they systematically introduce errors into the text anchors, either by manually perturbing the labels or by using a weaker VQA model, and then analyzing how these errors affect the quality of the learned concept embeddings. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to explore methods for making the framework more robust to errors in the text anchors, such as using an ensemble of VQA models or incorporating uncertainty estimates into the training process. Finally, the authors should explore techniques for reducing the computational cost of training a separate set of concept encoders for each dataset. This could involve using a shared encoder architecture or employing knowledge distillation techniques. Furthermore, a more detailed analysis of the training time and memory requirements for different dataset sizes would be valuable for assessing the practical applicability of the method. These improvements would address the identified weaknesses and enhance the overall quality and impact of the paper.

## Questions
I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. First, how does the generalization ability of the proposed method vary across different types of concepts and datasets? The training dataset is limited to five domains, and it is unclear how the method would perform on more complex or abstract concepts not represented in the training data. Have the authors evaluated the proposed method on held-out data that contains concepts and compositions unseen in the training set? Second, why were the specific baselines chosen for comparison, and how do they compare to more recent state-of-the-art methods in controllable generation and editing? The paper does not adequately justify the choice of these specific baselines over more recent and relevant methods. Third, what is the impact of BLIP's accuracy on the quality of the learned concept embeddings? The paper relies on BLIP for text anchors, but it does not analyze how errors in BLIP's predictions affect the disentanglement or compositionality of the learned representations. Have the authors tried using different VQA models and how does it affect the results? Fourth, what is the computational cost of training the concept encoders, and how does it scale with the size of the dataset? The paper mentions the training time for one dataset, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational requirements for different dataset sizes. Finally, how does the test-time finetuning process affect the disentanglement of the concept embeddings? The paper mentions that the anchor loss is omitted during finetuning, but it does not analyze the potential impact of this on the disentanglement of the learned representations. These questions target key uncertainties and methodological choices in the paper, and addressing them would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method's capabilities and limitations.
