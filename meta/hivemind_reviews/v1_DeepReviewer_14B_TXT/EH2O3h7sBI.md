## summary
This paper introduces Prompt Gradient Projection (PGP), a novel approach to continual learning that integrates prompt-tuning with a gradient projection technique. The core idea behind PGP is to leverage prompt-tuning to eliminate the need for task identifiers in gradient projection, while simultaneously using gradient projection to provide theoretical guarantees against forgetting, a common challenge in continual learning. The authors propose that by ensuring the prompt gradient is orthogonal to the subspace spanned by previous task gradients, they can prevent catastrophic forgetting. This orthogonality condition is achieved through a singular value decomposition (SVD) of the input and prompt spaces, which allows for the projection of the prompt gradient onto a subspace that minimizes interference with previously learned tasks. The method is evaluated on several benchmark datasets, including CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet, and ImageNet-R, under various incremental learning settings such as class-incremental, online class-incremental, and task-incremental learning. The empirical results demonstrate that PGP effectively reduces forgetting and achieves competitive performance compared to existing continual learning methods. The authors also provide a theoretical analysis to support their approach, arguing that the orthogonal projection of the prompt gradient provides a guarantee against forgetting. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative combination of prompt-tuning and gradient projection, offering a new perspective on how to address the challenges of continual learning. By eliminating the need for task identifiers and providing theoretical guarantees against forgetting, PGP presents a promising direction for future research in this field. The authors also explore the balance between stability and plasticity, showing that the method can be tuned to prioritize either anti-forgetting or learning new tasks. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to continual learning, with a strong theoretical foundation. However, as I will discuss in the weaknesses section, there are several areas where the paper could be improved to further strengthen its claims and broaden its impact.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. First and foremost, the core idea of combining prompt-tuning with gradient projection is genuinely innovative. This approach effectively leverages the strengths of both techniques: prompt-tuning eliminates the need for task identifiers, which are often cumbersome in continual learning scenarios, while gradient projection provides a mechanism to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. The theoretical underpinnings of the method are also a significant strength. The authors provide a clear derivation of the orthogonal condition for the prompt gradient, which is crucial for preventing forgetting. This theoretical analysis adds a layer of rigor to the proposed method and offers insights into the underlying mechanisms that contribute to its effectiveness. The use of SVD to achieve this orthogonal projection is a clever technical innovation. Furthermore, the empirical results presented in the paper are quite convincing. The authors conduct comprehensive experiments on several benchmark datasets and under various incremental learning settings, demonstrating the effectiveness of PGP in reducing forgetting. The comparisons with existing continual learning methods, including both prompt-tuning and other approaches, further highlight the competitive performance of the proposed method. The inclusion of an ablation study, which examines the impact of the threshold parameter, is also commendable. This analysis provides valuable insights into the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter settings and helps to understand the trade-off between stability and plasticity. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a broader audience. The authors clearly explain the motivation behind their approach, the details of the method, and the results of their experiments. Overall, the paper presents a novel, well-theorized, and empirically validated approach to continual learning, making a significant contribution to the field.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further attention. First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method, particularly concerning the SVD operation. As noted by multiple reviewers, the method relies on performing SVD on the sum space of the input and prompt embeddings, which can be computationally expensive, especially for high-dimensional data. While the paper does present a comparison of training time and memory cost between L2P-PGP and L2P-R in Table 2, this is insufficient to fully assess the computational overhead of PGP itself. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the time and memory complexity of the SVD operation in relation to the dimensionality of the input and prompt spaces, nor does it discuss how this scales with the number of tasks. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to evaluate the practicality of the approach for large-scale applications. The absence of a detailed computational complexity analysis is a significant limitation, as it hinders the assessment of the method's scalability and its applicability to real-world scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's method description clearly involves SVD, and the experimental section lacks a detailed computational analysis. Second, the paper's experimental evaluation is limited in scope. As pointed out by multiple reviewers, the experiments are primarily conducted on relatively small-scale datasets such as CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet, and a subset of ImageNet (ImageNet-R). While these datasets are commonly used in continual learning research, they do not fully reflect the complexity of real-world scenarios. The paper lacks experiments on larger datasets, such as the full ImageNet dataset, which would provide a more robust assessment of the method's scalability and effectiveness. Furthermore, the evaluation is limited to image classification tasks. The paper does not include experiments on more challenging vision tasks, such as object detection or semantic segmentation, which would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's capabilities and limitations. The lack of experiments on more complex tasks limits the generalizability of the findings. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the datasets used, and these are indeed smaller than full-scale datasets like ImageNet. Third, the paper's experimental evaluation is also limited in terms of the architectures used. The experiments are exclusively conducted on Vision Transformer (ViT) architectures. While ViT is a popular choice, it is important to evaluate the proposed method on other widely used architectures, such as ResNet, to determine its generalizability across different model types. The method's reliance on the self-attention mechanism of Transformers raises questions about its applicability to convolutional neural networks. The absence of experiments on other architectures limits the scope of the findings and makes it difficult to assess the method's practical relevance. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's experimental setup and baseline descriptions clearly indicate the use of Transformer-based models, with no mention of CNN architectures like ResNet. The paper should include comparisons with a more diverse set of state-of-the-art continual learning methods, including those that employ different strategies, such as gradient-based and rehearsal-based approaches. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the advantages and limitations of the proposed approach relative to the broader landscape of continual learning techniques. Furthermore, the paper does not address potential issues related to the stability of the training process and how to mitigate them. ## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method, with a particular focus on the SVD operation. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements of each step of the algorithm, considering the dimensionality of the input and prompt spaces, as well as the number of tasks. The authors should also provide empirical evidence of the computational cost by reporting the training time and memory usage for different experimental settings. This would allow readers to better assess the practicality of the method for large-scale applications. For example, the authors could provide a table showing the training time and memory usage for different datasets and different numbers of tasks, along with the corresponding dimensionalities of the input and prompt spaces. Second, the authors should expand the scope of their experimental evaluation. This should include experiments on larger datasets, such as the full ImageNet dataset, to assess the method's scalability and effectiveness. Furthermore, the evaluation should be extended to more challenging vision tasks, such as object detection and semantic segmentation. These tasks often involve more complex data and require more sophisticated models, which would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's capabilities and limitations. Third, the authors should evaluate the proposed method on a wider range of architectures, including ResNet. This would help to determine whether the method is specific to ViT or if it can be generalized to other architectures. The current focus on ViT limits the scope of the findings and makes it difficult to assess the method's practical relevance. Fourth, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with a broader range of state-of-the-art continual learning methods. This should include methods that utilize different continual learning strategies, such as regularization-based methods, replay-based methods, and methods that employ architectural modifications. The comparison should be performed on the same datasets and using the same evaluation metrics, to ensure a fair comparison. The authors should also provide a detailed discussion of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method relative to the other methods, highlighting the specific scenarios where the proposed method performs best. Fifth, the authors should provide more insights into the practical implementation details and potential challenges in real-world applications. This should include a discussion of the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter settings, such as the learning rate, the number of prompt tokens, and the threshold parameter. The authors should provide guidelines on how to tune these parameters for optimal performance, and discuss the potential issues related to the stability of the training process. Furthermore, the authors should address potential challenges related to the scalability of the method to more complex datasets and tasks. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the theoretical underpinnings of the method. This should include a clear statement of the assumptions made during the theoretical derivation, as well as a discussion of the limitations of the analysis. The authors should also discuss the relationship between the theoretical guarantees and the empirical results, and address any potential discrepancies between the theoretical predictions and the observed performance. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make it more impactful.
