## summary
This paper introduces a zero-shot image classification method that leverages the capabilities of multimodal large language models (LLMs) to enhance classification accuracy. The core idea revolves around using a multimodal LLM, specifically Gemini Pro, to generate detailed textual descriptions of input images. These textual representations, along with initial class predictions from the LLM, are then fused with visual features extracted from a cross-modal embedding model, such as CLIP. This fusion of visual and textual information is used to perform zero-shot classification using a linear classifier. The authors claim that their method achieves state-of-the-art results on multiple benchmark datasets without requiring dataset-specific prompt engineering. The method's simplicity and effectiveness are highlighted as key strengths. The approach involves a straightforward set of prompts to elicit rich textual representations from the LLM, which are then combined with visual features in a common embedding space. The fused features are then used to perform zero-shot classification with a linear classifier. The authors demonstrate the method's performance across ten benchmark datasets, showing significant improvements in accuracy compared to existing methods. The paper emphasizes that the method does not require prompt engineering for each dataset, making it more practical and easier to use. The authors also provide a failure case analysis, which highlights the limitations of the method when the generated text descriptions or initial predictions are inaccurate. The paper's overall significance lies in its attempt to leverage the power of multimodal LLMs for zero-shot image classification, offering a simple yet effective approach that achieves strong empirical results. However, the paper also acknowledges the computational cost associated with using large LLMs and the potential limitations of relying on the quality of the generated text descriptions. The paper's contribution is primarily empirical, demonstrating the effectiveness of combining multimodal LLMs with cross-modal embeddings for zero-shot image classification. While the individual components are not novel, the specific combination and application are presented as a valuable contribution to the field. The paper's findings suggest that multimodal LLMs can play a significant role in enhancing zero-shot image classification, but further research is needed to address the limitations and potential drawbacks of the proposed method.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its empirical demonstration of a simple yet effective method for zero-shot image classification. The authors have successfully shown that by leveraging multimodal LLMs to generate textual descriptions of input images, and then fusing these descriptions with visual features, they can achieve state-of-the-art results on multiple benchmark datasets. This is a significant achievement, as it suggests that multimodal LLMs can play a crucial role in enhancing zero-shot image classification. The method's simplicity is another notable strength. The authors have designed a straightforward approach that does not require complex architectural modifications or dataset-specific prompt engineering. This makes the method more practical and easier to use, as it can be applied to different datasets with a single set of prompts. The paper also provides a clear and concise explanation of the method, making it easy to understand and follow. The experimental results are well-presented, and the authors have taken care to compare their method with several existing approaches. The ablation studies further support the effectiveness of the proposed method. The authors also provide a failure case analysis, which is a valuable contribution as it highlights the limitations of the method and provides insights into potential areas for improvement. The paper's focus on practical applicability is also a strength. By avoiding dataset-specific prompt engineering, the authors have made their method more generalizable and easier to use in real-world scenarios. The paper's overall contribution is significant in that it demonstrates the potential of multimodal LLMs for zero-shot image classification, and it provides a simple and effective approach that can be used as a starting point for future research. The authors have successfully shown that the combination of multimodal LLMs and cross-modal embeddings can lead to improved performance in zero-shot image classification, and this is a valuable contribution to the field. The paper's clear presentation, strong empirical results, and focus on practical applicability make it a valuable contribution to the field of zero-shot image classification.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling approach to zero-shot image classification, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, echoed by multiple reviewers, is the limited novelty of the proposed method. While the authors emphasize the use of multimodal LLMs for generating image descriptions, the core components of the method—using LLMs for text generation and cross-modal embeddings for fusion—are not entirely new. The paper claims that its novelty lies in the specific combination of these components, but the overall approach can be seen as a straightforward application of existing techniques. The paper does not sufficiently demonstrate a novel way of integrating multimodal LLMs with the existing zero-shot classification pipeline. This lack of substantial novelty limits the paper's contribution to the field. Furthermore, the method's reliance on powerful multimodal LLMs, specifically Gemini Pro, introduces significant practical limitations. The computational cost and resource requirements of these models are substantial, as acknowledged in the paper's "Computation resources" section, which states that local processing requires an estimated 16 GPUs/TPUs with approximately 256 GB of memory. This makes the method less accessible and feasible for researchers or practitioners in resource-constrained environments. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead or the scalability of the proposed method, which is a critical aspect for real-world deployment. The reliance on cloud-based APIs for LLM access also introduces potential latency and cost issues that are not fully addressed. This dependence on external resources makes the method less robust and less predictable in terms of performance. Another significant weakness is the lack of a thorough analysis of the limitations and potential drawbacks of the proposed method, particularly concerning the impact of the quality of the generated text descriptions on the classification accuracy. While the paper includes a "Failure Cases Analysis" section, which provides examples of misclassifications due to inaccurate LLM predictions or image descriptions, it does not include a systematic sensitivity analysis on how variations in the quality of the generated text descriptions affect the final classification performance. The paper does not explore how the method would perform with noisy or inaccurate descriptions, which is a crucial consideration for robustness. The paper's analysis of failure cases is also limited in scope. While the provided examples are insightful, a more detailed analysis of the types of images where the method fails, and the reasons for these failures, would be valuable. This lack of a comprehensive analysis of the method's limitations makes it difficult to fully assess its robustness and applicability in real-world scenarios. The paper also lacks a detailed comparison with existing zero-shot image classification methods, particularly those that do not rely on LLMs. While the paper compares its method to several baselines, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method. For example, the paper does not discuss how the proposed method compares to other methods in terms of computational cost, memory requirements, and robustness to different types of images. This lack of a comprehensive comparison makes it difficult to position the proposed method within the existing literature and highlight its unique contributions. In summary, the paper's weaknesses include limited novelty, high computational cost, lack of a thorough analysis of limitations, and insufficient comparison with existing methods. These weaknesses significantly impact the paper's overall contribution and its applicability in real-world scenarios. The confidence level for these weaknesses is high, as they are supported by direct evidence from the paper and are consistent with the concerns raised by multiple reviewers.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, to enhance the novelty of the method, the authors should explore alternative ways of integrating multimodal LLMs into the zero-shot classification pipeline. This could involve investigating novel architectural components or training strategies that go beyond simply using the LLM for text generation. For example, the authors could explore methods for fine-tuning the LLM specifically for the task of zero-shot image classification, or they could investigate ways of incorporating visual information directly into the LLM's text generation process. This would require a more in-depth exploration of the interaction between visual and textual information within the LLM. Second, to address the computational cost and resource requirements, the authors should investigate alternative methods for generating textual representations that do not rely on computationally expensive multimodal LLMs. This could involve exploring techniques such as using pre-trained vision-language models to extract pseudo-textual embeddings directly from images, or employing lightweight text generation models that are less resource-intensive. This would make the method more accessible and practical for a wider range of applications. Furthermore, the authors should conduct a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed method, including the time and memory requirements for both training and inference. This analysis should compare the proposed method with existing approaches to provide a clear understanding of its computational efficiency. The authors should also investigate the scalability of the method, particularly when dealing with large-scale datasets, and discuss potential strategies for optimizing its performance in such scenarios. Third, to address the lack of a thorough analysis of limitations, the authors should conduct a systematic sensitivity analysis on the impact of the quality of the generated text descriptions on the classification accuracy. This analysis should involve generating text descriptions with varying levels of quality, perhaps by introducing noise or using different generation models, and then evaluating the impact on classification performance. This would provide valuable insights into the robustness of the method and its sensitivity to the quality of the generated text. The authors should also explore methods for improving the quality of the generated text descriptions, such as using techniques for error correction or incorporating feedback from the classification results. This would help to mitigate the impact of noisy or inaccurate descriptions and improve the overall performance of the method. Additionally, the authors should investigate the limitations of the method in handling ambiguous or complex images, where generating accurate text descriptions might be challenging. Fourth, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with existing zero-shot image classification methods, including those that do not rely on LLMs. This comparison should not only focus on accuracy but also consider other factors such as computational cost, memory requirements, and robustness. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of the proposed method in specific application scenarios, such as medical imaging or remote sensing, where the characteristics of the images might differ significantly from the datasets used in the evaluation. This would provide a more complete understanding of the applicability and limitations of the method and guide future research in this area. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the failure cases, including examples of images where the method performs poorly, and discuss the specific reasons for these failures. This analysis should go beyond simply stating that the method fails when the generated text is incorrect, but should delve into the specific reasons why the generated text is incorrect and how this leads to misclassification. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly improve the quality and impact of their work.
