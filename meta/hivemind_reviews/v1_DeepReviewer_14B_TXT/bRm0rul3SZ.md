## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to image-to-image translation, specifically focusing on the challenging task of translating between pinhole images and 360-degree panoramic images. The core contribution lies in the development of a method that leverages readily available pinhole images as the target domain for training, effectively bridging the significant domain gap between these two types of images. The authors propose a two-stage training scheme, employing a transformer-based architecture inspired by InstaFormer, but without instance-level approaches due to the absence of ground truth bounding box annotations. Key methodological innovations include the use of deformable convolution in the encoder to handle geometric distortions, spherical positional embedding to address discontinuities at panorama edges, distortion-free discrimination to improve training stability, and sphere-based rotation augmentation to enhance the model's robustness to panoramic image rotations. The proposed framework is evaluated on the StreetLearn dataset, with pinhole images from the INIT and Dark Zurich datasets used as the target domain. The results demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms existing image-to-image translation techniques, both qualitatively and quantitatively, in terms of structural coherence and rotation equivariance. The authors present a compelling solution to the problem of translating between pinhole and panoramic images, which has significant implications for applications such as virtual reality, panoramic image editing, and autonomous driving. However, the paper also presents some limitations, particularly in terms of the scope of the method, the lack of detailed implementation specifics, and the absence of a thorough computational analysis. Despite these limitations, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of image-to-image translation by addressing a challenging and under-explored problem.

## Strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper tackles a novel and challenging problem: image-to-image translation between pinhole and panoramic images. This is a significant contribution, as existing methods are often limited to translating between images with similar geometric structures. The authors have identified a clear need for this type of translation, and their approach is a valuable step forward. Second, the proposed method demonstrates clear technical innovations. The use of deformable convolution in the encoder to handle geometric distortions, spherical positional embedding to address discontinuities at panorama edges, and distortion-free discrimination to improve training stability are all well-motivated and contribute to the overall performance of the method. The sphere-based rotation augmentation is also a clever way to improve the model's robustness to panoramic image rotations. Third, the empirical results are compelling. The authors demonstrate through both qualitative and quantitative evaluations that their method outperforms existing image-to-image translation techniques. The qualitative results, shown in Figure 4, clearly illustrate the superior structural coherence and visual quality of the translated images. The quantitative results, presented in Tables 1 and 2, further support these findings, showing improvements in both FID and SSIM metrics. Finally, the paper is generally well-written and easy to understand. The authors clearly explain their methodology and provide sufficient details for the reader to follow their approach. The use of figures and tables also enhances the clarity of the presentation. The combination of a novel problem, technical innovations, strong empirical results, and clear writing makes this a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the scope of the proposed method is limited. As the authors themselves acknowledge, the method is specifically designed for translating between pinhole and panoramic images. This limitation is evident throughout the paper, from the problem definition in Section 3.1 to the experimental setup in Section 4.1, which uses only panoramic and pinhole datasets. This narrow focus restricts the general applicability of the method, as it cannot be directly applied to other image-to-image translation tasks. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world scenarios involve translating between different types of image domains. The lack of flexibility in handling diverse image types is a notable drawback. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed framework. As noted by multiple reviewers, the authors do not provide information about the model size, FLOPs, or inference time. This omission makes it difficult to assess the practicality of the method, especially when compared to other image-to-image translation techniques. Without this information, it is impossible to determine whether the method is computationally feasible for real-world applications. This is a critical oversight, as computational efficiency is a key consideration for any practical image processing method. Third, the paper does not include a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the authors present compelling results, they do not address potential failure cases or scenarios where the method might not perform well. For example, the paper does not discuss how the method handles highly dynamic scenes, significant occlusions, or unusual lighting conditions. This lack of discussion leaves the reader with an incomplete understanding of the method's robustness and applicability. A thorough discussion of the method's shortcomings is essential for a balanced evaluation. Fourth, the paper lacks specific implementation details, which hinders reproducibility. While the authors provide a high-level overview of the architecture, they do not specify crucial details such as the number of layers in the transformer, the kernel sizes in the deformable convolution, and the activation functions used. This lack of detail makes it challenging for other researchers to replicate the results and build upon this work. The paper mentions being inspired by InstaFormer, but relying solely on that for all architectural details is not sufficient for full reproducibility of this specific implementation. Finally, while the authors claim that the proposed method outperforms InstaFormer, the core architecture and methodology heavily rely on the existing framework. The modifications, while potentially beneficial, do not represent a fundamental shift in approach or a novel conceptual contribution. The performance gains, while appreciated, seem incremental rather than transformative. The method is explicitly stated to be inspired by InstaFormer, and the modifications, while significant, are built upon the existing architecture. This reliance on InstaFormer raises questions about the true novelty of the proposed approach. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight the need for further work to address the limitations of the proposed method and to provide a more complete and robust evaluation.

## Suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should broaden the scope of their method to handle more general image-to-image translation tasks. This could involve exploring different network architectures or loss functions that are less specific to the characteristics of panoramic and pinhole images. For example, the authors could investigate techniques for domain adaptation or meta-learning that would allow the model to generalize to new image domains not seen during training. This would significantly enhance the applicability of the method and make it more valuable to the broader research community. Second, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of their method. This should include reporting the model size (number of parameters), FLOPs, and inference time on a standard hardware setup. This analysis should also compare these metrics with existing image-to-image translation techniques to provide a clear understanding of the trade-offs between performance and computational efficiency. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to include a discussion on how the method's performance scales with different input resolutions and hardware configurations. This would allow readers to better assess the practicality of the proposed method in various real-world scenarios. Third, the authors should include a comprehensive discussion of the limitations of their method. This discussion should go beyond simply stating what the method does well and should instead focus on identifying specific scenarios where the method might fail or produce suboptimal results. For example, the authors could investigate the method's performance on images with extreme viewpoints, significant occlusions, or unusual lighting conditions. They could also analyze the types of artifacts that the method might produce and discuss potential strategies for mitigating these issues. This would provide a more balanced and realistic assessment of the method's capabilities and limitations. Fourth, the authors should provide a detailed description of the implementation details of their method. This should include specific information about the architecture of the transformer, the deformable convolution, and the training procedure. For example, the authors should specify the number of layers in the transformer, the kernel sizes and padding used in the deformable convolution, and the activation functions used in each layer. They should also provide details about the training data, the loss functions used, and the optimization algorithm. This level of detail is essential for ensuring the reproducibility of the results and for allowing other researchers to build upon this work. The authors should also consider releasing their code to further enhance reproducibility and facilitate future research. Finally, the authors should more clearly articulate the novelty of their approach beyond incremental improvements to InstaFormer. While the modifications are interesting, the core architecture remains largely unchanged. A more detailed analysis of the specific contributions of each modification, perhaps through ablation studies, would be beneficial. Furthermore, the authors should explore how their method could be extended to handle more general image-to-image translation tasks, rather than being limited to panoramic and pinhole images. This could involve exploring different network architectures or loss functions that are less specific to the characteristics of the chosen domains. The current approach seems very tailored to the specific input types, which limits its broader applicability. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their work and make it more impactful.

## Questions
I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. First, I would like to understand more about the computational complexity of the proposed method. Specifically, what is the model size in terms of the number of parameters, what are the FLOPs required for training and inference, and what is the actual inference time on a standard hardware setup? This information is crucial for assessing the practicality of the method. Second, I am curious about the limitations of the proposed method. What are the specific scenarios where the method might fail or produce suboptimal results? How does the method perform when dealing with highly dynamic scenes, significant occlusions, or unusual lighting conditions? What types of artifacts might the method produce, and how can these be mitigated? A more detailed discussion of the method's shortcomings would provide a more complete picture of its capabilities and applicability. Third, I would like to see more detailed implementation specifics. What is the exact architecture of the transformer, including the number of layers, the size of the attention heads, and the dimensionality of the embeddings? What are the kernel sizes and padding used in the deformable convolution? What activation functions are used in each layer? These details are essential for ensuring the reproducibility of the results. Fourth, I am interested in the specific contributions of each modification made to the InstaFormer architecture. What would happen if we apply sphere-based rotation augmentation and its ensemble technique to InstaFormer? Or what would happen if we use spherical positional embedding (SPE) and/or deformable convolution to InstaFormer? A more detailed ablation study would help to clarify the specific contributions of each modification. Finally, I would like to know more about the potential applications of the proposed method. While the authors mention virtual reality and panoramic image editing, how could this method be used in autonomous driving or robotics? What are the specific advantages of using this method over existing techniques in these applications? A more detailed discussion of the potential applications and extensions would highlight the broader impact of the proposed method.
