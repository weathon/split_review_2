## Summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for understanding and controlling the influence of context on large language models (LLMs) during summarization tasks, with a particular focus on mitigating hallucinations and addressing privacy concerns. The authors propose a definition for context influence, quantifying it as the change in the probability of a token given the full context versus a reduced context. They then introduce Context-Influence Decoding (CID), a decoding method that interpolates between the model's prior distribution and the posterior distribution conditioned on the context, using a parameter λ to control the degree of context influence. The core idea is that by adjusting λ, one can modulate the extent to which the LLM relies on the provided context versus its pre-trained knowledge, thereby potentially reducing hallucinations and limiting the regurgitation of private information. The authors demonstrate analytically that increasing context influence can reduce hallucinations, but also acknowledge a trade-off with summary quality. They further show that their context influence metric can serve as a lower bound for private information leakage. The paper presents empirical results on two datasets, CNN-DM and PubMedQA, using various LLMs including OPT, LLaMA 3, and GPT-Neo. The experiments explore the impact of model capacity, context size, response length, and different token n-grams on context influence and hallucination. The authors find that amplifying the context, i.e., reducing the influence of prior knowledge, increases the context's influence on the LLM and can help mitigate hallucination. They also show that their context influence metric can serve as a lower bound for the private information leakage of CID. The paper's main contribution lies in its formalization of context influence and its practical method for controlling it during decoding, offering a potential pathway for generating more faithful and privacy-aware summaries. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of various factors affecting context influence, including model capacity, context size, and response length. The paper concludes by highlighting the potential of their method to inform practitioners about privacy risks and guide the design of solutions that balance context influence, hallucination, and privacy.

## Strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The most notable is the introduction of a novel definition for context influence, which is grounded in the concept of Point-wise Cross-Mutual Information. This definition provides a quantifiable measure of how much the context affects the LLM's token predictions, and it serves as a solid foundation for the subsequent development of Context-Influence Decoding (CID). The CID method itself is a significant contribution, as it offers a practical way to control the degree of context influence during the decoding process. By interpolating between the prior and posterior distributions, the authors provide a mechanism to balance the reliance on the context with the model's pre-trained knowledge, which is crucial for mitigating hallucinations and addressing privacy concerns. The analytical results presented in the paper are also compelling. The authors demonstrate that amplifying the context, by reducing the influence of prior knowledge, increases the context's influence on the LLM and can help mitigate hallucination. Furthermore, they show that their context influence metric can serve as a lower bound for the private information leakage of CID, which is a valuable insight for understanding the privacy implications of using LLMs. The experimental evaluations are comprehensive and well-executed. The authors conduct experiments on two diverse datasets, CNN-DM and PubMedQA, and use multiple LLMs, including OPT, LLaMA 3, and GPT-Neo. This allows them to assess the generalizability of their method across different models and datasets. The analysis of various factors affecting context influence, such as model capacity, context size, and response length, is also thorough and provides valuable insights into the behavior of LLMs. The paper also includes a detailed analysis of the impact of different token n-grams on context influence, which further enhances our understanding of how context affects LLM predictions. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their motivation, methodology, and results, and they provide sufficient detail for others to replicate their work. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field by providing a novel framework for understanding and controlling context influence in LLMs, with a focus on mitigating hallucinations and addressing privacy concerns.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, while the paper discusses related work on hallucination and privacy, it lacks a comprehensive engagement with specific techniques and findings from the literature. For instance, the paper fails to cite and discuss the work "On Hallucinations in Abstractive Summarization with a Contrastive Objective" (NeurIPS 2022), which presents a relevant approach to mitigating hallucinations using contrastive training. This omission is significant because the contrastive objective directly addresses the problem of hallucination in summarization, and a comparison with this method would have provided valuable context for the proposed approach. The absence of a detailed discussion of such relevant work makes it difficult to fully assess the novelty and significance of the proposed method. Second, the paper does not provide a clear and formal definition of 'hallucination.' While the authors describe 'context-conflicting hallucination' and use faithfulness metrics like FactKB and AlignScore to measure it, they do not explicitly define what constitutes a hallucination. This lack of a formal definition makes it difficult to understand the scope and limitations of the proposed method. For example, it is unclear whether the method is designed to address all types of hallucinations or only those that directly contradict the provided context. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the chosen faithfulness metrics and how they might affect the evaluation results. Third, the paper lacks a clear and formal definition of 'private information.' The authors mention 'privacy-sensitive information such as Personal Identifiable Information (PII)' as an example, but they do not provide a comprehensive definition or a method for identifying private information within the context. This lack of a formal definition makes it difficult to assess the validity and generalizability of the proposed method for mitigating privacy leakage. It is unclear what types of information are considered private and how this information is identified in the context. Fourth, the paper does not provide a clear and formal definition of the 'tradeoff' between context influence and hallucination. While the authors discuss the relationship between these two concepts and show how changes in one can affect the other, they do not explicitly define what they mean by 'tradeoff' or propose a specific metric to quantify it. This lack of a formal definition makes it difficult to understand the implications of the proposed method for the practical application of their method. Finally, the paper does not provide a clear and formal definition of the 'goal' of their method. While the authors state the motivation and aims, they do not explicitly state what they are trying to achieve with their method and how they evaluate their success. For example, it is unclear whether the goal is to minimize hallucination, maximize context influence, or find an optimal balance between the two. The paper also lacks a clear statement of the evaluation criteria used to assess the success of their method. These weaknesses, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the clarity and completeness of the work. My confidence in these identified issues is high, as they are directly supported by the absence of explicit definitions, discussions, and comparisons within the paper's content.
