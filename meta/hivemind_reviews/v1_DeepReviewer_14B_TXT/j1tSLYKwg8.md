## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to scaling Diffusion Language Models (DLMs) by adapting pre-trained Autoregressive (AR) models, such as GPT-2 and LLaMA, into diffusion models. The core idea revolves around leveraging the existing knowledge and computational investment in AR models to overcome the resource-intensive training typically associated with DLMs. The authors propose techniques like attention mask annealing and shift operations to bridge the gap between the objectives of AR and diffusion models. Attention mask annealing gradually transitions the model from a causal mask, suitable for AR models, to a bidirectional mask, necessary for diffusion models. The shift operation, on the other hand, aligns the training dynamics of the diffusion model with those of the pre-trained AR model. The authors build upon the absorbing discrete diffusion framework, which provides a mechanism for modeling the transition from clean data to noisy data. The adapted models, termed DiffuGPT and DiffuLLaMA, are evaluated on a range of downstream tasks, including language modeling, reasoning, and infilling. The empirical findings demonstrate that DiffuGPT generally outperforms its AR counterpart, GPT-2, on most tasks, and that DiffuLLaMA, while not surpassing LLaMA2 in all tasks, shows competitive performance, particularly in infilling tasks. The paper's significance lies in its innovative approach to scaling DLMs by adapting pre-trained AR models, which could potentially reduce the computational burden of training large-scale DLMs from scratch. The authors also release their models, code, and scripts, enhancing the reproducibility and impact of their work. The paper's exploration of the adaptation process and its impact on model performance provides valuable insights into the relationship between AR and diffusion models, and opens up new avenues for research in this area. The work also highlights the potential of DLMs as a viable alternative to AR models in large-scale language modeling, particularly in tasks that benefit from their unique properties, such as parallel generation and controllability. However, the paper also reveals some limitations of the current approach, particularly in tasks that require strong autoregressive capabilities, which warrants further investigation.

## Strengths
This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall significance. First and foremost, the core idea of adapting pre-trained autoregressive models to diffusion models is a novel and valuable contribution. This approach directly addresses the challenge of resource-intensive training for DLMs, which has been a major hurdle in their development. By leveraging the existing knowledge and computational investment in AR models, the authors offer a more efficient pathway to scaling DLMs. The introduction of techniques like attention mask annealing and shift operations is another significant strength. These techniques effectively bridge the gap between the objectives of AR and diffusion models, enabling the successful adaptation of pre-trained AR models. The attention mask annealing process, which gradually transitions the model from a causal mask to a bidirectional mask, is a clever way to align the model's training dynamics with the requirements of diffusion models. Similarly, the shift operation ensures that the training dynamics of the diffusion model are consistent with those of the pre-trained AR model. The empirical results presented in the paper are also a major strength. The authors conduct extensive experiments on a variety of downstream tasks, demonstrating that the adapted models, DiffuGPT and DiffuLLaMA, achieve competitive performance compared to their AR counterparts. The fact that DiffuGPT outperforms GPT-2 on most tasks is a strong indication of the effectiveness of the adaptation approach. Furthermore, the paper's release of models, code, and scripts enhances the reproducibility and impact of the work, making it easier for other researchers to build upon their findings. The paper also highlights the potential of DLMs in specific tasks, such as infilling, where they demonstrate a clear advantage over AR models. This suggests that DLMs may be particularly well-suited for tasks that benefit from their unique properties, such as parallel generation and controllability. Finally, the paper's exploration of the adaptation process provides valuable insights into the relationship between AR and diffusion models, opening up new avenues for research in this area. The authors' approach of adapting existing AR models to diffusion models, rather than training from scratch, is a significant contribution to the field of diffusion language modeling.

## Weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling approach to scaling Diffusion Language Models (DLMs), several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, highlighted by multiple reviewers, is the lack of a deep mechanistic explanation for why diffusion models, despite the adaptation, still lag behind autoregressive (AR) models in certain capabilities. Specifically, the paper does not provide a detailed investigation into the specific mechanisms that cause diffusion models to underperform in tasks requiring strong autoregressive capabilities, such as complex reasoning or long-range dependencies. For instance, while DiffuGPT outperforms GPT-2 on most tasks, it underperforms on SIQA and PIQA, and DiffuLLaMA consistently underperforms LLaMA2 across all evaluated tasks. The paper acknowledges these performance gaps, attributing them partially to the amount of training data, but it does not delve into the underlying architectural reasons. This lack of analysis is a significant limitation, as it prevents a full understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the adapted models. The paper does not explore the information flow and gradient propagation within the diffusion model architecture, and how these differ from AR models, potentially leading to the observed performance gaps. This omission is particularly concerning because the adaptation process, while effective to some extent, does not fully bridge the performance gap between diffusion and AR models. The absence of a detailed analysis of the limitations of the approach and the potential reasons for the performance gap in certain areas is a critical weakness that needs to be addressed. Furthermore, the paper could benefit from a more thorough discussion of the potential advantages of DLMs over AR models. While the paper mentions some theoretical advantages, such as parallel generation and controllability, it does not sufficiently articulate these advantages in specific scenarios. For example, the paper does not explore the potential of diffusion models for generating diverse and creative text, or their robustness to adversarial attacks. The paper also does not investigate the ability of diffusion models to perform tasks that are difficult for autoregressive models, such as generating text with specific constraints or properties. This lack of a detailed discussion and experimental validation of the unique advantages of DLMs weakens the justification for the adaptation approach. The paper's focus on tasks where AR models are already strong makes it difficult to assess the unique capabilities of diffusion models. The paper does highlight the strength of DLMs in infilling tasks, which aligns with their any-order generation capability, but a more comprehensive exploration of their potential benefits is needed. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the approach compared to training AR models from scratch or using other non-autoregressive models. While the authors mention that diffusion models are more computationally expensive, they do not provide a quantitative comparison of the training and inference costs. This omission makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the approach and identify potential areas for improvement. The paper also does not discuss the scalability of the approach to larger models and datasets, which is a critical consideration for real-world applications. Finally, the paper does not explore techniques to improve the efficiency of diffusion models, such as reducing the number of diffusion steps or using more efficient sampling algorithms. This lack of attention to efficiency is a significant limitation, as it may hinder the practical applicability of the approach. In summary, the paper's weaknesses stem from a lack of in-depth analysis of the limitations of the approach, a failure to fully articulate the potential advantages of DLMs, and a lack of attention to computational efficiency. These weaknesses, while not invalidating the paper's contributions, highlight areas where further research is needed.

## Suggestions
To strengthen this paper, several concrete improvements can be made. First and foremost, the authors should delve deeper into the architectural differences between diffusion and autoregressive models, focusing on how these differences impact performance on various tasks. Specifically, they should analyze the gradient flow and information propagation within the diffusion model during the reverse process. This analysis should include visualizations of the attention patterns and how they differ from those in AR models, particularly in tasks requiring long-range dependencies. This would provide a more mechanistic understanding of why diffusion models underperform in certain tasks. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of the number of diffusion steps on model performance, as this parameter directly affects the model's ability to capture complex dependencies. A detailed study of how the model's performance varies with different numbers of steps, and how this relates to the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies, would be beneficial. This analysis should also consider the computational cost associated with increasing the number of steps, providing a trade-off analysis between performance and efficiency. This would provide a more complete picture of the capabilities and limitations of the proposed approach. Additionally, the authors should provide a more comprehensive discussion of the potential advantages of diffusion models over autoregressive models. This discussion should go beyond the general claim of parallel generation and delve into specific scenarios where diffusion models might excel. For example, they could explore the potential of diffusion models for generating diverse and creative text, or their robustness to adversarial attacks. The authors should also investigate the ability of diffusion models to perform tasks that are difficult for autoregressive models, such as generating text with specific constraints or properties. This could involve exploring the use of guidance techniques during the sampling process to steer the model towards desired outputs. A detailed analysis of how these techniques can be used to control the generation process and achieve specific goals would be valuable. This would provide a stronger justification for the adaptation approach. The authors should also consider exploring alternative training strategies for diffusion models that might better align with the autoregressive loss. This could involve investigating different noise schedules or loss functions that are more suitable for language modeling. The authors should also explore the use of techniques such as curriculum learning or adaptive sampling to improve the training process. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different model sizes on the performance of diffusion models, and how this relates to the scaling laws observed in autoregressive models. A detailed study of how the model's performance scales with the number of parameters, and how this compares to the scaling behavior of autoregressive models, would be beneficial. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential of their approach for real-world applications. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of their approach compared to training autoregressive models from scratch or using other non-autoregressive models. While the authors mention that diffusion models are more computationally expensive, they should provide a quantitative comparison of the training and inference costs. This would help to assess the practical feasibility of their approach and identify potential areas for improvement. The authors should also discuss the scalability of their approach to larger models and datasets. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential of their approach for real-world applications. Furthermore, the authors should explore techniques to improve the efficiency of diffusion models, such as reducing the number of diffusion steps or using more efficient sampling algorithms. This would make the approach more practical for real-world applications. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and provide a more complete and nuanced understanding of the potential and limitations of their approach.

## Questions
Several key questions arise from my analysis of this paper, focusing on the core methodological choices and assumptions. First, how do the training dynamics and convergence properties of diffusion models compare to those of AR models, especially at scale? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of this aspect, and it is crucial to understand how the adaptation process affects the training behavior of the models. Are there specific architectural considerations or hyperparameters that are crucial for the successful adaptation of AR models to diffusion models? The paper mentions attention mask annealing and shift operations, but a deeper understanding of their impact on the training process is needed. Second, what are the computational trade-offs between diffusion and AR models, particularly in terms of training and inference efficiency? The paper acknowledges that diffusion models are more computationally expensive, but a quantitative comparison of the training and inference costs is missing. How do these trade-offs scale with model size and dataset size? This is a critical question for assessing the practical feasibility of the approach. Third, can the proposed adaptation approach be extended to other types of non-autoregressive models, and what are the potential benefits or limitations of doing so? The paper focuses on adapting AR models to diffusion models, but it would be interesting to explore the potential of adapting other types of non-autoregressive models. This could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. Fourth, what are the specific advantages of using a time-embedding-free architecture for diffusion models, and how does this compare to other approaches that use time embeddings? The paper mentions that it uses a time-embedding-free architecture, but it does not provide a detailed justification for this choice. A comparison with other approaches that use time embeddings would be beneficial. Finally, how does the shift operation affect the training dynamics of the diffusion models, and what are the potential limitations of this approach? The paper mentions that the shift operation aligns the training dynamics of the diffusion model with those of the pre-trained AR model, but a more detailed analysis of its impact is needed. These questions highlight key uncertainties and areas where further investigation is needed to fully understand the potential and limitations of the proposed approach.
