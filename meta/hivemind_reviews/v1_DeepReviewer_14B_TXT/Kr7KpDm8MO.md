## summary
This paper introduces a novel perspective on the role of weight decay in neural network optimization, arguing that it leads to a state of 'rotational equilibrium' where the average rotation of neurons' weight vectors is balanced across layers. This equilibrium, the authors propose, is crucial for effective learning and generalization. The core idea is that weight decay, rather than simply acting as a regularizer, modulates the learning rate by controlling the angular updates of the weights. The authors provide a detailed theoretical analysis of this phenomenon, deriving steady-state equations for different optimizers, including AdamW, Adam with L2-regularization, Lion, and SGD with momentum. They model the parameter updates as a random walk, dominated by noise, which allows them to analytically derive the equilibrium magnitude and expected angular update for each optimizer. This analysis is supported by empirical results, which demonstrate the practical implications of the proposed framework. The paper also introduces 'Rotational Variants' (RVs) of these optimizers, which directly control angular updates, offering an alternative to traditional weight decay and normalization techniques. These RVs are designed to mimic the behavior of standard optimizers while explicitly controlling the rotational dynamics. The authors demonstrate that RVs can achieve similar performance to standard optimizers, suggesting that controlling rotational dynamics is a viable alternative to traditional regularization methods. The paper's key methodological approach involves a combination of theoretical analysis, based on a simplified random walk model, and empirical validation using various neural network architectures and tasks. The main empirical findings include the observation that weight decay leads to a balanced rotation across layers, that the equilibrium state can be predicted by the derived formulas, and that RVs can achieve comparable performance to standard optimizers. The overall significance of this work lies in its novel perspective on weight decay, which challenges the traditional view of it as a regularization technique and provides a new understanding of how it affects the learning process. The paper's findings suggest that controlling the rotational dynamics of weights is a crucial aspect of optimization and that the proposed RVs offer a promising alternative to traditional methods.

## strengths
This paper presents several notable strengths that contribute to its overall impact and significance. First, the introduction of the concept of 'rotational equilibrium' is a genuinely innovative contribution. This perspective moves beyond the traditional view of weight decay as a regularization technique and offers a fresh understanding of how weight decay influences the learning process by modulating the angular updates of the weights. This is a significant conceptual shift that has the potential to impact how we think about optimization in neural networks. Second, the paper provides a rigorous theoretical analysis of the neuronal update dynamics, deriving steady-state equations for different optimizers. This theoretical foundation, based on a simplified random walk model, is well-supported by experimental results, which demonstrate the practical implications of the proposed framework. The derivation of equilibrium states and the examination of both transient and steady-state behaviors contribute to a deeper understanding of optimizer performance. The authors' ability to translate these theoretical insights into practical Rotational Variants (RVs) of optimizers is another strength. These RVs, which directly control angular updates, offer a new approach to optimization that could lead to more efficient and effective training methods. The empirical validation of the theoretical findings is also a significant strength. The authors conduct experiments on a variety of neural network architectures and tasks, demonstrating that the predicted equilibrium states are indeed observed in practice and that the RVs can achieve comparable performance to standard optimizers. The use of visual aids, such as figures and tables, effectively illustrates the key findings and supports the theoretical analysis, making complex concepts accessible to readers. Finally, the paper is well-written and organized, which enhances its clarity and readability. The authors present complex ideas in a clear and concise manner, making the paper accessible to a broad audience. The combination of theoretical analysis, empirical validation, and clear presentation makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field of neural network optimization.

## weaknesses
Despite its strengths, this paper has several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the theoretical analysis relies heavily on the assumption of a random walk for parameter updates, which is a significant simplification of real-world neural network training. As the authors acknowledge, this assumption implies that gradients are uncorrelated and have constant variance, which is unlikely to hold true during the course of training. The paper states, 'We focus on a simplified setting where updates are dominated by noise, resulting in a type of random walk' (Section 3). While the authors justify this assumption by citing similar approaches in other works, the paper does not adequately address how the changing loss landscape and the adaptive nature of optimizers might affect the observed rotational equilibrium. The paper does include an appendix discussing the differences between a random walk and real neural network optimization, but it does not provide a comprehensive analysis of how these differences might impact the validity of the random walk assumption. This simplification limits the generalizability of the theoretical findings and raises questions about their applicability to more complex training scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the random walk assumption and the reviewer's point about the limitations of this assumption in dynamic training scenarios is valid. Second, the paper's focus on scale-invariant weights, while insightful, may limit the generalizability of the findings to other types of neural network architectures and settings. The initial analysis in Section 3.1 explicitly focuses on scale-invariant weights, and while the paper later discusses 'Rotational Dynamics of Scale-Sensitive Parameters' in Section 3.4, it does not fully explore the impact of different activation functions, which can introduce scale dependencies, or the effects of batch normalization, which can alter the effective learning rate of different layers. The paper mentions the final FC layer in ResNet-50 as being scale-sensitive, but it does not provide a systematic analysis of how these factors might affect the rotational equilibrium and the applicability of the proposed Rotational Variants. This lack of analysis raises concerns about the robustness of the findings across different architectures and settings. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does acknowledge scale-sensitive parameters but does not provide a detailed analysis of the impact of activation functions and batch normalization. Third, the paper lacks a systematic study of how different initialization methods affect rotational equilibrium. The paper does not explicitly discuss initialization methods in the context of rotational equilibrium, and the experimental setup in Appendix B mentions the initialization schemes used but does not systematically vary them to study their impact on rotational equilibrium. This is a significant oversight, as the initialization of neural network weights can significantly impact the training dynamics, and it's crucial to understand how different initialization schemes interact with the proposed rotational equilibrium. The paper does not address whether initializations that promote orthogonality or specific variance properties lead to faster convergence to rotational equilibrium. My confidence in this weakness is high, as there is no discussion or experiments related to the impact of different initialization methods on rotational equilibrium. Fourth, the practical implications of rotational equilibrium for hyperparameter tuning are not fully explored. While the paper introduces the concept of rotational equilibrium and provides analytical formulas for equilibrium states based on hyperparameters, it does not offer explicit strategies for choosing hyperparameters to achieve a desired rotational equilibrium. The paper shows that RVs can mimic standard optimizers, but it does not provide a prescriptive way to set hyperparameters based on the equilibrium concept. The experiments in Section 5.2 explore the interaction of hyperparameters but not a systematic approach to hyperparameter optimization based on the rotational equilibrium framework. My confidence in this weakness is high, as there is a lack of explicit hyperparameter tuning strategies based on rotational equilibrium, despite providing the theoretical tools. Finally, the paper does not adequately address the computational overhead of measuring and controlling rotational dynamics. The paper introduces the RVs and demonstrates their effectiveness but does not quantify the additional computational cost associated with measuring and controlling the angular updates. Algorithm 1 shows the steps involved, which would add some overhead compared to standard optimizers, but the paper does not provide any measurements of training time or memory usage for the RVs compared to standard optimizers. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the proposed methods, especially for large-scale models. ## suggestions
To strengthen this paper, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should investigate the validity of the random walk assumption more thoroughly. This could involve analyzing the autocorrelation of gradients during training to assess the degree of correlation between successive updates. Furthermore, the authors could explore how the variance of the gradients changes over time and how this affects the rotational equilibrium. It would be beneficial to conduct experiments on a wider range of architectures and datasets to assess the generalizability of the findings. Specifically, the authors should consider including experiments with different activation functions, batch normalization, and other common architectural components. This would help to determine the extent to which the observed rotational equilibrium is a general phenomenon or specific to certain types of networks. Additionally, the authors should explore the impact of different learning rate schedules on the rotational equilibrium, as the current analysis assumes a fixed learning rate. This would provide a more complete picture of the dynamics of the proposed Rotational Variants. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost and robustness of the proposed Rotational Variants. This could involve comparing the training time and memory usage of the RVs with standard optimizers. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the RVs to noisy gradients and different initialization schemes. It would be useful to explore whether the RVs require any specific hyperparameter tuning or whether they can be used with default settings. A more thorough analysis of these practical aspects would make the proposed methods more accessible and useful to the broader research community. Furthermore, the authors should consider the potential for adaptive angular update strategies, where the magnitude of the angular update is adjusted based on the current state of the network. This could potentially lead to more efficient and robust training. Third, the authors should provide more concrete guidance on how to leverage the concept of rotational equilibrium for hyperparameter tuning. This could involve developing adaptive optimization algorithms that dynamically adjust hyperparameters based on the observed rotational dynamics. For example, the learning rate could be modulated to maintain a desired level of rotational equilibrium, or the weight decay could be adjusted to promote balanced rotation across layers. The authors could also explore the use of metrics derived from rotational dynamics, such as the variance of angular updates, to guide hyperparameter selection. Furthermore, it would be valuable to conduct experiments on a wider range of architectures and datasets to validate the generalizability of the findings and identify potential limitations. Fourth, the authors should investigate the impact of different initialization methods on rotational equilibrium. This could involve comparing the convergence behavior of different initializations, such as Xavier, He, or orthogonal initialization, in terms of their ability to achieve and maintain rotational equilibrium. The analysis should also consider the interplay between initialization and other hyperparameters, such as learning rate and weight decay. Finally, the authors should discuss the potential limitations of the proposed approach in more detail. For example, the paper could explore whether the rotational equilibrium is always desirable or whether there are situations where it might be beneficial to deviate from this state. The authors should also consider the potential impact of the rotational equilibrium on the generalization performance of the network. It would be valuable to investigate whether the RVs lead to better or worse generalization compared to standard optimizers. Addressing these limitations would provide a more balanced and nuanced view of the proposed methods and their potential impact on the field. The authors should also discuss the potential for combining the Rotational Variants with other regularization techniques, such as dropout or batch normalization, to further improve the performance and robustness of the network.
