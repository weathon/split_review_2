## Summary
This paper introduces REMASKER, a novel method for imputing missing values in tabular data using a masked autoencoding framework. The core idea of REMASKER revolves around a 're-masking' strategy, where, in addition to the naturally occurring missing values in the data, a subset of observed values is randomly masked during training. This forces the model to learn more robust representations that are invariant to missingness. The method employs a standard masked autoencoder architecture, consisting of an encoder and a decoder, both implemented using transformer blocks. The encoder processes the re-masked data, and the decoder attempts to reconstruct the original values. The model is trained by minimizing the reconstruction error between the decoder's output and the original data. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of REMASKER through extensive experiments on various tabular datasets, under different missingness scenarios (MCAR, MAR, MNAR), and compare its performance against several state-of-the-art imputation methods. The empirical results show that REMASKER performs on par with or outperforms these baselines, with its performance advantage often increasing with the ratio of missing data. The authors also provide a theoretical justification for the effectiveness of REMASKER, arguing that the re-masking strategy encourages the model to learn representations that are insensitive to missing values. This is supported by a CKA similarity analysis, which shows that the representations of complete and incomplete inputs become increasingly similar as the model trains. Overall, the paper presents a simple yet effective approach to tabular data imputation, with strong empirical results and a clear theoretical motivation. The method's performance is particularly notable in scenarios with high missingness rates, suggesting its potential for real-world applications where data is often incomplete. The authors also explore the impact of various hyperparameters on the model's performance, providing practical insights for practitioners. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors have made a significant contribution to the field of tabular data imputation by introducing a novel and effective method based on the masked autoencoding framework.

Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. ## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the theoretical justification for REMASKER, while providing a clear intuition, lacks the rigor of a formal mathematical proof. The authors argue that the re-masking strategy encourages the model to learn missingness-invariant representations by minimizing the difference between representations under different masks. However, this argument is not supported by a detailed mathematical derivation. Specifically, the paper does not provide a clear theoretical explanation of why minimizing the reconstruction error of the masked autoencoder, with the added re-masking strategy, directly translates to improved imputation performance. The connection between the masked autoencoding objective and the imputation task is not clearly established. The paper would benefit from a more formal analysis of how the learned representations are related to the missing data distribution. For example, the authors could explore the relationship between the re-masking strategy and the generalization ability of the model, or provide a bound on the imputation error based on the properties of the learned representations. The theoretical analysis should also address the limitations of the proposed approach and discuss the conditions under which it is expected to perform well. This lack of a rigorous theoretical foundation weakens the overall contribution of the paper. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and efficiency of REMASKER. The authors do not provide a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each step of the algorithm, such as the re-masking, encoding, and decoding processes. It is unclear how the method scales with the number of features and samples, and there is no comparison of the computational efficiency of REMASKER with other state-of-the-art imputation methods. This lack of computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical implications of using REMASKER in real-world applications, especially when dealing with large-scale tabular datasets.
