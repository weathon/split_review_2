## summary
This paper introduces Physics-Informed Coarse-grained data Learning (PICL), a novel framework designed to address the challenges of modeling physical systems when only coarse-grained data is available. The core idea behind PICL is to learn a fine-grained representation of the system's state from this coarse data, which can then be used for more accurate predictions. The framework is composed of two key modules: an encoding module, which reconstructs the fine-grained state from the coarse-grained input, and a transition module, which predicts the subsequent state based on the reconstructed fine-grained representation. The authors propose a two-stage training process. The first stage, base-training, involves training both the encoding and transition modules using a combination of data loss and physics-informed loss. The physics-informed loss is calculated using a finite difference approximation of the governing partial differential equations (PDEs). The second stage, fine-tuning, focuses on further refining the transition module using physics loss, leveraging unlabeled data to enhance the model's generalization capabilities. The authors evaluate PICL on three different PDEs: the wave equation, the linear shallow water equation (LSWE), and the nonlinear shallow water equation (NSWE). The experimental results demonstrate that PICL outperforms several baselines, including FNO and PINO, in terms of predictive accuracy. The paper's main contribution lies in its approach to integrating physics-informed learning with coarse-grained data, addressing a practical challenge in many real-world applications where fine-grained data is not readily available. By learning a fine-grained representation, the authors aim to overcome the limitations of directly applying physics-informed methods to coarse data, which can lead to inaccurate derivative calculations and poor model performance. The two-stage training strategy, which leverages both labeled and unlabeled data, is also a notable aspect of the proposed framework. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and clearly explained approach to a relevant problem in physics-informed machine learning, with promising empirical results on the selected PDEs.

## strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be commendable. First, the paper tackles a relevant and challenging problem: learning physical systems from coarse-grained data. This is a common scenario in many real-world applications where obtaining fine-grained measurements is either impossible or prohibitively expensive. The authors clearly articulate this problem and propose a well-structured solution. The core idea of learning a fine-grained representation from coarse data is both intuitive and innovative. By introducing an encoding module, the authors aim to bridge the gap between the available data and the requirements of physics-informed learning, which often relies on accurate derivative calculations that are difficult to obtain from coarse data. The proposed PICL framework, with its encoding and transition modules, is a reasonable approach to address this challenge. The use of a neural network (U-Net) for the encoding module and a Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) for the transition module is a sensible choice, leveraging the strengths of these architectures for image-like data processing and operator learning, respectively. The two-stage training process, involving base-training and fine-tuning, is another strength of the paper. The base-training stage allows the model to learn the basic dynamics of the system, while the fine-tuning stage leverages unlabeled data to further improve the generalization capabilities of the transition module. The experimental results are also a strong point of the paper. The authors demonstrate that PICL outperforms several baselines, including FNO and PINO, on three different PDEs: the wave equation, LSWE, and NSWE. These results provide empirical evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methodology and experimental setup. The authors provide sufficient details for the reader to understand the core concepts and reproduce the results. The inclusion of ablation studies, which analyze the impact of different hyperparameters, is also a positive aspect of the paper, demonstrating the authors' attention to detail and thoroughness. Overall, I found the paper to be a valuable contribution to the field of physics-informed machine learning, addressing a practical problem with a well-designed and empirically validated approach.

## weaknesses
While I appreciate the contributions of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the authors present PICL as a novel approach, it is important to acknowledge that the method is largely an incremental extension of existing techniques. The framework combines elements of super-resolution, neural operators, and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), which are all well-established methods. While the specific combination and training strategy are novel, the individual components are not. This is not inherently a problem, but it does mean that the paper's contribution is more about the specific integration of these components rather than the introduction of entirely new concepts. This point was independently validated by multiple reviewers, indicating a clear consensus on this issue. Second, the experimental evaluation, while promising, is limited in scope. The authors test PICL on only three PDEs: the wave equation, LSWE, and NSWE. While these equations represent different types of physical phenomena, they do not fully capture the diversity of challenges encountered in physics-informed machine learning. Specifically, the evaluation lacks examples of more complex, chaotic systems, or systems with strong non-linearities beyond the shallow water equations. The performance of PICL on such systems is unclear, and it is important to demonstrate its robustness across a wider range of physical phenomena. This limitation was also independently identified by multiple reviewers, highlighting the need for a more comprehensive evaluation. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed framework. While the authors mention the use of U-Net for the encoding module and FNO for the transition module, there is no explicit discussion or comparison of training or inference times, memory usage, or the number of parameters for PICL and the baselines. This is a significant omission, as computational cost is a crucial factor in the practical applicability of any machine learning method. Without this analysis, it is difficult to assess the feasibility of using PICL in real-world scenarios. This lack of computational analysis was also independently noted by one of the reviewers, further emphasizing its importance. Another significant weakness is the limited comparison with baselines. The authors compare PICL with FNO, PIDL, and PINO*, but these baselines do not fully represent the state-of-the-art in physics-informed machine learning, particularly in the context of coarse-grained data. For example, methods that explicitly address the challenges of learning from low-resolution data, or those that use different neural network architectures, should be considered for a more comprehensive comparison. The current baselines are primarily operator learning methods or physics-informed methods without specific focus on coarse-grained data. This limitation was also independently identified by one of the reviewers, highlighting the need for a more thorough comparison. Finally, while the paper does include an ablation study on hyperparameter sensitivity, it lacks a dedicated discussion of the limitations of the proposed framework. The authors do not discuss the potential challenges of applying PICL to more complex physical systems, such as those with non-linearities or multi-scale phenomena. It would be helpful to understand the sensitivity of PICL to the quality of the coarse-grained data and how it performs when the data is noisy or incomplete. The paper also does not address the potential for overfitting or discuss the limitations of the current implementation of PICL and suggest potential avenues for future research. This lack of a dedicated discussion on limitations is a significant omission, as it is crucial for a balanced evaluation of the method's practical applicability. In summary, while the paper presents a promising approach, these weaknesses significantly limit the scope and impact of the findings. The lack of a comprehensive evaluation, detailed computational analysis, and thorough comparison with baselines, along with the limited discussion of limitations, are all areas that need to be addressed to strengthen the paper's contribution.

## suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for this paper. First, the authors should significantly expand the experimental evaluation to include a wider range of PDEs. This should include more complex systems, such as the Navier-Stokes equations, which govern fluid dynamics, or the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation, a model for chaotic systems. These systems present significant challenges due to their complex solution spaces and sensitivity to initial conditions. Furthermore, the authors should explore the performance of PICL on PDEs with different types of boundary conditions and forcing terms, as this would provide a more comprehensive understanding of its generalizability. This expanded evaluation would address the current limitation of only testing on three PDEs and provide a more robust assessment of the framework's capabilities. Second, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with state-of-the-art baselines. This should include methods that are specifically designed for learning from low-resolution data, as well as methods that use different neural network architectures, such as graph neural networks or transformers. This would provide a more complete picture of PICL's relative strengths and weaknesses and better contextualize its performance. It is also important to ensure that the baselines are implemented with comparable computational resources and hyperparameter tuning to ensure a fair comparison. Third, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed framework. This should include a breakdown of the computational complexity of each component, including the number of parameters, FLOPs, and memory requirements. Furthermore, a comparison of the computational cost of PICL with existing methods, such as FNO and PINNs, should be provided. This comparison should not only focus on training time but also on inference time, which is crucial for real-time applications. It would be beneficial to include a table or graph that shows how the computational cost scales with the input size and the number of training iterations. This analysis should also consider the impact of different hardware configurations on the computational performance of PICL. Fourth, the authors should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the proposed framework. This should include a discussion of the potential challenges of applying PICL to more complex physical systems, such as those with non-linearities or multi-scale phenomena. It would be helpful to understand the sensitivity of PICL to the quality of the coarse-grained data and how it performs when the data is noisy or incomplete. The authors should also discuss the potential for overfitting and how it can be mitigated. Furthermore, the paper should address the limitations of the current implementation of PICL and suggest potential avenues for future research. Finally, the authors should provide more details on the implementation of the PICL framework, including the specific choices of hyperparameters and network architectures. This would allow other researchers to reproduce the results and build upon the proposed method. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to include an ablation study to analyze the contribution of each component of the PICL framework, such as the encoding module and the transition module. This would provide a deeper understanding of the inner workings of the framework and help to identify areas for further improvement. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its contribution to the field.

## questions
Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe would benefit from further clarification. First, given that the proposed approach is an integration of existing techniques, what are the specific challenges that PICL addresses that cannot be handled by existing methods, such as standard PINNs or operator learning methods? I am particularly interested in understanding the limitations of applying these methods directly to coarse-grained data and how PICL overcomes these limitations. Second, how does the performance of PICL vary with different types of boundary conditions? The paper mentions the use of Neumann boundary conditions in the LSWE experiments, but it would be helpful to understand how the method performs under other types of boundary conditions, such as Dirichlet or Robin boundary conditions. Furthermore, how sensitive is the method to the quality of the coarse-grained data? Does the performance degrade significantly when the data is noisy or incomplete? Third, how is the physics loss calculated for the baseline methods, particularly FNO* and PINO*? The paper mentions that these methods are modified to include physics loss, but the specific implementation details are not entirely clear. A more detailed explanation of how the physics loss is applied to these baselines would be helpful for a fair comparison. Fourth, how does the proposed approach compare with methods that explicitly address the challenges of learning from low-resolution data, such as super-resolution techniques? I am particularly interested in understanding how PICL compares with methods that use super-resolution to enhance the quality of the input data before feeding it into the model. Finally, what are the potential avenues for future research based on the proposed framework? I am interested in understanding how PICL could be extended to address more complex physical systems, such as those with multi-scale phenomena or strong non-linearities. Furthermore, how could PICL be used for inference or inverse problems, such as estimating unknown parameters or initial conditions from coarse-grained data? Addressing these questions would provide a more complete understanding of the capabilities and limitations of the proposed framework and help to guide future research in this area.
