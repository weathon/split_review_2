## summary
This paper introduces Adaptive Prompt Prototype Learning (APPLe), a novel approach designed to enhance the zero-shot performance of Vision Language Models (VLMs), particularly CLIP. The core idea behind APPLe is to move beyond a single textual embedding per class and instead utilize multiple prompts as class prototypes. This strategy aims to capture the inherent visual variance within a class, addressing a limitation of methods that rely on a single, often ambiguous, class representation. The method leverages GPT-3 to generate diverse prompts for each class, which are then used as prototypes. To further refine the representation, APPLe incorporates an adaptive attention mechanism that assigns varying degrees of importance to different prototypes based on their relevance to a given visual input. This mechanism is complemented by a decorrelation loss, which encourages the prototypes to be distinct and non-redundant, thereby enhancing the overall discriminative power of the model. The authors evaluate APPLe across a range of tasks, including generalization to unseen classes, new target datasets, and unseen domain shifts. The empirical findings demonstrate that APPLe consistently outperforms several baseline methods, including CoOp, Co-CoOp, ProDA, PLOT, and MaPLe, across these tasks. Notably, the method shows significant improvements on datasets with high intra-class variance, such as DTD and FGVC Aircraft. The paper also introduces a training-free version of APPLe, which achieves competitive performance without requiring fine-tuning of the prototype embeddings. This version is particularly appealing for its simplicity and efficiency. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and effective approach to improving the zero-shot capabilities of VLMs, with a clear focus on addressing the limitations of single-embedding class representations. The use of multiple prototypes, combined with an adaptive attention mechanism and decorrelation loss, provides a robust framework for handling visual variance and improving generalization. The empirical results, while not without limitations, provide compelling evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method. The introduction of a training-free variant further enhances the practical applicability of the approach, making it a valuable contribution to the field of vision-language learning.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. First, the core idea of using multiple prompts as class prototypes is both intuitive and effective. By moving beyond a single, often ambiguous, class representation, APPLe is able to capture the inherent visual variance within a class more effectively. This approach directly addresses a key limitation of existing methods that rely on a single embedding, and it is a significant step forward in how we approach prompt learning for VLMs. The use of GPT-3 to generate diverse prompts is a practical and efficient way to create these multiple prototypes, and the paper demonstrates that this approach leads to tangible improvements in performance. Second, the adaptive attention mechanism is a clever way to weigh the importance of different prototypes based on their relevance to a given visual input. This allows the model to dynamically adjust its focus based on the specific characteristics of the input image, further enhancing its ability to handle visual variance. The decorrelation loss, which encourages the prototypes to be distinct and non-redundant, is also a valuable addition, as it prevents the prototypes from becoming overly similar and redundant. The combination of these three components—the multiple prototypes, the adaptive attention, and the decorrelation loss—forms a robust and effective framework for prompt learning. Third, the empirical results presented in the paper are generally convincing. The method consistently outperforms several baseline methods across a range of tasks, including generalization to unseen classes, new target datasets, and unseen domain shifts. The performance gains on datasets with high intra-class variance, such as DTD and FGVC Aircraft, are particularly noteworthy. The fact that APPLe achieves these improvements without requiring extensive fine-tuning of the model is a significant advantage. Finally, the introduction of a training-free version of APPLe is a valuable contribution. This version achieves competitive performance without requiring any fine-tuning of the prototype embeddings, making it a practical and efficient solution for many real-world applications. The fact that this training-free version still outperforms zero-shot CLIP by a significant margin demonstrates the inherent effectiveness of the proposed approach. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a wide audience. The motivation is clear, the method is well-explained, and the experimental results are presented in a clear and concise manner. Overall, I believe that this paper makes a significant contribution to the field of vision-language learning, and that the proposed method has the potential to have a significant impact on the way we approach prompt learning for VLMs.

## suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements to strengthen the paper. First, the authors should provide a more detailed technical comparison with existing prototype-based methods, particularly those that also utilize prompts. This comparison should go beyond a high-level overview and delve into the specific technical differences in how prototypes are generated, used, and optimized. For instance, the paper could discuss how the adaptive attention mechanism in APPLe differs from the prototype update strategies in other methods. A more granular analysis of the mathematical formulations and algorithmic steps would help clarify the novelty of the proposed approach. This would address the concern that the technical contribution is not fully established. Second, the authors should include a detailed analysis of the computational cost and efficiency of APPLe. This analysis should include a comparison of the training and inference time, as well as the memory requirements, with other recent approaches. The paper should also investigate how the performance of APPLe scales with the number of prototypes and classes. This analysis should include a discussion of the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. For example, the authors could explore how the number of prototypes affects the accuracy and the computational resources required. This would provide a more practical perspective on the applicability of the proposed method. Furthermore, the authors should consider including ablation studies to demonstrate the effectiveness of each component of APPLe, such as the adaptive attention mechanism and the multiple prompt prototypes. This would provide a more convincing argument for the effectiveness of the proposed approach. Third, to address the inconsistent performance across datasets, the authors should investigate the characteristics of datasets where the method performs poorly. This could involve analyzing the diversity of visual features within each class, the complexity of the background, and the overall data distribution. It would be helpful to provide a more detailed analysis of the failure cases and discuss why the proposed method struggles on certain datasets. The authors could also explore adaptive strategies for selecting the number of prototypes based on the characteristics of the dataset, or consider using different types of prompts for different datasets. This would help to improve the robustness and generalizability of the method. Fourth, the ablation study in Table 3 needs to be significantly improved to demonstrate the effectiveness of each component of the proposed method. The authors should conduct a more granular ablation study that isolates the impact of the attention mechanism and the prototype decorrelation loss. This could involve comparing the performance of the method with and without each component, and also exploring different variations of each component. For example, the authors could compare different attention mechanisms or different decorrelation loss functions. The ablation study should also include a detailed analysis of the results, explaining why each component is necessary and how it contributes to the overall performance of the method. This would provide a more convincing argument for the effectiveness of the proposed approach. Fifth, the authors should investigate methods to reduce the reliance on fine-tuning the prototype embeddings for new classes. One potential approach could be to explore techniques for generating more robust and generalizable prototypes that can adapt to new classes without requiring extensive retraining. This could involve incorporating techniques from meta-learning or few-shot learning to enable the model to quickly adapt to new classes with minimal fine-tuning. Another direction could be to explore methods for learning disentangled representations of the prompts, which could allow the model to focus on the most relevant aspects of the prompts and ignore noisy or irrelevant keywords. This could improve the robustness of the method to variations in prompt quality. Finally, to address the issue of prompt quality, the authors should explore methods for automatically evaluating and filtering the generated prompts. This could involve using a separate model to assess the semantic relevance of the prompts to the target classes and filtering out prompts that are not semantically aligned. Another approach could be to use a combination of different prompt generation techniques and select the best prompts based on their performance on a validation set. Furthermore, the authors could investigate methods for incorporating uncertainty into the prompt representations, which could allow the model to be more robust to noisy or ambiguous prompts. This could involve using techniques such as Bayesian neural networks or Monte Carlo dropout to model the uncertainty in the prompt embeddings. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and address the identified weaknesses.

## questions
Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe would benefit from further clarification. First, how does the method handle cases where the visual features of a class are highly diverse and do not align well with any single prototype? While the paper proposes using multiple prototypes to capture visual variance, it is unclear how the method would perform when the visual features are so diverse that they do not strongly correlate with any of the generated prompts. This is a critical question, as it addresses the core assumption of the method that visual variance can be effectively captured by a set of textual prototypes. Second, how does the method perform when the quality of the prompts is poor or when there is a high degree of ambiguity in the prompts? The paper acknowledges that the effectiveness of the method is tethered to the quality of the prompts, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of how the method would perform under such conditions. This is a crucial question, as it highlights a potential vulnerability of the method to the quality of the input prompts. Third, what is the sensitivity of the method to the number of prototypes used per class? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how the performance of the method varies with the number of prototypes. This is an important question, as it would help to understand the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. Specifically, is there a point of diminishing returns where adding more prototypes does not significantly improve performance? Fourth, how does the adaptive attention mechanism handle cases where multiple prototypes are equally relevant to a given visual input? The paper describes the attention mechanism as assigning varying degrees of importance to different prototypes, but it does not specify how the mechanism would behave when multiple prototypes are equally relevant. This is a critical question, as it addresses the core functionality of the attention mechanism. Fifth, what is the computational cost of generating the multiple prompt prototypes using GPT-3, and how does this cost compare to the computational cost of other prompt learning methods? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the prompt generation process, which is a critical component of the method. This is an important question, as it would help to understand the overall computational efficiency of the proposed approach. Finally, how does the method perform on datasets with fine-grained visual differences, where the visual variance within a class is subtle? The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of the method on datasets with high intra-class variance, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of how the method would perform on datasets with fine-grained visual differences. This is a crucial question, as it would help to understand the limitations of the method in handling subtle visual variations. These questions, if addressed, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method and its limitations.
