## Summary
This paper investigates the robustness of deep neural networks under varying degrees of out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts, a critical concern for deploying machine learning models in real-world scenarios. The authors challenge the common assumption that robustness to a certain degree of distribution shift implies robustness to other degrees, even slightly higher or lower ones. They explore this by examining the performance of models trained on modified versions of MNIST and CIFAR10 datasets, where the distribution shifts are introduced through noise and rotation. The core methodology involves training empirical risk minimization (ERM) and domain generalization (DG) models on these shifted datasets and then evaluating their performance across a range of shift degrees. The authors find that models robust to moderate shifts are not necessarily robust to higher shifts, and vice versa, highlighting a brittleness in model robustness that is often overlooked. Furthermore, the paper extends this analysis to large pre-trained models like CLIP, demonstrating that these models, despite their impressive performance on many tasks, are also susceptible to even mild distribution shifts in downstream tasks. This finding underscores the importance of evaluating model robustness across a spectrum of shift degrees, rather than relying on a limited set of evaluations. The paper's main contribution lies in its empirical demonstration of the non-monotonic behavior of model robustness across different degrees of distribution shifts, challenging the assumption that robustness is a gradual, continuous property. The authors argue that current evaluation practices, which often focus on a limited set of shift degrees, may lead to an incomplete understanding of a model's true robustness. By highlighting the brittleness of robustness, the paper calls for a more comprehensive evaluation of OOD generalization, which is crucial for developing safer and more reliable AI applications. The authors also show that training on higher degrees of shift does not guarantee robustness to lower degrees, further complicating the landscape of robust model training. The paper's findings have significant implications for how we train and evaluate machine learning models, particularly in safety-critical applications where robustness to distribution shifts is paramount. The study's focus on the degree of shift, rather than just the type of shift, provides a valuable perspective on the challenges of OOD generalization. The paper's exploration of both ERM/DG methods and large pre-trained models like CLIP provides a broad view of the robustness issue across different model types. Overall, the paper makes a compelling case for a more nuanced and comprehensive approach to evaluating and understanding model robustness under distribution shifts.

Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its clear motivation, well-executed empirical approach, significant findings, and clear presentation.

## Weaknesses
While this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks sufficient novelty in its core findings. While the authors demonstrate the brittleness of model robustness across different degrees of distribution shift, this phenomenon is not entirely new. As pointed out by multiple reviewers, similar observations have been made in previous works, although perhaps not with the same focus on the degree of shift. The paper does not adequately discuss or differentiate itself from these prior works, which weakens its claim of novelty. For instance, the paper could have benefited from a more thorough literature review that explicitly addresses works that have observed similar non-monotonic behavior of robustness across different conditions. The absence of a detailed comparison to existing literature makes it difficult to ascertain the unique contribution of this paper. This is a significant weakness because it undermines the paper's claim of presenting a novel perspective on model robustness. Second, the paper lacks an in-depth analysis of the underlying reasons for the observed brittleness. While the authors effectively demonstrate the phenomenon empirically, they do not provide a detailed mechanistic explanation for *why* models behave this way. The paper does not delve into the theoretical reasons behind the observed brittleness, nor does it explore the factors that contribute to this phenomenon. For example, the paper could have investigated the feature representations learned by the models under different shift degrees, or explored the role of spurious correlations in the training data. The lack of such analysis limits the paper's contribution to a descriptive analysis rather than a predictive or explanatory one. This is a major weakness because it prevents us from gaining a deeper understanding of the fundamental causes of the observed brittleness. The paper presents results showing the brittleness (e.g., Figure 3, Figure 4) but does not provide a detailed analysis of *why* this occurs at a mechanistic level. The GradCAM visualization in Figure 5 offers some insight into different generalization patterns but doesn't fully explain the underlying causes of brittleness. This lack of mechanistic explanation limits the paper's ability to provide actionable insights for developing more robust models. Third, the paper lacks a dedicated discussion of the limitations of the study and potential avenues for future research. While the conclusion mentions encouraging future research to adopt a more comprehensive evaluation, it does not explicitly outline the limitations of the current study or suggest specific future research directions based on those limitations. The paper does not discuss the limitations of its experimental setup, such as the specific datasets and types of distribution shifts considered. It also does not discuss the potential impact of these limitations on the generalizability of its findings. For example, do the observed phenomena hold for other types of distribution shifts, such as those involving changes in style or texture? This is a significant weakness because it makes the paper feel less complete and less helpful for guiding future research in the area. A more thorough discussion of the limitations and future directions would significantly enhance the impact of the paper. Finally, while the paper includes experiments on ImageNet subsets, the initial experiments are conducted on relatively simple datasets like MNIST and CIFAR10. This raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings to more complex datasets. Although the paper does include experiments on ImageNet subsets, the initial reliance on simpler datasets is a valid point for consideration. The authors should have provided a more thorough justification for the choice of datasets and discussed the potential impact of these choices on the generalizability of their findings. This is a weakness because it limits the confidence in the generalizability of the findings to real-world scenarios.

## Suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more thorough literature review to better contextualize their findings and highlight the unique contributions of their work. This should include a detailed comparison to existing literature on the nuances of OOD generalization, particularly those that have observed similar non-monotonic behavior of robustness across different conditions. The authors should explicitly discuss how their work differs from these prior works and what new insights their study provides. This would strengthen the paper's claim of novelty and provide a more solid foundation for its contributions. Second, the authors should delve deeper into the underlying mechanisms causing the observed brittleness. This could involve analyzing the feature representations learned by the models under different shift degrees. Are there specific features that are robust to certain shifts but not others? Do the models learn different feature representations as the degree of shift increases? Techniques like feature visualization or ablation studies could be used to gain a better understanding of the learned representations. Furthermore, the authors should explore the role of spurious correlations in the training data and how these correlations might contribute to the observed brittleness. A more detailed analysis of the feature space and the learned representations is needed to provide a more complete picture of the phenomenon. This would move the paper beyond a descriptive analysis and towards a more explanatory one. Third, the authors should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the study and potential avenues for future research. This should include a discussion of the limitations of the experimental setup, such as the specific datasets and types of distribution shifts considered. The authors should also discuss the potential impact of these limitations on the generalizability of their findings. For example, do the observed phenomena hold for other types of distribution shifts, such as those involving changes in style or texture? Furthermore, the authors should propose specific future research directions that could address the limitations of their study. This could include exploring new algorithms that are more robust to varying degrees of distribution shifts, or developing new evaluation metrics that can better capture the nuances of OOD generalization. This would make the paper more helpful for guiding future research in the area. Fourth, the authors should consider including experiments on more complex datasets to demonstrate that their observations are not specific to MNIST and CIFAR10. While the inclusion of ImageNet subsets is a step in the right direction, the authors could consider using the full ImageNet dataset or other more challenging datasets. This would increase the confidence in the generalizability of their findings and make the paper more relevant to real-world applications. Finally, the authors should consider exploring the relationship between the model's architecture and its robustness properties. Are certain architectures more prone to this kind of brittleness than others? This would add a layer of technical depth that is currently missing. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make a more substantial contribution to the field of OOD generalization.

## Questions
I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. First, given the observed brittleness of model robustness across different degrees of distribution shift, what specific strategies can be employed to develop models that are more consistently robust across a wider range of shift degrees? The paper highlights the problem but does not offer concrete solutions. It would be valuable to understand what the authors believe are the most promising directions for addressing this challenge. Second, the paper focuses on noise and rotation as the primary types of distribution shifts. How do the authors expect their findings to generalize to other types of distribution shifts, such as those involving changes in style, texture, or semantic content? Are there specific types of shifts where the observed brittleness is more or less pronounced? Exploring this question would help to clarify the scope of the paper's findings. Third, the paper demonstrates that training on higher degrees of shift does not guarantee robustness to lower degrees. What are the implications of this finding for the development of domain generalization algorithms? Should we be focusing on training methods that are robust to a specific range of shifts, or should we be aiming for methods that are robust to a wider spectrum of shifts? This question is crucial for guiding future research in domain generalization. Fourth, the paper shows that large pre-trained models like CLIP are also susceptible to even mild distribution shifts. What specific characteristics of these models make them vulnerable to such shifts? Is it related to the way they are trained, or is it an inherent limitation of their architecture? Understanding this vulnerability is crucial for developing more robust pre-trained models. Finally, the paper's analysis is primarily empirical. What theoretical frameworks can be used to explain the observed brittleness of model robustness? Are there specific properties of the loss landscape or the feature space that contribute to this phenomenon? Exploring the theoretical underpinnings of this problem would provide a deeper understanding of the fundamental causes of the observed brittleness.
