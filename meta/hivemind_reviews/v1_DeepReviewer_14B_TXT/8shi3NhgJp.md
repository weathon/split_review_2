## summary
This paper introduces Imprecise Bayesian Continual Learning (IBCL), a novel algorithm designed to address the challenge of Continual Learning under Specific Trade-offs (CLuST). The core idea behind IBCL is to enable the generation of models that balance stability and plasticity according to user-defined preferences, without requiring retraining. The method achieves this by maintaining a knowledge base represented as a finitely generated credal set (FGCS), which is a convex hull of model parameter distributions learned from previous tasks. When a new task arrives, IBCL updates the FGCS by incorporating the new task's posterior distribution, ensuring that the knowledge base reflects the accumulated knowledge. To generate a model tailored to a specific trade-off, IBCL performs a convex combination of the extreme points of the FGCS, weighted according to the user's preference vector. This process allows for the zero-shot generation of models that are Pareto-optimal with respect to the desired balance between stability and plasticity. The paper presents empirical results on image classification and NLP benchmarks, demonstrating that IBCL achieves significant improvements in task accuracy and backward transfer compared to existing methods. The authors also show that IBCL maintains a constant training overhead per task, independent of the number of preferences requested, which is a notable advantage for scalability. The paper's key contribution lies in its novel formulation of the CLuST problem and the introduction of a Bayesian approach that leverages the properties of credal sets to efficiently generate models that satisfy specific trade-off requirements. The use of a convex hull of model parameter distributions to represent the knowledge base is an innovative idea, allowing for efficient model generation through convex combinations. The experimental results are compelling, showing substantial improvements in task accuracy and backward transfer compared to existing methods. The constant training overhead, regardless of the number of preferences, is a notable achievement that enhances the scalability of the approach. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in its computational complexity analysis and the scope of its evaluation, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of continual learning by addressing a practical challenge in deploying continual learning systems in real-world applications, where user-defined trade-offs between stability and plasticity are often required.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the paper introduces a novel and practically relevant problem formulation: Continual Learning under Specific Trade-offs (CLuST). This formulation addresses a critical gap in the existing continual learning literature, which often focuses on balancing stability and plasticity without explicitly considering user-defined preferences. The authors clearly articulate the importance of this problem, highlighting the need for models that can adapt to new tasks while maintaining performance on previous tasks, all while adhering to specific trade-off requirements. This is a significant contribution that has the potential to make continual learning more applicable in real-world scenarios. Second, the proposed IBCL algorithm is theoretically grounded and well-explained. The use of a Bayesian approach, combined with the concept of finitely generated credal sets (FGCS), is innovative and provides a solid foundation for the method. The authors provide a detailed analysis of the algorithm's properties, including its ability to generate Pareto-optimal models and its probabilistic guarantees. The idea of representing the knowledge base as a convex hull of model parameter distributions is particularly elegant, as it allows for efficient model generation through convex combinations. This approach eliminates the need for retraining, which is a major advantage in terms of computational efficiency. The paper also provides two theorems, Theorem 1 (Selection Equivalence) and Theorem 2 (Probabilistic Pareto-optimality), which further support the theoretical soundness of the proposed method. Third, the experimental results are comprehensive and demonstrate the effectiveness of IBCL. The authors evaluate the algorithm on various benchmarks, including image classification and NLP tasks, and show significant improvements in task accuracy and backward transfer compared to existing methods. The ablation studies provide valuable insights into the impact of different hyperparameters on the algorithm's performance. The fact that IBCL maintains a constant training overhead per task, independent of the number of preferences, is a notable achievement that enhances the scalability of the approach. This is a crucial aspect for practical applications where computational resources are often limited. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly presents the problem, the proposed method, and the experimental results. The authors provide a detailed explanation of the algorithm and its theoretical underpinnings, making it easy for the reader to understand the key concepts. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the experimental results and the behavior of the algorithm. Overall, the paper presents a strong contribution to the field of continual learning, with a novel problem formulation, a theoretically sound algorithm, and compelling empirical results.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithm. While the authors claim that the training overhead is constant and that model generation is zero-shot, a more rigorous analysis of the time and space complexity is needed. Specifically, the process of updating the FGCS, which involves adding new posterior distributions and potentially removing others, needs further clarification. The paper mentions that the FGCS update involves iterating through a fixed number of distributions (`m`) and performing operations, and that the zero-shot generation involves a convex combination of the extreme elements. However, the paper does not provide a formal analysis of the time complexity of the convex hull update operation or the space complexity of storing the FGCS. The claim of constant-time model generation might be an oversimplification, as the number of extreme elements can grow, potentially impacting the time complexity. The memory requirements for storing the distributions are also not explicitly analyzed. The paper states that the buffer growth is sublinear, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of how the size of the FGCS impacts the overall computational cost. This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method in resource-constrained environments. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide the necessary analysis to support its claims of constant-time updates and constant training overhead. Second, the paper's evaluation is limited in its comparison with state-of-the-art continual learning methods. While the authors compare IBCL with some baselines, a more comprehensive evaluation against a wider range of methods would strengthen the paper's claims. The paper compares IBCL with GEM, A-GEM, VCL, and L2P, which are relevant baselines for the CLuST problem. However, the paper does not compare against other prominent continual learning methods that address the stability-plasticity trade-off, such as regularization-based methods (e.g., EWC, SI) and replay-based methods (e.g., iCaRL, Deep Generative Replay). Including such comparisons would provide a broader context for evaluating IBCL's performance and allow for a more meaningful comparison with existing approaches. The absence of these comparisons limits the assessment of IBCL's relative performance within the broader landscape of continual learning algorithms. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper acknowledges the limited number of existing baselines for the specific CLuST problem, and the lack of comparison with other prominent CL methods is a clear limitation. Third, the problem setup seems too restricted. The paper explicitly states that it focuses on domain-incremental learning for classification models, where the task distribution changes but the output space remains the same. This focus on domain-incremental learning limits the applicability of the proposed method to scenarios where the task distribution changes but the output space remains the same. Many real-world continual learning problems involve the introduction of new classes or tasks over time, which is not addressed by the current formulation. This limitation significantly restricts the practical applicability of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly defines the problem as domain-incremental learning, and the method is designed accordingly. Fourth, the assumption of task similarity is too strong. The paper assumes that all tasks are similar to each other, as defined by a bounded Wasserstein distance between their distributions. This assumption is not justified and may not hold in many practical applications. The paper does not provide any analysis of how the performance of the method degrades when the tasks are dissimilar. The use of a Wasserstein distance threshold to control the addition of new distributions to the knowledge base does not fully address the issue of task dissimilarity, as the initial assumption still restricts the scope of the method. This assumption limits the robustness of the approach and its applicability to real-world scenarios where tasks can be very different. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly makes the assumption of task similarity using the Wasserstein distance, and there is no analysis or experimental evidence on how the method performs when this assumption is violated. Finally, while the paper presents two theorems related to the method's properties, it lacks standard theoretical analyses regarding convergence, stability, and generalization guarantees. The provided theorems are specific to the probabilistic nature of the method and do not address broader theoretical properties. The lack of these standard theoretical analyses makes it difficult to assess the conditions under which the method is expected to perform well and when it might fail. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide the standard theoretical analysis that is common in machine learning papers.

## suggestions

This would allow readers to better assess the practical applicability of the proposed method in resource-constrained environments. The authors should also provide empirical measurements of the time and memory usage of the algorithm on different datasets and with varying numbers of tasks and preferences. This would provide a more concrete understanding of the practical computational cost of the proposed method. This comparison should include methods that explicitly address the stability-plasticity trade-off, such as regularization-based methods (e.g., EWC, SI) and replay-based methods (e.g., iCaRL, Deep Generative Replay). The comparison should be performed on a wider range of datasets and tasks to ensure the robustness of the results. Furthermore, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the performance of IBCL under different scenarios, such as varying degrees of task similarity and different levels of task complexity. This would provide a more complete picture of the proposed method's strengths and weaknesses relative to the current state of the art. Third, the authors should broaden the scope of their problem formulation to include task-incremental and class-incremental learning scenarios. This would involve modifying the problem formulation to handle the introduction of new tasks or classes over time. Furthermore, the evaluation should include benchmarks that are commonly used in task-incremental and class-incremental learning to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's performance. The authors could consider using a more robust measure of task similarity or explore techniques for adapting the method to handle dissimilar tasks. Additionally, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the impact of the Wasserstein distance threshold on the performance of the method, as this parameter plays a crucial role in controlling the addition of new distributions to the knowledge base. Finally, the authors should include a more standard theoretical analysis of the proposed method. This analysis should include proofs of convergence, stability, and generalization guarantees. The authors could consider using tools from Bayesian non-parametrics or statistical learning theory to derive theoretical results. This would provide a better understanding of the conditions under which the method is expected to perform well and when it might fail.
