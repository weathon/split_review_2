## summary
This paper introduces Federated Dual Prompt Tuning (Fed-DPT), a novel federated learning approach designed to address the challenges of domain shift and communication efficiency in decentralized data settings. The core idea of Fed-DPT revolves around leveraging a pre-trained vision-language model, specifically CLIP, and employing both visual and textual prompt tuning to facilitate domain adaptation across different clients. The method operates under the assumption that each client represents a distinct domain, and thus, it learns domain-specific prompts for each client. These prompts are then used to modulate the visual and textual representations, enabling the model to better generalize across diverse domains. The method involves training learnable tokens as prompts, which are then used to condition the CLIP model's visual and textual encoders. The visual prompts are learned for each domain, while the textual prompts are shared across clients, with a momentum update mechanism to ensure consistency. The authors conduct extensive experiments on three benchmark datasets: DomainNet, OfficeHome, and PACS, demonstrating the effectiveness of Fed-DPT in domain-aware federated learning. The results show significant improvements over existing methods, including zero-shot CLIP, single-domain tuning, and other federated learning approaches like FedAvg and FedProx. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the contributions of different components of the proposed method, such as the visual and textual prompts. The authors emphasize the parameter efficiency of their approach, which minimizes communication costs by only updating a small number of prompt parameters. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to federated learning in the presence of domain shift, leveraging the power of prompt tuning and pre-trained vision-language models. However, as I will discuss, there are several limitations that need to be addressed to further enhance the practical applicability and robustness of the proposed method. The paper's focus on distinct domains per client, while simplifying the problem, raises questions about its generalizability to more complex real-world scenarios where domain boundaries are not so clear-cut. Furthermore, the lack of a detailed computational complexity analysis and a thorough discussion of privacy implications are also significant concerns that warrant further investigation.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative approach to federated learning by combining the power of pre-trained vision-language models with prompt tuning techniques. The proposed Federated Dual Prompt Tuning (Fed-DPT) method effectively addresses the challenge of domain shift in federated learning by introducing domain-specific visual and textual prompts. This approach allows the model to adapt to different domains without requiring extensive retraining of the entire model, leading to improved parameter efficiency and reduced communication costs. The use of a pre-trained CLIP model as the backbone is a clever choice, as it leverages the rich visual and textual representations learned from large-scale datasets. The method's design, which involves learning visual prompts for each domain and using a momentum-updated shared textual prompt, is a novel way to facilitate domain adaptation in a federated setting. The experimental results presented in the paper are compelling, demonstrating significant improvements over existing methods on three benchmark datasets. The ablation studies provide valuable insights into the contributions of different components of the proposed method, highlighting the importance of both visual and textual prompts. The paper is also well-written and organized, making it easy to follow the proposed method and its evaluation. The authors provide a clear explanation of the problem, the proposed solution, and the experimental results. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the concepts and findings. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of their approach and suggest potential future research directions. The paper's focus on parameter efficiency is also a significant strength, as it addresses a critical challenge in federated learning, where communication costs are often a bottleneck. The method's ability to achieve strong performance with only a small number of trainable parameters is a notable achievement. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, technically sound, and empirically validated approach to federated learning in the presence of domain shift, making a valuable contribution to the field.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of the proposed method, several weaknesses need to be addressed to enhance the practical applicability and robustness of Fed-DPT. First, the paper's assumption that each client corresponds to a distinct domain is a significant limitation. As explicitly stated in Section 4.1, the method is designed under the premise that "the n clients possess their own training data that originate from n distinct domains." This assumption does not align with many real-world federated learning scenarios, where clients may have data from multiple or overlapping domains. For instance, in medical imaging, a single hospital (client) might have data from different imaging modalities or patient populations, representing diverse domains. The method's reliance on distinct prompts for each client-domain combination could lead to an explosion in the number of prompts required, making it impractical for such scenarios. This limitation is further exacerbated by the fact that the paper does not address how to handle clients with mixed-domain data, which is a significant concern. The experimental setup, as described in Section 5.1, also reinforces this limitation, as the datasets used (DomainNet, OfficeHome, and PACS) are structured such that each client corresponds to a distinct domain. This lack of consideration for more complex domain scenarios raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed method. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is directly supported by the problem formulation and experimental design. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. While the authors mention parameter efficiency as a motivation (Abstract, Introduction), they do not provide a formal analysis of the computational cost associated with prompt generation, optimization, and inference. This is a critical omission, especially when considering the potential deployment of the method in resource-constrained environments. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the computational cost associated with each step of the proposed method, nor does it compare the computational complexity of Fed-DPT with that of existing federated learning approaches. This makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method, especially when dealing with a large number of clients and complex models. The absence of a computational complexity analysis is a significant weakness, and my confidence in this assessment is high, as it is evident from the lack of any such analysis in the method and experimental sections. Third, the paper does not adequately address the potential privacy concerns associated with the proposed method. While the authors briefly mention privacy in Section 4.4, stating that "our method with shared prompts has the same level of privacy-preserving capabilities to FedAvg which shares model parameters globally," this is an oversimplification. The sharing of prompt parameters, even if they are learned, could potentially leak information about the local client data. The paper does not analyze how the prompt tuning process might leak information about the local client data through the shared prompts. A more thorough analysis of the privacy implications is needed, especially considering the sensitivity of data in federated learning settings. The paper does not explore the use of differential privacy or other privacy-preserving techniques to mitigate potential privacy risks. This lack of a comprehensive privacy analysis is a significant weakness, and my confidence in this assessment is high, as it is based on the limited discussion of privacy in the paper. Fourth, the paper could benefit from a more comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art methods in federated learning and domain adaptation. While the paper compares with some existing methods, including Zero-shot CLIP, Single-domain tuning, PromptFL, FedCLIP, FedAvg, and FedProx (Section 5.1), it does not include a comparison with other prompt-based federated learning methods or domain adaptation techniques that are not based on prompt learning. The comparison could also benefit from including metrics beyond accuracy, such as communication cost and convergence speed. The lack of a more comprehensive comparison makes it difficult to fully assess the performance of Fed-DPT relative to other methods. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is evident from the limited set of baselines and metrics used in the experimental section. Finally, while the paper does specify the optimizers and learning rates used for training, it omits crucial details such as the batch size and hardware specifications used for the experiments. This lack of implementation details makes it difficult to reproduce the results and further limits the practical applicability of the method. My confidence in this assessment is high, as these details are clearly missing from the experimental section.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, several improvements can be made to the proposed method and the paper. First, the authors should explore techniques for handling clients with mixed-domain data. This could involve developing mechanisms for learning domain-invariant prompts or dynamically selecting or generating prompts based on the specific domain distribution of each client's data. For instance, the authors could explore domain classification techniques to identify the domain of each data point and then use domain-specific prompts accordingly. Alternatively, they could investigate meta-learning approaches to adapt prompts to different domain distributions. This would significantly enhance the practical applicability of the method in real-world scenarios. Second, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of the computational complexity of Fed-DPT. This analysis should include a breakdown of the computational cost associated with each step of the method, such as prompt generation, optimization, and inference. The analysis should also compare the computational complexity of Fed-DPT with that of existing federated learning approaches. Furthermore, the authors should provide empirical results on the computational time and memory usage of the method, considering the impact of the number of clients, the size of the model, and the number of prompts on the overall computational cost. This analysis would allow readers to better understand the practical feasibility of the method in resource-constrained environments. Third, the authors should include a more detailed discussion of the privacy implications of the proposed method. This should involve analyzing the potential for information leakage through the shared prompts and exploring techniques for mitigating these risks. The authors could investigate the use of differential privacy or other privacy-preserving techniques to protect the privacy of the client data. A more rigorous analysis of the privacy implications is essential to ensure the responsible deployment of the proposed method in real-world federated learning settings. Fourth, the authors should expand the comparison with other state-of-the-art methods in federated learning and domain adaptation. This should include a comparison with other prompt-based federated learning methods, as well as domain adaptation techniques that are not based on prompt learning. The comparison should also include metrics beyond accuracy, such as communication cost, convergence speed, and the number of parameters that need to be communicated. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the performance of Fed-DPT and help to identify its strengths and weaknesses compared to other methods. The authors could also consider using a wider range of datasets to evaluate the generalization ability of Fed-DPT across different domains and tasks. Finally, the authors should provide more details on the implementation of the proposed method, including the specific choices of hyperparameters and the computational resources required for training. This includes specifying the batch size, the hardware specifications used for the experiments, and the sensitivity of the method to different hyperparameter settings. Furthermore, the authors should consider releasing the source code of Fed-DPT to facilitate further research and development in this area. This would allow other researchers to easily replicate the results and build upon the proposed method. These changes would significantly enhance the robustness, practical applicability, and reproducibility of the proposed method.
