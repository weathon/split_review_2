## summary
This paper introduces Secure Distributed DP-Helmet, a novel non-interactive distributed learning algorithm designed to enhance privacy in collaborative machine learning. The core idea revolves around a 'blind averaging' technique, where each participating party trains a local model, adds noise for differential privacy, and then securely aggregates these models using a secure summation protocol. This approach aims to minimize communication overhead by requiring only a single round of interaction between parties, contrasting with iterative methods like federated learning. The authors specifically focus on two learning algorithms: Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Softmax-activated Single Layer Perceptrons (Softmax-SLP), providing both theoretical analysis and empirical evaluations for these methods. The theoretical framework relies on the assumption that the objective functions are smooth, Lipschitz, and strongly convex, which allows for the derivation of privacy and utility guarantees. The empirical results, primarily conducted on the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets, demonstrate the algorithm's performance under varying privacy budgets and user counts. The paper claims that the proposed method achieves a similar utility-privacy tradeoff to centralized training with only one invocation of secure summation, highlighting the potential for efficient and privacy-preserving distributed learning. The authors also explore the impact of varying the number of users and the privacy budget on the model's accuracy. The paper's main contributions lie in the proposal of the blind averaging technique, the theoretical analysis of its privacy and utility properties, and the empirical demonstration of its effectiveness on image classification tasks. However, the paper also acknowledges limitations, particularly concerning the convexity assumptions and the scope of the considered learning algorithms. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a significant step towards practical and privacy-preserving distributed learning, offering a promising alternative to traditional iterative methods. The authors also provide an ablation study in the appendix, which includes experiments with models trained from scratch, offering a more comprehensive view of the algorithm's performance. The paper's focus on non-interactive communication and secure aggregation makes it a valuable contribution to the field of distributed machine learning, especially in contexts where communication efficiency and data privacy are paramount.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its proposal of a novel, non-interactive distributed learning algorithm, Secure Distributed DP-Helmet, which leverages a blind averaging technique. This approach significantly reduces communication overhead by requiring only a single round of interaction between parties, a notable improvement over iterative methods like federated learning. The use of secure summation protocols to aggregate the locally trained models ensures that individual user data remains private, which is a crucial aspect for collaborative learning scenarios involving sensitive information. The paper's theoretical analysis, while relying on certain assumptions, provides a solid foundation for understanding the privacy and utility properties of the proposed algorithm. Specifically, the authors derive differential privacy guarantees and establish convergence rates for the SVM algorithm, demonstrating the theoretical soundness of their approach. The empirical evaluations, conducted on the CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 datasets, provide evidence of the algorithm's practical effectiveness. The results show that Secure Distributed DP-Helmet can achieve high accuracy while maintaining strong privacy guarantees, even with varying numbers of users and privacy budgets. The paper's focus on two specific learning algorithms, SVM and Softmax-SLP, allows for a detailed analysis of their performance within the proposed framework. The authors also explore the impact of varying the number of users and the privacy budget on the model's accuracy, providing valuable insights into the algorithm's behavior under different conditions. The inclusion of an ablation study in the appendix, which includes experiments with models trained from scratch, further strengthens the empirical findings. The paper's emphasis on non-interactive communication and secure aggregation makes it a valuable contribution to the field of distributed machine learning, especially in contexts where communication efficiency and data privacy are paramount. The authors also provide a detailed description of the experimental setup, including the datasets used, the evaluation metrics, and the parameters of the algorithm, which enhances the reproducibility of their results. The paper's exploration of the trade-off between utility and privacy is also a significant contribution, providing a practical framework for balancing these two critical aspects of distributed learning. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and technically sound approach to privacy-preserving distributed learning, with both theoretical and empirical evidence to support its claims.

## weaknesses
Despite its strengths, the paper suffers from several weaknesses that significantly impact its clarity, generalizability, and overall contribution. A primary concern is the paper's presentation, which I found to be dense and difficult to follow. The excessive use of mathematical notation, without sufficient explanation or intuitive examples, makes it challenging for readers not deeply familiar with the field to grasp the core concepts. The multiple definitions of 'sensitivity' across different sections (Definition 1, Lemma 2, and Lemma 13) are a case in point. While the underlying concept is consistent, the specific definitions and bounds in different contexts are not clearly distinguished, leading to confusion. This lack of clarity extends to the explanation of key concepts such as the secure summation protocol and computational differential privacy, which are not adequately explained in the main text. The paper also lacks a clear and explicit statement of its main contributions. While the introduction mentions the problem of minimizing communication overhead, the proposed solution and its advantages over existing methods are not clearly articulated until later in the paper. This makes it difficult for the reader to understand the novelty and significance of the proposed work. The paper's comparison with existing work is also inadequate. While the related work section (Section 2) discusses DP-FL and MPC-based secure summation, it does not provide a detailed comparison table highlighting the advantages of DP-Helmet over existing methods in terms of communication rounds, utility, and privacy guarantees. Table 1 provides some comparison but could be more prominent and detailed in the main text. This lack of a clear comparison makes it difficult to assess the paper's contribution relative to the state-of-the-art. Another significant weakness is the paper's reliance on the assumption that the objective functions are smooth, Lipschitz, and strongly convex. This assumption, explicitly stated in Section 4.1 and Section 5, is crucial for the theoretical guarantees but may not hold for many real-world problems, particularly those involving non-convex objective functions. The paper does not thoroughly discuss the implications of this assumption or provide any empirical analysis of the algorithm's performance on non-convex problems. This limits the generalizability of the presented results and raises concerns about the applicability of the proposed method to a wider range of learning tasks. Furthermore, the paper's focus on only two specific learning algorithms, SVM and Softmax-SLP, is a significant limitation. While these are common algorithms, the applicability of the proposed method to other learning algorithms, especially non-convex ones like deep neural networks, is not clear. The paper does not discuss the challenges of applying blind averaging to other learning algorithms or provide any guidance on how to extend the method to other settings. This narrow focus limits the versatility of the proposed method and raises questions about its broader applicability. The threat model presented in Section 4 is also problematic. The assumption that the adversary has full knowledge about each user's dataset, except for one data point, is overly strong and unrealistic. This assumption gives the adversary too much information, potentially undermining the privacy guarantees of the algorithm. The paper does not adequately justify this assumption or discuss its implications for the security analysis. The paper also makes an incorrect claim regarding output sensitivity. The authors state that output sensitivity suffices for strong privacy results, but all the sensitivity definitions in the paper are based on input sensitivity. This contradiction undermines the validity of the claim and raises concerns about the accuracy of the privacy analysis. Finally, the paper's experimental section, while providing some valuable insights, could be improved. The main experiments use pre-trained models, which limits the practical relevance of the findings. While the ablation study in Appendix G includes experiments with models trained from scratch, the main results would be more meaningful if they included such experiments. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the experimental results, focusing primarily on comparing accuracy under different privacy budgets and user counts. A deeper explanation of why the proposed method performs as it does would be beneficial. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the communication overhead of the proposed method. While the paper claims that the method is non-interactive, it is not clear how the communication cost compares to other distributed learning methods. The paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the communication cost, including the number of communication rounds and the size of the messages exchanged. It would also be useful to compare the communication cost of the proposed method to other distributed learning methods, such as federated learning. The paper also makes a claim about computational DP in Theorem 8, but the computational aspect is not clearly explained, and the term 'nu' is not defined. The theorem relies on the secure summation protocol, which is not introduced until the appendix, making the theorem difficult to understand in the main text. These issues collectively undermine the paper's clarity, rigor, and overall contribution.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First and foremost, the paper needs a significant overhaul in terms of presentation and clarity. The authors should strive to make the paper more accessible to a broader audience by providing more intuitive explanations of key concepts and avoiding excessive use of technical jargon. A high-level overview of the algorithm should be provided before diving into the technical details. The multiple definitions of 'sensitivity' should be clarified, and the authors should consider using a unified definition that encompasses all the different cases. A table summarizing the key notations and definitions would also be beneficial. The paper should also clearly state its main contributions and explicitly compare them to existing work. A detailed comparison table highlighting the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method relative to other approaches would be invaluable. This table should include metrics such as communication rounds, utility, and privacy guarantees. The authors should also provide a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the smooth, Lipschitz, and strongly convex assumption. They should include an empirical analysis of the algorithm's performance on non-convex problems, even if theoretical guarantees are not available. This could involve testing the algorithm on datasets with known non-convex loss functions, such as those arising in deep learning. Furthermore, the paper should discuss potential modifications to the algorithm that could make it more robust to non-convexity, such as using different optimization techniques or adding regularization terms. The paper should also broaden the scope of considered learning algorithms. The authors should discuss the challenges of applying blind averaging to other learning algorithms, particularly deep neural networks. They should explore potential solutions to these challenges, such as using techniques for approximating the loss function or using different aggregation methods. The paper should also discuss the potential for extending the method to other types of learning algorithms, such as reinforcement learning or generative models. The threat model should be revised to be more realistic. The assumption that the adversary has full knowledge of each user's dataset, except for one data point, should be relaxed. The authors should clearly define the adversary's capabilities and limitations and explain how these assumptions impact the security analysis. The claim regarding output sensitivity needs to be clarified and justified. The authors should either provide a clear definition of output sensitivity and demonstrate how it is used in their analysis, or rephrase their claim to accurately reflect the input sensitivity framework. The concerns about Theorem 8 need to be addressed. The authors should clearly explain the computational aspect of the differential privacy guarantee and how it arises from the secure summation protocol. The term 'nu' needs to be defined, and the connection between the theorem and the secure summation protocol should be made explicit. The experimental section should be significantly improved. The authors should include experiments with models trained from scratch, not just pre-trained models. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method's performance and its applicability to real-world scenarios. The experimental results should be presented in a clear and concise way, with appropriate visualizations. The paper should also provide a detailed analysis of the experimental results, explaining the observed trends and patterns. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the communication overhead of the proposed method. This should include a breakdown of the communication cost in terms of the number of communication rounds and the size of the messages exchanged. The paper should also compare the communication cost of the proposed method to other distributed learning methods, such as federated learning. This comparison should be done both theoretically and empirically. It would also be useful to discuss potential techniques for reducing the communication cost of the proposed method, such as using compression techniques or using asynchronous communication protocols. Finally, the paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and suggest future research directions. This would help to contextualize the paper's contribution and identify areas for further investigation.

## questions
Several key uncertainties and methodological choices in this paper warrant further clarification. First, I am curious about the practical implications of the convexity assumption. While the paper acknowledges this assumption, I would like to understand how the algorithm would perform on real-world datasets with non-convex loss functions. What are the potential failure modes, and how could the algorithm be modified to address these issues? Second, I am interested in the challenges of extending the proposed method to other learning algorithms, particularly deep neural networks. What are the specific hurdles in applying blind averaging to non-convex models with a large number of parameters? Are there any techniques for approximating the loss function or using different aggregation methods that could make this extension feasible? Third, I would like to understand the trade-offs between communication efficiency and privacy guarantees in more detail. How does the communication cost of the proposed method compare to other distributed learning methods, such as federated learning, in different settings? Are there any techniques for reducing the communication cost of the proposed method without compromising its privacy guarantees? Fourth, I am concerned about the strong assumption in the threat model that the adversary has full knowledge of each user's dataset, except for one data point. What is the justification for this assumption, and how does it impact the security analysis? Are there any alternative threat models that would be more realistic? Fifth, I would like to understand the computational aspect of the differential privacy guarantee in Theorem 8. How does the secure summation protocol ensure computational DP, and how is the term 'nu' defined? What are the practical implications of this computational DP guarantee? Sixth, I am curious about the choice of using pre-trained models in the main experiments. What is the rationale behind this choice, and how does it impact the generalizability of the results? Would the algorithm perform differently if trained from scratch? Finally, I would like to understand the limitations of the proposed method in terms of the types of learning tasks it can be applied to. Are there any types of learning tasks for which the proposed method is not suitable? What are the potential avenues for future research to address these limitations?
