## summary
This paper investigates the problem of approximating shortest path distances in graphs using a local-global approach. The core idea is to first compute distances from a set of landmark nodes to all other nodes in the graph, and then use these distances to approximate all-pairs shortest paths using the triangle inequality. The authors make two primary contributions. First, they provide a theoretical analysis of the approximation quality of this approach on Erdős-Rényi (ER) random graphs, demonstrating that it can achieve a (1±ε) approximation with high probability using a lower embedding dimension than what is typically required in worst-case scenarios. This theoretical analysis is a valuable contribution to the understanding of the performance of local-global algorithms on random graphs. Second, the authors propose a modification to the local computation step by incorporating Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). Specifically, they use GNNs to learn embeddings that approximate the distances to the landmark nodes, aiming to automate and improve the computational efficiency of this step. The authors demonstrate empirically that this GNN-augmented approach performs better than the traditional method of using exact distances, on both ER graphs and benchmark graph datasets. The paper's approach is to first establish the theoretical guarantees of the local-global approach on ER graphs, and then to improve the practical performance of this approach by using GNNs for the local computation. The empirical results show that the GNN-based approach can achieve comparable or better performance than the traditional approach, while also being more computationally efficient. The authors also show that the GNNs trained on small graphs can be transferred to larger graphs, which is a valuable finding for practical applications. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated approach to approximating shortest path distances, combining theoretical analysis with practical improvements using GNNs. The theoretical analysis provides a solid foundation for the approach, while the GNN-based modification enhances its practical applicability. The paper's findings are significant in that they provide a theoretical understanding of local-global algorithms on random graphs and demonstrate a practical way to improve their performance using GNNs.

The theorems (3.2 and 3.4) are explicitly stated for graphs sampled from the ER distribution. This limitation raises concerns about the generalizability of the theoretical results and the practical applicability of the proposed GNN-augmented algorithm to more complex network topologies. The analysis should be extended to other graph families to validate the robustness of the findings.
