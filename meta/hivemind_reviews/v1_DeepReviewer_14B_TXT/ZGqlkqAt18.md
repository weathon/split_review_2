## summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to offline safe reinforcement learning (RL) by addressing the challenge of learning a safe policy from an offline dataset that lacks explicit cost labels, relying instead on a small set of safe demonstrations. The authors propose a two-stage optimization framework called Diffusion-guided Safe Policy Optimization (DSPO). In the first stage, a return-agnostic discriminator, termed the SafetyTransformer, is trained to generate trajectory-wise safety signals. This discriminator is designed to distinguish between safe and unsafe trajectories without being influenced by the returns of those trajectories. The core idea is to learn a notion of safety that is independent of the reward, which is crucial when high-reward trajectories might be unsafe. In the second stage, a conditional diffusion model is trained using these safety signals and trajectory returns. This diffusion model is tasked with generating trajectories that are both high-return and safe. Finally, a policy is derived through behavior cloning (BC) from the trajectories generated by the diffusion model. The authors evaluate DSPO across a range of environments, including SafetyGym, BulletGym, and MetaDrive, demonstrating that it can achieve safe policies with high returns, outperforming various established baselines. The paper's central contribution lies in its ability to learn safe policies from offline data without relying on Markovian cost labels, which is a significant step towards addressing real-world safe RL problems where such labels are often unavailable or difficult to obtain. The use of a return-agnostic discriminator and a conditional diffusion model is a novel combination that allows the method to effectively leverage both safety and return information. The empirical results, while promising, also reveal some limitations, particularly in certain environments where the performance gains over simpler baselines are not always substantial. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of offline safe RL, offering a practical approach to learning safe policies from datasets without explicit cost labels.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. First, the paper tackles a very practical and important problem in offline safe RL: learning a safe policy from an offline dataset that lacks explicit cost labels. This is a significant contribution because obtaining comprehensive Markovian cost labels can be difficult in many real-world scenarios. The authors' approach of using a small set of safe demonstrations to guide the learning process is a clever way to address this challenge. Second, the use of a conditional diffusion model to generate trajectories conditioned on both return and safety signals is a novel and interesting approach. The diffusion model's expressive capability allows it to capture the complex relationship between safety and return, leading to the generation of high-performing and safe trajectories. This is a key technical innovation that distinguishes this work from other offline safe RL methods. The return-agnostic discriminator is also a notable contribution, as it allows the method to learn safety signals that are not biased by the returns of the trajectories. This is crucial in situations where high-reward trajectories might be unsafe. Third, the paper provides a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method across multiple environments, including SafetyGym, BulletGym, and MetaDrive. The results demonstrate that DSPO can achieve safe policies with high returns, significantly outperforming various established baselines. This thorough experimental evaluation provides strong empirical support for the effectiveness of the proposed method. The ablation studies and sensitivity analyses further validate the effectiveness of the proposed components and design choices. The authors have clearly put effort into demonstrating the robustness of their approach. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly explains the proposed method and its underlying principles. The authors have done a good job of presenting their work in a way that is easy to understand, which is essential for the dissemination of their research.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First and foremost, the paper lacks a theoretical analysis of the proposed method. While the empirical results are promising, there is no formal analysis of the convergence properties of the two-stage optimization process, nor is there any discussion of the conditions under which the learned safety signals accurately reflect true safety. This absence of theoretical grounding makes it difficult to fully understand why and how the method works, and it also limits the ability to assess its robustness and reliability. Furthermore, there is no discussion on the potential for error propagation between the discriminator and the diffusion model. This is a critical oversight, as errors in the discriminator's safety signals could propagate through the diffusion model and negatively impact the final policy. The lack of theoretical analysis is a significant limitation, and I have high confidence in this assessment. Second, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. For example, the paper does not discuss how the performance of DSPO scales with the complexity of the task or the size of the offline dataset. It is unclear how the method would perform in environments with very long time horizons or with high-dimensional state spaces. The paper also lacks an analysis of the sensitivity of the method to the quality and quantity of the safe demonstrations. This is a crucial omission, as the method relies on these demonstrations to learn safety signals. The absence of this discussion makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method in different scenarios. I have high confidence in this assessment as well. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. Training a conditional diffusion model can be computationally expensive, and it is important to understand the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. The paper should include a breakdown of the computational resources required for each stage of the method, including the training time and memory usage, and compare these costs to other offline safe RL methods. It is also unclear how the computational cost scales with the size of the offline dataset and the complexity of the environment. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method. I have high confidence in this assessment. Finally, while the paper presents a novel combination of existing techniques, the novelty of the proposed method is somewhat limited. The core components, a discriminator and a diffusion model, are established techniques. The return-agnostic discriminator, while a useful addition, does not represent a fundamental departure from existing approaches that use similar discriminators for imitation learning or safety certification. The conditional diffusion model, while effective, is also an existing technique, and its application here, while practical, lacks substantial novelty. Additionally, the experimental results are not entirely convincing. In Table 1, the performance of DSPO is not significantly better than BC-Safe in many tasks. Additionally, the return of DSPO in the CarButton task is much lower than that of CDT-V, which is counterintuitive given that DSPO uses additional offline data. The similar performance to BC-Safe suggests that the method may not be effectively leveraging the full offline dataset in many scenarios, and the lower performance than CDT-V in CarButton raises concerns about the method's ability to effectively utilize the offline data in all environments. I have medium confidence in this assessment, as the method does show improvements in some environments, but the lack of consistent and substantial gains is a concern.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the paper would greatly benefit from a more in-depth theoretical analysis of the proposed method. This should include an investigation of the convergence properties of the two-stage optimization process, perhaps by analyzing the loss landscapes of the discriminator and the diffusion model. It would also be beneficial to investigate the conditions under which the learned safety signals accurately reflect true safety, and to quantify the potential for error propagation between the discriminator and the diffusion model. This could involve analyzing the sensitivity of the diffusion model to noisy or inaccurate safety signals. Furthermore, the authors should consider providing a theoretical bound on the performance of the learned policy, perhaps by relating it to the quality of the safe demonstrations and the accuracy of the learned safety signals. Such analysis would greatly enhance the paper's contribution and provide a more solid foundation for future research. Second, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the limitations of the proposed method. This should include experiments on a wider range of environments, including those with longer time horizons and higher-dimensional state spaces. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the method to the quality and quantity of the safe demonstrations. For example, how does the performance of DSPO change when the number of safe demonstrations is significantly reduced, or when the safe demonstrations are noisy or suboptimal? It would also be useful to explore the method's robustness to out-of-distribution data, and to discuss the potential for the method to fail in certain scenarios. This analysis would provide a more complete picture of the method's strengths and weaknesses, and would help guide future research in this area. Third, the paper should include a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. This should include a breakdown of the computational resources required for each stage of the method, including the training time and memory usage. The authors should also compare these costs to other offline safe RL methods, and discuss the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. For example, how does the training time of DSPO scale with the size of the offline dataset and the complexity of the environment? Are there any ways to reduce the computational cost of the method, such as by using more efficient training techniques or by reducing the size of the diffusion model? This analysis would be essential for practitioners who are considering using DSPO in real-world applications. Finally, the authors should address the concerns about the novelty of the method by more clearly articulating the specific challenges that are addressed by their particular combination of techniques and how it differs from existing approaches. They should also investigate why the performance of DSPO is not always significantly better than BC-Safe and why it underperforms CDT-V in the CarButton task. This could involve analyzing the trajectories generated by DSPO and BC-Safe to identify any differences in their behavior, and investigating the data used by each method in the CarButton task. It would also be beneficial to include statistical significance tests in the experimental results to provide a more rigorous assessment of the performance differences between the proposed method and the baselines.
