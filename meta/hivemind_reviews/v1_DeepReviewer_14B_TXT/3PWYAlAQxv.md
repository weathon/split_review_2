## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to neural network training, focusing on permutation-based methods where the weights of the network are fixed, and only their order is altered during training. This approach is motivated by potential applications in hardware implementations, where modifying connection locations might be easier than changing the numerical values of weights. The core contribution of the paper is a theoretical proof demonstrating that a single-layer ReLU network, trained using this permutation-based method, can approximate any continuous function on the interval [0,1] to arbitrary precision. The authors achieve this by constructing specific step function approximations using ReLU networks with permuted weights. The theoretical analysis is supported by numerical experiments that validate the effectiveness of the method in regression tasks, particularly in one-dimensional settings. The paper also explores the influence of different initialization strategies and observes the patterns of weight permutations during training. While the paper provides a strong theoretical foundation for permutation-based training in ReLU networks, it is limited to one-dimensional continuous functions and single-layer architectures. The practical implications of this approach, especially in higher-dimensional settings and with different activation functions, remain open for future research. Overall, the paper presents a significant theoretical contribution to the field of neural network training, offering a new perspective on how neural networks can learn and generalize under constrained weight conditions.

## Strengths
One of the primary strengths of this paper is its exploration of a unique and under-explored area in neural network training: permutation-based methods with fixed weights. This approach is particularly relevant for hardware implementations, where modifying connection locations might be more feasible than changing the numerical values of weights. The paper's theoretical contribution is significant, as it provides a rigorous proof that a single-layer ReLU network, trained using permutation-based methods, can approximate any continuous function on the interval [0,1] to arbitrary precision. This is a non-trivial result, as it demonstrates the potential of permutation-based training to achieve universal approximation, a key property of neural networks. The authors' construction of step function approximations using ReLU networks with permuted weights is both elegant and effective, showcasing their deep understanding of ReLU network properties. Furthermore, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the complex theoretical concepts accessible to a broader audience. The numerical experiments, while limited in scope, provide empirical evidence supporting the theoretical findings, demonstrating the effectiveness of the method in regression tasks. The exploration of different initialization strategies and the observation of permutation patterns during training also offer valuable insights into the behavior of this training method. The paper's focus on a constrained training scenario, where weights are fixed, is a novel contribution that opens up new avenues for research in neural network training and optimization. The authors have successfully demonstrated that even with such constraints, neural networks can achieve universal approximation, which is a significant theoretical achievement.

## Weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling theoretical framework for permutation-based training, several limitations restrict its practical applicability and generalizability. First, the theoretical analysis is explicitly limited to one-dimensional continuous functions. The core of the proof relies on constructing specific step function approximations using ReLU networks with permuted weights, a process that is highly dependent on the one-dimensional nature of the problem. As the paper states in the 'main_idea' section, the focus is on 'one-dimensional continuous functions'. The theorems are also stated for functions in C([0,1]), indicating a one-dimensional domain. The paper does not provide any theoretical analysis or discussion on how this construction could be extended to higher-dimensional functions. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world applications involve multi-dimensional data. The paper's 'Experiments' section includes a subsection on 'Approximating Multi-Dimensional Continuous Functions,' but this is presented as an empirical extension, not a theoretical one. The lack of theoretical support for higher dimensions raises questions about the generalizability of the results. Second, the analysis is specific to ReLU activation functions. The theoretical proof relies heavily on the piecewise linearity and non-negativity of the ReLU activation function. The construction of the step function approximations, as detailed in the 'method' section, is deeply intertwined with these properties. The paper does not discuss the limitations of ReLU in this context or provide any insights into how the results might be extended to other activation functions, such as sigmoid or tanh. This is a crucial limitation, as different activation functions are often used in practice, and their behavior under permutation-based training is unknown. The paper's reliance on ReLU restricts the applicability of the theoretical results. Third, the paper does not consider multi-layer networks. The theoretical analysis and the experiments are limited to single-hidden-layer networks. The 'method' section describes a 'one-hidden-layer feed-forward ReLU network', and the theoretical results are derived within this context. The paper does not extend its theoretical guarantees to deeper architectures, which are more commonly used in practice. This is a significant limitation, as the behavior of permutation-based training in multi-layer networks is unknown. Finally, while the paper provides a theoretical guarantee for the approximation capabilities of permutation-based training, it does not address the question of how the width of the network scales with the desired approximation accuracy. The theorems state that 'for any function f* ∈ C([0,1]) and any small number ε > 0, there exists a large integer n ∈ Z+...', but the paper does not provide a concrete analysis of how this 'large integer n' scales with ε. This is a crucial aspect of universal approximation results, and the lack of discussion on this point limits the practical implications of the theoretical findings. The numerical simulations, while demonstrating the effectiveness of the method, are also quite limited in scope. The paper explores different dimensions and initialization strategies, but the number of distinct experimental setups is relatively small. This limits the generalizability of the empirical findings. In summary, the paper's limitations in dimensionality, activation function, network architecture, and the lack of analysis on network width scaling significantly restrict the practical applicability and generalizability of the results. These limitations are not speculative but are directly evident from the paper's content and theoretical framework. My confidence in these identified issues is high, as they are clearly supported by the paper's explicit statements and the design of the theoretical proofs.
