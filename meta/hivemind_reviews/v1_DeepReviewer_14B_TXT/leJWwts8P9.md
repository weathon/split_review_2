## summary
This paper introduces a novel statistical framework for determining whether two language models have been trained independently, a problem of increasing importance in the era of widespread model sharing and reuse. The core contribution lies in the development of a family of statistical tests that yield exact p-values under the null hypothesis of independent training. These tests are designed to be architecture-agnostic, relying on comparisons of weight and activation similarities between the models under investigation and independently trained copies. The methodology involves simulating independent training runs to establish a baseline distribution of similarity, against which the similarity between the models being tested is compared. The authors evaluate their approach on a diverse set of 21 open-weight models, forming 210 pairs, demonstrating the method's ability to reliably identify pairs of fine-tuned models, even after substantial modifications. Notably, the tests remain effective even when one model has been fine-tuned on a significantly larger dataset, as shown by the example of Llama 2 and Llemma. Furthermore, the paper explores transformations that can evade the proposed tests without altering model outputs, and proposes a mechanism for matching hidden activations between MLP layers of different models to enhance robustness. This work is significant because it provides a principled and empirically validated approach to model provenance, addressing a critical need in the machine learning community. The authors not only present a method but also thoroughly investigate its limitations and propose solutions, demonstrating a commitment to practical applicability. The paper's findings suggest that the proposed statistical tests can be a valuable tool for verifying model independence and detecting derivation, contributing to greater transparency and accountability in the use of large language models.

## strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the core idea of using statistical tests to determine model independence is both novel and highly relevant. The authors have successfully translated this idea into a concrete methodology that is both principled and empirically sound. The use of exact p-values, as opposed to approximate ones, is a significant strength, providing a more rigorous statistical foundation for the tests. The architecture-agnostic nature of the method, as claimed by the authors, is also a major advantage, making it applicable to a wide range of models. The extensive experimental evaluation is another key strength. The authors have tested their method on a large and diverse set of models, including many real-world examples of fine-tuning. The results convincingly demonstrate the method's ability to detect dependence between models, even in challenging scenarios involving substantial fine-tuning. The inclusion of the Llama 2 and Llemma example is particularly compelling, showcasing the method's robustness in the face of significant data augmentation. Furthermore, I appreciate the authors' commitment to addressing the limitations of their approach. The identification of weight transformations that can evade the tests and the proposal of a robust matching mechanism for hidden activations demonstrate a thorough and thoughtful approach to the problem. This proactive stance towards potential weaknesses enhances the credibility and practical value of the work. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the complex technical details accessible to a broad audience. The clear presentation of the methodology, the experimental setup, and the results contributes significantly to the overall quality of the paper.

## weaknesses
While I find the paper to be generally strong, there are several weaknesses that I have identified through my analysis. First, while the authors claim that their method is architecture-agnostic, the detailed description of the weight and activation similarity measures suggests a reliance on direct comparison of corresponding layers. Specifically, the method description, particularly in the 'Weight and Activation Similarity' section, implies a direct comparison of weights and activations in corresponding layers of the two models being compared. This approach would be problematic when comparing models with different architectures, where there isn't a one-to-one correspondence between layers. Although the authors state that the tests are 'architecture-agnostic,' the examples provided, such as comparing the 'first layer of the MLP,' suggest a focus on models with similar structures for direct comparison. The introduction of 'Robust Matching of Hidden Activations' attempts to address some limitations, but it doesn't explicitly tackle the issue of fundamentally different architectures. This discrepancy between the claim of architecture-agnosticism and the implementation details raises concerns about the method's applicability in real-world scenarios where models may have diverse architectures. This is a significant limitation, as it restricts the method's utility in situations where models are adapted from pre-trained models with different structures. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is directly supported by the method description and the lack of explicit handling of different architectures. 

Second, the method's reliance on access to model weights and activations is a significant practical limitation. As stated in the 'Method/Overview' section, the tests are 'based on simulating independent copies of the models and comparing various measures of similarity in their weights and activations.' This requirement for internal model parameters severely restricts the method's applicability in real-world scenarios where models are often proprietary or only accessible through APIs. In many cases, users only have access to the input-output behavior of models, not their internal weights and activations. This dependence on internal parameters limits the method's use in situations where model weights are protected by intellectual property or when dealing with black-box models. This is a critical limitation that significantly reduces the method's practical applicability. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is explicitly stated in the method description. 

Third, while the paper demonstrates the method's robustness to fine-tuning, it acknowledges that certain weight transformations can evade detection. The 'main_idea' section mentions identifying transformations that break the tests, and the 'Robust Matching of Hidden Activations' section proposes a mechanism to address this. While the paper shows the robust matching mechanism is effective against some transformations, it does not fully explore the implications of these transformations, particularly in the context of adversarial attacks designed to circumvent the proposed test. The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the types of transformations that are most problematic, nor does it investigate more sophisticated weight manipulation techniques such as weight shuffling, low-rank approximations, or quantization. The robustness of the method against these more complex transformations remains unclear. This is a significant concern, as adversaries may intentionally use such transformations to obfuscate model provenance. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper itself acknowledges the existence of such transformations and the lack of a comprehensive analysis of more sophisticated techniques. 

Finally, while the paper includes experiments on pruned models, the method's reliance on direct weight comparison makes it potentially unsuitable for scenarios where models have significantly different structures due to pruning. The 'Weight and Activation Similarity' section describes comparing weights, and pruning changes the weight structure by removing weights. Although the 'Robust Matching of Hidden Activations Evaluation' includes results for pruned models, the initial reliance on direct weight comparison suggests that the method may not be robust to significant structural changes caused by pruning. This is a concern because pruning is a common technique for model optimization, and the inability to handle pruned models would limit the method's practical use. My confidence in this limitation is medium, as the paper does include experiments on pruned models, but the initial method description raises concerns about its applicability in such scenarios.

## suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete suggestions for improving this work. First, to address the limitation of the method's applicability to models with different architectures, the authors should explore techniques such as feature alignment or representation learning. These techniques could be used to map the internal representations of different models into a common space where similarity can be measured. For example, the authors could investigate using adversarial training to learn a mapping between the hidden layers of two different architectures, allowing for a more meaningful comparison of their internal states. This would significantly broaden the applicability of the method and make it more robust to variations in model architecture. This would involve developing new methods for comparing models with different layer structures, activation functions, or even numbers of parameters. 

Second, to overcome the reliance on access to model weights and activations, the authors should investigate alternative approaches that can be used when only input-output access is available. One potential direction is to explore methods based on analyzing the input-output behavior of models. For example, the authors could investigate the use of adversarial examples or carefully crafted input sequences to probe the internal representations of models. By comparing the responses of different models to these inputs, it might be possible to infer whether they share a common origin or have been trained independently. This would require developing new techniques for analyzing input-output patterns and establishing statistical tests that can detect subtle differences in model behavior. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of techniques such as model distillation or surrogate models to approximate the behavior of proprietary models, allowing for the application of their method in situations where direct access is not possible. This would involve training a more accessible model to mimic the behavior of the proprietary model and then applying the proposed statistical test to the surrogate model. 

Third, to address the limitations regarding weight transformations, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the types of transformations that can evade detection. This could involve exploring a wider range of weight manipulation techniques, including more complex operations such as weight shuffling, low-rank approximations, and quantization. It is crucial to understand the limitations of the proposed method in the face of these transformations, as adversaries may intentionally use them to obfuscate model provenance. The paper should also explore potential defenses against these evasion techniques, such as incorporating more robust similarity metrics or using adversarial training to make the test more resilient. A more detailed analysis of the trade-offs between robustness and computational cost is also needed. This would involve a more comprehensive investigation of the impact of different weight transformations on the statistical properties of the model weights and activations. 

Finally, the authors should provide more detailed guidance on how to apply their method in practice. This includes specifying the required resources, such as computational power and memory, as well as the steps involved in preparing the models for testing. The authors should also discuss the limitations of their method in more detail, including the types of models and training scenarios where it may not be applicable. This would help potential users to understand the strengths and weaknesses of the method and to make informed decisions about its applicability to their specific use cases. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the statistical properties of their test, including the distribution of p-values under the null hypothesis and the power of the test to detect different levels of model dependence. This would help to establish the statistical validity of the method and to provide a more rigorous basis for its interpretation.

## questions

First, how does the computational cost of the proposed method scale with the size of the models being compared? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity, and it would be important to understand the practical limitations of the method in terms of the size of the models it can handle. Are there any optimizations that can be applied to improve efficiency, especially when dealing with very large language models? This is a critical question for the practical applicability of the method. 

Second, the paper mentions that the test remains effective even after substantial fine-tuning. Is there a limit to the amount of fine-tuning that the test can reliably detect? How does the test perform when only a small portion of the model has been fine-tuned? It would be valuable to understand the sensitivity of the test to different levels of fine-tuning and to identify any thresholds where the test may become less effective. This would help to establish the practical limits of the method. 

Third, you mention that certain weight transformations can break the effectiveness of the test. Are there other types of transformations that could potentially evade detection? How robust is the proposed mechanism for matching hidden activations against a wider range of weight transformations? It would be beneficial to have a more comprehensive understanding of the limitations of the method in the face of various weight transformations, especially those that could be used adversarially. This would help to identify potential vulnerabilities and guide future research. 

Fourth, how does the choice of similarity metric affect the performance of the test? Are there other similarity metrics that could be more effective in certain scenarios? The paper uses cosine similarity, but it would be valuable to explore other metrics and to understand their impact on the test's sensitivity and robustness. This could lead to improvements in the method's performance. 

Finally, the paper focuses on transformer-based LLMs. How well does the proposed method generalize to other types of models (e.g., smaller language models, non-LLM architectures)? It would be important to understand the method's applicability to a broader range of models and to identify any modifications that may be necessary for different architectures. This would help to establish the generalizability of the method and its potential for wider adoption.
