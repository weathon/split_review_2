## Summary
This paper introduces MiniLLM, a novel knowledge distillation (KD) approach for large language models (LLMs) that leverages reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) instead of the traditional forward KLD. The authors argue that forward KLD, commonly used in standard KD, is suboptimal for generative tasks performed by LLMs because it can lead to the student model overestimating low-probability regions of the teacher's distribution. To address this, they propose minimizing the reverse KLD, which encourages the student model to focus on the major modes of the teacher's distribution, effectively ignoring less probable outputs. This approach, they claim, is more suitable for generative language models, where capturing the core patterns of the teacher's output is more important than mimicking every detail. To optimize this objective, the authors derive a policy gradient-based approach and introduce several techniques to stabilize and improve training, including single-step decomposition to reduce variance, teacher-mixed sampling to prevent reward hacking, and length normalization to mitigate length bias. The student models trained with this method are named MiniLLM. The authors conduct extensive experiments across various generative language models, ranging in size from 120 million to 13 billion parameters, in an instruction-following setting. They evaluate the performance of MiniLLM using five datasets, employing metrics such as ROUGE-L and human judgment. The results demonstrate that MiniLLM consistently outperforms standard KD baselines, generating more precise responses with higher overall quality, lower exposure bias, better calibration, and improved long-text generation performance. The method is also shown to be scalable across different model families. The authors' work offers a compelling alternative to traditional KD methods for LLMs, with a focus on improving the quality and reliability of distilled models for generative tasks. The core contribution lies in the application of reverse KLD and the associated optimization techniques, which are shown to be effective in the context of LLM distillation. The empirical findings suggest that MiniLLM is a promising approach for creating smaller, more efficient models that retain the core capabilities of their larger counterparts. However, the paper also has limitations, particularly in the scope of evaluation and the depth of analysis, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its novel application of reverse KLD for knowledge distillation of large language models. The authors make a compelling argument that forward KLD, the standard approach in many KD methods, is not well-suited for generative tasks due to its tendency to overemphasize low-probability regions of the teacher's output distribution. By contrast, reverse KLD encourages the student model to focus on the major modes of the teacher's distribution, which is more appropriate for capturing the core patterns of generative models. This is a significant contribution, as it challenges the conventional wisdom in KD and offers a more tailored approach for LLMs. Furthermore, the authors' derivation of a policy gradient-based optimization method for the reverse KLD objective is a notable technical achievement. This is not a trivial undertaking, and the authors provide a clear and well-motivated approach. The additional techniques they introduce, such as single-step decomposition, teacher-mixed sampling, and length normalization, further enhance the practicality and effectiveness of their method. These techniques address specific challenges in training with reverse KLD, such as variance reduction and reward hacking, demonstrating a thorough understanding of the practical issues involved. The empirical results presented in the paper are also a significant strength. The authors conduct extensive experiments across a range of model sizes and families, demonstrating the scalability and robustness of their approach. The consistent outperformance of MiniLLM over standard KD baselines across multiple datasets and evaluation metrics provides strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method. The fact that MiniLLM achieves higher quality responses, lower exposure bias, better calibration, and improved long-text generation performance is particularly impressive. The authors also provide ablation studies that demonstrate the importance of each of the optimization techniques they introduce, further strengthening the empirical findings. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, technically sound, and empirically validated approach to knowledge distillation for LLMs, making a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, while the authors propose the use of reverse KLD as a core innovation, the novelty of this approach is somewhat limited. As the authors themselves acknowledge by citing Tailor, the use of reverse KLD in knowledge distillation has been explored in prior work. While the specific application to LLMs and the associated optimization techniques are novel, the core idea of using reverse KLD is not entirely new. This is not to say that the paper's contribution is insignificant, but it is important to acknowledge the existing body of work on reverse KLD in KD. Second, and more importantly, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art knowledge distillation methods for LLMs. Specifically, the authors do not compare their approach with methods like Tailor, GKD, or Mini-1.3B, all of which are relevant baselines for this task. The absence of these comparisons makes it difficult to assess the relative performance and advantages of MiniLLM. For instance, the reviewer notes that Mini-1.3B achieves better performance than the proposed approach in Table 1, which raises concerns about the practical significance of the proposed method. Without a direct comparison, it is unclear whether the gains observed are due to the reverse KLD objective or other factors in the training procedure. This lack of comparison significantly limits the paper's ability to establish the superiority of MiniLLM over existing techniques. Furthermore, the paper's evaluation is primarily focused on instruction-following tasks. While the authors use multiple datasets within this domain, they do not evaluate their method on other important NLP tasks such as summarization, translation, or question answering. This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings. For example, summarization often requires capturing the essence of long documents, which might expose weaknesses in the distilled model's ability to handle long-range dependencies. Similarly, translation tasks would test the model's ability to handle cross-lingual semantics, which is not directly assessed by instruction-following tasks. The lack of evaluation on these diverse tasks makes it difficult to ascertain the robustness of the proposed method. The authors also claim that standard KD objectives are sub-optimal for LLMs due to the complex and high-dimensional output spaces of generative tasks. While they do provide a brief explanation of the limitations of forward KLD, citing that it can lead to the student model assigning unreasonably high probabilities to the void regions of the teacher's distribution, this explanation could be more detailed. A more in-depth discussion of the mismatch between the training objective and the generative nature of LLMs would be beneficial. The authors also propose reverse KLD to address this issue, but they do not provide a detailed analysis of the potential drawbacks of this approach. While focusing on the major modes might improve performance on common tasks, it could lead to a loss of diversity in the student model's output, potentially hindering its ability to generalize to less frequent but still valid outputs. This trade-off between mode-seeking and diversity needs further exploration. The authors do address the potential loss of diversity in the 'Generation Diversity' section, but the analysis is limited. The authors also propose several optimization strategies, such as single-step decomposition, teacher-mixed sampling, and length normalization. While they provide ablation studies to show the effectiveness of these strategies, they do not provide a detailed analysis of how these strategies compare to other variance reduction techniques or how the optimal mixing ratio for teacher samples is determined. A more rigorous analysis of these techniques is needed. Finally, the authors use ROUGE-L and human judgment as evaluation metrics, but they do not discuss the limitations of these metrics. ROUGE-L primarily measures n-gram overlap and may not capture the semantic quality or coherence of the generated text. Similarly, human judgment can be subjective and may not be consistent across different evaluators. The authors should acknowledge these limitations and consider incorporating additional metrics that capture different aspects of text quality. The lack of discussion about the limitations of the evaluation metrics weakens the paper's overall analysis. In summary, while the paper presents a promising approach to knowledge distillation for LLMs, the lack of comprehensive comparisons, the narrow scope of evaluation, and the limited analysis of the proposed method's limitations are significant weaknesses that need to be addressed.

## Suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more thorough comparison with other state-of-the-art knowledge distillation methods for LLMs. Specifically, they should include a direct comparison with methods like Tailor, GKD, and Mini-1.3B. This comparison should not only focus on overall performance metrics but also delve into the specific strengths and weaknesses of each method. For example, it would be useful to analyze the computational cost, the training time, and the memory requirements of each method. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different hyperparameters on the performance of both their method and the baseline methods. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the trade-offs between different approaches and help to identify the most effective strategies for knowledge distillation of large language models. The authors should also consider ablating different components of their method to understand the contribution of each component to the overall performance. Second, the authors should expand the scope of their evaluation beyond instruction-following tasks. Specifically, they should include summarization tasks, such as those found in the CNN/DailyMail dataset, to assess the model's ability to handle long-form text and extract key information. Similarly, they should incorporate translation tasks, such as those in the WMT dataset, to evaluate the model's cross-lingual capabilities. Furthermore, they should include question answering tasks, like SQuAD, to evaluate the model's ability to understand and reason about factual information. These additional evaluations would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses across different task types and help to establish the generalizability of the proposed approach. The authors should also analyze the performance of the distilled model on these tasks in relation to the teacher model, providing a clear picture of the knowledge transfer process. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the limitations of standard knowledge distillation (KD) objectives when applied to generative tasks performed by large language models (LLMs). Specifically, they should elaborate on why the forward KL divergence, which is commonly used in standard KD, is not well-suited for capturing the complexities of generative tasks. This could involve a discussion of how the forward KL divergence might lead to a mismatch between the training objective and the desired output distribution of the student model. For example, the authors could analyze how the forward KL divergence might encourage the student model to overfit to the teacher's training data, rather than learning to generate diverse and high-quality text. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more rigorous justification for using reverse KL divergence, including a discussion of its potential drawbacks. While the mode-seeking behavior of reverse KL divergence might be beneficial in some cases, it could also lead to a loss of diversity in the student model's output. The authors should explore this trade-off and discuss how they mitigate the potential negative effects of mode-seeking. This could involve analyzing the impact of reverse KL divergence on the student model's ability to generate diverse and creative text, and comparing it to the performance of standard KD methods in this regard. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the effectiveness of the proposed optimization strategies. For example, they should explain how single-step decomposition specifically reduces variance compared to other variance reduction techniques, such as importance sampling or control variates. They should also provide a more thorough analysis of the teacher-mixed sampling strategy, including a discussion of how the optimal mixing ratio is determined and how it affects the student's learning process. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how length normalization eliminates length bias, and how this bias affects the performance of the student model. This could involve analyzing the impact of different length normalization techniques on the student model's ability to generate text of varying lengths. The authors should also consider including ablation studies to demonstrate the effectiveness of each of these strategies individually and in combination. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the limitations of the evaluation metrics used in the paper. While ROUGE-L and human judgment are commonly used in text generation tasks, they have limitations that should be acknowledged. For example, ROUGE-L primarily measures n-gram overlap and may not capture the semantic quality or coherence of the generated text. Similarly, human judgment can be subjective and may not be consistent across different evaluators. The authors should discuss these limitations and consider incorporating additional metrics that capture different aspects of text quality, such as semantic similarity, coherence, and diversity. They could also consider using metrics that are specifically designed for evaluating generative models, such as BLEU or METEOR. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the human evaluation process, including the instructions given to the evaluators and the measures taken to ensure consistency and reliability. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and provide a more comprehensive and convincing evaluation of their proposed method.

## Questions
Several key questions arise from my analysis of this paper. First, why did the authors choose reverse KL divergence over other divergence metrics, such as the Total Variation Distance (TVD) used in Tailor and GKD, for knowledge distillation of LLMs? What are the specific benefits and drawbacks of using reverse KL compared to these alternative metrics in the context of LLMs? A more detailed discussion of the rationale behind this choice would be beneficial. Second, how does the proposed approach compare with other state-of-the-art KD methods for LLMs, such as Tailor, GKD, and Mini-1.3B, in terms of both performance and computational cost? The paper lacks a direct comparison with these methods, making it difficult to assess the relative advantages of MiniLLM. A more thorough comparison would be essential to establish the significance of the proposed method. Third, how does the proposed method perform on tasks other than instruction-following, such as summarization, translation, and question answering? The paper's evaluation is primarily focused on instruction-following, which limits the generalizability of the findings. An evaluation on a broader range of tasks would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses. Fourth, what is the impact of the hyperparameters on the performance of the proposed method, and how were these hyperparameters chosen? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the method to different hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, the batch size, and the number of training epochs. A more thorough analysis of the hyperparameter space would be beneficial. Fifth, what is the computational cost of the proposed method compared to other KD methods, and how does this cost scale with the size of the models? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method, which is an important consideration for practical applications. A comparison with other KD methods in terms of computational cost would be valuable. Finally, what are the limitations of the proposed method, and what are the potential directions for future research? The paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method, which is an important aspect of any research paper. A more thorough discussion of the limitations and potential future directions would be beneficial.
