## summary
This paper introduces FoundTS, a novel benchmark designed for the comprehensive evaluation of time series forecasting (TSF) foundation models. The core contribution of this work lies in the creation of a standardized evaluation pipeline that encompasses a diverse set of datasets, models, and forecasting strategies. The authors aim to address the lack of a unified framework for assessing the performance of foundation models in TSF, which has become an increasingly important area of research. The benchmark includes a variety of foundation models, such as large language models (LLMs) and pre-trained time series models, and supports evaluation under zero-shot, few-shot, and full-shot learning scenarios. The methodological approach involves a rigorous standardization of data processing, including dataset splitting, loading, normalization, and sampling, ensuring fair comparisons across different models. The empirical findings presented in the paper highlight the strengths and weaknesses of various foundation models under different data availability conditions. For instance, the results demonstrate that while some models exhibit strong zero-shot capabilities, they may not consistently outperform traditional time series models in full-shot scenarios. The paper also explores the impact of pre-training on model performance, revealing that the choice of pre-training dataset can significantly influence the effectiveness of a foundation model. The authors further analyze the computational efficiency of the models, considering factors such as runtime and the number of parameters. Overall, the significance of this work lies in its provision of a much-needed standardized benchmark for the evaluation of TSF foundation models, which will undoubtedly facilitate future research in this area. By offering a comprehensive evaluation framework, the authors enable researchers to compare different models on a level playing field, accelerating the development of more robust and generalizable time series forecasting techniques. The paper's findings also provide valuable insights into the practical implications of using foundation models for TSF, highlighting the trade-offs between different models and evaluation strategies. The benchmark is intended to serve as a valuable resource for the community, promoting further exploration and innovation in the field of time series forecasting.

This is a significant omission, as computational cost is a critical factor for real-world deployment. Without a detailed breakdown of the computational demands of each model, practitioners are left without crucial information for making informed decisions about which models are suitable for their specific hardware and latency requirements. This weakness is evident in the 'Model efficiency analysis' section, where the authors present runtime and parameter counts but do not delve into a more granular analysis of computational resources. ## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the computational demands of the evaluated foundation models. This should include a detailed analysis of the training and inference time complexities, as well as memory requirements. Specifically, providing a breakdown of the FLOPs required for training and inference, along with the memory footprint of each model, would allow practitioners to make informed decisions about which models are suitable for their specific hardware and latency requirements. Furthermore, the paper should explore the trade-offs between model size, computational cost, and forecasting accuracy, which would provide valuable insights into the efficiency of different foundation models. This analysis should also consider the impact of different batch sizes and hardware configurations on the observed computational costs. Secondly, the authors should delve deeper into the limitations of current foundation models for time series forecasting. This should include an analysis of the specific scenarios where these models struggle, such as time series with complex seasonality, high levels of noise, or non-stationary behavior. Furthermore, the paper should explore the potential reasons for these limitations, such as the inability of current models to capture long-range dependencies or the lack of robustness to outliers. This analysis should also include a discussion of the limitations of the benchmark itself, such as the types of datasets included and the evaluation metrics used, and how these choices might influence the conclusions drawn. Thirdly, the authors should explore the impact of different pre-training datasets on the performance of the foundation models. This should involve conducting experiments to systematically compare the performance of models pre-trained on datasets with different characteristics, such as those with varying levels of noise, seasonality, or length. This analysis would provide valuable insights into the importance of pre-training data selection and could guide future research on developing more robust and generalizable foundation models. Fourthly, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the models to different hyperparameters. This should involve conducting experiments to explore how different hyperparameters, such as learning rate, batch size, and architecture-specific parameters, affect the performance of the models. This analysis would provide valuable guidance for practitioners who need to fine-tune these models for specific tasks. Fifthly, the authors should explore the use of ensemble methods to combine the predictions of multiple foundation models. This should involve conducting experiments to investigate the potential benefits of combining the predictions of multiple models, which could potentially improve the overall forecasting performance. This analysis should also explore different ensemble techniques, such as bagging, boosting, or stacking. Sixthly, the authors should include a more thorough analysis of potential biases within the datasets used for evaluation. This should involve a detailed analysis of how these characteristics might influence the performance of different foundation models, and whether the benchmark adequately accounts for these variations. Furthermore, the paper should explore the impact of data quality issues, such as missing values or outliers, on the evaluation results. Finally, the authors should incorporate a more qualitative analysis of the foundation models' behavior. This could involve analyzing the models' predictions on specific data subsets, such as those with high seasonality, trend, or noise, and visualizing the prediction errors to identify systematic biases. Furthermore, the authors could explore the models' sensitivity to different data preprocessing techniques, such as normalization or detrending, and how these choices impact the final forecasting performance. These improvements would significantly enhance the robustness, comprehensiveness, and practical utility of the benchmark.
