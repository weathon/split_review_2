## Summary
This paper introduces a systematic evaluation framework for tabular data synthesis algorithms, addressing a critical need in the field. The authors rightly point out the limitations of existing evaluation metrics, which often fail to capture the nuances of fidelity, privacy, and utility in synthetic data. To overcome these shortcomings, they propose a set of novel metrics: the Wasserstein distance for fidelity, a membership disclosure score (MDS) for privacy, and a unified tuning objective that aims to improve the quality of synthetic data across all methods. The core of their approach lies in a modular framework, which they call \mymethod, that allows for the evaluation of various synthesizers using these new metrics. The authors conduct extensive experiments on twelve real-world datasets, evaluating eight different synthesizers, and present their findings in a clear and informative manner. Their results reveal a trade-off between fidelity and privacy, and highlight the effectiveness of diffusion models in generating high-quality synthetic data. The paper's main contribution is the provision of a comprehensive and robust evaluation framework that can aid researchers and practitioners in comparing and selecting appropriate synthesizers for their specific needs. By introducing new metrics and a unified tuning objective, the authors have made a significant step towards standardizing the evaluation of tabular data synthesis algorithms. The experimental results, while insightful, also reveal some limitations of the proposed framework, which I will discuss in detail in the 'Weaknesses' section. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field, providing a much-needed systematic approach to evaluating tabular data synthesis methods.

## Strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the paper addresses a significant and timely problem: the lack of a comprehensive and standardized evaluation framework for tabular data synthesis algorithms. The authors have correctly identified the limitations of existing metrics and have proposed a set of new metrics that are well-suited to the task. The use of the Wasserstein distance for measuring fidelity is a notable improvement, as it can handle both numerical and categorical attributes, unlike many existing metrics. The introduction of the membership disclosure score (MDS) for privacy is also a valuable contribution, as it provides a worst-case privacy measurement. Furthermore, the unified tuning objective is a practical approach for improving the performance of synthesizers, and the authors demonstrate its effectiveness through extensive experiments. The experimental design is another strength of the paper. The authors have conducted experiments on a wide range of synthesizers and datasets, which enhances the credibility and applicability of their findings. The results are presented in a clear and concise manner, and the authors provide a detailed analysis of the findings. The paper also makes a significant contribution to the field of data synthesis by providing a systematic and robust evaluation framework that can help researchers and practitioners to compare and choose different synthesizers. The authors also reveal some interesting findings, such as the trade-off between fidelity and privacy, and the effectiveness of diffusion models. The paper's clear and well-structured presentation also contributes to its overall strength, making it easy to follow and understand the proposed methods and results. The authors have successfully combined theoretical insights with practical applications, resulting in a paper that is both academically rigorous and practically relevant.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed metrics and framework. While the authors introduce the Wasserstein distance in Section 3.2 and mention that solving the optimization problem is an instance of linear programming, they do not provide a formal analysis of its time and space complexity, especially concerning the number of rows and columns in the datasets. Similarly, the Membership Disclosure Score (MDS) is introduced in Section 4.2, and while the implementation details mention training `m` models, the computational cost of this process is not analyzed. The Machine Learning Affinity (MLA) and Query Error metrics, described in Section 5.2, involve training multiple ML models and executing queries, but again, the paper does not provide a formal complexity analysis. This omission is significant because it makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the proposed framework, particularly when dealing with large-scale datasets. The absence of a discussion on how the computational cost of the proposed metrics scales with the number of rows and columns in the dataset is a crucial oversight, as it directly impacts the applicability of the framework to real-world scenarios. This lack of analysis also makes it difficult to pinpoint potential bottlenecks in the framework, which could hinder its practical use. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is clearly evident from the absence of any computational complexity analysis in the method descriptions (Sections 3.2, 4.2, 5.2, 6) and the lack of runtime information in the experimental setup (Section 7.1). Second, the paper does not provide sufficient guidance on hyperparameter selection. While the authors introduce a unified tuning objective in Section 6.1 and mention using Optuna for hyperparameter optimization, they do not offer specific recommendations on setting the search space or optimization strategy for different datasets. The paper mentions that default settings lead to suboptimal performance (Introduction, Motivation), highlighting the importance of hyperparameter tuning, but it does not provide detailed guidance on how to choose appropriate hyperparameter values for different scenarios. The paper also does not discuss how the choice of hyperparameters affects the trade-off between fidelity, privacy, and utility, which is a critical consideration in practical applications. Furthermore, the paper does not provide any guidelines on how to adapt the hyperparameter selection process to different types of tabular data, such as data with varying degrees of skewness or cardinality. This lack of practical guidance makes it difficult for practitioners to effectively use the proposed framework. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is clearly evident from the lack of detailed guidance on hyperparameter selection in the method description (Section 6.1) and experimental setup (Section 7.1). Third, the paper does not adequately discuss the limitations of the proposed framework and metrics. For example, the paper does not address how the proposed metrics handle high-dimensional or skewed data. While Section 3.2 mentions that the Wasserstein distance can be computationally expensive, it does not explicitly discuss its behavior with high-dimensional data. Similarly, Section 4.2 discusses the limitations of MDS, noting it's not suitable for all types of synthesizers, but does not address its performance on skewed data or against specific attacks. The paper also lacks a discussion on the framework's resilience against potential attacks and the ethical and legal implications of data synthesis. This omission is significant because it limits the applicability of the framework in certain scenarios and fails to address important considerations for responsible data synthesis. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is clearly evident from the absence of discussion on handling high-dimensional/skewed data, adversarial attacks, and ethical/legal implications in the method descriptions (Sections 3.2, 4.2) and experimental setup (Section 7.1). Finally, the paper does not provide sufficient guidelines or recommendations for practitioners on how to use the framework or metrics in real-world scenarios. While the paper introduces the framework and metrics, it does not offer a step-by-step guide for practitioners. The "Takeaways for Practitioners" section in the Discussion provides some high-level recommendations, but it lacks detailed guidance on synthesizer selection, result interpretation, and trade-off management. This lack of practical guidance makes it difficult for practitioners to effectively use the proposed framework in real-world applications. ## Suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should include a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed metrics and framework. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time complexity of each step in the framework, such as data preprocessing, model training, and metric calculation. Specifically, the authors should analyze how the runtime of their methods scales with the number of rows and columns in the input datasets. For instance, the Wasserstein distance calculation, which is a core component of the fidelity metric, can be computationally expensive, especially for high-dimensional data. A detailed analysis of the time complexity of this step, along with other computationally intensive parts of the framework, would be beneficial. Furthermore, the authors should provide empirical results on the runtime of their framework for datasets of varying sizes to demonstrate its scalability in practice. This would help readers understand the practical limitations of the proposed approach and identify potential bottlenecks. The authors should also consider the memory requirements of the framework, especially when dealing with large datasets. Second, the authors should provide more practical guidance on how to choose appropriate hyperparameter values for different datasets and applications. This should include a discussion on how the choice of hyperparameters affects the trade-off between fidelity, privacy, and utility. For example, the authors could analyze how the privacy parameter epsilon in differentially private synthesizers affects the fidelity and utility of the synthetic data. The authors should also provide guidelines on how to adapt the hyperparameter selection process to different types of tabular data, such as data with varying degrees of skewness or cardinality. This could involve providing a set of heuristics or rules of thumb that practitioners can use to guide their hyperparameter selection process. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of automated hyperparameter tuning techniques, such as grid search or Bayesian optimization, to simplify the process for practitioners. Third, the authors should delve deeper into the limitations of the proposed evaluation framework, particularly concerning high-dimensional and skewed data. For high-dimensional data, the authors should explore how the Wasserstein distance behaves, as its computational cost increases significantly with dimensionality. It would be beneficial to analyze the performance of the proposed metrics on datasets with varying dimensionality and provide insights into when the framework might become less effective. For skewed data, the authors should investigate how the proposed metrics, especially the fidelity metric based on Wasserstein distance, are affected by imbalanced distributions. It is possible that the metrics might be dominated by the majority class or attribute, leading to inaccurate evaluations. The authors could consider incorporating techniques such as re-sampling or weighted metrics to address this issue and provide a more robust evaluation framework. Furthermore, the paper should include a discussion on the sensitivity of the proposed metrics to different types of data transformations, such as normalization or encoding, as these transformations are commonly used in practice. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion on how practitioners can use the proposed framework and metrics in real-world scenarios. This should include a step-by-step guide on how to select the appropriate synthesizer for a given dataset and task. The authors could consider providing a decision tree or a set of rules based on the characteristics of the data and the desired trade-offs between fidelity, privacy, and utility. For example, they could suggest using statistical methods when privacy is paramount, and deep generative models when fidelity is the priority. The paper should also provide a detailed explanation of how to interpret the results of the evaluation, including the meaning of the different metrics and their relative importance. The authors could provide examples of how to use the results to make informed decisions about the choice of synthesizer and the quality of the synthetic data. Furthermore, the paper should discuss how to deal with the trade-offs between different aspects, such as fidelity, privacy, and utility, and provide practical recommendations on how to balance these competing objectives. Finally, the authors should include a more thorough discussion of the ethical and legal implications of data synthesis. This should include a discussion of data ownership, data governance, and data bias. The authors should consider the potential risks of using synthetic data, such as the possibility of perpetuating existing biases or creating new ones. They should also discuss the legal and regulatory frameworks that might apply to data synthesis and the use of synthetic data. The paper should provide practical recommendations on how to mitigate these risks and ensure that data synthesis is used in an ethical and responsible manner. This could include guidelines on data anonymization, data validation, and data auditing. The authors should also consider the potential impact of data synthesis on different stakeholders, including data subjects, data owners, and data users.
