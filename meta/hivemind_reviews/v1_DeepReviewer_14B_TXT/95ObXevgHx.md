## Summary
This paper presents a compelling investigation into the relationship between the internal representations of deep language models (DLMs) and the temporal dynamics of neural activity in the human brain during natural language processing. The authors employ electrocorticography (ECoG) to record neural activity from patients listening to a 30-minute narrative, while simultaneously feeding the same narrative into a high-performing DLM, specifically GPT2-XL. This allows for a fine-grained analysis of how the layer-wise representations in the DLM correlate with the temporal evolution of neural responses. The core methodological approach involves extracting contextual embeddings from each of the 48 layers of GPT2-XL and then using linear encoding models to predict neural activity from these embeddings at different time lags relative to word onset. The authors find that intermediate layers of the DLM are most predictive of neural activity across various language regions, and that the temporal dynamics of neural activity unfold in a way that mirrors the sequential processing of information across the layers of the DLM. Specifically, earlier layers tend to be more predictive of neural activity earlier in time, while later layers show stronger correlations later. This temporal unfolding of layer-wise predictive power is most pronounced in higher-order language areas, such as the anterior superior temporal gyrus (aSTG) and the temporal pole (TP), compared to lower-level areas like the middle superior temporal gyrus (mSTG). These findings suggest a potential parallel between the hierarchical processing in DLMs and the temporal dynamics of neural processing in the human brain, with the layer-by-layer accumulation of contextual information in the DLM mirroring the timing of neural activity in high-order language areas. The authors propose that these results provide insights into the neural basis of language processing and could inform the development of more brain-inspired language models. Overall, this study offers a novel perspective on the relationship between artificial and biological intelligence, leveraging the high temporal resolution of ECoG to uncover subtle dynamics that might be missed by other methods.

## Strengths

The high temporal resolution of ECoG allows the authors to investigate the precise timing of neural responses and their relationship to the sequential processing of information in DLMs. This is a significant strength, as it enables the exploration of fine-grained dynamics that are crucial for understanding the neural mechanisms of language comprehension. Second, the results presented are robust and consistent across different language areas, suggesting that the observed correlations are not specific to a particular region of the brain. The finding that intermediate layers of the DLM are most predictive of neural activity in various language regions is particularly compelling, as it aligns with the idea that these layers capture more abstract and contextually relevant information. Furthermore, the observation that the temporal dynamics of neural activity unfold in a way that mirrors the sequential processing of information across the layers of the DLM is a novel and intriguing finding. This suggests a potential parallel between the hierarchical processing in DLMs and the temporal dynamics of neural processing in the human brain. The authors' approach of using linear encoding models to predict neural activity from DLM embeddings is well-established and provides a clear and interpretable framework for analyzing the relationship between artificial and biological intelligence. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the complex methodology and findings accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their research question, methodology, and results, and they provide a thoughtful discussion of the implications of their findings. The combination of a rigorous methodology, novel findings, and clear presentation makes this a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

First, the authors do not provide a sufficiently strong justification for using ECoG over fMRI, particularly regarding the necessity of the superior temporal resolution. While they acknowledge that ECoG provides higher spatiotemporal resolution, they do not explicitly detail why the millisecond-level temporal resolution is crucial for addressing their research question. The authors state that ECoG's temporal resolution is essential for observing the sequential processing across layers in the brain, but they do not elaborate on specific hypotheses that require this level of detail. For instance, they could have explained how the temporal unfolding of layer-wise predictive power, which is a key finding, cannot be captured by the slower hemodynamic response of fMRI. This lack of a clear justification weakens the argument for using the more invasive ECoG method. My confidence in this limitation is medium, as the authors do mention the importance of temporal resolution, but they could have been more explicit about the specific hypotheses that require millisecond-level temporal resolution. Second, the authors' method of averaging encoding performance over lags to obtain an average encoding performance per layer is problematic. As stated in the results section, "We averaged over lags to get an average encoding performance per layer (Fig 2B)." This approach conflates the neural response to the current word with the neural response to the previous word, as the neural signal is time-shifted relative to each word's onset. Averaging across all lags would mix the responses to different words at different processing stages, making it difficult to interpret the results. The paper lacks a clear explanation of how this averaging was performed and why it is a valid approach. This is a significant methodological flaw that undermines the validity of the average encoding performance per layer. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper explicitly states that they averaged over lags without providing a clear justification or addressing the potential confound of mixing neural responses to different words. They should elaborate on the specific hypotheses that require millisecond-level temporal resolution and explain why fMRI, with its slower hemodynamic response, would be insufficient. For example, if the hypothesis involves tracking the propagation of information through different layers of the language network on a timescale of tens of milliseconds, then this would be a valid justification. They should also discuss the limitations of fMRI in this context, such as its inability to capture fast neural dynamics, and explain why these limitations are critical for their study. Furthermore, the authors should consider whether the same research question could be addressed with fMRI data, and if so, why ECoG is essential. Second, the authors need to clarify how they calculated the average encoding performance per layer. Averaging over lags, as described, is problematic because it mixes the neural response to the current word with the response to the previous word, making it difficult to interpret the results. The authors should explain how they handled the lag variable when calculating the average encoding performance. For example, did they average the encoding performance at each lag separately for each layer, and then average across lags, or did they use a different approach? It would be helpful if the authors could provide a more detailed description of the averaging process, including the specific formula used. Additionally, the authors should consider whether it would be more informative to analyze the encoding performance at specific lags of interest, rather than averaging across all lags. This would allow for a more fine-grained analysis of the temporal dynamics of the neural response and its relationship to the DLM representations. The authors should also clarify whether the lag variable was treated as a continuous or discrete variable in their analysis.
