## summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to mitigating catastrophic forgetting in language models during the refinement process, specifically when correcting errors. The core idea revolves around forecasting which examples from the pre-training dataset will be forgotten when the model is updated to address specific errors. To achieve this, the authors propose two distinct forecasting methods: a partially interpretable logit-change based model and a black-box representation-based model. The logit-change method leverages first-order approximations of logit changes to predict forgetting, while the representation-based model uses a neural network to learn the relationship between examples and their likelihood of being forgotten. The authors demonstrate that by replaying the forecasted forgotten examples, they can significantly reduce catastrophic forgetting compared to random replay, thereby improving the model's stability and performance. The empirical evaluation is thorough, encompassing various model architectures, including BART and T5, and different fine-tuning setups. The results show that the representation-based model generally performs well across different scenarios, while the logit-change method exhibits more inconsistent performance. The paper also explores the generalization of the forecasting methods to continual learning scenarios, where multiple errors are fixed sequentially. The findings suggest that the proposed approach can effectively mitigate forgetting in these more complex settings, although the performance of the forecasting models may degrade over time. Overall, this work makes a significant contribution to the field of continual learning and model stability by introducing a practical and effective method for forecasting and mitigating catastrophic forgetting during model refinement. The paper's focus on error correction in language models is particularly relevant, and the empirical results demonstrate the potential of the proposed methods for real-world applications. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the computational efficiency of their methods, which is crucial for practical use. The paper's exploration of both interpretable and black-box forecasting methods offers valuable insights into the dynamics of forgetting and provides a solid foundation for future research in this area. The authors' approach of targeting replay based on predicted forgetting is a significant step forward in addressing the challenges of continual learning in large language models.

This is a significant contribution to the field of continual learning, as it shifts the focus from simply mitigating forgetting to proactively predicting which examples are most likely to be forgotten. This approach allows for more targeted and efficient replay strategies, which is crucial for practical applications. The proposed forecasting methods, particularly the representation-based model, are innovative and demonstrate promising results across various setups and model architectures. The representation-based model's ability to generalize across different models and tasks highlights its potential for practical use. ## weaknesses
While this paper presents a compelling approach to forecasting forgetting in language models, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern lies with the inconsistent performance of the logit-change based forecasting method across different model architectures. As the authors themselves acknowledge, this method works well on BART but fails on FLAN-T5. This inconsistency raises serious questions about the generalizability of the method and its underlying assumptions. The paper does not provide a clear explanation for this discrepancy, which is a significant oversight. The method's reliance on first-order approximations of logit changes might be more suitable for models with simpler architectures or specific training regimes, and this needs to be explored further. The lack of analysis on why the logit-based method fails on FLAN-T5, especially given its success on BART, is a significant limitation. This inconsistency undermines the robustness of the proposed approach and raises concerns about its applicability to a wider range of models. The paper's explanation that the method fails due to the simplification of the kernel matrix is not sufficient. A more detailed analysis of the model-specific characteristics that influence the effectiveness of this method is needed. For example, the authors could investigate how the depth of the model, the type of attention mechanisms, or the pre-training objectives affect the logit change transfer. This lack of a thorough investigation into the reasons behind the failure of the logit-change method on FLAN-T5 is a major weakness that significantly impacts the paper's conclusions. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly acknowledges the performance difference in the Introduction and Section 3.2, and the experimental results in Table 1 clearly demonstrate the discrepancy. 

Another significant weakness is the limited interpretability of the partially interpretable model. While the logit-change based model offers some insights into the forgetting process, its interpretation is limited to the transfer of logit changes. This approach does not capture other potential factors that might contribute to forgetting, such as the interaction between examples or the specific characteristics of the model's internal representations. The paper does not provide a comprehensive analysis of the forgetting process, and the interpretation provided by the logit-change model is incomplete. A more detailed analysis of the internal mechanisms of the model and how they change during the learning process is needed to fully understand the dynamics of forgetting. The paper's focus on logit change transfer as the primary mechanism of forgetting is a simplification that may not fully capture the complexity of the phenomenon. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper itself acknowledges the "partially interpretable" nature of the logit-change model, and the method is explicitly designed around logit change transfer, which is a specific aspect of forgetting dynamics. 

Furthermore, the paper could benefit from a more detailed analysis of the computational efficiency of the forecasting methods, especially when applied to very large language models. While the authors mention the computational cost and provide a theoretical analysis in Section 5.3, a more thorough analysis of the time and memory requirements for each method is needed. The paper focuses on the number of operations, which is a theoretical measure, but lacks empirical runtime comparisons or memory footprint analysis, especially for very large models. The paper does not provide a comparison of the computational cost of their methods to other continual learning techniques, such as regularization-based methods or replay-based methods. This lack of a detailed computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed methods. The paper should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each step of the methods, such as the computation of logits, the training of the forecasting models, and the replay of forgotten examples. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does provide some analysis in Section 5.3 and Table 3, but it lacks the depth requested by the reviewer regarding time, memory, and comparison to other methods. 

Finally, the paper's focus on sequence-to-sequence models limits the generalizability of the findings. The experiments are conducted using BART and FLAN-T5, which are sequence-to-sequence models, and the tasks used for evaluation are also suitable for these models. The paper does not explore the effectiveness of the forecasting methods on other types of language models or tasks, such as text classification or masked language modeling. This lack of experiments on other model architectures and tasks raises concerns about the broad applicability of the proposed forecasting methods. It is important to evaluate the method on a wider range of tasks to demonstrate its robustness and versatility. The paper should include experiments on other model architectures and tasks to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the proposed methods. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's description of the methods and the experimental setup clearly focus on sequence-to-sequence models, and there is no evidence of the methods' effectiveness on other model types or tasks.

## suggestions

First, the paper should delve deeper into the reasons behind the inconsistent performance of the logit-change based forecasting method across different model architectures. A more detailed analysis of the model-specific characteristics that influence the effectiveness of this method is needed. For example, the authors could investigate how the depth of the model, the type of attention mechanisms, or the pre-training objectives affect the logit change transfer. Furthermore, exploring the impact of different fine-tuning strategies on the performance of the logit-change based method could provide valuable insights. It would also be beneficial to explore alternative methods for approximating logit changes that are less sensitive to model architecture, potentially using techniques from sensitivity analysis or adversarial training. This would help in making the method more robust and widely applicable. The authors should also consider conducting experiments with a wider range of model architectures to better understand the limitations of the logit-change method. This would involve testing the method on models with different depths, attention mechanisms, and pre-training objectives. By systematically varying these factors, the authors could gain a better understanding of the conditions under which the logit-change method is effective and identify potential areas for improvement. 

To enhance the interpretability of the model, the authors should consider incorporating techniques that can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the forgetting process. For instance, they could explore methods for visualizing the internal representations of the model and how they change during the learning process. Analyzing the activation patterns of different layers could reveal which parts of the model are most affected by the fine-tuning process and contribute to forgetting. Additionally, investigating the relationship between the input examples and the model's internal representations could provide insights into why certain examples are more prone to forgetting than others. This could involve techniques such as probing, concept activation vectors, or other methods for analyzing the model's learned features. A more detailed analysis of the interaction between examples, beyond simple similarity measures, could also be beneficial. The authors should also explore the possibility of using more sophisticated models for the logit-change forecasting that can capture more complex relationships between examples. This could involve using neural networks or other machine learning techniques to model the logit change transfer process. 

The paper should include a more detailed analysis of the computational efficiency of the proposed forecasting methods. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each step of the methods, such as the computation of logits, the training of the forecasting models, and the replay of forgotten examples. The authors should also compare the computational cost of their methods to other continual learning techniques, such as regularization-based methods or replay-based methods. This comparison should consider both the training time and the memory requirements. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the scalability of their methods to larger datasets and model sizes. A thorough analysis of the computational efficiency would help in assessing the practical applicability of the proposed methods. The authors should also consider exploring techniques for reducing the computational cost of the forecasting methods, such as using more efficient algorithms or approximations. 

Finally, the paper should expand the scope of its experiments to include other types of language models and tasks. The current focus on sequence-to-sequence models limits the generalizability of the findings. The authors should evaluate the proposed forecasting methods on other tasks, such as text classification, masked language modeling, or other tasks that are commonly used in natural language processing. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the robustness and versatility of the proposed methods. The authors should also investigate whether the forecasting methods are sensitive to the specific characteristics of the tasks, such as the length of the input sequences or the complexity of the output space. How would the forecasting methods need to be adapted to handle these different types of updates? Would the performance of the forecasting methods be affected by the type of model update? How does the logit change transfer relate to the internal representations of the model? Can this knowledge be used to develop more robust models that are less prone to forgetting? It would be valuable to investigate how the forecasting methods perform in more realistic continual learning scenarios where the model is updated on a stream of data over an extended period.
