## summary
This paper introduces Uni-O4, a novel reinforcement learning framework designed to unify offline and online learning without relying on additional conservatism or regularization. The core idea revolves around leveraging an ensemble of policies and a unique offline policy evaluation (OPE) method to achieve stable multi-step policy improvement during the offline phase. The authors argue that by using an on-policy objective for both offline and online learning, they can seamlessly integrate these two phases, addressing a key challenge in the field. The method begins by training an ensemble of policies using behavior cloning with a disagreement penalty to encourage diversity. These policies are then iteratively improved using a novel OPE method that employs a learned dynamics model for multi-step rollouts, allowing for more accurate policy evaluation in the offline setting. The policies are then used to initialize a standard online PPO algorithm for further fine-tuning. The empirical results presented in the paper demonstrate that Uni-O4 achieves strong performance on both simulated benchmarks and real-world robot tasks, outperforming state-of-the-art offline and offline-to-online RL algorithms. Specifically, the method shows significant improvements on the AntMaze tasks, highlighting its ability to handle complex environments. The real-world experiments, while limited in scope, demonstrate the method's potential for transferring from simulation to real-world scenarios and adapting to new environments. The authors emphasize that their approach avoids the need for explicit conservative regularization, which is often used in offline RL, and instead relies on the inherent properties of the on-policy objective and the ensemble of policies to achieve stable and effective learning. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to unifying offline and online RL, with promising empirical results that suggest a significant step forward in the field. However, as I will discuss, there are several limitations that need to be addressed to fully validate the method's practical applicability and robustness.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its novel approach to unifying offline and online reinforcement learning through an on-policy objective, effectively eliminating the need for explicit conservative regularization. This is a significant contribution, as it addresses a key challenge in the field by providing a seamless transition between offline and online learning phases. The use of an ensemble of policies, trained with a disagreement penalty, is another notable innovation. This approach allows for a more robust representation of the behavior policy and facilitates stable multi-step policy improvement in the offline setting. The proposed offline policy evaluation (OPE) method, which leverages a learned dynamics model for multi-step rollouts, is also a significant technical contribution. This method enables more accurate policy evaluation compared to single-step methods, leading to more effective policy improvement. Empirically, the paper presents a thorough evaluation of the proposed method, demonstrating strong performance across a range of simulated benchmarks and real-world robot tasks. The results on the AntMaze tasks are particularly impressive, showcasing the method's ability to handle complex environments with sparse rewards. The real-world experiments, while limited in scope, provide valuable evidence of the method's potential for transferring from simulation to real-world scenarios and adapting to new environments. The authors' decision to use the PPO objective with a clip function and the Implicit Q-learning (IQL) framework for value function learning, while avoiding explicit conservatism, is also a strength. These choices contribute to the method's overall simplicity and effectiveness. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the technical details accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their motivation, methodology, and results, which enhances the overall impact of the work. The ablation studies, particularly the one on ensemble size, provide valuable insights into the method's behavior and the impact of different hyperparameters. In summary, the paper presents a novel and effective approach to unifying offline and online RL, supported by strong empirical results and clear technical explanations.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses need to be addressed to fully validate the proposed method. One significant concern is the reliance of the offline policy evaluation (OPE) method on an approximate dynamics model, denoted as \(\hat{T}\). As the paper states, this model is trained using a maximum likelihood objective on the offline dataset. However, the paper does not explicitly address the potential for compounding errors that arise from using this learned model for multi-step rollouts. This is a critical issue because inaccuracies in the model's predictions can accumulate over multiple steps, leading to inaccurate value estimates and suboptimal policy updates. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss how the uncertainty in the model's predictions is handled, which can be significant, especially when extrapolating beyond the offline dataset. This lack of consideration for model uncertainty and compounding errors raises concerns about the reliability of the OPE method, particularly in complex environments. The paper's method description focuses on the mechanics of using the model for OPE but lacks details on error mitigation strategies. This is a significant limitation, as it undermines the accuracy of the policy improvement process. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the method description clearly indicates the use of an approximate model for multi-step rollouts without addressing the associated error propagation. Another significant weakness is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the ensemble methods and OPE. While the paper claims that the OPE method is computationally efficient in terms of inference, it does not provide a breakdown of the time complexity for training the ensemble policies versus a single policy. This is a crucial omission, as training multiple policies can significantly increase the computational burden, especially as the number of policies in the ensemble grows. Furthermore, the paper does not quantify the computational overhead of the OPE method, particularly the Monte Carlo rollouts, which are used for policy evaluation. This lack of a comprehensive computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method, as the increased computational demands could limit its use in real-world scenarios. The paper does not discuss how the computational cost scales with the number of policies in the ensemble or the number of rollouts used in OPE. This is a significant limitation, as it prevents a clear understanding of the trade-offs between performance and computational efficiency. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks any detailed computational analysis beyond a brief mention of OPE's inference complexity. Finally, while the real-world experiments are valuable, they are limited in scope and do not fully demonstrate the method's robustness and generalizability. The experiments focus on a quadruped robot navigating different terrains, but they do not explore the challenges of real-world robotic systems, such as noisy sensors, model inaccuracies, and external disturbances. The paper does not present experiments that systematically evaluate the method's performance in more complex environments with higher dimensional state and action spaces. This limited evaluation makes it difficult to ascertain the method's true robustness and generalizability to diverse real-world applications. The paper acknowledges the sim-to-real gap and proposes the online-offline-online framework to address it, but the experiments do not fully validate this claim. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the experimental section clearly shows the limited scope of the real-world evaluation, lacking systematic testing of robustness to noise and disturbances.
