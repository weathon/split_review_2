## Summary
This paper introduces Adversarial Perturbation Dropout (APD), a novel technique aimed at enhancing the transferability of adversarial examples in deep neural networks. The core idea behind APD is to disrupt the synergy between perturbations in different regions of an image by selectively dropping out perturbations during the optimization process. This is achieved by leveraging Class Activation Maps (CAM) to identify salient regions within the image, and then randomly dropping square regions around the local maximum points of the CAM. The authors argue that by reducing the mutual influence between perturbation regions, the generated adversarial examples become more robust and transferable across different models, even when the target model's attention focuses on only a subset of the perturbed regions. The method is implemented by integrating a dropout mechanism into an iterative gradient-based attack, specifically I-FGSM. During each iteration, multiple perturbed versions of the input image are generated by dropping out different regions, and the gradients from these versions are averaged to update the adversarial example. The authors conduct extensive experiments on the ImageNet dataset, demonstrating that APD outperforms state-of-the-art methods in terms of attack success rates in black-box settings. They also show that APD can be seamlessly integrated with existing adversarial attack methods to further improve their performance. The paper's main contribution lies in the introduction of a perturbation dropout mechanism guided by CAM, which offers a new perspective on improving adversarial transferability by reducing the interaction between different perturbation regions. The empirical findings suggest that APD is effective in generating adversarial examples that are more transferable across different models, highlighting the importance of reducing perturbation synergy for enhancing black-box attack success rates. The paper's significance lies in its novel approach to adversarial transferability and its potential to inform the development of more robust defense mechanisms. However, the paper also has several limitations, including its reliance on CAM, its focus on image classification, and its computational overhead, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. First, the core idea of using perturbation dropout to enhance adversarial transferability is both intuitive and novel. The authors' approach of disrupting the synergy between perturbation regions by selectively dropping out parts of the perturbation is a creative way to address the limitations of existing methods that often spread perturbations across the entire image. This approach is grounded in the observation that target models may only focus on specific attention regions, and therefore, attacks should be robust even when part of the perturbations are ignored. The use of Class Activation Maps (CAM) to guide the dropout process is also a strength, as it allows the method to focus on salient regions of the image, avoiding the dropping of irrelevant areas. This targeted approach is more effective than random dropout, as it ensures that the dropped regions are meaningful in the context of the model's decision-making process. The experimental results presented in the paper are also a significant strength. The authors conduct extensive experiments on the ImageNet dataset, demonstrating that APD outperforms state-of-the-art methods in terms of attack success rates in black-box settings. The fact that APD can be seamlessly integrated with existing adversarial attack methods to further improve their performance is also a notable achievement. This suggests that APD is a versatile technique that can be readily adopted by the research community. Furthermore, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain the motivation behind their approach, the details of their method, and the results of their experiments. The use of figures and tables also enhances the clarity of the paper. Finally, the paper's focus on improving adversarial transferability is highly relevant to the field of deep learning security. The ability to generate adversarial examples that are transferable across different models is crucial for evaluating the robustness of deep neural networks and developing effective defense mechanisms. In summary, the paper's strengths lie in its novel approach, its effective use of CAM, its strong empirical results, and its relevance to the field.
