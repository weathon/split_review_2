## summary
This paper introduces HD-Explain, a novel instance-level, example-based explanation method for neural network classifiers, leveraging the properties of Kernelized Stein Discrepancy (KSD). The core idea is to identify training samples that provide the most predictive support for a given test point by maximizing the KSD between the test point and training samples. The authors argue that this approach captures the model's learned representation and decision boundaries, offering a more precise and consistent explanation compared to existing methods. The method is presented as model-agnostic, applicable to any trained classifier, and computationally efficient. The authors conduct extensive experiments across multiple classification domains, including image classification tasks on CIFAR-10, Brain Tumor (MRI), Ovarian Cancer (Histopathology), and SVHN datasets. They demonstrate that HD-Explain outperforms existing methods in terms of preciseness, consistency, and computational efficiency. The paper also introduces quantitative metrics, such as hit rate and coverage, to evaluate the quality of the explanations. The empirical results suggest that HD-Explain can identify training samples that are most relevant to a test point, providing fine-grained explanations that are consistent across different test instances. The authors claim that their method offers a more robust and efficient approach to example-based explanations, addressing limitations of methods like Influence Function, RPS, and TracIn. The paper's main contribution lies in the novel application of KSD to the problem of example-based explanations, providing a theoretically grounded and empirically validated approach. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the computational efficiency of their method, showing that it is faster than existing methods. Overall, the paper presents a promising new direction for explainable AI, with a focus on providing precise and efficient example-based explanations for neural network classifiers. The authors' use of KSD is a significant departure from existing methods, and their empirical results suggest that this approach has the potential to be widely adopted in various applications. The paper also contributes to the field by introducing new quantitative metrics for evaluating the quality of example-based explanations, which can be used in future research to compare different explanation methods. The authors' focus on computational efficiency is also a valuable contribution, as it makes their method more practical for real-world applications. The paper's clear presentation and thorough experimental evaluation make it a valuable contribution to the field of explainable AI.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling approach, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's introduction to Kernelized Stein Discrepancy (KSD) is relatively brief, focusing primarily on the mathematical formulation. While the core definitions are present, a more detailed explanation of the intuition behind KSD, its properties, and its connection to the notion of 'predictive support' would greatly benefit readers unfamiliar with this technique. As Reviewer 1 noted, the paper lacks a deeper dive into the mathematical underpinnings of KSD, including its relationship to divergence measures and its specific properties that make it suitable for identifying predictive support. This lack of intuitive explanation makes it difficult to fully grasp why maximizing KSD between a test point and training points would identify training samples that provide the best predictive support. The paper demonstrates that KSD can distinguish between distributions, but it fails to articulate why maximizing KSD would identify training samples that are most similar to a test point in the context of a trained model. This lack of clear motivation weakens the theoretical foundation of the method. Second, the paper's experimental setup lacks crucial details, hindering reproducibility and making it difficult to assess the fairness of comparisons. As Reviewer 2 pointed out, the paper does not provide specific details about the ResNet-18 architecture used, the optimization algorithms, learning rates, and other training hyperparameters. Furthermore, the hyperparameter settings for the baseline methods, such as the influence function and TracIn, are not specified. This lack of detail makes it difficult for other researchers to replicate the results and to understand the performance of the proposed method in different contexts. The paper also only provides the source code for the proposed method, not for the experiments, further hindering reproducibility. Third, the paper's evaluation is limited to a relatively small number of datasets, primarily focused on image classification tasks. As Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 2 both noted, the paper lacks evaluation on more diverse datasets and tasks, such as text or time-series data. This limited evaluation makes it difficult to assess the generalizability of the method and its performance on tasks with different levels of complexity. The paper also does not explicitly address performance on datasets with varying class imbalance or noise levels. Fourth, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. As Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 3 pointed out, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the method, beyond a high-level comparison in Table 1. The paper also does not discuss the potential for the method to be biased towards certain types of explanations. The paper also makes a strong assumption that the data distribution $P_D$ is uniformly distributed, which is unrealistic, as noted by Reviewer 4. While the authors attempt to justify this assumption, it remains a significant limitation that could affect the performance of the method in real-world scenarios. Fifth, the proposed method is only applicable to generative models that can provide $P(y|x)$, as Reviewer 4 correctly pointed out. This limits its applicability compared to methods that can work with any black-box model providing only input-output pairs. Finally, the paper primarily focuses on explaining correctly classified samples, and it does not provide a detailed analysis of the explanations for misclassified samples, as noted by Reviewer 4. This is a significant limitation, as explaining misclassified samples is often more important for understanding model behavior. The paper also lacks a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the method and potential future research directions, as noted by Reviewer 3. The paper also lacks a more comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art explanation methods, as noted by Reviewer 3. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight the need for further research and development to address the limitations of the proposed method.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the paper should provide a more thorough explanation of Kernelized Stein Discrepancy (KSD) and its connection to the proposed explanation method. This should include a more detailed discussion of the mathematical underpinnings of KSD, its relationship to divergence measures, and its specific properties that make it suitable for identifying predictive support. The paper should also elaborate on how the KSD is computed in practice, including the choice of kernel and its impact on the results. Furthermore, a discussion on the theoretical guarantees of KSD, such as its consistency and convergence properties, would add more rigor to the proposed method. This would help readers appreciate the theoretical foundations of the method and its potential advantages over other explanation techniques. The paper should also provide a more compelling justification for using KSD, explaining why maximizing KSD between a test point and training points would identify training samples that provide the best predictive support. Second, the paper should provide a detailed description of the experimental setup, including the specific architectures used for each model, the optimization algorithms, learning rates, and other training hyperparameters. The hyperparameter settings for the baseline methods should also be clearly specified. The authors should also provide the source code for the experiments, not just the implementation of the proposed method. This would allow other researchers to easily replicate the experiments and compare their methods with HD-Explain. The experimental code should include all the necessary scripts for data preprocessing, model training, and evaluation. Furthermore, the authors should provide detailed instructions on how to run the experiments, including the required software and hardware specifications. The authors should also consider releasing the trained models used in the experiments, which would further facilitate reproducibility and comparison. Third, the paper should conduct a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method on a wider range of datasets and tasks. This should include datasets from different domains, such as text, time-series, and tabular data. The authors should also consider evaluating the method on tasks with different levels of complexity, such as multi-class classification, regression, and anomaly detection. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's capabilities and limitations. For example, evaluating on datasets with varying degrees of class imbalance or noise would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's capabilities. Additionally, the authors should analyze the performance of the method on datasets with different sizes to assess its scalability. Fourth, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method, including a breakdown of the time complexity of each step in the algorithm. The authors should also compare the actual runtime of the proposed method with the baselines on different hardware configurations. This analysis should include the time required for both training and inference. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the memory requirements of the proposed method and how they scale with the size of the training dataset. Fifth, the paper should address the limitation that the proposed method is only applicable to generative models. The authors should discuss the potential for extending the method to other types of models, such as discriminative models, which are widely used in practice. The paper should also provide a more detailed comparison of the proposed method with existing example-based explanation methods, particularly in terms of their applicability to different types of models. The authors should also consider the practical implications of this limitation and discuss potential workarounds or alternative approaches for applying the method to non-generative models. Sixth, the paper should include a more comprehensive evaluation of the method, including an analysis of its performance on misclassified samples. This is crucial for understanding the method's ability to identify the reasons for model errors. Finally, the paper should include a dedicated section discussing the limitations of the method and potential future research directions. This section should address the sensitivity of the method to the choice of kernel and its parameters, the computational complexity of the KSD calculation, and the potential for the method to be biased towards certain types of explanations. The authors should also discuss the limitations of the method in scenarios where the data distribution is highly complex or multimodal, and how this might affect the quality of the explanations. The authors should also provide more concrete examples of how their method can be used in real-world applications. For instance, in medical diagnosis, how can the identified training samples be used to provide insights into the model's decision-making process? Can these samples be used to identify potential biases in the training data or to highlight areas where the model may be underperforming? The authors should also discuss how their method can be integrated into existing machine learning workflows and tools. This would involve addressing practical challenges such as the selection of appropriate hyperparameters and the interpretation of the identified training samples. Furthermore, the authors should explore the potential of using their method for model debugging and improvement, such as identifying mislabeled or noisy training samples that may be negatively impacting model performance. A more detailed discussion of these practical aspects would significantly increase the value of the paper. The paper should also include a more comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art explanation methods. While the paper compares against some existing methods, a more detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method is needed. For example, how does the proposed method compare to methods based on gradient-based attribution or perturbation-based techniques? The authors should also consider comparing their method with other example-based explanation methods, such as those based on counterfactual reasoning or prototype learning. A more thorough comparison should include a quantitative analysis of the explanation quality, such as the fidelity of the explanations to the model's predictions, as well as a qualitative analysis of the interpretability of the explanations. This would provide a more complete picture of the advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method compared to existing approaches and help to establish its position in the field of explainable AI.

## questions
Several key questions arise from my analysis of this paper. First, can the authors provide more details on the choice of the kernel function used in the KSD calculation? How does the choice of kernel affect the results, and what are the trade-offs between computational cost and explanation quality when using different kernels? Specifically, how does the method perform with kernels that are less sensitive to high-frequency features? Second, the authors mention that the proposed method can be used to identify training samples that provide the best predictive support to a test point. Can the authors provide more details on how this is achieved? How does the method determine which training samples are most relevant to a given test point, and what is the precise mechanism by which the KSD captures this relevance? Third, the authors mention that the proposed method is computationally efficient. Can the authors provide more details on the computational complexity of the method? How does the method compare to other example-based explanation methods in terms of computational efficiency, and what are the memory requirements of the proposed method? Fourth, why do the authors assume $P_\theta(x) = P_D(x)$? What is the impact of this assumption on the performance of the method, and are there any alternatives that could be considered? Fifth, how do the authors get the gradient in equation (2), especially the gradient w.r.t y? What is the precise mathematical derivation of this gradient, and what are the implications of this derivation for the method's performance? Sixth, how does the proposed method compare to other state-of-the-art explanation methods in terms of explanation quality and computational efficiency?
