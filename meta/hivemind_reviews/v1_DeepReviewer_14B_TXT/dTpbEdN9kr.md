## summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for human motion generation and control, leveraging diffusion models as a prior. The authors propose three distinct methods: DoubleTake for generating long motion sequences, ComMDM for coordinating two-person interactions, and DiffusionBlending for fine-grained motion control. DoubleTake addresses the challenge of long sequence generation by iteratively generating short sequences and merging them, using a handshake mechanism to ensure smooth transitions. ComMDM tackles the problem of multi-person motion generation by training a slim communication block to coordinate the motion of two individuals, using a shared communication channel to align their movements. DiffusionBlending enables precise control over motion by blending fine-tuned models, allowing for joint and trajectory-level manipulation. The authors evaluate these methods on the HumanML3D and BABEL datasets, demonstrating state-of-the-art results in various motion generation tasks. The core idea is to use a pre-trained motion diffusion model (MDM) as a strong prior and then extend it to address more complex motion generation scenarios. The paper's main contribution lies in the innovative use of diffusion models for motion composition, offering a flexible and efficient approach to human motion synthesis. The empirical results show that the proposed methods achieve impressive performance, particularly in long sequence generation and fine-grained control. The authors also provide qualitative results to demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach. Overall, this work presents a significant step forward in human motion generation, offering a versatile framework that can be adapted to various tasks and applications. The use of a pre-trained MDM as a prior allows for efficient and high-quality motion generation, while the proposed composition methods enable the creation of complex and diverse motion sequences. The paper's findings suggest that diffusion models hold great promise for advancing the field of human motion synthesis, and the proposed framework provides a solid foundation for future research in this area.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The core idea of using a pre-trained motion diffusion model (MDM) as a prior for motion composition is both novel and effective. This approach allows the authors to leverage the power of diffusion models for generating high-quality motion sequences, while also providing a flexible framework for addressing more complex motion generation tasks. The proposed methods, DoubleTake, ComMDM, and DiffusionBlending, each tackle a distinct challenge in human motion generation, and they do so with impressive results. DoubleTake's approach to long sequence generation, using a handshake mechanism to merge short sequences, is a clever solution to a common problem in motion synthesis. The results presented in Table 1, particularly the R-precision and FID scores, demonstrate the effectiveness of this method. ComMDM's approach to two-person motion generation, using a slim communication block to coordinate the motion of two individuals, is also a significant contribution. The user study results presented in Figure 6 show that ComMDM outperforms both MDM and MRT in terms of interaction level, completion of the prefix, and overall quality. DiffusionBlending's ability to enable fine-grained control over motion is another notable strength. The results presented in Table 2, particularly the L2 error values, demonstrate the precision of this method. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the proposed methods and their evaluation. The authors provide sufficient details about their implementation, making it possible for other researchers to reproduce their results. The qualitative results, while not as extensive as I would have liked, also provide a good visual demonstration of the capabilities of the proposed methods. The use of a pre-trained MDM as a prior is a key strength of this work, as it allows the authors to achieve state-of-the-art results without having to train a model from scratch. The proposed composition methods are also well-designed and effectively address the specific challenges they target. Overall, I believe that this paper makes a significant contribution to the field of human motion generation, and the proposed framework has the potential to be widely adopted by other researchers.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the paper's reliance on a fixed MDM trained on HumanML3D for all tasks is a significant limitation. While using a pre-trained model is efficient, it also restricts the generalizability of the proposed methods. As noted by multiple reviewers, it would be valuable to see how these methods perform with other MDMs trained on different datasets or using different architectures. This is particularly important because the characteristics of the training data can significantly influence the quality and diversity of the generated motion. The paper does not explore this aspect, and it is unclear whether the proposed methods would be as effective with a different underlying motion prior. Second, the evaluation of two-person motion generation is limited to the 3DPW dataset. While this dataset is a valuable resource, it is not sufficient to demonstrate the generalization ability of the proposed method. As pointed out by multiple reviewers, evaluating on more diverse datasets, including those with different types of interactions and motion characteristics, is crucial to assess the robustness of the method. The lack of such evaluation makes it difficult to determine whether the method can handle a wide range of interaction scenarios. Third, the evaluation of fine-grained control is limited to a comparison with the original MDM. While this comparison is useful, it does not provide a comprehensive assessment of the proposed method's performance relative to other state-of-the-art techniques in fine-grained motion control. As noted by multiple reviewers, comparing with other methods that enable similar control capabilities is essential to understand the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. The paper does not include such comparisons, and it is unclear how the proposed method compares to existing techniques in terms of precision, flexibility, and overall performance. Fourth, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed methods. While the conclusion section mentions some limitations, such as inconsistencies in long sequences and limited generalization in two-person motion, it does not provide a thorough analysis of specific failure cases or drawbacks. A more in-depth discussion of the types of motions that the method struggles with, and the reasons for these failures, would be beneficial. For example, the paper could explore scenarios where DoubleTake produces abrupt transitions or repetitive motion patterns, or where ComMDM fails to coordinate complex interactions. Fifth, the paper does not discuss the computational cost of the proposed methods. While the paper mentions the hardware used for experiments and some training times, it lacks a systematic analysis of the computational resources required for training and inference. This is a significant omission, as computational cost is a crucial factor for practical applications. The paper should provide a breakdown of the training time, inference time, and memory usage for each method, and compare these costs to existing approaches. Finally, while the paper mentions a user study, the details of the study are not fully explained. As noted by one reviewer, the paper does not clearly specify the rating scale used by the participants or the specific instructions given to them. This lack of detail makes it difficult to assess the validity and reliability of the user study results. Additionally, the paper compares MRT with ComMDM in the user study for the text-to-motion task, which is questionable given that MRT is primarily designed for motion completion. The rationale for including MRT in the text-to-motion user study is not explicitly justified, and this comparison may not be appropriate.

## suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for this paper. First, the authors should conduct a more thorough evaluation of their methods using different motion diffusion models (MDMs). This would involve training or using pre-trained MDMs on different datasets or with different architectures and then applying the proposed methods (DoubleTake, ComMDM, and DiffusionBlending) to these alternative MDMs. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the generalizability of the proposed framework and its sensitivity to the underlying motion prior. Second, the authors should expand the evaluation of two-person motion generation to include more diverse datasets. This would involve using datasets that contain different types of interactions, such as those involving more complex movements or different social contexts. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's ability to handle a wide range of interaction scenarios. Third, the authors should compare their fine-grained control method with other state-of-the-art techniques in this area. This would involve implementing and evaluating other methods that enable similar control capabilities, such as those based on latent space manipulation or conditional generation. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the proposed method's performance and its advantages over existing techniques. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed methods. This would involve identifying specific failure cases and drawbacks for each method, and discussing the reasons for these failures. For example, the authors could analyze scenarios where DoubleTake produces abrupt transitions or repetitive motion patterns, or where ComMDM fails to coordinate complex interactions. This analysis should also include a discussion of the types of motions that the method struggles with, and the trade-offs between control and motion quality. Fifth, the authors should include a detailed discussion of the computational cost of the proposed methods. This would involve providing a breakdown of the training time, inference time, and memory usage for each method, and comparing these costs to existing approaches. The authors should also discuss the potential for optimizing the computational cost of the proposed methods, such as through parallelization or model compression techniques. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the user study, including the specific instructions given to the participants, the rating scale used, and the rationale for comparing MRT with ComMDM in the text-to-motion task. This would help to ensure the validity and reliability of the user study results. Additionally, the authors should consider including a baseline method that is designed for text-to-motion tasks in the user study to provide a fair comparison. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and provide a more comprehensive and robust evaluation of their proposed methods.
