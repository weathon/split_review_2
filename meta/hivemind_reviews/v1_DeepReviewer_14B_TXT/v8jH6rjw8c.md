## Summary
This paper tackles the challenging problem of learning from long-tailed datasets with noisy labels, a scenario frequently encountered in real-world applications. The authors observe that existing methods often fail to consistently improve learning across different sub-populations within such datasets, sometimes even exacerbating performance disparities. To address this, they propose a novel Fairness Regularizer (FR), designed to reduce performance gaps between sub-populations. The core idea is to incorporate fairness constraints into the learning objective, encouraging the model to achieve more uniform performance across different groups. The FR is implemented as a Lagrangian of fairness constraints, which is then relaxed for practical application. The authors conduct extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets, including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and Clothing1M, demonstrating that the proposed FR, when combined with existing robust or class-balanced methods, can improve overall learning performance and, crucially, the performance of tail sub-populations. The empirical results show that the FR can be effectively integrated with various loss functions and learning strategies, leading to improved performance, particularly for the under-represented and often noisy tail classes. The paper's main contribution lies in its introduction of a fairness-based approach to address the coupled problems of long-tailed distributions and noisy labels, and in demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach through comprehensive experiments. While the paper presents a compelling empirical case, it also raises important questions about the theoretical underpinnings of the proposed method and its practical limitations, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

The observation that some methods can improve performance for some sub-populations while simultaneously hurting others is a crucial point that motivates the need for a more nuanced approach. The proposed Fairness Regularizer (FR) is a novel and interesting solution to this problem. The idea of incorporating fairness constraints into the learning objective to encourage more uniform performance across sub-populations is both intuitive and effective. The authors' implementation of the FR, using a Lagrangian formulation and a practical relaxation, is well-described and easy to follow. The experimental results are another strength of the paper. The authors conduct extensive experiments on a variety of datasets, including both synthetic and real-world examples. They demonstrate that the FR can be effectively combined with existing robust and class-balanced methods to improve overall performance and, more importantly, the performance of tail sub-populations. The inclusion of figures showing per-sub-population and per-class performance is particularly valuable, as it allows for a detailed analysis of the method's impact. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain their methodology and results, and the paper is well-organized. Overall, I believe the paper makes a significant contribution to the field by introducing a novel approach to a challenging problem and providing strong empirical evidence of its effectiveness.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. The most significant concern revolves around the lack of a clear theoretical justification for why the proposed Fairness Regularizer (FR) effectively mitigates the impact of noisy labels in long-tailed distributions. While the authors observe that existing methods often exhibit disparate impacts on sub-populations, the connection between reducing performance disparities and improving robustness to label noise is not well-established. The paper argues that by preventing some sub-populations from being overly penalized, the overall learning becomes more stable and potentially more robust to noise. However, this connection is more of an intuitive leap than a rigorously proven fact. The paper does not provide a theoretical analysis or empirical evidence to support the claim that minimizing the performance gap between head and tail classes directly addresses the problem of noisy labels, which could be randomly distributed or disproportionately affect certain classes. This lack of theoretical grounding is a significant limitation, as it makes it difficult to fully understand the underlying mechanisms of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide any theoretical analysis or empirical evidence to support this claim. Another significant weakness is the paper's reliance on pre-defined sub-population information during training. The authors propose two methods for sub-population separation: clustering and using pre-trained models. However, they do not adequately address the practical challenges of obtaining these groupings in real-world scenarios. The paper acknowledges that obtaining a good representation extractor is non-trivial, but it does not fully explore the implications of using potentially flawed sub-population definitions. The sensitivity of the method to different sub-population definitions is not thoroughly analyzed, and the paper does not discuss how the method would perform if the sub-populations are not well-defined or accurately identified. This reliance on pre-defined sub-populations limits the real-world applicability of the proposed method, as obtaining accurate sub-population information can be difficult or impossible in many cases. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide a detailed discussion on the practical challenges and sensitivity to inaccurate sub-population definitions. Finally, while the experimental evaluation is extensive, it could be improved by including a wider range of datasets with varying characteristics.
