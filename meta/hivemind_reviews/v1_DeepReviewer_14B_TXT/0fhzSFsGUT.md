## summary
This paper introduces PETRA, a novel method for parallelizing the training of reversible architectures. The core idea behind PETRA is to decouple the forward and backward passes of the network, enabling different stages to be computed independently on separate devices. This decoupling is achieved by leveraging the properties of reversible architectures, which allow for the reconstruction of intermediate activations during the backward pass, thus eliminating the need to store extensive computational graphs and reducing memory overhead. The method employs a delayed, approximate inversion of activations during the backward pass, which allows for parallel gradient computations without the need for weight stashing. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of PETRA on several benchmark datasets, including CIFAR-10, ImageNet32, and ImageNet, using RevNet architectures. They report competitive accuracies compared to standard backpropagation, while also highlighting the potential for reduced memory consumption. The paper also includes a custom autograd-like training framework to manage the parallel execution and parameter updates. The authors claim that PETRA achieves a linear speedup compared to standard backpropagation with respect to the number of stages the network is split into, although this claim is not supported by detailed timing analysis. The paper also explores the impact of an accumulation factor on the performance of PETRA, showing that increasing this factor reduces the effective staleness of gradients and improves accuracy. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach for parallelizing the training of reversible architectures, with a focus on reducing memory overhead and enabling parallel computation. However, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead, convergence behavior, and a thorough comparison with other parallelization techniques, which limits the assessment of its practical benefits and generalizability. The paper also acknowledges a slight accuracy drop compared to standard backpropagation, which is attributed to the use of stale gradients, but a more thorough investigation into the root causes and potential mitigation strategies is needed.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the authors should conduct a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of PETRA. This analysis should include a breakdown of the FLOPs and latency introduced by the method, with a per-layer analysis comparing the computational cost of the reversible layers and the custom autograd framework to standard backpropagation. The authors should also provide a comparison of the training time per epoch for their method and standard backpropagation, using the same hardware and software configurations. This analysis should be performed on multiple datasets to ensure the results are generalizable. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different batch sizes on the computational overhead, as this can significantly affect the overall training time. Secondly, the authors should explore the impact of different reversible architectures on the performance of PETRA. This should include experiments using a variety of reversible architectures, such as those based on different coupling layers (e.g., affine coupling layers, spline coupling layers) or attention mechanisms. This would help to understand the sensitivity of the proposed method to architectural choices. The authors should also analyze the impact of different architectural parameters, such as the number of layers or the size of the hidden dimensions, on the performance of the proposed method. This analysis should include a comparison of the accuracy, memory overhead, and computational cost for different architectures. Thirdly, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the convergence behavior of PETRA. This should include learning curves that show the training and validation loss and accuracy over time. This would allow for a comparison of the convergence speed and stability of the proposed method to standard backpropagation. The authors should also analyze the impact of the decoupled forward and backward passes on the optimization landscape. This could involve visualizing the loss surface or analyzing the gradient norms during training. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different optimization algorithms and learning rate schedules on the convergence behavior of the proposed method. Fourthly, the authors should include detailed timing results for their experiments. This should include the time taken for forward and backward passes, communication overhead, and the overall training time. These results should be compared against a well-optimized backpropagation baseline, with a clear indication of the number of devices used in both cases. It is crucial to demonstrate the scaling behavior of PETRA by varying the number of devices and showing the corresponding speedup. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different stage configurations on the overall performance. Fifthly, to address the accuracy gap between PETRA and backpropagation, a more in-depth analysis of the gradient staleness is needed. The authors should investigate how the staleness affects the convergence rate and the final accuracy. They could explore techniques to mitigate the staleness, such as using more frequent parameter updates or employing adaptive learning rates. A detailed study of the trade-off between batch size and accuracy is also necessary, as increasing the batch size might not be the optimal solution in all scenarios. The authors should also explore the impact of different accumulation factors on the accuracy and training time. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the impact of the delayed, approximate inversion on the training dynamics. This should include a study on the sensitivity of the method to different approximation parameters. For instance, how does the accuracy and training time vary with the degree of approximation in the inversion? Furthermore, it would be valuable to investigate the potential for error accumulation due to the delayed inversion and how this error might propagate through the network during training. This analysis should include both theoretical considerations and empirical results, possibly visualizing the error propagation and its impact on the loss landscape.
