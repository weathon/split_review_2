## summary
This paper introduces a novel framework called Curvature-Constrained Message Passing (CCMP), designed to enhance the performance of Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs) by leveraging the concept of edge curvature. The core idea behind CCMP is to selectively propagate information across the graph based on the curvature of the edges, aiming to mitigate the over-squashing phenomenon that often plagues GNNs. The authors propose two distinct edge curvature measures: Ollivier curvature and Augmented Forman curvature, each giving rise to different variants of CCMP. The method can be applied to any MPNN architecture, making it a versatile approach. The authors argue that by focusing message passing on edges with positive curvature, which tend to connect nodes within the same community, and by limiting or eliminating message passing on edges with negative curvature, which often connect nodes in different communities, CCMP can improve information flow and reduce the bottlenecks that lead to over-squashing. The paper presents a detailed explanation of how these curvature measures are calculated and how they are integrated into the message-passing process. The authors explore different strategies for using curvature, such as one-hop and two-hop message passing, and propagating information based on either positive or negative curvature edges. The empirical evaluation of CCMP is conducted on eleven datasets, encompassing both homophilic and heterophilic graphs, and the results are compared against several existing graph rewiring techniques. The authors demonstrate that CCMP consistently outperforms these baselines, particularly on heterophilic datasets, suggesting that curvature-based message passing is an effective strategy for improving GNN performance. The paper also provides some intuition for why CCMP works, linking the curvature measures to the structural properties of the graph and the flow of information. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to improving GNNs, offering a new perspective on how to address the over-squashing problem. The use of curvature to guide message passing is a novel idea that has the potential to inspire further research in this area. While the paper has some limitations, such as a lack of theoretical analysis and some presentation issues, the core contribution of introducing curvature-based message passing is significant and warrants further investigation.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its introduction of a novel approach to message passing in GNNs, specifically the Curvature-Constrained Message Passing (CCMP) framework. The idea of using edge curvature to guide information flow is both intuitive and innovative. By selectively propagating messages based on the curvature of edges, the authors aim to address the over-squashing problem, which is a significant challenge in GNNs. The paper's exploration of two different curvature measures, Ollivier and Augmented Forman, and the subsequent development of different CCMP variants, demonstrates a thorough approach to the problem. The fact that CCMP can be applied to any MPNN architecture is a significant advantage, making it a versatile tool for researchers and practitioners. The empirical results presented in the paper are also a notable strength. The authors conduct a comprehensive evaluation of CCMP on eleven datasets, including both homophilic and heterophilic graphs, and demonstrate that CCMP consistently outperforms several existing graph rewiring techniques. The performance gains on heterophilic datasets are particularly impressive, suggesting that CCMP is effective in mitigating over-squashing in scenarios where it is most problematic. The paper also provides some intuition for why CCMP works, linking the curvature measures to the structural properties of the graph and the flow of information. The explanations in Section 3.4, while not fully establishing a theoretical foundation, offer a plausible rationale for the observed performance gains. The paper's clear presentation of the method and the experimental setup also contributes to its overall strength. The authors provide sufficient detail for others to understand and potentially replicate their work. Finally, the paper's identification of the limitations of existing methods and the motivation for a new approach is well-articulated, setting the stage for the introduction of CCMP.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling approach to message passing, several weaknesses need to be addressed. First, the motivation for the proposed method, while eventually presented, is not immediately clear in the introduction. The paper begins by discussing the over-squashing problem and mentions two general approaches to mitigate it: incorporating global graph features and rewiring the input graph. However, it fails to explicitly connect the concept of curvature to these approaches at the outset. It is only later, in Section 3.4, that the paper provides some intuition for why curvature-based message passing might be effective. This delayed explanation makes it difficult for the reader to immediately grasp the rationale behind the proposed method. The introduction should be revised to clearly articulate how curvature relates to the existing strategies for mitigating over-squashing. This lack of initial clarity undermines the paper's ability to effectively communicate its core idea. Second, while the specific method of using curvature to guide message passing is novel, the overall idea of using graph rewiring to mitigate over-squashing is not. The paper acknowledges the existence of rewiring methods in the introduction and related work sections, but it does not fully address the fact that the high-level approach is not entirely new. This limited novelty, while not a fatal flaw, should be acknowledged and discussed more explicitly. The experimental evaluation, while thorough, also uses standard datasets and baselines, which further contributes to the perception of limited novelty. The paper should more clearly differentiate its contribution from existing rewiring methods, emphasizing the unique aspects of its curvature-based approach. Third, the presentation of the paper suffers from several issues that detract from its overall quality. There are instances of spelling and grammatical errors, such as the use of "wich" instead of "which" in the introduction. These errors, while minor, undermine the paper's credibility. More significantly, the figures are not of high quality, with small font sizes making them difficult to read. The figures should be improved to enhance their clarity and readability. The small font size makes it difficult to discern the details of the figures, which is crucial for understanding the experimental results. Finally, the paper lacks a theoretical analysis of the proposed method. While the authors provide some intuition for why CCMP might work, they do not offer any formal proofs or guarantees regarding its convergence, expressiveness, or other theoretical properties. This lack of theoretical analysis is a significant limitation, as it makes it difficult to fully understand the behavior of CCMP and its limitations. The paper should include a theoretical analysis to provide a deeper understanding of the method and its potential. The absence of such analysis makes it difficult to assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed approach. These weaknesses, while not invalidating the core contribution of the paper, need to be addressed to improve its overall quality and impact. The lack of clear initial motivation, the limited novelty, the presentation issues, and the absence of theoretical analysis are all significant concerns that need to be addressed.

This will help to provide a more complete understanding of the method and its practical implications. These suggestions, if implemented, will significantly improve the quality and impact of the paper.
