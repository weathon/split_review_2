## summary
This paper introduces Newton Losses, a novel approach to improve the optimization of difficult loss functions by leveraging second-order information. The core idea is to replace the original loss function with a quadratic approximation derived from its second-order Taylor expansion, effectively creating a locally convex loss landscape. This is achieved by utilizing either the empirical Fisher matrix or the Hessian matrix to approximate the curvature of the loss function. The authors propose two variants of Newton Losses: one based on the Hessian and another based on the empirical Fisher matrix. The Hessian-based variant, while potentially offering greater improvements, requires the computation of second derivatives, whereas the Fisher-based variant is computationally more efficient as it only requires first derivatives. The method involves computing the optimal output of the quadratic approximation and then using the mean squared error between this optimal output and the actual output of the neural network as the new loss function. This allows the authors to optimize the loss using standard gradient descent while implicitly incorporating second-order information. The authors evaluate Newton Losses on a range of differentiable algorithms for sorting and shortest-path problems, demonstrating consistent performance improvements across various baselines. Specifically, they apply their method to eight differentiable algorithms, including NeuralSort, SoftSort, and differentiable sorting networks for ranking, and relaxed Bellman-Ford, stochastic smoothing, and perturbed optimizers for shortest paths. The empirical results show that Newton Losses consistently improves the performance of these algorithms, even when they are already well-optimized. The authors also provide a runtime analysis, showing that the Fisher-based variant has a comparable runtime to standard gradient descent, while the Hessian-based variant incurs a manageable overhead. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative use of second-order information to modify the loss function, rather than directly applying second-order optimization methods to the neural network parameters. This approach offers a computationally efficient way to improve the optimization of hard-to-train models, particularly in the context of differentiable algorithms. The authors also provide a theoretical analysis showing that the proposed method is equivalent to a single Newton step, further supporting the validity of their approach. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of differentiable algorithms, offering a practical and effective method for improving the optimization of hard-to-train models.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the paper's experimental evaluation, while comprehensive within its chosen domain, is limited in scope. The authors focus exclusively on differentiable algorithms for sorting and shortest-path problems, which are relatively narrow in the broader landscape of differentiable algorithms. Specifically, the paper evaluates Newton Losses on eight differentiable algorithms, including NeuralSort, SoftSort, and differentiable sorting networks for ranking, and relaxed Bellman-Ford, stochastic smoothing, and perturbed optimizers for shortest paths. While these are well-established benchmarks, the lack of experiments on other types of differentiable algorithms, such as those used in reinforcement learning or generative modeling, raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed method. This limitation is significant because many real-world applications of differentiable algorithms involve non-convex loss landscapes and high-dimensional input spaces, which are not fully explored in this paper. The absence of experiments on these types of algorithms makes it difficult to assess the broad applicability of Newton Losses. This is a high-confidence weakness, as the paper explicitly states the benchmarks used and the specific algorithms evaluated, which are limited to sorting and shortest-path tasks. Second, the paper lacks a detailed theoretical analysis of the convergence properties of Newton Losses. While the authors provide a theoretical analysis showing that the proposed method is equivalent to a single Newton step, they do not provide any theoretical guarantees on the convergence rate or the optimality of the method. The paper describes the method as 'locally approximating loss functions with a quadratic with second-order Taylor expansion,' and states that 'Newton Losses provides a (locally) convex loss leading to better optimization behavior.' However, the paper does not explore how this local convexity affects the global optimization landscape, nor does it provide any guarantees about convergence to a local minimum or a saddle point. This lack of theoretical analysis is a significant weakness, as it leaves open questions about the conditions under which the method is guaranteed to converge and the rate of convergence. This is a high-confidence weakness, as the paper does not present any theorems or proofs regarding the convergence rate or guarantees of the proposed method. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison with other second-order optimization methods, such as quasi-Newton methods or natural gradient methods. The paper mentions second-order optimization methods in the introduction, citing works such as 'While the success of neural network training is primarily due to the backpropagation algorithm and stochastic gradient descent (SGD), there is also a promising line of work on second-order optimization for neural network training.' However, the experimental section does not include direct comparisons with these methods. The paper focuses on improving the loss function using second-order information, which is different from applying second-order optimization methods directly to the neural network parameters. The lack of a direct comparison with these other second-order methods makes it difficult to contextualize the contribution of this work and assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of each approach. This is a high-confidence weakness, as the experimental section only compares against baselines of differentiable algorithms, not against other second-order optimization methods for neural network training. Fourth, while the paper includes a runtime analysis, it lacks a detailed theoretical analysis of the computational cost of Newton Losses. The paper provides empirical runtime data in the 'Runtime Analysis' section, stating that 'the runtimes between the baseline and empirical Fisher-based Newton Losses are indistinguishable for all cases' and that 'for the analytical relaxations of differentiable sorting algorithms, where the computation of the Hessian can become expensive with automatic differentiation... we observed overheads between 10% and 2.6x.' However, the paper does not provide a breakdown of the computational cost of each step in the algorithm, including the computation of the Hessian and Fisher matrix, and how this cost scales with the size of the problem and the number of parameters. This lack of a detailed computational complexity analysis makes it difficult to fully understand the practical implications of the method, especially for large-scale applications. This is a high-confidence weakness, as the paper provides empirical runtime data but lacks a detailed theoretical computational complexity analysis. Finally, while the paper includes an ablation study on the regularization parameter λ, it does not provide a broader discussion of the potential limitations of the method or the sensitivity to other hyperparameter choices. The paper introduces the Tikhonov regularization parameter λ in Section 3.2 and includes an 'Ablation Study' on the regularization strength in Section 4.4. However, the paper does not explicitly discuss conditions where the method might be ineffective or degrade performance, beyond the empirical observations in the ablation study. The interaction of λ with other hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, is also not deeply explored.
