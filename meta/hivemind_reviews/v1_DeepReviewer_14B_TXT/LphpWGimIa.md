## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to interpreting the internal workings of transformer models by applying sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to the output of attention layers. The authors' core contribution lies in demonstrating that SAEs can effectively decompose these high-dimensional attention outputs into sparse, interpretable features, offering a new lens through which to examine the behavior of attention mechanisms. They further develop a weight-based head attribution technique to associate these learned features with specific attention heads, addressing the challenge of polysemanticity, where a single head might perform multiple functions. The authors also propose Recursive Direct Feature Attribution (RDFA), a method that leverages the linear structure of transformers to trace model computations through attention layers, enhancing circuit analysis. Empirically, the authors validate their approach by analyzing the Indirect Object Identification (IOI) circuit, identifying causally meaningful intermediate variables, and investigating the roles of different attention heads, particularly induction heads. They distinguish between long-prefix and short-prefix induction heads, providing a more nuanced understanding of their respective contributions. The authors support their findings with experiments on GPT-2 Small and Gemma-2B models, and they provide open-source tools, including trained SAEs and a visualization tool for RDFA, to facilitate further research and exploration. Overall, this work makes a significant contribution to the field of mechanistic interpretability by providing a valuable tool for understanding attention layer outputs and by offering new insights into the inner workings of transformer models.

## Strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the application of sparse autoencoders to attention layer outputs is a valuable contribution. While SAEs have been used for other components of transformers, their use on attention outputs provides a new perspective on understanding the information flow within these models. The authors demonstrate that these SAEs can effectively extract sparse and interpretable features, which is a crucial step for gaining deeper insights into model behavior. Second, the introduction of the weight-based head attribution method is a significant technical innovation. This method allows for a more granular analysis of attention heads by associating specific features with individual heads. This is particularly important given the polysemantic nature of attention heads, and it provides a way to disentangle the various roles that a single head might play. The authors clearly explain the mathematical formulation of this technique, making it easy to understand and potentially replicate. Third, the development of Recursive Direct Feature Attribution (RDFA) is a notable achievement. This method leverages the linear structure of transformers to trace model computations through attention layers, which is a powerful approach for circuit analysis. The authors provide a clear explanation of the method's basis and refer to the appendix for a full algorithmic description, which is helpful for those interested in implementing it. Finally, the empirical validation of the proposed methods is compelling. The authors use their techniques to analyze the IOI circuit and to investigate induction heads, providing concrete examples of how these methods can be used to gain new insights into model behavior. The distinction between long-prefix and short-prefix induction heads is a particularly interesting finding. The open-sourcing of the trained SAEs and the RDFA visualization tool further enhances the value of this work, making it easier for other researchers to build upon their findings.

## Suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should provide a more detailed description of the synthetic datasets used to differentiate between long-prefix and short-prefix induction heads. This description should include the specific parameters used to generate the datasets, the criteria for distinguishing between long and short prefixes, and a more diverse set of examples to ensure the robustness of the analysis. Furthermore, the authors should include error bars in Figure 6 to allow for a more accurate assessment of the statistical significance of the observed differences. They should also consider using a larger sample size to increase the statistical power of their analysis. Second, the authors should expand their evaluation to include a more diverse set of models and tasks. This should include models with different architectures, such as those using different attention mechanisms or layer configurations, and tasks that involve different types of data, such as images or audio. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's effectiveness and its potential for practical applications. In addition, the authors should compare their method to other existing methods for decomposing attention outputs. This comparison should include both quantitative and qualitative analyses, and it should help to better understand the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method. Third, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of their proposed method. This analysis should include both the time and memory requirements of the method, and it should discuss the scalability of the method and its potential for use in large-scale applications. The authors should also discuss potential optimizations that could be used to reduce the computational complexity of the method. ## Questions
I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. First, how do the features learned from attention outputs compare to those learned from value vectors or MLP layers in terms of interpretability and model behavior? The paper focuses on attention outputs, but a comparative analysis with other components could provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's internal representations. Second, what are the limitations of using SAEs for feature decomposition in transformers, and how might these limitations affect the interpretation of the results? The paper acknowledges that SAEs are not a perfect solution, but a more detailed discussion of their limitations would be beneficial. Third, how does the proposed RDFA method compare to other circuit analysis techniques in terms of effectiveness and computational efficiency? The paper does not benchmark RDFA against other methods, and a comparison would help to better understand its strengths and weaknesses. Fourth, in Section 4.3, the authors state that "Attention Output SAEs immediately reveal the positional signal by decomposing these activations into interpretable features." However, it is unclear how the proposed features are used to identify the positional signal. In the subsequent paragraph, the authors mention that they "localized and interpreted causally relevant SAE features from the outputs of the attention layers that contain induction heads (Layers 5 and 6) with zero ablations." It is unclear how the causally relevant features are localized. Is the process similar to the one described in Section 4.2, involving intervention on real examples? Additionally, why are the ablations performed at the feature level rather than at the head level? Finally, in the introduction, the authors state that "Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are a popular method for decomposing the internal activations of trained transformers into sparse, interpretable features." However, it is not clear why the features extracted by SAEs should be inherently interpretable. The interpretability of these features depends on various factors, such as the quality of the SAEs, the criteria used for interpretation, and the specific examples analyzed. A more accurate statement would be that SAEs are a popular method for decomposing internal activations into sparse features, and that these features can potentially be interpretable under certain conditions.
