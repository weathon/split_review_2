## summary
This paper investigates the potential of Mamba, a State Space Model (SSM), as an alternative to Transformers for 3D volumetric medical image segmentation. The authors address three key questions: whether Mamba can replace Transformers for long-range dependency modeling, whether it can enhance multi-scale representation learning, and whether complex scanning strategies are necessary. To this end, they introduce UlikeMamba, a U-shaped Mamba-based network, and compare its performance against a Transformer-based network, UlikeTrans, on three public datasets: AMOS, TotalSegmentator, and BraTS. The core methodological approach involves adapting Mamba for 3D image processing by incorporating 3D depthwise convolutions and a multi-scale Mamba block. The authors also explore different scanning strategies, including single-scan, dual-scan, and a novel Tri-scan approach, to optimize spatial relationship modeling. The main empirical findings indicate that Mamba, particularly when combined with 3D depthwise convolutions and the multi-scale block, outperforms Transformer-based models in both accuracy and computational efficiency. The Tri-scan approach further improves performance in complex segmentation tasks. The authors position Mamba as a promising alternative for long-range dependency modeling in medical imaging, offering a more efficient solution compared to Transformers. The paper's overall significance lies in its exploration of a novel architecture for medical image segmentation and its potential to address the computational limitations of Transformer-based models. The study provides a comprehensive evaluation of Mamba's capabilities in 3D medical image segmentation, addressing key questions about its effectiveness compared to Transformers. The introduction of 3D depthwise convolutions and the multi-scale Mamba block are innovative and show improved performance in capturing both fine-grained details and broader anatomical structures. The paper is well-organized and clearly presents its objectives, methodologies, and findings. The experimental setup and results are detailed, allowing for easy understanding of the research process. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in the scope of its comparisons and the depth of its analysis, which I will discuss in detail below.

## strengths
This paper presents a compelling exploration of Mamba for 3D medical image segmentation, demonstrating several notable strengths. First, the paper provides a comprehensive evaluation of Mamba's capabilities, directly addressing its effectiveness compared to Transformers, a dominant architecture in this field. The authors' choice to compare Mamba against a Transformer-based network, UlikeTrans, on three public datasets (AMOS, TotalSegmentator, and BraTS) provides a solid empirical foundation for their claims. The introduction of UlikeMamba, a U-shaped Mamba-based network, is a significant contribution. The incorporation of 3D depthwise convolutions and a multi-scale Mamba block are innovative technical approaches that enhance the model's ability to capture both fine-grained details and broader anatomical structures. These modifications are crucial for adapting Mamba to the specific challenges of 3D medical image segmentation. The exploration of different scanning strategies, including single-scan, dual-scan, and the novel Tri-scan approach, is another strength. The Tri-scan approach, in particular, demonstrates improved performance in complex segmentation tasks, highlighting the importance of spatial relationship modeling. The paper's clear and well-organized presentation of its objectives, methodologies, and findings is commendable. The experimental setup and results are detailed, allowing for easy understanding of the research process. The authors also provide quantitative results, such as Dice scores and FLOPs, which allow for a clear comparison of the different models. The paper's focus on computational efficiency is also a strength. The authors demonstrate that Mamba-based models can achieve comparable or better performance than Transformer-based models with lower computational costs, which is a crucial consideration for practical applications. The paper's overall contribution lies in its successful adaptation of Mamba for 3D medical image segmentation and its demonstration of the potential of Mamba as an alternative to Transformers. The authors have clearly identified a relevant problem, proposed a novel solution, and provided empirical evidence to support their claims. The paper's focus on practical considerations, such as computational efficiency, further enhances its value.

## weaknesses
Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the limited scope of the baseline comparisons. While the authors compare Mamba against CNN-based models like nnUNet and Transformer-based models like CoTr, UNETR, and SwinUNETR, as well as an existing Mamba-based model, U-Mamba, they fail to include comparisons with other state-of-the-art models that are not based on these architectures. Specifically, the absence of comparisons with models based on graph neural networks or diffusion models is a significant oversight. This narrow focus makes it difficult to fully contextualize Mamba's performance relative to the broader landscape of medical image segmentation. The paper's experimental design, while thorough in its comparison to Transformers, leaves open the question of whether Mamba truly represents a significant advancement over other existing segmentation techniques. This limitation is evident in the 'Comparison with Advanced Baselines' section (Section 7) and Figure 4, which clearly show the limited range of models used for comparison. ## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly broaden the scope of their baseline comparisons. This should include not only CNN-based and Transformer-based models but also other state-of-the-art architectures, such as those based on graph neural networks and diffusion models. This would provide a more complete picture of Mamba's relative performance and help to contextualize its advantages and limitations. Specifically, I suggest including models like nnUNet, which is a widely used baseline in medical image segmentation, as well as more recent models that have shown promise in the field. Furthermore, the evaluation should include a variety of metrics beyond Dice score, such as Hausdorff distance and average surface distance, to provide a more comprehensive assessment of segmentation quality. This broader comparison would help to better understand the specific scenarios where Mamba excels and where it may fall short compared to other established methods. Second, the authors should expand the scope of their study beyond segmentation tasks. This could involve exploring the applicability of Mamba to other relevant medical imaging tasks, such as image registration and generation. For image registration, Mamba's ability to model long-range dependencies could be beneficial for aligning images from different modalities or time points. In image generation, Mamba could be used to synthesize realistic medical images for data augmentation or anomaly detection. Investigating these tasks would not only demonstrate the versatility of Mamba but also provide a more comprehensive understanding of its potential in the medical domain. This would also help to position the work more strategically within the broader landscape of medical image analysis research. The authors could consider adapting the UlikeMamba architecture to these tasks and evaluating its performance against existing methods. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost and memory requirements of the proposed Mamba-based model. This should include not only FLOPs and parameter counts but also memory usage and inference time. The analysis should also consider the impact of different model sizes and configurations on both performance and computational cost, providing a more nuanced understanding of the trade-offs involved. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the potential for optimizing the Mamba architecture for specific hardware platforms to improve its efficiency. This analysis should also consider the impact of these choices on different hardware platforms, such as GPUs with varying memory capacities. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to explore potential optimizations to mitigate the computational burden, such as pruning or quantization techniques, to make the model more practical for real-world applications. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the scanning strategies employed by the Mamba model, particularly the Tri-scan approach. This should include a visual representation of the scanning paths, as well as a discussion of the rationale behind the chosen scanning order. Furthermore, the paper should analyze the computational trade-offs associated with different scanning strategies, including the impact on FLOPs, memory usage, and inference time. This analysis should also consider the potential for adaptive scanning strategies that dynamically adjust the scanning pattern based on the input data, which could further improve the model's efficiency and performance. Finally, the authors should provide a more in-depth analysis of the mechanisms by which Mamba captures long-range dependencies compared to Transformers, particularly in the context of 3D volumetric data. This could involve visualizing the learned representations or conducting ablation studies to understand the contribution of different components of the Mamba architecture. This deeper mechanistic understanding would strengthen the claims made about Mamba's advantages and provide valuable insights for future research.
