## summary
This paper introduces PEARL, a novel framework designed to enhance the robustness of large language models (LLMs) when faced with variations in the order of demonstrations during in-context learning (ICL). The core idea behind PEARL is to employ distributional robust optimization (DRO) to train LLMs against worst-case permutations of input demonstrations. To achieve this, the authors propose a hard permutation mining network (P-Net), which identifies challenging permutations using optimal transport and the Sinkhorn algorithm. The P-Net is trained adversarially to generate permutations that maximize the LLM's loss, thereby forcing the LLM to become more resilient to variations in demonstration order. The training process involves an adversarial game between the P-Net and the LLM, where the P-Net aims to find the most challenging permutations, and the LLM seeks to minimize its loss on these permutations. The paper demonstrates the effectiveness of PEARL through extensive experiments on both synthetic data and real-world instruction tuning tasks. The empirical results show that PEARL significantly improves both the average and worst-case performance of LLMs across various tasks, effectively mitigating the vulnerability of LLMs to permutation-based attacks. The authors evaluate PEARL on tasks such as synthetic linear function learning and instruction tuning using datasets from Super-Natural Instructions. The results consistently show that PEARL enhances the robustness of LLMs without sacrificing average performance, which is a notable achievement. The paper also explores the performance of PEARL on different LLMs, including LLaMA-3-8B, LLaMA2-7B/13B, Mistral-7B, and Gemma-7B, demonstrating the generalizability of the approach. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field by addressing a critical vulnerability in LLMs and providing a robust solution that improves their performance in in-context learning scenarios. The use of DRO and the P-Net for identifying challenging permutations is innovative and well-integrated into the learning framework. The empirical results are compelling, showing significant improvements in worst-case performance without sacrificing average performance, which is a notable achievement. The paper's findings suggest that PEARL has the potential to enhance the reliability and performance of LLMs in real-world applications where input permutations are a concern.

## strengths
The paper's primary strength lies in its innovative approach to addressing the permutation sensitivity of large language models (LLMs) in in-context learning (ICL). The introduction of PEARL, a permutation-resilient learning framework based on distributional robust optimization (DRO), is a significant contribution to the field. This approach is novel, as it directly tackles the instability of LLMs to variations in the order of demonstrations, a problem that has been largely overlooked. The use of a hard permutation mining network (P-Net) to identify challenging permutations is another key innovation. The P-Net, which leverages optimal transport and the Sinkhorn algorithm, effectively generates permutations that push the LLM to its limits, thereby enhancing its robustness. The adversarial training process between the P-Net and the LLM is well-designed and effectively forces the LLM to learn representations that are invariant to demonstration order. The paper's methodology is well-supported by theoretical analysis and empirical results. The authors provide a clear explanation of the problem, the proposed solution, and the experimental setup. The experimental results are compelling, demonstrating significant improvements in both average and worst-case performance across various tasks. The paper's evaluation on both synthetic data and real-world instruction tuning tasks further strengthens the validity of the findings. The authors also evaluate PEARL on different LLMs, including LLaMA-3-8B, LLaMA2-7B/13B, Mistral-7B, and Gemma-7B, which demonstrates the generalizability of the approach. The fact that PEARL improves worst-case performance without sacrificing average performance is a notable achievement, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed framework. The paper is also well-structured and clearly explains the problem, methodology, and results, making it accessible to readers with a background in the field. The authors have successfully identified a critical vulnerability in LLMs and have provided a robust solution that has the potential to improve the reliability and performance of LLMs in real-world applications.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling approach to enhancing the robustness of LLMs, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, independently validated by both reviewers, is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of the PEARL framework. The paper describes the P-Net architecture and the use of the Sinkhorn algorithm, but it does not provide a rigorous analysis of the time complexity of the Sinkhorn algorithm in relation to the number of demonstrations or the size of the language model. The paper mentions that the P-Net is a "small pre-trained model" (Section 4.2) and initialized as a BERT-base (Section 5.2), but it does not quantify the computational overhead associated with this component. Furthermore, the experimental section lacks a direct comparison of computational resources, such as training time and memory usage, between PEARL and standard training methods. This absence of a detailed computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of PEARL, especially when applied to very large language models. The paper also lacks a thorough discussion of the potential limitations and trade-offs introduced by the PEARL framework. While the authors claim that PEARL improves both worst-case and average performance, they do not explore potential drawbacks, such as increased training time, the potential for overfitting on the P-Net, or sensitivity to hyperparameters. The paper focuses on the benefits of PEARL without thoroughly exploring potential downsides. This lack of a balanced discussion of limitations weakens the paper's overall impact. Another significant weakness, also identified by both reviewers, is the lack of exploration of PEARL's scalability with increasing numbers of demonstrations. The paper acknowledges the exponential growth of permutations (Introduction, Section 3), but it does not explicitly discuss the limitations of the approach as the number of demonstrations becomes very large (e.g., >10). The experiments are limited to a maximum of 6 demonstrations (Section 3, Figure 1), and there are no experiments exploring the performance with significantly larger numbers of demonstrations. This lack of experimental evidence makes it difficult to assess how PEARL performs in scenarios with a very large number of demonstrations, where the factorial growth of permutations could pose a computational bottleneck. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the convergence properties of the adversarial training process between the P-Net and the LLM. While the paper describes the adversarial optimization process (Section 4.3), it does not provide a formal analysis or proof of convergence for this specific setup. The paper mentions training until convergence (Section 5.2), but it does not provide details on convergence criteria or visualizations of the loss curves for both networks. This lack of a detailed convergence analysis raises concerns about the stability and reliability of the training process. Finally, while the paper evaluates PEARL on synthetic and real-world instruction tuning tasks, it does not provide in-depth examples of how PEARL would be applied and what the specific benefits and drawbacks would be in concrete real-world applications beyond these evaluations. This lack of detailed application examples limits the paper's ability to demonstrate the practical relevance of the proposed method. The confidence level for each of these weaknesses is high, as they are supported by the absence of specific information or analysis within the paper.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the paper should include a more rigorous analysis of the computational demands of the PEARL framework. This analysis should include a detailed breakdown of the time complexity of the Sinkhorn algorithm in relation to the size of the input permutations and the number of iterations required for convergence. The paper should also quantify the computational overhead associated with the P-Net, including the number of parameters and the computational cost of the forward and backward passes. Furthermore, the paper should provide a comparison of the computational resources required by PEARL versus standard training methods, including the number of FLOPs, memory usage, and training time, for different model sizes and permutation set sizes. This analysis should be presented for both training and inference time. Second, the paper needs a more thorough discussion of the potential trade-offs and limitations introduced by the PEARL framework. This discussion should include an investigation into whether the adversarial training process leads to overfitting on the P-Net and how this affects the generalization performance of the LLM. The paper should also explore the sensitivity of PEARL to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and the number of adversarial training iterations. A sensitivity analysis would help to understand the robustness of the framework and its practical applicability. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the potential limitations of PEARL in handling more complex input structures or tasks, beyond the simple permutation of demonstrations. Third, the paper should delve deeper into the practical limitations of the proposed PEARL framework, especially concerning its scalability with increasing numbers of demonstrations. The paper should explore the performance of PEARL when the number of demonstrations exceeds a certain threshold (e.g., 10, 15, or 20), and how the P-Net's ability to identify challenging permutations degrades in such scenarios. It would be beneficial to include experiments that specifically test the boundaries of the method's applicability, perhaps by systematically increasing the number of demonstrations and observing the resulting impact on both average and worst-case performance. Furthermore, the authors should discuss potential strategies for mitigating the computational burden associated with the increasing number of permutations, such as approximation techniques or sampling methods that could be integrated into the P-Net. Fourth, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the convergence properties of the adversarial training process between the P-Net and the LLM. This analysis should include a discussion of whether the training process converges to a stable solution or oscillates between different states. It would be beneficial to see a convergence analysis, including the loss curves of both the P-Net and the LLM, and how these curves change over time. The paper should also discuss the potential for instability during the adversarial training process and how this can be mitigated. Finally, the paper should explore the potential impact of the PEARL framework on real-world applications in more detail. The authors should provide concrete examples of how the method could be applied in practice, such as in question answering or text summarization. The paper should also discuss the potential for integrating PEARL with other techniques, such as retrieval-augmented generation, to further improve the robustness and performance of LLMs. These improvements would provide a more complete understanding of the practical trade-offs associated with the PEARL framework and enhance the paper's overall impact.

## questions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I have several questions that would help to clarify the paper's findings and limitations. First, how does the computational complexity of PEARL scale with the size of the language model and the number of permutations considered? Specifically, what is the time complexity of the Sinkhorn algorithm in relation to the number of demonstrations, and how does the computational overhead of the P-Net scale with the size of the language model? Second, are there any trade-offs or potential performance degradations in certain scenarios when using PEARL compared to standard training methods? For instance, does the adversarial training process lead to overfitting on the P-Net, and how does this affect the generalization performance of the LLM? What is the sensitivity of PEARL to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and the number of adversarial training iterations? Third, how does the PEARL framework perform when the number of demonstrations is very large (e.g., >10)? Does the factorial growth of permutations pose a computational bottleneck, and how does the P-Net's ability to identify challenging permutations degrade in such scenarios? Fourth, can the authors provide more details on the computational overhead introduced by PEARL, especially in terms of training time and memory requirements? What is the breakdown of the computational cost associated with the P-Net, the Sinkhorn algorithm, and the adversarial training process? How does this overhead compare to standard training methods? Fifth, how does the performance of PEARL compare to other permutation-handling methods, such as those based on demonstration selection or ensembling, in terms of both effectiveness and efficiency? Finally, how does the framework ensure convergence during the adversarial training process between the P-Net and the LLM, and what measures are in place to prevent overfitting? What are the convergence criteria, and how are they determined? These questions aim to address the core uncertainties and limitations identified in the paper and would help to provide a more complete understanding of the PEARL framework.
