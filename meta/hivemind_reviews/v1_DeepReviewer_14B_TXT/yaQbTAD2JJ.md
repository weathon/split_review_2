## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to 3D visual understanding by developing a large-scale language-image pretraining dataset, termed LV3D, that combines existing 2D and 3D recognition datasets under a unified multi-turn question-answering framework. The authors then leverage this dataset to train a multi-modal large language model (MLLM) named Cube-LLM, which demonstrates strong 3D perception capabilities without relying on specialized 3D architectural designs or training objectives. The core idea is to treat 3D understanding as a language modeling problem, where the model learns to reason about 3D spatial relationships through language. The LV3D dataset is constructed by projecting 3D bounding boxes onto 2D images, allowing the model to learn from both 2D and 3D annotations simultaneously. This approach enables the model to leverage the vast amount of existing 2D data while still acquiring 3D reasoning abilities. The Cube-LLM model is trained using a next-token prediction task on the LV3D dataset, which encourages the model to learn to predict 3D properties from the given context. The authors demonstrate that Cube-LLM exhibits several intriguing properties, such as the ability to perform chain-of-thought prompting, follow complex instructions, and adapt to diverse input and output formats. Furthermore, they show that Cube-LLM can be visually prompted with 2D bounding boxes or candidate 3D boxes from specialist models. The experimental results show that Cube-LLM significantly outperforms existing baselines on 3D grounded reasoning and complex reasoning tasks in driving scenarios. Specifically, the model achieves substantial improvements on the Ttc dataset for 3D grounded reasoning and the dLM dataset for complex reasoning about driving scenarios. The paper also demonstrates that Cube-LLM achieves state-of-the-art performance on 2D referring expression comprehension tasks. Overall, this work presents a compelling approach to 3D understanding by leveraging the power of large language models and a carefully curated multi-modal dataset. The authors show that data scaling plays a crucial role in achieving strong 3D perception capabilities, and that a unified training framework can be effective for both 2D and 3D tasks. This work has the potential to significantly advance the field of 3D visual understanding and open up new avenues for research in multi-modal learning.

Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a wide audience. ## Weaknesses

While the authors mention the potential for scaling up, they do not delve into the specific challenges that might arise when dealing with significantly larger datasets or more complex 3D scenes. For instance, the paper does not discuss how the model's performance might degrade with increased scene complexity, such as more cluttered environments or a higher density of objects. Furthermore, the paper does not address the potential for overfitting to the specific types of 3D scenes present in the training data, and how this might affect generalization to unseen environments. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed approach in real-world scenarios. ## Suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the limitations of the proposed approach, particularly regarding scalability. This analysis should include experiments on datasets with varying levels of scene complexity, such as more cluttered environments or a higher density of objects. The authors should also explore the potential for overfitting to the specific types of 3D scenes present in the training data and discuss strategies for mitigating this issue. This could involve data augmentation techniques or more efficient training algorithms. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of data quality and biases on the performance of Cube-LLM, especially when dealing with real-world datasets. Second, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art methods for 3D visual understanding. This comparison should not only focus on overall performance but also on specific aspects such as the ability to handle occlusions, variations in viewpoint, and the accuracy of 3D bounding box predictions. It would be particularly valuable to compare the performance of Cube-LLM with other MLLMs that explicitly model 3D geometry using point clouds or depth maps. This comparison should also include an analysis of the computational cost and memory requirements of Cube-LLM compared to other MLLMs. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed approach. This analysis should include specific details on training time, memory usage, and a breakdown of the computational cost of different components of the model, such as the visual encoder, the language model, and the 3D projection layer. The authors should also discuss the potential for optimizing the model for efficiency, such as through model compression or quantization techniques. Fourth, to strengthen the claims regarding Cube-LLM's LLM-like properties, the authors should conduct more rigorous evaluations. For the chain-of-thought prompting, they should compare the model's performance with and without this prompting strategy on 3D understanding tasks, using metrics that specifically measure the quality of the reasoning process. For example, they could use metrics that evaluate the correctness of intermediate steps in the reasoning chain. To support the claim about following complex instructions, the authors should provide examples of diverse input/output formats that the model can handle, along with quantitative metrics that measure the model's accuracy on these tasks. For the visual prompting claim, the authors should conduct experiments that compare the model's performance with and without visual prompts, such as 2D boxes or 3D candidate boxes, and report the performance gains achieved by using these prompts. These experiments should be conducted on a variety of datasets to ensure the robustness of the results. Fifth, to validate the claim about the impact of data scaling, the authors should perform ablation studies that systematically vary the amount of training data and analyze the resulting performance changes. This would help to isolate the effect of data scaling from other factors. The authors should also provide a detailed analysis of the model's performance on indoor and outdoor scenes separately, using metrics that are appropriate for each setting. This analysis should include a comparison of the model's performance on different types of indoor and outdoor scenes to identify any potential biases or limitations. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different data compositions on the model's performance, such as the proportion of 2D and 3D data, and the diversity of the data. Finally, to support the claim about qualitative results in 3D grounding, the authors should provide a more comprehensive set of qualitative examples, including both successful and failure cases. These examples should cover a wide range of non-driving scenes, such as indoor scenes with different object arrangements and outdoor scenes with varying lighting conditions. The authors should also provide a detailed analysis of the failure cases, explaining the reasons for the model's errors. This would help to identify the limitations of the model and guide future research directions.
