## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to explaining Natural Language Processing (NLP) model predictions by generating counterfactuals (CFs) guided by causal graphs. The core idea is to leverage the causal structure of the problem to create more accurate and faithful explanations. The authors propose two main methods for generating CFs: one that uses Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate CFs directly based on interventions on the causal graph, and another that learns a causal representation space where matching techniques can be used to identify CFs. The LLM-based approach involves prompting an LLM to modify specific concepts in a text while keeping other related concepts fixed, based on the causal graph. The causal representation learning approach, on the other hand, fine-tunes an encoder to minimize a contrastive loss, aiming to create a space where CFs are close to the original text and misspecified examples are far away. The paper also introduces a new benchmark dataset, CEBaB, for evaluating explanation methods, and demonstrates that the proposed methods outperform existing baselines on this benchmark. The authors further introduce the concept of 'order-faithfulness,' a criterion for evaluating the faithfulness of explanations, and show that their proposed methods satisfy this criterion. The empirical results show that the proposed methods, particularly the LLM-based CF generation and the causal representation learning for matching, achieve better performance in explaining model predictions compared to existing methods. The paper's main contribution lies in its novel use of causal graphs to guide the generation of CFs for explaining NLP models, and the introduction of a new benchmark dataset for evaluating such methods. The authors also provide a theoretical framework for evaluating the faithfulness of explanations based on the concept of order-faithfulness. Overall, this work presents a significant step towards more reliable and causally grounded explanations of NLP model behavior.

This approach allows for a more structured and theoretically grounded way of creating explanations, moving beyond simple perturbations of input text. The paper's introduction of the 'order-faithfulness' criterion is another significant contribution, providing a clear and measurable way to assess the faithfulness of explanations. The authors convincingly demonstrate that their proposed methods satisfy this criterion, which strengthens the validity of their approach. Furthermore, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors show that their methods, particularly the LLM-based CF generation and the causal representation learning for matching, outperform existing baselines on the CEBaB benchmark. This provides strong evidence for the effectiveness of their approach. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain their methods and provide sufficient detail for replication. Finally, the introduction of the CEBaB dataset is a valuable contribution to the field, providing a much-needed benchmark for evaluating explanation methods in NLP. The paper's focus on model-agnostic explanations is also a strength, as it allows for the explanation of various NLP models without requiring access to their internal workings.
