## summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for addressing signal delay in deep reinforcement learning (DRL), a critical challenge often overlooked in the field. The authors formalize the problem using delayed-observation Markov decision processes (DOMDPs), extending the standard MDP framework to explicitly incorporate signal delays. They demonstrate that traditional DRL algorithms and generic methods for partially observable tasks struggle significantly when faced with delayed feedback. To overcome these challenges, the authors propose a suite of strategies, including delay-reconciled training for critics, state augmentation for actors, and complementary techniques like prediction and encoding. These methods aim to improve the learning process and performance of DRL agents in environments with delayed observations. The core idea behind delay-reconciled training is to adjust the critic's training process to account for the delay, ensuring that the critic's value estimates are more accurate despite the delayed feedback. State augmentation, on the other hand, involves enriching the actor's state representation with historical information, allowing it to make more informed decisions based on past observations. The complementary techniques, such as prediction and encoding, further enhance the actor's ability to handle delayed signals by predicting future states or encoding the delayed observations. The authors evaluate their proposed framework on a series of continuous robotic control tasks using the MuJoCo simulator, demonstrating that their methods achieve remarkable performance, even with large delays, often approaching the performance of DRL agents trained without delays. The experimental results show that the proposed techniques significantly outperform traditional DRL algorithms and other methods designed for partially observable environments when dealing with delayed signals. The paper's contributions are significant because they address a practical problem that is often encountered in real-world applications of DRL, such as robotics and autonomous systems. By formalizing the problem of signal delay and proposing effective strategies to mitigate its impact, the authors provide a valuable contribution to the field of DRL. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the proposed methods and experimental setup. The authors also provide a theoretical analysis of their methods, showing that they are guaranteed to converge under certain conditions. Overall, this paper presents a significant advancement in the field of DRL, offering a practical and effective solution to the problem of signal delay. The proposed framework has the potential to improve the performance of DRL agents in a wide range of real-world applications, making it a valuable contribution to the field.

This formalization is a crucial step in developing effective solutions for handling delayed feedback. Furthermore, the paper proposes a novel model architecture designed to overcome the challenges associated with signal delays. The proposed framework, which includes delay-reconciled training for critics and state augmentation for actors, is a significant technical innovation. The delay-reconciled training method adjusts the critic's training process to account for the delay, leading to more accurate value estimates. The state augmentation technique enriches the actor's state representation with historical information, enabling it to make more informed decisions. These techniques are not only innovative but also practically effective, as demonstrated by the experimental results. The paper is also well-structured and easy to follow. The authors clearly define the problem of signal delay in DRL and provide a detailed explanation of the proposed methods. The experimental results are presented in a clear and concise manner, and the figures and tables are well-designed and informative. This clarity in presentation makes the paper accessible to a wider audience and facilitates a deeper understanding of the proposed methods. The experimental results are another significant strength of the paper. The authors demonstrate that their proposed methods achieve remarkable performance in continuous robotic control tasks with large delays, often yielding results comparable to those in non-delayed cases. This is a significant achievement, as it shows that the proposed framework can effectively mitigate the negative impact of signal delays. The paper also compares its methods against several baselines, including popular RL algorithms for continuous control and DRL algorithms for POMDPs, as well as DATS, a model-based algorithm for DRL with delays. The comparison shows that the proposed methods outperform these baselines, further highlighting their effectiveness. Finally, the paper addresses an important issue in DRL that has been overlooked in previous studies. Signal delay is a common problem in many real-world applications, and the proposed methods can be applied to a wide range of tasks. The paper's contributions have the potential to advance the field of DRL and improve the performance of DRL-based solutions in practical scenarios. ## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, there are several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One of the most significant limitations is the paper's reliance on simulated robotic control environments for evaluation. As the authors themselves acknowledge in the conclusion, their research is primarily focused on simulated environments, which may not fully capture the complexities and uncertainties present in real-world applications. The use of MuJoCo environments, while standard in the field, often lacks the nuances of sensor noise, imperfect actuation, and dynamic environmental conditions that are inherent in real-world robotic systems. This discrepancy between simulated and real-world environments raises concerns about the practical applicability of the proposed method. The paper explicitly states the use of MuJoCo environments for experiments, mentioning specific environments like Ant-v4, Walker2d-v4, Hopper-v4, and HalfCheetah-v4. This limitation is further acknowledged in the conclusion, where the authors state, "Our research has certain limitations due to its focus on simulated robotic control environments. Dealing with signal delay in real-world applications could potentially encounter greater levels of uncertainty and additional challenges." This reliance on simulation is a significant weakness because it limits the generalizability of the results and raises questions about how the proposed methods would perform in more complex and unpredictable real-world scenarios. Another critical weakness is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed methods. The paper does not provide a thorough discussion of the time and space complexity of the delay-reconciled training for critics, state augmentation for actors, or the complementary techniques. This omission is significant because understanding the computational cost of these methods is crucial for assessing their scalability and practicality, especially when applied to large-scale problems. The paper describes the components of the method and their implementation details, but there is no explicit section or discussion dedicated to the computational complexity. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to evaluate the trade-offs between performance and computational cost, and it raises concerns about the feasibility of deploying these methods in resource-constrained environments. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed methods. While the conclusion briefly mentions limitations related to simulated environments, it lacks a comprehensive discussion of other potential challenges and limitations. For example, the paper does not address the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter settings, the potential for instability during training, or the limitations of the method when dealing with highly stochastic environments. This lack of discussion is a significant weakness because it provides an incomplete picture of the method's capabilities and its potential for future development. The paper does not include a dedicated section discussing the sensitivity to hyperparameters, training instability, or performance in highly stochastic environments. This omission is problematic because it leaves open questions about the robustness and reliability of the proposed methods. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion of the practical challenges and considerations when deploying the proposed methods in real-world scenarios. The paper does not discuss the sensitivity of the proposed methods to hyperparameter settings and the potential for overfitting to the training environment. Additionally, the paper does not address the challenges of adapting the proposed methods to different robotic platforms and tasks, and the potential for domain adaptation techniques to improve the generalizability of the results. The paper also does not discuss the robustness of the proposed methods to noisy sensor data and unexpected environmental changes. This lack of discussion is a significant weakness because it limits the practical relevance of the paper and raises questions about the feasibility of deploying these methods in real-world applications. The paper does not include a detailed discussion on hyperparameter sensitivity, overfitting, adaptation to different platforms/tasks, or robustness to noise. These omissions are problematic because they leave open questions about the practical applicability of the proposed methods. In summary, the paper's weaknesses stem from its limited evaluation in simulated environments, lack of computational complexity analysis, and insufficient discussion of the method's limitations and practical challenges. These weaknesses significantly impact the generalizability and practical relevance of the proposed framework.
