## summary
This paper introduces Strategist, a novel framework that integrates the strengths of large language models (LLMs) and traditional planning methods, specifically Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), to enhance decision-making in complex, multi-agent environments. The core idea behind Strategist is to leverage LLMs for the generation of high-level strategic abstractions, which are then refined and executed using MCTS. This approach is designed to operate without extensive prior training data, relying instead on self-play and population-based methods for self-improvement. The authors evaluate Strategist in two challenging games: the Game of Pure Strategy (GOPS), a two-player card game, and Avalon, a multi-agent social deduction game with hidden identities. The results demonstrate that Strategist outperforms traditional reinforcement learning (RL) approaches, other LLM-based skill acquisition techniques, and even human players in certain aspects, particularly in strategic randomization and policy refinement. The framework's ability to learn and improve strategies through self-play simulations without requiring prior training data is a significant contribution. The paper presents a bi-level approach where the LLM generates high-level strategies, and MCTS refines these strategies into executable policies. The authors also compare their method with a range of baselines, including RL-based methods and other LLM agents, and conduct human evaluations to demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach. The paper highlights the potential of combining LLMs with traditional planning methods for complex decision-making tasks, particularly in adversarial multi-agent settings. The authors emphasize that their method does not require extensive training of a value network, and that the LLM is used in a distinct manner, not as a replacement for a value function but as a generator of high-level strategies. The paper also includes a detailed analysis of the computational efficiency of the proposed method, showing that it is more computationally efficient than the deep RL baselines. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to integrating LLMs with traditional planning methods, demonstrating its effectiveness in complex games and highlighting its potential for broader applications.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative combination of large language models (LLMs) with a bi-level tree search framework for strategy learning in adversarial multi-agent environments. This integration is a creative solution to the limitations of LLMs in complex decision-making scenarios, allowing the LLM to generate high-level strategic abstractions that are then refined by a low-level executor, often MCTS. This approach leverages the strengths of both LLMs and traditional planning methods, resulting in a framework that is both powerful and adaptable. The methodology is well-defined, with a clear distinction between high-level strategy learning and low-level policy refinement. The use of population-based self-play and a modular improvement method for high-level strategies is a significant contribution, allowing the framework to learn and improve without extensive prior training data. The experimental results are compelling, demonstrating that Strategist outperforms existing LLM-based self-improvement methods and traditional RL approaches in both GOPS and Avalon. The framework's ability to match human performance in Avalon while employing sophisticated mixed strategies is particularly noteworthy. The paper includes a comprehensive evaluation, comparing Strategist against various baselines, including RL-based methods and other LLM agents. The results are supported by detailed analysis and visualizations, enhancing the credibility of the findings. The authors also demonstrate that their method is more computationally efficient than the deep RL baselines, which is a significant advantage. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methodology and experimental setup. The authors have successfully demonstrated the potential of leveraging LLMs for generating high-level strategic abstractions, while using low-level tree search to iteratively refine the policy. The framework's ability to learn and improve strategies through self-play simulations without requiring prior training data is a significant contribution. The paper also includes a detailed analysis of the computational efficiency of the proposed method, showing that it is more computationally efficient than the deep RL baselines. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to integrating LLMs with traditional planning methods, demonstrating its effectiveness in complex games and highlighting its potential for broader applications.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling approach, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the novelty of the proposed method is not as clear as it could be. The paper describes a high-level strategy generation by the LLM, followed by low-level refinement using MCTS. This is conceptually similar to the approach taken by AlphaGo, where a value network guides MCTS. While the paper mentions AlphaGo in the related work, it does not explicitly detail the differences in how the LLM is used compared to the value network in AlphaGo. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to assess the true novelty of the approach. The paper states that the LLM generates high-level strategies, which are then refined by MCTS. This is similar to how AlphaGo uses a value network to guide MCTS. The paper should make this distinction clearer. Second, the presentation of the paper, particularly in Section 2, is not as clear as it could be. Section 2 introduces the notation and defines the environment with general terms, without immediately grounding it in a concrete example. The roles of different agents, their action spaces, and the transition function are not clearly delineated, making it difficult to grasp the practical implications of the proposed framework. The paper should make it clearer that they are focusing on multi-agent environments and provide a detailed example of the environment (e.g., Avalon) when introducing the notation. The current description lacks clarity on how the agents interact within the defined environment, and the notation is not sufficiently grounded in a concrete example. Specifically, the roles of different agents, their action spaces, and the transition function are not clearly delineated, making it difficult to grasp the practical implications of the proposed framework. Third, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of Strategist, particularly in scenarios with highly dynamic or unpredictable environments. While the framework shows promise in the tested games, its performance in real-world applications with more complex and less structured environments remains unclear. The reliance on self-play for strategy generation might lead to a lack of robustness when faced with novel strategies or unexpected behaviors from opponents not encountered during training. The paper should also address the potential for the framework to converge to suboptimal strategies due to the limited exploration of the strategy space during self-play. The paper focuses on the effectiveness of Strategist in GOPS and Avalon, but it does not discuss the limitations related to dynamic or unpredictable environments or the potential for converging to suboptimal strategies due to limited exploration. Fourth, the computational cost of Strategist, especially the bi-level tree search and the iterative self-improvement process, is not thoroughly analyzed. The paper lacks a detailed breakdown of the time complexity of the high-level strategy search and the low-level policy refinement, making it difficult to assess the feasibility of deploying Strategist in resource-constrained environments. Furthermore, the memory requirements for storing the population of strategies and the computational overhead of the LLM-based strategy generation should be discussed. While the paper compares Strategist to traditional RL methods, it does not provide a detailed analysis of its computational efficiency. The paper mentions controlling for the number of simulated episodes, but it doesn't provide a detailed breakdown of the computational resources required. Fifth, the generalizability of Strategist is limited by the fact that it is tested primarily on two games, GOPS and Avalon. While these games provide a good starting point, they may not fully represent the diversity of real-world decision-making scenarios. It would be beneficial to evaluate the framework in more varied environments, including those with continuous action spaces, stochastic transitions, and more complex state representations, to better assess its generalizability and robustness. The current evaluation is limited to games with perfect information, and it is unclear how the framework would perform in games with partial observability or stochastic transitions. The paper should also consider the impact of the number of agents on the performance of Strategist, as the computational cost and the complexity of the strategy space are likely to increase significantly with more agents. Finally, the paper does not address how Strategist handles ethical considerations, particularly in adversarial settings where deception and manipulation might be advantageous. It would be beneficial to discuss the ethical implications of using such a framework in real-world applications and how these concerns could be mitigated. The paper focuses on the technical aspects of the framework and its performance in games, but it does not address the potential for Strategist to be used for manipulative or unethical purposes. The lack of discussion on ethical implications is a significant oversight.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, several improvements can be made. First, the authors should more clearly articulate the specific differences between their approach and existing methods like AlphaGo. While AlphaGo uses a value network trained by deep RL to guide MCTS, the authors use an LLM to generate strategies which are then refined by MCTS. The authors should emphasize that their method does not require extensive training of a value network, and that the LLM is used in a distinct manner, not as a replacement for a value function but as a generator of high-level strategies. This distinction should be made explicit in the introduction and methodology sections. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of their approach compared to deep RL methods, including the number of LLM calls and the depth of the MCTS search. This would help to clarify the practical advantages of their method. Second, to improve the presentation of Section 2, the authors should begin by explicitly stating that they are focusing on multi-agent environments. They should then provide a detailed example of the environment, such as Avalon, when introducing the notation. This example should include a clear description of the roles of different agents, their action spaces, and the transition function. The authors should also provide a concrete example of how the LLM generates high-level strategies for a specific scenario in Avalon, and how these strategies are then refined by MCTS. This would help to ground the notation in a practical example and make the section more accessible to readers. The authors should also consider using diagrams or flowcharts to illustrate the interaction between the LLM and MCTS, as well as the flow of information within the environment. Third, to address the limitations regarding dynamic environments, the authors should consider incorporating mechanisms that allow Strategist to adapt to novel situations. This could involve introducing a form of online learning or meta-learning that enables the framework to adjust its strategies based on the observed behavior of opponents. For instance, the framework could be enhanced with a module that detects deviations from expected opponent behavior and triggers a re-evaluation of the current strategy. Additionally, the authors could explore the use of adversarial training techniques to generate more diverse and challenging opponent strategies during self-play, which would improve the robustness of the learned policies. The paper should also include a more detailed analysis of the exploration-exploitation trade-off in the high-level strategy search, and discuss how the framework balances the need to explore new strategies with the need to refine existing ones. Fourth, to improve the analysis of computational cost, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the time and space complexity of each component of Strategist. This should include an analysis of the number of LLM calls, the depth of the MCTS search, and the size of the strategy population. The paper should also discuss the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the computational cost and the performance of the framework. Furthermore, the authors could explore techniques for optimizing the computational efficiency of Strategist, such as using more efficient search algorithms or reducing the size of the strategy population. The paper should also include a discussion of the practical limitations of the framework in terms of the number of agents and the complexity of the environment, and suggest potential solutions for scaling the framework to more complex scenarios. Fifth, to enhance the generalizability of Strategist, the authors should evaluate its performance in a wider range of adversarial multi-agent environments, including games with partial observability, stochastic transitions, and varying numbers of agents. This could involve testing the framework in benchmark environments from the multi-agent reinforcement learning literature. The paper should also discuss the challenges of applying Strategist to real-world problems, such as the need for domain-specific knowledge and the difficulty of defining appropriate reward functions. The authors could also explore the use of transfer learning techniques to adapt the framework to new environments, which would reduce the need for extensive training from scratch. Finally, the paper should address the ethical implications of using Strategist in real-world applications. The authors should discuss the potential for the framework to be used in malicious ways, such as in adversarial settings where deception and manipulation might be advantageous. The authors should discuss the ethical implications of using such a framework in real-world applications and how these concerns could be mitigated. This could involve incorporating ethical guidelines into the training process or developing mechanisms to detect and prevent malicious behavior. A thorough discussion of these issues would enhance the paper's overall impact and relevance.
