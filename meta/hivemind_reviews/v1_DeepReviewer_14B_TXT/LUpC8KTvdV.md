## Summary
This paper introduces MaskTAS, a novel self-supervised neural architecture search (NAS) method specifically designed for vision transformers. The core idea behind MaskTAS is to leverage masked image modeling (MIM) to guide the search process, thereby eliminating the need for expensive labeled data typically required by supervised NAS methods. The method employs a teacher-student architecture, where a pre-trained teacher network provides supervision for the student network, which is a weight-sharing supernet encompassing various candidate architectures. The student network is trained to reconstruct masked images and match the feature representations of the teacher network. This is achieved through a combination of a reconstruction loss and a knowledge distillation loss. The architecture search is performed using an evolutionary algorithm, where candidate architectures are evaluated based on their consistency with the teacher network's feature representations, an unsupervised metric. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of MaskTAS through experiments on several image classification datasets, including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, PETS, Flowers, and ImageNet, as well as on semantic segmentation using ADE20K. The results indicate that the architectures discovered by MaskTAS achieve competitive performance compared to manually designed architectures and other NAS methods, without relying on labeled data for the search process. The paper highlights the potential of self-supervised learning for efficient and cost-effective neural architecture search, particularly for vision transformers. The authors emphasize the efficiency of their method, claiming it converges faster than AutoFormer, a supervised NAS method. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to self-supervised NAS, but it also reveals several areas that require further investigation and refinement to solidify its contributions and practical applicability.

## Strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. The core strength lies in the paper's exploration of self-supervised learning for neural architecture search, a direction that holds significant promise for reducing the reliance on labeled data in NAS. The use of masked image modeling (MIM) as the guiding principle for the search process is a clever and effective approach. By leveraging the pre-trained teacher network, the authors successfully address the challenge of providing a strong supervisory signal for the student network, which is crucial for effective self-supervised learning. The teacher-student framework, coupled with knowledge distillation, allows for efficient training of the supernet, which is a significant advantage. The proposed unsupervised evaluation metric, based on the consistency of feature representations with the teacher network, is a novel contribution that enables the evolutionary search algorithm to operate without labeled data. The experimental results, while not without limitations, demonstrate the potential of MaskTAS to discover competitive architectures across various image classification datasets and a semantic segmentation task. The authors' claim of faster convergence compared to AutoFormer, a supervised NAS method, is also a notable achievement, suggesting the efficiency of their approach. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, which facilitates understanding of the proposed method. The combination of MIM, teacher-student distillation, and unsupervised evaluation metric, while not entirely novel in isolation, is effectively integrated into a coherent framework for self-supervised transformer architecture search. The paper's focus on vision transformers is also timely and relevant, given the increasing importance of these architectures in computer vision. The authors have successfully demonstrated the feasibility of using self-supervised learning for NAS in this domain, which is a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A recurring concern is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational cost of MaskTAS. While the paper mentions the parameter size and FLOPs of the searched architectures and compares them with some baselines (Table 1), it does not provide a comprehensive breakdown of the computational demands of the method. Specifically, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the training time, GPU hours, and FLOPs for each stage of the method, including the teacher pre-training, student training, and architecture search phases. This omission makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method and compare it with existing NAS techniques. The paper claims that MaskTAS converges faster than AutoFormer, but a more detailed comparison of training time and computational resources is needed to substantiate this claim. The absence of such analysis undermines the paper's claim of efficiency. Furthermore, the paper does not adequately address the sensitivity of the method to various hyperparameters and design choices. While the authors include an ablation study on the masking ratio (Section 3.3), they do not explore the impact of other crucial hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, batch size, and the choice of teacher network architecture. The performance of self-supervised learning methods is often highly dependent on these parameters, and a thorough analysis of their impact is necessary to ensure the robustness of the method. The paper mentions using pre-trained models from the official MAE implementations as the teacher model (Section 3.1), but it does not investigate the effect of different teacher network architectures or pre-training strategies on the final performance. This lack of analysis raises concerns about the generalizability of the method and its dependence on specific hyperparameter settings. The paper's technical contribution, while novel in its combination of techniques, can be viewed as incremental. The core components of MaskTAS, such as masked image modeling (MIM) and knowledge distillation, are established techniques. The paper combines these techniques for self-supervised transformer architecture search, but the individual components are not novel. The paper acknowledges using MIM and knowledge distillation, but the novelty lies in the specific combination and the proposed unsupervised metric. However, the paper does not clearly articulate the specific challenges in applying MIM to supernet training and how the proposed method addresses these challenges. The paper also does not provide a detailed comparison with other existing methods that use MIM for training, and a discussion of why those methods are not directly applicable to NAS. The experimental evaluation, while covering several datasets, is limited in scope. The paper primarily focuses on image classification tasks and includes one semantic segmentation task (ADE20K). However, it lacks evaluations on other downstream tasks, such as object detection or tasks on different data modalities. This limited evaluation makes it difficult to assess the generalizability of the method and its applicability to real-world problems. The paper also lacks comparisons with state-of-the-art transformer architectures, such as ConvNeXt and Swin Transformer. While the paper compares against several baselines, including supervised and NAS methods, the absence of comparisons with these prominent architectures weakens the claim of state-of-the-art performance. The paper provides results for CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, PETS, Flowers, and ImageNet, but it does not present them in a comparative table with other methods for all of these datasets. This lack of comprehensive comparative tables makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of MaskTAS across different datasets. The ablation studies are also weak, as they only explore the masking ratio and training efficiency. The paper does not include ablation studies on the impact of the teacher model or the effectiveness of the proposed unsupervised evaluation metric. The choice of teacher model can significantly impact the performance of the student model, and a thorough analysis of this aspect is necessary. Additionally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the proposed unsupervised evaluation metric, demonstrating its effectiveness in selecting optimal architectures. The absence of these ablation studies makes it difficult to understand the contribution of each component of the method and its sensitivity to different design choices. Finally, the paper does not adequately address the potential limitations of the proposed method. The paper does not discuss the applicability of MaskTAS to different types of data, such as medical images or satellite imagery. The authors also do not discuss the potential for bias in the searched architectures and how to mitigate it. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the proposed unsupervised evaluation metric and how it might affect the quality of the searched architectures. This lack of discussion leaves several open questions about the practical limitations of the method and its potential for real-world applications.

## Suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the paper should include a more thorough analysis of the computational demands of MaskTAS. Specifically, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the FLOPs, memory usage, and training time for each stage of the method, including the teacher pre-training, student training, and architecture search phases. This analysis should be compared against existing supervised and self-supervised NAS methods to provide a clear understanding of the computational trade-offs. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the scalability of MaskTAS with respect to the size of the search space and the number of candidate architectures. This would help to determine the practical limitations of the approach and identify potential bottlenecks. Second, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive hyperparameter study. This should include a systematic exploration of the impact of different masking ratios, learning rates, and batch sizes on the performance of the searched architectures. The authors should also investigate the effect of different teacher network architectures and pre-training strategies on the final performance. It would be beneficial to provide a sensitivity analysis that shows how the performance of MaskTAS varies with different hyperparameter settings. This analysis should also include a discussion of the optimal hyperparameter ranges for different datasets and tasks. The authors should also consider using techniques like Bayesian optimization to automatically tune the hyperparameters of the method. Third, the paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This should include a discussion of the potential challenges in applying MaskTAS to different types of data, such as medical images or satellite imagery. The authors should also discuss the potential impact of the choice of the teacher network on the performance of the searched architectures. It would be beneficial to provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence properties of the proposed method and to discuss the potential for instability during the training process. The authors should also consider the potential for bias in the searched architectures and discuss how this bias can be mitigated. Fourth, the paper should expand the experimental evaluation to include a wider range of downstream tasks, such as object detection and tasks on different data modalities. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's generalization capabilities. The authors should also include comparisons with state-of-the-art transformer architectures, such as ConvNeXt and Swin Transformer, to establish the competitiveness of the proposed method. The paper should also include comprehensive comparative tables for all evaluated datasets, not just ImageNet. Fifth, the ablation studies should be significantly strengthened. The authors should conduct ablation studies on the impact of the teacher model, exploring different architectures and pre-training strategies. They should also provide a more detailed analysis of the proposed unsupervised evaluation metric, demonstrating its effectiveness in selecting optimal architectures. The ablation studies should also include an analysis of the sensitivity of the method to different hyperparameter settings. Finally, the paper should more clearly articulate the specific novelty in the proposed method beyond the combination of existing techniques. The authors should discuss the specific challenges in applying MIM to supernet training and how the proposed method addresses these challenges. A more detailed comparison with other existing methods that use MIM for training, and a discussion of why those methods are not directly applicable to NAS, would be beneficial.

## Questions
Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe are crucial for a deeper understanding of the proposed method. First, how does the computational cost of MaskTAS compare to existing supervised and self-supervised NAS methods, specifically in terms of training time, GPU hours, and FLOPs for each stage of the method? Second, how sensitive is the performance of the searched architectures to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the masking ratio, learning rate, and batch size, and what are the optimal hyperparameter ranges for different datasets and tasks? Third, what is the impact of different teacher network architectures and pre-training strategies on the performance of the student network and the overall search process, and what are the trade-offs between using a larger, more accurate teacher network and a smaller, more efficient one? Fourth, how does the proposed unsupervised evaluation metric compare to other potential unsupervised metrics, and what are its limitations and potential biases? Fifth, how does the performance of MaskTAS generalize to other downstream tasks, such as object detection and tasks on different data modalities, and what are the potential challenges in applying the method to these tasks? Sixth, what are the specific challenges in applying MIM to supernet training, and how does the proposed method address these challenges, and how does it compare to other existing methods that use MIM for training? Finally, what are the theoretical convergence properties of the proposed method, and what are the potential instabilities during the training process, and how can these instabilities be mitigated?
