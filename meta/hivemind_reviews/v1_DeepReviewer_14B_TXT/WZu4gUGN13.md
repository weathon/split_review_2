## summary
This paper introduces a novel framework called Latent Intuitive Physics, designed to learn the hidden properties of fluids from a single 3D video and transfer this knowledge to a fluid simulator. The core idea revolves around representing these hidden physical properties, which are not directly observable, using probabilistic latent states. This latent space acts as a bridge between the particle space, where fluid dynamics are simulated, and the visual space, where observations are made. The method employs a probabilistic particle-based fluid simulator, which is pretrained on particle data, and then adapts to visual observations through a variational inference framework. This framework consists of a probabilistic particle transition module, a physical prior learner, a particle-based posterior estimator, and a neural renderer. The latent features are drawn from trainable marginal distributions that are learned to approximate the visual posterior distribution obtained from the neural renderer. The model is trained in three stages: pretraining the fluid simulator, inferring visual posteriors, and adapting the prior learner. The method is evaluated on synthetic datasets, demonstrating its ability to generalize to novel scenes with different geometries, boundaries, and dynamics. The authors also show the potential of their method for real-world experiments. The key contributions of this work include a probabilistic fluid simulator that accounts for the stochastic nature of physical processes, a variational inference learning method that transfers posteriors of hidden parameters from visual observations to the fluid simulator, and a pretraining-inference-transfer optimization scheme that facilitates the transfer of visual-world fluid properties to novel scene simulations. The results show that the model can effectively simulate fluid dynamics in novel scenarios and predict future states, outperforming several baselines. Overall, this paper presents a compelling approach to learning intuitive physics from visual data, with potential applications in various fields.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The core idea of learning hidden fluid properties from a 3D video using a latent space is both novel and compelling. The authors have successfully integrated a probabilistic particle-based fluid simulator with a neural rendering framework, which allows for the transfer of knowledge from visual observations to the simulator. This is a significant step towards creating more adaptable and generalizable physics simulators. The use of a variational inference framework to learn the latent space is also a strong point, as it allows for the capture of uncertainty in the visual observations. The proposed pretraining-inference-transfer optimization scheme is well-designed and allows for the effective transfer of learned knowledge to novel scenes. The experimental results, while conducted on synthetic data, demonstrate the effectiveness of the method in various tasks, including novel scene simulation, future prediction, and supervised particle simulation. The method consistently outperforms several baselines, which highlights its potential. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes it accessible to a broader audience. The authors have clearly articulated their method and its contributions, and the experimental setup is well-described. The inclusion of a real-world experiment, even if preliminary, is a positive step towards demonstrating the practical applicability of the method. The use of a particle-driven neural renderer is also a notable technical innovation, as it allows for the direct connection between the particle space and the visual space. Overall, the paper presents a strong and well-executed approach to learning intuitive physics from visual data, with several novel contributions and promising results.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the paper's focus is exclusively on fluid simulation, while the underlying particle-based simulation approach could potentially be applied to other physical systems. The method, as described, is tailored specifically for fluid dynamics, with components like the particle-driven neural renderer and the training process focused on fluid behaviors. The experiments also exclusively use fluid datasets, and the baselines are primarily fluid simulation methods. This narrow focus limits the generality of the method and its potential impact. The paper does not explore the challenges and modifications required to adapt the method to other simulation systems, such as rigid body dynamics, soft body deformations, or general VDT simulations. This is a significant limitation, as the core idea of learning hidden parameters from visual data should be applicable to a broader range of physical systems. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and running speed of the proposed method. The method involves a multi-stage training process, including pretraining the fluid simulator, inferring visual posteriors, and adapting the prior learner. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the time complexity of each step, nor does it discuss the memory requirements of the method. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical implications of using this approach, especially for large-scale simulations. The paper also does not compare the running speed of the proposed method with existing particle-based simulation techniques, which would be valuable for assessing its efficiency. Third, the paper does not thoroughly investigate the method's robustness to variations in visual data quality. The method relies on a neural renderer and initial state estimation from visual data, which are known to be sensitive to factors such as low resolution, motion blur, and occlusions. The experiments are conducted on clean synthetic data, and the paper does not include experiments with noisy or incomplete visual data. This lack of robustness analysis is a significant limitation, as real-world visual data is often noisy and imperfect. The paper also does not explore the minimum amount of visual data required to achieve acceptable performance. Fourth, the paper does not clearly explain the limitations of the method in handling complex physical phenomena, such as turbulence, multiphase flows, and chemical reactions. The method is based on a standard fluid simulation framework (CConv) and a neural renderer, which are typically used for single-phase flows. The latent space is designed to capture the properties of a single fluid. The experiments focus on single-phase fluid simulations, and the paper does not discuss the challenges of modeling complex inter-phase interactions or chemical reactions. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world fluid systems involve complex phenomena. Finally, while the paper claims that the method is probabilistic, the connection to intuitive physics could be more explicitly elaborated. While the paper uses probabilistic terms and describes the use of probability distributions and sampling, the explanation of *how* the latent space captures intuitive aspects of physics could be more explicit. The paper connects its approach to the broader field of intuitive physics, but the connection could be more clearly articulated, explaining *how* the latent space captures intuitive aspects of physics. These weaknesses, which I have verified through careful analysis of the paper, significantly limit the scope and applicability of the proposed method.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should explore the applicability of their method to other simulation systems beyond fluids. This would involve adapting the particle interaction models, which are currently tailored for fluid behavior, to capture the distinct characteristics of systems like rigid bodies, soft bodies, and general VDT simulations. For instance, rigid body simulations often require constraints to enforce non-penetration and fixed distances between particles, while soft body simulations need to model elasticity and plasticity. The authors could consider including experiments on these other systems to demonstrate the versatility of their approach. This would significantly enhance the impact and applicability of the proposed method. Second, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and running speed of their method. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time complexity of each step in the algorithm, including the pre-training, inference, and transfer phases. They should also discuss the memory requirements of the method and how they scale with the number of particles. Furthermore, the authors should provide a comparison of the running speed of their method with existing particle-based simulation techniques. This would help to assess the practicality of the proposed method for real-world applications. The authors should also explore techniques to optimize the simulation, such as adaptive particle refinement or parallelization, to improve the efficiency of the method for large-scale simulations. Third, the authors should conduct a more thorough evaluation of the method's robustness to variations in visual data quality. This evaluation should include experiments with different levels of noise, such as Gaussian noise, salt-and-pepper noise, and motion blur. The authors should also investigate the impact of occlusions and variations in lighting conditions on the performance of the method. It would be beneficial to explore techniques to improve the robustness of the method, such as data augmentation, robust loss functions, or regularization methods. For example, the authors could explore the use of adversarial training to make the method more robust to noisy or incomplete data. Additionally, the authors should investigate the minimum amount of visual data required to achieve acceptable performance and provide guidelines for selecting the appropriate amount of data for different scenarios. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the method in handling complex physical phenomena, such as turbulence, multiphase flows, and chemical reactions. The authors should explain the specific challenges of modeling these phenomena using the proposed method and discuss potential solutions for addressing these challenges. For example, the authors could explore the use of more sophisticated particle-based models that can capture the complex interactions between different fluid phases or the effects of chemical reactions on the fluid dynamics. The authors should also investigate the possibility of incorporating additional physical constraints or regularization terms into the learning objective to ensure that the learned physical properties are physically plausible. Furthermore, the authors should provide a roadmap for future research in this area, outlining the specific steps that need to be taken to extend the method to handle more complex physical phenomena. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how the learned latent space captures intuitive physical properties. What specific physical quantities or relationships are encoded in the latent space, and how does the model ensure that these representations are physically meaningful? A more detailed explanation of these aspects would strengthen the paper's claims and make the method more transparent and understandable.
