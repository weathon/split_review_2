## summary
This paper delves into the critical issue of scale sensitivity in score-based structure learning of Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs). The authors convincingly demonstrate that common score functions, such as the mean squared error (MSE) and its variants, are significantly influenced by the scale of the variables, which can lead to the incorrect recovery of the underlying graph structure. This problem is not merely theoretical; it has practical implications for various fields where causal inference and structure learning are essential. The core contribution of the paper is the introduction of the Scale Robust Loss (SRL), a modified loss function designed to mitigate the adverse effects of variable scaling. The SRL achieves this by subtracting the variance of nodes without parents from the original loss, thereby reducing the bias introduced by scale differences. The authors provide a rigorous theoretical analysis, proving that minimizing the MSE can result in the selection of an incorrect graph structure, even in linear Gaussian models. They also extend this analysis to non-linear relationships, showing that the scale sensitivity issue persists in these cases. Empirically, the paper presents results on both synthetic and real-world data, demonstrating that the SRL can recover the true graph structure more accurately than standard loss functions when scale differences are present. The synthetic experiments use chains, forks, and colliders, while the real-world data is manipulated to introduce scale differences in specific substructures. The paper's findings are significant because they highlight a common pitfall in structure learning and offer a practical solution. However, the scope of the empirical evaluation and the theoretical analysis is somewhat limited, focusing primarily on basic graph structures and Gaussian noise. The paper also does not provide a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of the SRL or a comprehensive comparison with other scale-invariant methods. Despite these limitations, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field by raising awareness of the scale sensitivity issue and proposing a novel solution.

## strengths
The paper addresses a crucial and often overlooked issue in the field of structure learning: the sensitivity of score-based methods to the scale of the variables. This is a significant contribution because it highlights a potential pitfall that can lead to incorrect causal inferences, which are essential in many scientific and practical applications. The theoretical analysis is rigorous and well-presented, providing a solid foundation for understanding the problem. The authors prove that minimizing the MSE can result in the selection of an incorrect graph structure, even in linear Gaussian models, and extend this analysis to non-linear relationships. This theoretical work is complemented by empirical validation on both synthetic and real-world data. The synthetic experiments are designed to cover basic graph structures such as chains, forks, and colliders, and the results clearly demonstrate the effectiveness of the SRL in mitigating scale sensitivity. The real-world data experiments, while limited in scope, provide a practical demonstration of the SRL's utility. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the theoretical results and empirical findings. The use of illustrative examples and visualizations helps to convey the key concepts effectively. The introduction of the SRL is a novel contribution, and the authors provide a clear motivation for its development. The SRL is designed to be robust to scale changes by subtracting the variance of nodes without parents from the original loss, which is a practical and intuitive solution to the problem. The paper also discusses the limitations of the SRL, such as its applicability to discrete structure learners, which shows a balanced and honest approach to the research.

## weaknesses
Despite the paper's significant contributions, several limitations and areas for improvement have been identified. One of the primary concerns is the limited scope of the empirical evaluation. The synthetic data experiments focus on basic graph structures such as chains, forks, and colliders, and do not explore a wide range of graph structures, edge densities, or noise levels. This limitation is evident in the description of the experiments, which states, '10,000 data points from 10-variable ground truth DAGs (ch=chain, fo=fork or co=collider) with additive Gaussian noise' (Section 4, Table 1 caption). The real-world data experiments are also limited, using a single dataset from Sachs et al. (2005) and focusing on specific substructures. The paper does not provide a detailed description of how the 'ground truth' graph is obtained for the real-world dataset, which is crucial for reproducibility and understanding the manipulation of the scale-affected variable. The lack of a comprehensive empirical evaluation hinders the paper's ability to fully demonstrate the effectiveness of the SRL in a variety of settings. Another significant limitation is the paper's focus on linear Gaussian models. While the authors mention that their results may extend to other models, the theoretical analysis and empirical evidence are primarily confined to this specific case. The paper states, 'Assuming $(I)$ a distribution p over random variables $X_{1},	ext{...},X_{d}$ which factorizes according to some DAG $	ext{G}$ which is represented by an adjacency W, (2) Gaussian, additive noise $	ext{ε}_{j}$ for each $X_{j}$ in the graph, (3) possibly non-linear transformations $f_{j}$ s.t. $X_{j}=f_{j}(	ext{Pa}_{X_{j}})+	ext{ε}_{j}$ , (4) data $	ext{X}	ext{∈}	ext{R}^{n×d}$ sampled from $p$ and (5) a solution space of DAG adjacency matrices W, then optimizing the MMSE is equivalent to optimizing the log-likelihood' (Proposition 7). However, the exploration of non-linear dependencies is limited to a cosine function, and the paper does not provide a detailed theoretical or empirical analysis of how the SRL performs under different types of non-linear relationships or non-Gaussian noise. This restriction to linear Gaussian models and a single type of non-linearity limits the generalizability of the findings. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of the SRL. The SRL is defined as $	ext{L}_{s}(	ext{X};	ext{W})=	ext{L}(	ext{X};	ext{W})−	ext{∑}_{i∈Z}	ext{Var}(	ext{X}_{i}^{T})$ (Section 3.3), which involves calculating the variance of the columns of X and subtracting it from the original loss. While this operation is computationally inexpensive, the paper does not provide a formal analysis of the time and space complexity of the algorithm or compare it to other structure learning algorithms. This omission is important because the computational cost of an algorithm can be a major factor in its usability, especially for large-scale datasets. The paper's theoretical analysis is primarily focused on three specific structures: chains, forks, and colliders. The authors state, '(A1) Immiscible Structures: $G$ is either single chain, fork or collider with $d$ variables' (Section 2.1). While these structures are fundamental, real-world graphs often consist of a mixture of these and other more complex structures. The paper does not adequately address how the findings extend to these more realistic scenarios, and it is unclear how the scale sensitivity of individual structures translates to the overall graph structure when these components are interconnected. The analysis seems to assume that the effects of scaling are localized to the specific structure being analyzed, which may not hold true in practice. The paper also does not provide a detailed explanation of why condition (A1) is crucial for its theoretical results. The proofs for Propositions 2-6 rely on the specific forms of the MMSE for chains, forks, and colliders, but the underlying mechanisms affecting scale sensitivity might be similar across these structures. A more detailed explanation of the specific mathematical reasons why this decomposition is necessary and how it relates to the underlying mechanisms of scale sensitivity would be beneficial. Finally, the proposed solution of using the SRL seems overly simplistic. The SRL is defined as $	ext{L}_{s}(	ext{X};	ext{W})=	ext{L}(	ext{X};	ext{W})−	ext{∑}_{i∈Z}	ext{Var}(	ext{X}_{i}^{T})$ (Section 3.3), which involves subtracting the variance of nodes without parents from the original loss. While this approach is practical, it does not consider the potential impact of non-linear relationships between variables, which could still lead to scale-dependent behavior even after normalization. The paper should explore alternative methods for addressing scale sensitivity, such as using invariant loss functions or developing more sophisticated normalization techniques. The confidence level in these identified issues is high, as they are well-supported by the content of the paper and the evidence provided in the experimental and theoretical sections.

## suggestions
To address the limitations identified in the paper, several concrete and actionable improvements are recommended. First, the empirical evaluation should be significantly expanded to include a wider range of graph structures, edge densities, and noise levels. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the SRL's effectiveness and robustness. For synthetic data, the authors should consider generating graphs with varying degrees of sparsity and density, as well as different types of noise distributions, such as non-Gaussian noise. This would help to assess the SRL's performance under more realistic conditions. For real-world data, the authors should include a more diverse set of datasets, representing different domains and characteristics. This would help to generalize the findings and demonstrate the practical applicability of the SRL. The authors should also provide a detailed description of how the 'ground truth' graph is obtained for the real-world datasets, or if it is not known, how the expected graph structure is determined. This would improve the reproducibility of the experiments and allow for a better understanding of the manipulation of the scale-affected variable. Second, the paper should extend its theoretical analysis to other types of models beyond linear Gaussian models. While the current analysis is rigorous, it is limited in scope. The authors should investigate the behavior of the SRL under non-linear relationships and non-Gaussian noise. This could involve generating synthetic data with non-linear dependencies and evaluating the performance of the SRL in these settings. The authors could also explore the use of kernel methods or neural networks to model non-linear relationships and assess the performance of the SRL in these more complex scenarios. A theoretical analysis of the SRL's behavior under non-linear conditions would be a valuable contribution, even if it is limited to specific classes of non-linear functions. Third, the paper should include a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of the SRL. This should include an analysis of the time and space complexity of the algorithm, as well as a comparison to the computational cost of other structure learning algorithms. The authors should provide empirical results on the runtime of the SRL on different datasets and graph sizes. This would help to assess the practical feasibility of the SRL and its suitability for different applications. The authors should also discuss any potential optimizations that could be used to improve the computational efficiency of the SRL, such as parallelization or approximation techniques. Fourth, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of why condition (A1) is crucial for its theoretical results. The authors should explain the specific mathematical reasons why the decomposition into chains, forks, and colliders is necessary and how it relates to the underlying mechanisms of scale sensitivity. This explanation should go beyond simply stating that the analysis is broken down into these structures and should discuss how the different orientations of edges in these structures affect the error terms and how this leads to different scale dependencies. The authors should also explore the potential limitations of this decomposition and whether it can be generalized to other types of graph structures. This would help to clarify the scope of the theoretical results and their applicability to different scenarios. Finally, the paper should explore alternative solutions for addressing scale sensitivity beyond the proposed SRL. This could involve investigating invariant loss functions that are inherently robust to scale changes or developing more sophisticated normalization techniques that take into account the specific characteristics of the data. For example, the authors could explore the use of contrastive learning methods, which have been shown to be effective in learning representations that are invariant to certain transformations. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed SRL and under what conditions it might fail to address the scale sensitivity issue. This would help to provide a more complete picture of the problem and potential solutions.
