## summary
This paper introduces SPaR, a novel self-play framework designed to enhance the instruction-following capabilities of large language models (LLMs). The core idea behind SPaR is to use a tree-search algorithm to refine the responses of an LLM, creating more effective preference pairs for training. Unlike traditional methods that rely on independently sampled responses, which can introduce irrelevant variations, SPaR's tree-search approach focuses on refining incorrect responses until a correct one is found, thus minimizing unnecessary variations and highlighting the key differences that contribute to successful instruction following. The framework operates iteratively, with an actor model generating responses and a refiner model critiquing and refining these responses. The refined pairs are then used to train both the actor and refiner models, leading to continuous self-improvement. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of SPaR through extensive experiments, showing that a LLaMA3-8B model trained with SPaR surpasses GPT-4-Turbo on the IFEval benchmark, while maintaining general capabilities and demonstrating promising scalability with larger models like LLaMA3-70B. The paper also explores the impact of inference scaling on model performance, showing that integrating tree-search during inference can further enhance the quality of instruction following. The authors argue that SPaR addresses the limitations of existing methods by minimizing irrelevant variations in preference pairs, thereby focusing on the key differences that contribute to successful instruction following. The experimental results are compelling, showing that SPaR-trained models outperform existing methods, including surpassing GPT-4-Turbo on the IFEval benchmark. The scalability of SPaR to larger models like LLaMA3-70B further demonstrates its potential. The paper is well-structured and clearly explains the methodology, experiments, and results, providing a thorough analysis of the approach and its effectiveness in enhancing instruction-following capabilities. Overall, SPaR presents a significant advancement in the field of LLM alignment, offering a novel and effective approach to improving instruction-following capabilities through self-play and tree-search refinement.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the core idea of using a tree-search algorithm to generate preference pairs for training is both novel and effective. By refining incorrect responses through a structured search process, the authors minimize irrelevant variations and focus on the key aspects of instruction following. This approach contrasts with traditional methods that rely on independently sampled responses, which can introduce noise and make it harder for the model to learn. The iterative nature of the SPaR framework, where an actor model generates responses and a refiner model critiques and refines them, is also a significant strength. This self-play approach allows the model to continuously improve its instruction-following capabilities. The experimental results are compelling, demonstrating that SPaR-trained models outperform existing methods, including surpassing GPT-4-Turbo on the IFEval benchmark. This is a notable achievement, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the scalability of SPaR to larger models like LLaMA3-70B is promising, suggesting that the method can be applied to a wide range of model sizes. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methodology, experiments, and results. The authors provide a thorough analysis of the approach and its effectiveness in enhancing instruction-following capabilities. The use of both breadth-first search (BFS) and depth-first search (DFS) strategies for refinement, while not fully explored in the paper, adds to the novelty of the approach. Finally, the authors' exploration of inference scaling and its impact on model performance is a valuable contribution, showing that integrating tree-search during inference can further enhance the quality of instruction following. These strengths collectively demonstrate the significance and potential of the SPaR framework for improving LLM alignment.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the claim that SPaR "does not damage general abilities" is not sufficiently supported by the current evaluation. While the authors do evaluate general abilities on benchmarks like GSM8k, TriviaQA, MMLU, and HumanEval, the instruction-following evaluation is conducted using IFEval and FollowBench, which might overlap with the training data. As the paper states, "Following this, we perform a three-iteration self-play training using 10k prompts per iteration from our generated dataset." This raises concerns that the model's improvements on IFEval and FollowBench might be due to memorization rather than genuine generalization. The lack of evaluation on a held-out set of prompts that were not seen during training makes it difficult to assess the true generalization capabilities of the model. This is a significant limitation, as it casts doubt on the robustness of the reported improvements. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper does not provide evidence to refute this concern. Second, the paper lacks a thorough analysis of the tree search parameters, specifically the depth of the tree and the number of samples generated per node. The paper mentions using both BFS and DFS, but it does not provide details on how these strategies were implemented or which one was used in the main experiments. As the paper states, "We implement both breadth-first search (BFS) and depth-first search (DFS) strategies for this refinement." The impact of these parameters on the quality of the generated preference data is not explored. A deeper tree might lead to more refined negative samples, but it could also increase the risk of overfitting. Similarly, the number of samples generated per node could affect the computational cost and the diversity of the generated data. The absence of this analysis limits our understanding of the method's sensitivity to these choices and potential optimizations. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper does not provide any experimental results or analysis on these parameters. Third, the paper does not include a detailed analysis of the computational costs associated with the SPaR framework. Given that SPaR involves iterative training and tree-search refinement, it is crucial to understand the computational resources required for each stage of the framework. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the FLOPs required for tree search, refinement, and training, nor does it compare the computational costs of SPaR with other methods. Furthermore, the paper does not quantify the inference time scaling when using tree search during inference, comparing it to standard greedy decoding. This lack of computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility and resource requirements of SPaR. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper does not include any discussion or data on computational costs. Fourth, the paper's evaluation primarily focuses on instruction-following tasks, and it does not include experiments on a broader range of NLP tasks. While the authors demonstrate SPaR's effectiveness on IFEval and FollowBench, it is unclear how well the framework generalizes to other tasks such as text summarization, machine translation, or question answering. The paper does evaluate on some general benchmarks, but a more comprehensive evaluation on a wider range of NLP tasks is needed to demonstrate the generalizability of the proposed framework. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper's experimental section is primarily focused on instruction-following benchmarks. Fifth, the paper lacks a detailed explanation of the tree search algorithm used in SPaR. While the paper mentions using BFS and DFS, it does not provide specific implementation details, such as the criteria used to determine when to stop the tree search and how this stopping criterion affects the quality of the generated preference pairs. A more detailed explanation of the tree search algorithm is needed to understand the inner workings of SPaR and its key design choices. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper provides only a high-level description of the tree search process. Finally, the paper does not include a discussion of the potential ethical implications of using SPaR to improve the capabilities of LLMs, particularly in terms of bias and fairness. This is a significant omission, as the improved capabilities of LLMs could potentially amplify existing biases or introduce new ones. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper does not include any discussion on ethical considerations.

This should include a discussion of the specific implementation details of the BFS and DFS strategies, and how these strategies are used to generate comparable preference pairs. The paper should also discuss the criteria used to determine when to stop the tree search, and how this stopping criterion affects the quality of the generated preference pairs. Finally, the authors should include a discussion of the potential ethical implications of using SPaR to improve the capabilities of LLMs, particularly in terms of bias and fairness.
