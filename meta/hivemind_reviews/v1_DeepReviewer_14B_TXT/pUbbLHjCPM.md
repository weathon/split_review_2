## summary
This paper introduces Progressive Thought Refinement (PTR), a novel framework designed to enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) by enabling them to iteratively refine their responses. The core idea behind PTR is to stimulate the model's intrinsic ability to improve its answers over multiple attempts, moving beyond simple task-specific fine-tuning. The framework operates in two distinct phases. First, a 'Thought data construction' phase leverages a weak-strong model collaborative selection strategy to generate a high-quality dataset for progressive refinement. This involves using a weaker model to produce initial thoughts or reasoning attempts, which are then refined by a stronger model. The key here is that this process doesn't rely on explicit labels, but rather on the stronger model's ability to improve upon the weaker model's output. Second, a 'Thought-Mask Fine-Tuning' phase is employed, where a training structure is designed to mask parts of the 'thought' during training and adjust loss weights to encourage the LLM to refine its prior thoughts. This is intended to teach the model to implicitly understand 'how to improve' rather than just 'what is correct'. The authors claim that PTR significantly improves LLM performance across ten diverse tasks without requiring task-specific fine-tuning. The experimental results, presented in the paper, show an average performance increase from 49.6% to 53.5% across these tasks. The authors emphasize that this improvement is achieved by activating the model's inherent progressive refinement capabilities, thus enhancing its generalization potential. The paper's contribution lies in its novel approach to stimulating intrinsic refinement abilities in LLMs, its efficient data construction strategy, and its creative fine-tuning method. However, as I will detail in the following sections, the paper also has several limitations that need to be addressed to fully validate its claims and establish its significance.

## strengths
The paper presents several notable strengths that warrant recognition. First and foremost, the core concept of Progressive Thought Refinement (PTR) is a novel and promising approach to improving LLM reasoning. The idea of stimulating a model's intrinsic ability to refine its responses, rather than relying solely on task-specific fine-tuning, is a valuable contribution to the field. The authors' attempt to move beyond simple supervised fine-tuning and encourage a more iterative, self-improvement process in LLMs is a significant step forward. The proposed two-stage framework, consisting of thought data construction and thought-mask fine-tuning, is also a strength. The weak-strong model collaborative selection strategy for data construction is an efficient way to generate high-quality training data without the need for explicit labels. This approach leverages the strengths of both weak and strong models to create a dataset that encourages progressive refinement. The thought-mask fine-tuning method, which aims to teach the model to implicitly understand 'how to improve,' is another creative aspect of the paper. By masking parts of the thought process during training and adjusting loss weights, the authors attempt to guide the model towards better reasoning. Furthermore, the paper demonstrates empirical results that suggest the effectiveness of the proposed method. The reported average performance increase across ten diverse tasks, while not uniformly significant across all tasks, does indicate that PTR has the potential to enhance LLM performance. The fact that these improvements were achieved without task-specific fine-tuning is particularly noteworthy, as it suggests that PTR can enhance the generalization capabilities of LLMs. Finally, the paper's attempt to address the limitations of existing methods, which often struggle to generalize across different tasks, is a valuable contribution. By focusing on stimulating the model's intrinsic refinement abilities, the authors aim to create a more robust and adaptable approach to LLM improvement. These strengths, taken together, highlight the potential of PTR as a valuable contribution to the field of LLM research.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of the proposed framework, several weaknesses significantly impact the paper's conclusions and overall validity. One major concern is the lack of clarity regarding the weak and strong model selection process. While the paper mentions using 'Model Parameter Strength,' 'Model Version,' and 'Domain-Specific Fine-Tuning' as selection criteria (Section 3.2.2), it fails to provide specific details about the models used in the experiments. It remains unclear what specific model architectures or sizes constitute 'weak' and 'strong' in this context, and how their relative capabilities impact the quality of the generated data. For example, are 'weak' models significantly smaller or trained on less diverse datasets? This lack of detail makes it difficult to understand the collaborative selection process and its impact on the final results. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper's description of the selection process is vague and lacks concrete examples. Another significant weakness is the lack of a detailed analysis of the impact of the proposed method on different types of tasks and domains. The performance gains of PTR are not consistently significant across all tasks, as shown in Table 1. While some tasks show substantial improvements, others exhibit minimal gains. The paper does not provide a thorough analysis of why the method works well on some tasks but not on others. The lack of task-specific analysis makes it difficult to understand the generalizability of the approach. For example, are there specific task characteristics that make them more amenable to the PTR framework? This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper acknowledges the varying performance gains but does not delve into the reasons behind them. Furthermore, the paper does not address the potential limitations and challenges of the proposed method, such as the computational cost of the two-stage training process and the potential for bias in the generated data. The computational overhead of the iterative refinement process is not quantified, and the paper does not discuss how the method scales with larger models or datasets. The potential for the weak-strong model collaboration to introduce biases or reinforce existing biases in the training data is also not explored. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper completely omits any discussion of these critical limitations. The paper also lacks a comprehensive comparison with existing methods for improving LLMs' reasoning capabilities. While the paper mentions related work, the comparison is not deeply analytical, and it doesn't provide a detailed breakdown of the differences in methodology and performance. The paper does not clearly articulate how the proposed method differs from and improves upon existing approaches, such as self-improvement or other iterative refinement techniques. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper's comparison to existing methods is superficial and lacks a detailed analysis of the advantages and disadvantages of each approach. Additionally, the paper's writing is not always clear, particularly in the abstract and introduction. For example, the abstract does not clearly define what a 'thought' is, nor does it explain why the authors propose to mask the thought, and how to adjust the loss weights. The introduction's claim about domain-specific errors is also not well-explained, and it would benefit from concrete examples. This is a high-confidence concern, as the lack of clarity in these sections makes it difficult for the reader to understand the core concepts of the paper. The paper also lacks sufficient justification for the specific combination of techniques used in PTR. While the individual components might have precedents, the combination and the specific motivation of teaching 'how to improve' are presented as novel. However, the paper does not provide a clear rationale for why this specific combination is beneficial compared to existing techniques. This is a medium-confidence concern, as the paper does present a novel combination of techniques, but the justification could be strengthened. Finally, the experimental section lacks crucial details that impact reproducibility. While the paper provides some experimental setup details, it does not provide specifics on how the loss weights are dynamically adjusted, which impacts reproducibility. The paper also reports average performance gains but lacks standard deviations or results from multiple runs, making it difficult to assess the statistical significance of the improvements. This is a medium-confidence concern, as the paper provides some experimental details, but the lack of specifics on loss weight adjustment and statistical significance is a significant limitation. Furthermore, the paper claims that the weak-strong model collaborative selection strategy ensures the quality of the final answer surpasses that of the previous thoughts, but it does not provide any quantitative evidence to support this claim. There is no evaluation of the 'thoughts' generated by the weak model or a direct comparison of the quality of weak and strong model outputs using specific metrics. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper makes a claim without providing any supporting evidence. The paper also claims that the weighted thought-mask fine-tuning encourages LLMs to improve responses based on previous thoughts and ensures logical consistency, but it lacks empirical evidence through ablation studies to support this. The mechanism for determining loss weights is also not detailed. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper makes claims without providing supporting evidence through ablation studies or detailed explanations of the loss weight determination mechanism.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the weak and strong model selection process. This should include specific information about the model architectures, sizes, and training data used for both the weak and strong models. The authors should also elaborate on the mechanism by which the collaboration between these models leads to improved performance. For example, do the weak models generate initial thoughts that are then refined by the strong models, or is there a more complex interaction? A concrete example of how this collaboration works in practice would be beneficial. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of this approach, such as the risk of introducing biases from the strong model or the computational cost of using multiple models. It would be useful to see an ablation study that examines the impact of different model combinations on the final performance. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the impact of the proposed method on different types of tasks and domains. This should include an analysis of the characteristics of the tasks where the method performs well and compare them to the tasks where the method does not show significant improvements. For example, are the tasks with significant gains primarily reasoning-based, or are there other common factors? The authors should also investigate the reasons for the varying performance across tasks. Is it due to the nature of the task, the quality of the training data, or the inherent limitations of the proposed method? A more in-depth analysis of the error patterns on different tasks would also be helpful. Furthermore, the authors should consider conducting ablation studies to understand the contribution of each component of the proposed method to the overall performance. This would help to identify the key factors that drive the improvements and to understand the limitations of the approach. Third, the authors need to address the potential limitations and challenges of the proposed method, such as the computational cost of the two-stage training process and the potential for bias in the generated data. The computational overhead of the iterative refinement process should be quantified, and the paper should discuss how the method scales with larger models or datasets. The potential for the weak-strong model collaboration to introduce biases or reinforce existing biases in the training data should also be addressed. This could involve analyzing the generated data for biases and comparing it to the original training data. Fourth, the paper needs a more comprehensive comparison with existing methods for improving LLMs' reasoning capabilities. The authors should clearly articulate how their method differs from and improves upon existing approaches, such as self-improvement or other iterative refinement techniques. This should include a detailed comparison of the proposed method with state-of-the-art methods, highlighting the advantages and disadvantages of each approach. The authors should also discuss the potential limitations of their method, such as the computational cost of the two-stage training process and the potential for bias in the generated data. Fifth, the authors should improve the clarity of the paper's writing, particularly in the abstract and introduction. The abstract should clearly define what a 'thought' is, explain why the authors propose to mask the thought, and how to adjust the loss weights. The introduction should elaborate on the limitations of existing methods, providing specific examples of how they fail to generalize across different tasks due to varying error types. The authors should then clearly explain how their method overcomes these limitations, providing a compelling motivation for their approach. Sixth, the authors should provide a more detailed justification for the specific combination of techniques used in PTR. They should explain why this combination is beneficial compared to existing techniques and provide a clear rationale for the specific loss function and the masking strategy. Seventh, the authors should provide more details on the experimental setup, including the specific models used, the prompts and parameters used for data generation, and the results for the baseline methods. The authors should also provide a detailed explanation of how the weights in the loss function are determined, including any specific formulas or algorithms used. The authors should also include an ablation study to evaluate the impact of different components of the proposed method, such as the masking strategy and the loss weights. Finally, the authors should provide quantitative metrics for evaluating the quality of thoughts and answers generated by both the weak and strong models. This could involve using metrics such as BLEU score, ROUGE score, or other relevant metrics for the specific tasks. The authors should also provide a clear definition of 'quality' in the context of their work. This would help to clarify the criteria used for selecting the final answer and provide a more objective measure of the effectiveness of the collaborative selection strategy. Furthermore, the authors should provide examples of the thoughts and answers generated by the weak and strong models to illustrate the refinement process. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and provide a more compelling case for the effectiveness and generalizability of the proposed PTR framework.

## questions
Several key questions remain unanswered, and addressing them would significantly enhance the paper's clarity and validity. First, how does the proposed method compare to existing methods for improving LLMs' reasoning capabilities in terms of performance, computational cost, and generalization ability? A detailed comparison with state-of-the-art methods is needed to establish the novelty and effectiveness of the proposed approach. Second, what are the potential limitations and challenges of the proposed method, and how can they be addressed? Specifically, how does the computational cost of the two-stage training process scale with larger models and datasets? What steps can be taken to mitigate the potential for bias in the generated data? Third, how does the weak-strong model collaborative selection strategy ensure the quality of the generated thoughts and answers, and what are the criteria for selecting the weak and strong models? What specific model architectures and sizes were used in the experiments? How do their relative capabilities impact the quality of the generated data? Fourth, how does the weighted thought-mask fine-tuning method encourage LLMs to refine their responses, and what are the key factors that contribute to the effectiveness of this approach? How are the weights in the loss function determined, and why is this specific weighting scheme used? What is the mechanism by which the masking strategy encourages refinement? Fifth, how does the proposed method perform on tasks that require common sense reasoning, and how does it compare to other methods that are specifically designed for common sense reasoning tasks? The paper's evaluation does not include specific tasks that focus on common sense reasoning, and it would be valuable to understand how PTR performs in this domain. Sixth, what is the impact of the number of iterations in the progressive refinement process on the final performance? Is there a point of diminishing returns, and how can the optimal number of iterations be determined? Seventh, how sensitive is the proposed method to the choice of the initial prompt used to generate the first thought? Does the performance vary significantly with different prompts, and how can this sensitivity be mitigated? Finally, what is the impact of the quality of the weak model's initial thoughts on the final performance? Does the method perform well even if the weak model generates poor initial thoughts, or does it require a certain level of quality in the initial thoughts? Addressing these questions would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method and its limitations.
