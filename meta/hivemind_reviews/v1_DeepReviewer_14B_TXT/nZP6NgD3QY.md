## Summary
This paper introduces AdaMerging, a novel adaptive model merging technique designed for multi-task learning (MTL). The core idea revolves around learning task-specific merging coefficients, either at the task level or at the layer level within each task's feature extractor, to combine multiple pre-trained models into a single, unified multi-task model. Unlike traditional model merging methods that often rely on fixed coefficients or require access to original training data, AdaMerging leverages entropy minimization on unlabeled test samples as a surrogate objective to iteratively refine these coefficients. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where access to original training data is limited, a common challenge in real-world applications. The authors' methodology draws inspiration from test-time adaptation techniques, adapting model parameters to unseen data, but instead focuses on adapting the merging coefficients to optimize the performance of the combined model. The experimental evaluation is comprehensive, encompassing eight image classification datasets, multiple model architectures, and various evaluation scenarios, including generalization to unseen tasks and robustness to test data distribution shifts. The results consistently demonstrate that AdaMerging outperforms existing task vector-based model merging methods, showcasing significant improvements in both average accuracy and robustness. Specifically, the layer-wise approach generally achieves superior performance compared to the task-wise approach, suggesting that different layers within a neural network learn features with varying degrees of task specificity. The paper's findings highlight the potential of adaptive model merging as a practical and effective approach for multi-task learning, particularly in situations where access to original training data is constrained. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field by introducing a novel method for combining pre-trained models, and by demonstrating its effectiveness across a range of tasks and evaluation scenarios. The use of entropy minimization as a surrogate objective for learning merging coefficients is a particularly interesting and practical idea, and the experimental results provide compelling evidence for the method's efficacy. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in terms of its computational cost analysis, its sensitivity to hyperparameters, and its theoretical justification, which I will discuss in more detail below.
