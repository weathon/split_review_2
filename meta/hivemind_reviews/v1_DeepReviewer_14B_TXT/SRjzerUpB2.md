## summary
This paper introduces Fat-to-Thin Policy Optimization (FtTPO), a novel offline reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm designed to learn sparse continuous policies from logged datasets. The core idea behind FtTPO is to maintain two policies: a 'fat' proposal policy, which is heavy-tailed and learns from the offline data, and a 'thin' target policy, which is sparse and responsible for decision-making. The fat policy, instantiated using a q-Gaussian distribution with a higher entropic index (q=2), is designed to explore the action space more broadly, capturing the support of the offline data. The thin policy, also a q-Gaussian but with q=0, is encouraged to concentrate its probability mass on a smaller set of actions, promoting sparsity. Knowledge is transferred from the fat policy to the thin policy through a reverse KL divergence minimization, effectively distilling the knowledge of the fat policy into a sparse action distribution. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of FtTPO in a safety-critical treatment simulation and the MuJoCo benchmark, showing that FtTPO can learn a sparse policy that outperforms popular baselines. The empirical results suggest that the proposed fat-to-thin approach is a promising method for learning safe and effective policies in offline RL settings. The paper's main contribution lies in its novel approach to addressing the challenges of learning sparse policies in offline RL, particularly the out-of-support action issue, by using a two-policy framework with a heavy-tailed proposal policy and a sparse target policy. The use of q-Gaussian distributions allows for a unified framework encompassing both heavy-tailed and sparse policies, and the empirical results demonstrate the potential of this approach in safety-critical scenarios. The authors also provide ablation studies to analyze the impact of different design choices, such as the choice of the heavy-tailed policy and the use of a squashed Gaussian policy. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to offline RL with sparse policies, which is a significant contribution to the field.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the introduction of the Fat-to-Thin Policy Optimization (FtTPO) algorithm is a significant contribution. The idea of using a heavy-tailed 'fat' policy to explore the action space and then distilling this knowledge into a sparse 'thin' policy is both novel and elegant. This approach directly addresses the challenge of out-of-support actions that often plague sparse policy learning in offline RL. The use of q-Gaussian distributions to parameterize both the fat and thin policies is another strength, as it provides a unified framework that encompasses both heavy-tailed and sparse policies. This allows for a smooth transition from exploration to exploitation and enables the learning of sparse policies in a principled manner. The empirical evaluation of FtTPO is also commendable. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their method in both a safety-critical treatment simulation and the MuJoCo benchmark, showing that FtTPO can achieve competitive performance compared to popular baselines. The inclusion of ablation studies further strengthens the paper, as it provides insights into the impact of different design choices, such as the choice of the heavy-tailed policy and the use of a squashed Gaussian policy. The visualization of policy evolution also provides valuable insights into the learning process, making the paper more accessible and understandable. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly explains the motivation, methodology, and results. The authors provide a clear and concise explanation of the problem, the proposed solution, and the experimental setup, which makes the paper easy to follow and understand. The overall presentation of the paper is excellent, and the authors have done a great job of communicating their ideas effectively.
