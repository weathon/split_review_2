## summary
This paper tackles a critical yet often overlooked aspect of continual learning (CL): the impact of data chunking on model performance. The authors argue that the division of training data into discrete chunks, even in the absence of task shifts, significantly degrades performance compared to learning from the entire dataset at once. This phenomenon, which they term the 'chunking problem,' is demonstrated through empirical evaluations using standard CL benchmarks. The authors show that common CL methods do not effectively address this issue, performing comparably to plain Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) when task shifts are absent. Their analysis reveals that the primary cause of this performance drop is not catastrophic forgetting, as is often the case in CL with task shifts, but rather a form of forgetting induced by the chunking process itself. To mitigate this, the authors propose a simple yet effective method: per-chunk weight averaging. This technique involves averaging the model's weights after training on each chunk, rather than relying solely on the final weights after all chunks have been processed. They motivate this approach by analyzing a linear case, showing that weight averaging approximates Bayesian linear regression, which is known to be robust to data fragmentation. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of per-chunk weight averaging across various datasets and show that it improves performance not only in the chunking setting but also generalizes to the full CL setting. This work highlights the importance of considering data chunking as a distinct challenge in CL, separate from task shifts, and offers a practical solution that could be easily integrated into existing CL frameworks. The paper's core contribution lies in its identification and analysis of the chunking problem, and the empirical demonstration of a simple method to mitigate it, thereby opening up a new avenue for research in continual learning.

## strengths
I found several strengths in this paper that contribute to its overall value. First, the paper introduces a novel perspective by focusing on the 'chunking problem' within continual learning. This is a significant contribution because it highlights a previously overlooked aspect of CL that has a substantial impact on performance. By isolating the effect of data chunking from task shifts, the authors provide a clearer understanding of the challenges inherent in incremental learning. This fresh angle is valuable for the field, as it encourages researchers to consider the impact of data presentation on model performance, beyond the typical focus on task boundaries and catastrophic forgetting. Second, the paper provides a thorough empirical analysis of the impact of chunking on CL performance. The authors demonstrate, through experiments on standard CL benchmarks, that chunking alone can lead to a significant drop in performance, even when there are no task shifts. This finding is crucial because it shows that the way data is presented to the model can be as important as the learning algorithm itself. The experiments are well-designed and clearly presented, making it easy to understand the magnitude of the problem. Furthermore, the authors' analysis of why performance drops when learning occurs in chunks is insightful. They show that the primary cause of the performance degradation is not catastrophic forgetting, but rather a form of forgetting induced by the chunking process itself. This distinction is important because it suggests that the mechanisms underlying performance drops in CL are more complex than previously thought. Finally, the paper proposes a simple yet effective method to mitigate the chunking problem: per-chunk weight averaging. This method is easy to implement and shows significant improvements in performance across various datasets. The authors' motivation for this method, based on the analysis of the linear case and its connection to Bayesian linear regression, is also compelling. The fact that this method generalizes to the full CL setting further enhances its value, as it suggests that addressing the chunking problem can lead to more robust and effective CL algorithms.
