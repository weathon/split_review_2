## Summary
This paper investigates the training dynamics of CLIP models, particularly focusing on those trained on smaller datasets. The authors observe that these models may not be fully optimized with standard training procedures and propose a simple yet effective strategy to enhance their performance. The core idea involves resetting the learning rate scheduler to its initial state and training for a few additional epochs after the original training process has seemingly converged. This approach is motivated by the observation that CLIP models trained on smaller datasets often exhibit performance saturation, where further training does not lead to significant improvements. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their method through experiments on various CLIP architectures, including ResNet-50, ViT-B-32, and ViT-B-16, and evaluate the models on zero-shot ImageNet classification tasks, as well as other downstream tasks. The empirical results show that this simple additional training can significantly improve the zero-shot accuracy of CLIP models on downstream tasks. The paper also explores the impact of the proposed method on models trained on larger datasets, such as LAION-400M, and compares it with other existing methods for improving CLIP performance. The authors find that while the method is effective on smaller datasets, its impact is less pronounced on models trained on larger datasets, suggesting that undertraining is less of an issue at scale. The paper's main contribution lies in identifying a potential undertraining issue in CLIP models trained on smaller datasets and proposing a simple and practical solution to address it. The findings suggest that the standard training procedures for CLIP models, particularly those trained on smaller datasets, may not fully optimize their performance, and that additional training with a reset learning rate scheduler can lead to significant improvements. This work highlights the importance of carefully considering training procedures for CLIP models and provides a practical approach for improving their performance, especially when training data is limited. The paper's exploration of the impact of the proposed method on models trained on larger datasets also provides valuable insights into the scaling behavior of CLIP models and suggests that the undertraining issue may be less relevant for models trained on large-scale datasets. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically supported approach for improving the performance of CLIP models, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

## Strengths
This paper presents a compelling investigation into the training dynamics of CLIP models, particularly those trained on smaller datasets, and offers a simple yet effective method to improve their performance. The core strength of this work lies in its clear identification of a potential undertraining issue in CLIP models. The authors astutely observe that these models, when trained on smaller datasets, often exhibit performance saturation, where further training does not lead to significant improvements. This observation is crucial as it highlights a potential limitation of standard training procedures for CLIP models. The proposed method, which involves resetting the learning rate scheduler and training for a few additional epochs, is remarkably straightforward and easy to implement. This simplicity is a significant advantage, making the method accessible to a wide range of practitioners. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through a series of well-designed experiments on various CLIP architectures, including ResNet-50, ViT-B-32, and ViT-B-16. The empirical results, presented in tables and figures, clearly show that the proposed method can significantly improve the zero-shot accuracy of CLIP models on downstream tasks, such as ImageNet classification. The paper also explores the impact of the proposed method on models trained on larger datasets, such as LAION-400M, and finds that while the method is effective on smaller datasets, its impact is less pronounced on models trained on larger datasets. This finding is valuable as it provides insights into the scaling behavior of CLIP models and suggests that the undertraining issue may be less relevant for models trained on large-scale datasets. Furthermore, the paper compares the proposed method with other existing approaches for improving CLIP performance, demonstrating that it achieves competitive results. The authors also explore the impact of the number of additional training epochs, showing that a small number of extra epochs can lead to significant improvements. This is an important finding as it suggests that the method is efficient and does not require extensive additional training. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their motivation, methodology, and findings, and the paper is well-organized and structured. Overall, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field by identifying a potential undertraining issue in CLIP models trained on smaller datasets and proposing a simple and effective solution to address it. The findings have practical implications for researchers and practitioners working with CLIP models, particularly in resource-constrained settings.

## Weaknesses
While this paper presents a valuable contribution to the field, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the lack of theoretical justification for the proposed method. The authors introduce the approach as a practical trick, demonstrating its effectiveness through empirical results, but they do not provide a theoretical framework to explain why resetting the learning rate scheduler and training for a few additional epochs leads to performance improvements. This absence of theoretical grounding makes it difficult to understand the underlying mechanisms at play and limits the generalizability of the findings. Specifically, the paper does not offer any mathematical formulation or analysis of the loss landscape to explain why the model continues to improve after the learning rate has decayed to zero. This is a significant limitation, as it leaves the reader with an incomplete understanding of the method's behavior. The paper also lacks a thorough investigation into the reasons behind the observed undertraining phenomenon. While the authors hypothesize that CLIP models trained on smaller datasets might be undertrained, they do not provide a detailed analysis of the training dynamics, loss landscape, or learned representations to pinpoint the exact causes. For instance, it is unclear whether the undertraining is due to a lack of diversity in the training data, inherent limitations in the optimization process, or other factors. A deeper analysis, perhaps involving visualization of the loss landscape or the learned representations, would be beneficial. The experimental evaluation, while demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed method, also has some limitations. The experiments primarily focus on a few CLIP architectures (ResNet-50, ViT-B-32, and ViT-B-16) and primarily evaluate on zero-shot ImageNet classification. While Table 2 shows results on other datasets (CC3M and CC12M), the evaluation could be more comprehensive by including a wider range of downstream tasks and datasets. This limited scope makes it difficult to assess the generalizability of the method to different tasks and datasets. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed method. While the method is simple to implement, it is important to understand the additional computational overhead it introduces, especially when compared to other methods. The paper should include a discussion of the training time and resource requirements, and how these scale with the size of the dataset and the complexity of the model. This is crucial for assessing the practical applicability of the method. The paper also lacks a systematic ablation study on different learning rate schedules. While the paper explores a cyclic learning rate scheduler, a more comprehensive ablation study on different learning rate schedules (e.g., step decay, polynomial decay) in conjunction with the proposed method is missing. This makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the method and to determine whether the observed improvements are specific to the learning rate schedule used in the original CLIP training or a more general phenomenon. Finally, the results presented in Table 7 show that the proposed method achieves competitive performance but does not consistently outperform existing methods by a significant margin. For example, the proposed method achieves 41.7% on ImageNet, while SLIP achieves 42.1%. This suggests that the proposed method is not a significant improvement over existing techniques. The paper also lacks a comprehensive comparison with a broader range of state-of-the-art techniques, including those using different data augmentation strategies. The comparison is limited to a few selected methods and does not include a qualitative analysis of the learned representations or other aspects. These limitations, taken together, highlight the need for further investigation and analysis to fully understand the potential and limitations of the proposed method. The lack of theoretical grounding, the limited experimental scope, and the absence of a detailed computational cost analysis all contribute to the overall weakness of the paper.

## Suggestions
To strengthen this paper, I recommend several concrete improvements. First and foremost, the authors should delve deeper into the theoretical underpinnings of their proposed method. This could involve analyzing the loss landscape of CLIP models and how the additional training phase affects the model's convergence properties. For instance, the authors could investigate the gradient behavior during the additional training phase and compare it to the initial training phase. This would provide a deeper understanding of the method's behavior and help to identify the conditions under which it is most effective. The authors should also explore the connection between the proposed method and existing theories on transfer learning and generalization. This would help to contextualize their findings within the broader literature and provide a more rigorous theoretical foundation for their work. Second, the authors should expand the experimental evaluation to include a wider range of CLIP models, datasets, and tasks. This should include experiments with different CLIP architectures, such as larger models and models with different vision and text backbones. The authors should also evaluate the method on a more diverse set of downstream tasks, including image retrieval, image captioning, and other tasks that are commonly used to evaluate CLIP models. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of the proposed method on models trained on larger datasets, such as LAION-400M or LAION-5B, and compare the results to the models trained on smaller datasets. This would help to assess the generalizability of the method and to identify potential limitations. Third, the authors should conduct a more systematic ablation study on different learning rate schedules. This should include experiments with different learning rate schedules, such as step decay, polynomial decay, and cosine annealing, and compare the results to the proposed method. This would help to understand whether the observed improvement is specific to the learning rate schedule used in the original CLIP training or a more general phenomenon. The authors should also explore the impact of different hyperparameters, such as the batch size, weight decay, and optimizer, on the performance of the method. This would help to identify the optimal settings for the proposed method and to understand its sensitivity to different hyperparameter choices. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with their proposed method. This should include a discussion of the training time and resource requirements, and how these scale with the size of the dataset and the complexity of the model. The authors should also consider comparing the computational cost of their method to other existing approaches. This would help to assess the practical applicability of the method and provide a more complete picture of its trade-offs. Finally, the authors should provide a more comprehensive comparison with existing approaches for improving CLIP model performance. This should include a detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method compared to other techniques, including those that modify the training objective, use different data augmentation strategies, or employ different optimization algorithms. The comparison should include quantitative results on a variety of downstream tasks, as well as qualitative analysis of the learned representations. This would help to establish the true value of the proposed method and provide a more complete picture of its performance relative to other state-of-the-art techniques. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make a more compelling case for the proposed method.

## Questions
Several key questions arise from my analysis of this paper. First, what are the specific reasons behind the undertraining phenomenon observed in CLIP models trained on smaller datasets? While the paper identifies this issue and proposes a solution, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the underlying causes. Is it due to a lack of diversity in the training data, inherent limitations in the optimization process, or other factors? A deeper understanding of the causes of undertraining would help to develop more targeted solutions. Second, how does the proposed method compare to other state-of-the-art methods for improving CLIP performance in terms of both accuracy and computational cost? While the paper shows that the proposed method achieves competitive results, it does not provide a comprehensive comparison with a broader range of existing techniques. A more detailed comparison, including quantitative results and qualitative analysis, is needed to establish the true value of the proposed method. Third, what is the impact of the proposed method on the robustness of CLIP models to distribution shifts and adversarial attacks? The paper primarily focuses on zero-shot ImageNet classification, but it does not explore the impact of the proposed method on other aspects of model performance, such as robustness to distribution shifts and adversarial attacks. A more comprehensive evaluation is needed to assess the practical applicability of the method. Fourth, why does the proposed additional training procedure only include 1 to 3 epochs? Is this too arbitrary? The paper shows that a small number of additional epochs can lead to significant improvements, but it does not provide a clear explanation for why this is the case. A more detailed analysis of the training dynamics during the additional epochs would be beneficial. Fifth, what is the difference between the proposed additional training procedure and the learning rate warm-up technique? Both involve manipulating the learning rate during training, and it would be helpful to understand the specific differences between these two approaches. Finally, could the authors provide the results of CLIP models pre-trained on larger datasets, such as CLIP (ViT-B-32, OpenAI), CLIP (ViT-L-14, OpenAI), or EVA-CLIP? The paper primarily focuses on models trained on CC12M and LAION-400M, but it would be valuable to see how the proposed method performs on models trained on even larger datasets. These questions highlight key uncertainties and areas for further investigation that would help to strengthen the paper and provide a more complete understanding of the proposed method.
