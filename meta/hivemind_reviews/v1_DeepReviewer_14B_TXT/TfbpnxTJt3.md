## summary
This paper introduces FedDPCont, a novel federated learning framework designed to address the challenge of open-set noisy labels across distributed clients. The core problem tackled is the degradation of model performance in federated learning when local datasets contain noisy labels and, crucially, when the label spaces observed by different clients are non-overlapping subsets of the global label space. This 'open-set' scenario, where each client only sees a portion of the entire label set, coupled with label noise, poses a significant challenge to traditional federated learning methods. The authors propose a solution that leverages the concept of contrastive learning, but with a unique twist tailored to the federated setting. Instead of relying on local information alone, FedDPCont employs a mechanism for sharing 'contrastive labels' among clients. These labels are not the original noisy labels, but rather, randomly selected labels from the global label space, shared in a differentially private manner. The key idea is to use these shared contrastive labels to prevent the local models from overfitting to the noisy labels present in their own limited open-set label space. The method involves each client calculating a differentially private version of their local labels and sharing these with the server. The server then aggregates these private labels and broadcasts a global label distribution back to the clients. Each client then samples a contrastive label from this distribution to use in their local loss function. The local loss function is modified to include a contrastive term, which penalizes the model for incorrectly classifying the contrastive label. This approach aims to guide the local models towards a more robust representation that is less susceptible to the noise in the local labels. The authors provide theoretical guarantees for the privacy of the shared labels, demonstrating that the label sharing mechanism satisfies epsilon-differential privacy. They also provide a theoretical analysis that connects the gradient updates of the proposed method to the corresponding centralized loss. The empirical evaluation of FedDPCont is conducted on both synthetic and real-world datasets. The synthetic experiments involve creating controlled noisy label scenarios with varying noise ratios and open-set configurations. The real-world experiments utilize datasets like CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100, with added noise to simulate real-world label corruption. The results demonstrate that FedDPCont outperforms several baseline methods, including standard federated averaging (FedAvg) and other methods designed for noisy labels, under various noise ratios and open-set conditions. The paper's significance lies in its attempt to address a practical and challenging problem in federated learning. The combination of open-set label spaces and noisy labels is a realistic scenario in many real-world applications, and the proposed method offers a novel approach to tackling this problem. By leveraging globally shared private contrastive labels, FedDPCont aims to improve the robustness and generalization of federated models in the presence of label noise and limited local label spaces. The theoretical guarantees for privacy and the empirical results on both synthetic and real-world datasets provide evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed framework. However, the paper also has limitations, particularly in the strong assumption of a shared underlying label space and the lack of a detailed analysis of computational complexity and scalability. These limitations will need to be addressed in future work to further enhance the practical applicability of the proposed method.

## strengths
The paper's primary strength lies in its novel approach to addressing the challenging problem of federated learning with open-set noisy labels. The proposed FedDPCont framework introduces a creative solution by leveraging globally shared private contrastive labels. This approach is a significant departure from traditional methods that often rely on local information alone. The idea of using contrastive labels to prevent overfitting to noisy local labels is both innovative and intuitively appealing. By sharing information about the global label space, the method aims to guide local models towards a more robust representation, which is a crucial step in improving the performance of federated learning in realistic scenarios. The paper's theoretical contributions are also noteworthy. The authors provide a formal proof that the label sharing mechanism satisfies epsilon-differential privacy, which is essential for ensuring the privacy of client data in federated learning. Additionally, the theoretical analysis connecting the gradient updates of the proposed method to the corresponding centralized loss provides a solid foundation for the approach. This theoretical grounding adds credibility to the proposed method and demonstrates a deeper understanding of the underlying principles. The empirical results presented in the paper further support the effectiveness of FedDPCont. The experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms several baseline methods under various noise ratios and open-set conditions. The consistent improvement across different datasets and noise levels provides strong evidence for the practical applicability of the framework. The use of both synthetic and real-world datasets is a strength, as it allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the method's performance under controlled and more realistic conditions. The paper is generally well-written and clearly structured. The authors provide a detailed description of the proposed method, including the label communication mechanism and the contrastive loss function. The mathematical notation is clear and consistent, and the theoretical proofs are presented in a logical manner. The experimental setup is also well-described, making it easy to understand the evaluation process. The paper's focus on a practical problem in federated learning, combined with its novel approach, theoretical guarantees, and strong empirical results, makes it a valuable contribution to the field.

## weaknesses
Despite its strengths, the paper has several significant weaknesses that need to be addressed. One major concern is the strong assumption of a shared underlying label space across all clients. While the paper addresses the 'open-set' scenario where clients observe different subsets of the labels, it still assumes that the complete set of labels is the same for all clients. This assumption is not realistic in many practical federated learning scenarios, where clients may have access to entirely different sets of labels due to the heterogeneity of data sources. For instance, in a scenario involving multiple hospitals, each hospital might have data related to a completely different set of diseases. The paper's approach of sharing information about the global label space might not be effective in such cases, and the method's performance could be severely limited. The paper does not adequately address this limitation, and the lack of experiments with heterogeneous label spaces further weakens the claim of practical applicability. The paper's core assumption is stated in Section 3, where it is mentioned that "Assume there is no overlap among $D_{c},\forall c$." This implies that while the datasets themselves have no overlap, the underlying label space is shared, which is a strong and potentially unrealistic assumption. This assumption significantly limits the applicability of the proposed method in real-world scenarios where data heterogeneity is a major challenge. Another significant weakness is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. The paper introduces a label communication mechanism and a contrastive loss function, both of which add computational overhead compared to standard federated learning algorithms like FedAvg. However, the paper does not provide a formal analysis of the time and space complexity of these components or the overall algorithm. There is no quantification of the time or memory required for the label perturbation and recovery process, nor for the computation of the contrastive loss. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method, especially in resource-constrained environments. The absence of a comparison with existing federated learning algorithms in terms of computational cost further exacerbates this issue. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of the scalability of the proposed method. The experiments are conducted with a fixed number of clients and datasets of fixed sizes. There is no investigation into how the performance of FedDPCont scales with the number of clients or the size of the datasets. This is a crucial aspect for understanding the practical applicability of the method in real-world scenarios, where the number of clients and the size of datasets can vary significantly. The paper should have included experiments with a varying number of clients to demonstrate the scalability of the approach. It is important to understand how the performance degrades as the number of clients increases, especially in terms of convergence speed and final accuracy. Similarly, the impact of dataset size on the performance of the proposed method should have been investigated. The lack of such analysis makes it difficult to assess the method's robustness and generalizability. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed sensitivity analysis of the hyperparameters. The performance of the proposed method likely depends on several hyperparameters, such as the learning rate, the number of communication rounds, and the parameters related to the differential privacy mechanism. However, the paper does not investigate the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the performance of the method. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the robustness of the method and to provide practical guidance on parameter tuning. A thorough sensitivity analysis is crucial for understanding the behavior of the method and for ensuring its reliable performance in different settings. The paper's writing could also be improved in certain areas. While the introduction introduces the key concepts and the problem being addressed, the flow could be smoother, and the motivation for focusing on this specific problem could be strengthened. The transition from the general problem of noisy labels to the specific challenge of 'openset noisy labels' could be smoother and more clearly explained. The motivation for focusing on this specific problem could be strengthened with more compelling examples or a more detailed explanation of why existing methods fail in this scenario. The introduction should clearly explain the challenges of federated learning with noisy labels and open-set data, and why the proposed method is needed. Finally, while the paper acknowledges the relation to existing contrastive learning methods, it could more explicitly contrast its approach with these methods in the FL context. The novelty lies in the specific application and the use of globally shared private labels, but this distinction could be made clearer. The paper could also benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and the scenarios where it might not be effective. For example, how does the method perform when the noise is not random but rather systematic or adversarial?

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I would suggest several concrete improvements. First and foremost, the paper needs to relax the assumption of a shared underlying label space across all clients. Future work should explore methods to handle heterogeneous label spaces, potentially by incorporating techniques from open-set learning or domain adaptation. This could involve modifying the label communication mechanism to account for the possibility of unseen labels and adapting the contrastive loss function to handle the open-set nature of the problem. For example, the method could be extended to allow clients to share information about their label spaces, enabling the server to better understand the overall label distribution and adapt the learning process accordingly. This could involve using techniques like prototype learning or meta-learning to learn representations that are robust to label space variations. Furthermore, the paper should investigate the impact of different levels of label space overlap on the performance of the proposed method. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's robustness and applicability in real-world scenarios. Second, the paper needs to include a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. This analysis should consider the cost of the label communication mechanism and the impact of the contrastive loss function on the overall computational burden. Specifically, the authors should analyze the computational cost of the label perturbation and recovery process, as well as the cost of computing the contrastive loss. It would be beneficial to compare the computational cost of the proposed method with existing approaches, such as FedAvg and other noisy label learning algorithms. This analysis should also consider the impact of the number of clients and the size of the dataset on the computational cost of the proposed method. The authors should also provide a detailed analysis of the memory requirements of the proposed method, especially when dealing with large datasets and a large number of clients. Third, the paper should include a more comprehensive experimental evaluation, including a sensitivity analysis of the hyperparameters and a scalability analysis with a varying number of clients. The sensitivity analysis should investigate the impact of different hyperparameter settings on the performance of the proposed method, providing practical guidance on parameter tuning. The scalability analysis should demonstrate how the performance of the method changes as the number of clients increases, especially in terms of convergence speed and final accuracy. This analysis should also consider the impact of client heterogeneity on the scalability of the method. The paper should also explore the impact of different noise ratios on the performance of the proposed method, providing a more comprehensive understanding of its robustness to label noise. Fourth, the paper should improve the clarity of the introduction by providing a smoother transition from the general problem of noisy labels to the specific challenge of 'openset noisy labels.' The motivation for focusing on this specific problem could be strengthened with more compelling examples or a more detailed explanation of why existing methods fail in this scenario. The introduction should clearly explain the challenges of federated learning with noisy labels and open-set data, and why the proposed method is needed. Fifth, the paper should more explicitly contrast its approach with existing contrastive learning methods in the FL context. While the paper acknowledges the relation to existing contrastive learning methods, it could more clearly articulate how its approach differs from these methods and why these differences are significant. The paper could also benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and the scenarios where it might not be effective. For example, how does the method perform when the noise is not random but rather systematic or adversarial? Finally, the paper should consider incorporating techniques to reduce the communication overhead, such as gradient compression or sparsification. This would be particularly important in scenarios with a large number of clients or limited bandwidth. The paper should also investigate the impact of different communication strategies on the performance of the proposed method. These suggestions are aimed at enhancing the paper's practical applicability, robustness, and clarity. By addressing these weaknesses, the paper can make a more significant contribution to the field of federated learning.
