## summary
This paper introduces a novel Shift-Resilient Diffusive Imputation (SRDI) framework designed to address the challenges of Variable Subset Forecasting (VSF). VSF is a problem that arises when a forecasting model, trained on a complete set of variables, must make predictions using only a subset of those variables, often due to sensor failures or data collection issues. The authors identify two primary types of distribution shifts that occur in VSF: inter-series shift, which refers to changes in correlations between variables, and intra-series shift, which refers to distribution differences within the same variable over different time windows. To tackle inter-series shift, the SRDI framework employs a divide-and-conquer strategy within a diffusion-based imputation model. This strategy decomposes the time series data into invariant and variant patterns, aiming to capture stable underlying correlations and dynamic, shift-prone components, respectively. The invariant patterns are intended to provide a robust foundation for the model, while the variant patterns address the dynamic correlations that are susceptible to shift. For intra-series shift, the framework incorporates a meta-learning paradigm. This paradigm treats each time window as a distinct task, enabling the model to rapidly adapt to new distributions. The meta-learning approach involves a meta-training stage, where initial parameters are optimized through learning across multiple diffusion models and a forecasting backbone, and an adaptation stage, where the model quickly adjusts to the specific variable subset at inference time. The authors conduct extensive experiments on four real-world datasets, demonstrating that SRDI outperforms state-of-the-art methods in VSF tasks. The core contribution of this paper lies in the specific combination of a diffusion-based imputation model with a divide-and-conquer strategy for inter-series shift and a meta-learning framework for intra-series shift, tailored to the VSF problem. The empirical results, while promising, are limited by the scope of the datasets and the lack of detailed analysis of the model's sensitivity to hyperparameter settings and computational cost. The paper also lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed framework, particularly in scenarios with highly non-stationary time series or extremely sparse data. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field by addressing a practical and challenging problem with a novel approach.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. First, the authors have identified a significant and practical problem in the form of Variable Subset Forecasting (VSF). This is a scenario that is highly relevant to real-world applications, such as sensor networks and IoT systems, where data is often incomplete due to various reasons. The paper clearly articulates the challenges associated with VSF, particularly the distribution shifts that arise when forecasting with a subset of variables. Second, the proposed SRDI framework is a novel and well-reasoned approach to address these challenges. The integration of a diffusion-based imputation model with a divide-and-conquer strategy to handle inter-series shift is a clever way to decompose the time series data into invariant and variant patterns. This allows the model to capture both stable and dynamic correlations, which is crucial for effective imputation. The use of a meta-learning paradigm to address intra-series shift is also a significant contribution. By treating each time window as a distinct task, the model can rapidly adapt to new distributions, which is essential for handling the temporal variability in time series data. The experimental results, while limited in scope, demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed SRDI framework. The authors have conducted extensive experiments on four real-world datasets, and the results show that SRDI outperforms state-of-the-art methods in VSF tasks. The ablation study also provides valuable insights into the contribution of each component of the SRDI framework. Finally, the paper is well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to understand the proposed method and its underlying rationale. The authors have provided a detailed explanation of the SRDI framework, including the mathematical formulations and the experimental setup. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field by addressing a practical and challenging problem with a novel and well-executed approach.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a detailed explanation of the meta-learning strategy employed to address intra-series shift. While the authors mention the use of a meta-learning approach and describe the meta-training and adaptation stages, they do not specify the exact meta-learning algorithm used (e.g., MAML, Reptile). This lack of specificity makes it difficult to fully understand the implementation and effectiveness of this component. Furthermore, the paper does not provide sufficient detail on how the time series data is preprocessed and fed into the meta-learning framework, nor does it explain how the tasks are defined (i.e., how different time windows are operationalized as distinct tasks). This lack of clarity makes it challenging to reproduce the results and assess the validity of the approach. The paper also fails to justify the choice of meta-learning over other methods for handling intra-series shift, such as domain adaptation techniques. The authors state the goal is to enable rapid adaptation but do not compare it to alternative strategies. The loss function used for the meta-learning process is also not explicitly stated, which further hinders a complete understanding of the method. This lack of detail regarding the meta-learning strategy is a significant limitation, as it undermines the reproducibility and interpretability of the results. ## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the meta-learning strategy. This should include the specific meta-learning algorithm used (e.g., MAML, Reptile), along with a clear rationale for its selection. The authors should also provide a detailed description of how the time series data is preprocessed and fed into the meta-learning framework, how the tasks are defined (e.g., using different time windows), and how the meta-learner is trained to adapt to new tasks. The loss function used for meta-learning should also be explicitly stated. This would greatly enhance the clarity and reproducibility of the proposed method. Second, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the limitations of the SRDI framework. This should include a discussion of the method's performance under highly non-stationary time series, extreme data sparsity, and abrupt changes in the underlying data distribution. The authors should also conduct a detailed ablation study, systematically varying key hyperparameters within the meta-learning framework, such as learning rates, batch sizes, and the number of inner-loop iterations. This analysis should include a discussion of the computational cost associated with different hyperparameter settings, providing practical guidance for users of the SRDI model. Furthermore, the authors should include an analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method, comparing it to simpler imputation techniques. Third, the authors should expand their experimental evaluation to include a more diverse set of datasets. This should include datasets with varying characteristics, such as different sampling rates, noise levels, and types of distribution shifts. The authors should also consider including datasets with different spatial dependencies, as the current datasets may not fully represent the complexity of real-world spatial-temporal data. Additionally, the authors should evaluate the method's performance in more complex real-world scenarios, such as those involving multiple interacting time series or those with abrupt changes in the underlying data distribution. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed justification for the imputation-then-forecasting approach. This should include a discussion of the limitations of directly training a forecasting model on the available subset of variables and why imputation is a necessary step. The authors should also compare the proposed method to alternative approaches, such as training a forecasting model directly on the available variables. Finally, the authors should provide a more rigorous theoretical justification for the proposed denoising approach. This should include a discussion of the properties of the diffusion model that make it suitable for this task, and how the proposed denoising process aligns with these properties. The authors should also provide more experimental evidence to support the effectiveness of the decomposed invariant and variant patterns. This could include visualizing these patterns or using quantitative metrics to assess their stability and variability. The authors should also reconsider the use of meta-learning for handling intra-series shift and explore alternative approaches that are more suitable for adapting to distribution shifts within a single task, such as domain adaptation techniques. These changes would significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its impact on the field.

I am particularly interested in understanding why they chose this specific algorithm over alternatives like MAML or Reptile, and how the algorithm is adapted to the specific characteristics of the time series data. Second, how does the performance of SRDI vary with different sizes of the variable subset, and what is the minimum number of variables required for effective forecasting? This is a critical question for practical applications, as the availability of variables may vary significantly in real-world scenarios. Third, what are the computational complexities of the proposed SRDI framework, and how do they compare to existing methods? I am particularly interested in understanding the computational cost of the meta-learning component, as this could be a limiting factor in resource-constrained environments. Fourth, could the authors discuss the potential for extending the SRDI model to handle other types of time series data or forecasting tasks beyond VSF? This would provide insights into the generalizability of the proposed method and its potential impact on other areas of time series analysis. Fifth, why is the approach of first imputing and then forecasting necessary in VSF? How does this differ from traditional time series forecasting, and what are the limitations of directly training a forecasting model on the available subset of variables? Finally, how does the proposed denoising method for decomposing time series data into invariant and variant patterns differ from existing techniques? What evidence supports its effectiveness, and how does the model handle the temporal dependencies of the variant patterns, given that they are extracted at each time step? These questions target key uncertainties and methodological choices, and I believe that addressing them would significantly enhance the paper's clarity and impact.
