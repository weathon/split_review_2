## summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to enhance group robustness in machine learning models, particularly when dealing with spurious correlations. The authors propose a unified framework based on Empirical Bayes, which they use to reinterpret existing group robustness methods. Their core contribution is a technique called "Learn from Known Unknowns" (LfKU), which leverages the epistemic uncertainty of biased Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) models to selectively reweight samples during retraining. The method operates in two phases: first, a standard ERM model is trained with evidence regularization to estimate uncertainty, and second, only the last layer of the model is retrained using a reweighted loss based on these uncertainty estimates. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of LfKU across diverse datasets, including Colored MNIST, Waterbirds, CelebA, MultiNLI, and CivilComments, showing improved worst-group accuracy and reduced reliance on hyperparameter tuning. The paper's key idea is to use the uncertainty of an ERM model, which is biased towards the majority group, to identify samples that are likely from the minority group. These samples are then given more weight during the retraining phase, which helps to mitigate the impact of spurious correlations and improve the model's performance on the underrepresented group. The authors argue that this approach is more efficient than retraining the entire model and that it provides a more principled way to address group robustness compared to existing methods. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling, showing consistent improvements in worst-group accuracy across various datasets. The authors also demonstrate the method's efficiency in terms of computational cost and memory usage compared to existing approaches. Overall, the paper presents a novel and practical approach to group robustness, offering a unified perspective on existing methods and introducing a new technique that leverages uncertainty to improve model performance on minority groups. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several limitations that need to be addressed to further strengthen the paper's contribution.

## strengths
The paper's primary strength lies in its novel perspective on group robustness, framing several existing methods within an Empirical Bayesian framework. This provides a more unified and principled understanding of how to address spurious correlations and improve model robustness. By interpreting the problem through the lens of Empirical Bayes, the authors offer a fresh way to think about group robustness, which is a valuable contribution to the field. The proposed "Learn from Known Unknowns" (LfKU) method is another significant strength. The idea of using epistemic uncertainty from a biased ERM model to guide the retraining process is innovative and practical. This approach allows for a more adaptive and efficient way to mitigate the impact of spurious correlations without requiring explicit group labels. The method's reliance on retraining only the last layer of the model makes it computationally efficient, which is a significant advantage over methods that require retraining the entire model. Furthermore, the paper is well-organized and clearly written, with detailed explanations of the problem, related work, and the proposed method. The authors provide a detailed description of the LfKU method, including the mathematical formulation and implementation details, which makes it easy to understand and reproduce. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling, showing consistent improvements in worst-group accuracy across various datasets. The authors also demonstrate the method's efficiency in terms of computational cost and memory usage compared to existing approaches. The experiments are comprehensive, including multiple datasets and tasks (image and text), which further strengthens the validity of the results. The authors also provide a good literature review of previous group robustness methods and summarizing them with the proposed empirical Bayesian framework. The writing is clear and easy to follow. The paper also includes a synthetic experiment using the Colored MNIST dataset, which helps to demonstrate the concept of LfKU and provides a clear example of how the method works. The use of t-SNE visualizations and GradCAM visualizations also helps to provide a better understanding of the method's behavior.
