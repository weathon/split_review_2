## summary
This paper introduces NARes, a large-scale dataset of 15,625 Wide Residual Network (WRN) architectures trained using adversarial training (AT). The authors' primary contribution is the creation of this comprehensive dataset, which includes detailed metrics such as clean and adversarial accuracies, training statistics, stable accuracy, and empirical Lipschitz constants. The dataset is intended to facilitate research in neural architecture search (NAS) for adversarial robustness by providing a readily available resource for evaluating the robustness of different WRN architectures. The methodology involves training each architecture using Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) adversarial training on the CIFAR-10 dataset, and then evaluating the models against various adversarial attacks, including FGSM, PGD, PGD-CW, and a compact version of AutoAttack. The authors also explore the relationships between architectural parameters, such as depth and width, and adversarial robustness, finding that increasing the Multiply-Accumulate Operations (MACs) budget is more beneficial than increasing the number of parameters. They also observe that stable accuracy and lower Lipschitz constants are indicative of better robustness. Furthermore, the paper demonstrates the utility of NARes as a benchmark for NAS algorithms, showing that it can be used to identify robust architectures. The authors also challenge existing principles of robust architecture design by analyzing the dataset. Overall, the paper presents a valuable resource for the community, offering a comprehensive dataset and insights into the relationship between architecture and adversarial robustness. However, the study is limited by its focus on WRN architectures and the CIFAR-10 dataset, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the practical implications of the findings for designing robust architectures, nor does it explore the impact of different adversarial training methods on the observed relationships. Despite these limitations, the dataset represents a significant contribution to the field, enabling further research into the interplay between architecture and adversarial robustness.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in the creation of NARes, a large-scale and comprehensive dataset of 15,625 WRN architectures evaluated for adversarial robustness. This is, to my knowledge, the first dataset of its kind, offering a valuable resource for the community. The authors have meticulously trained and evaluated each architecture, providing a wealth of information including clean and adversarial accuracies, training statistics, stable accuracy, and empirical Lipschitz constants. This level of detail is crucial for researchers seeking to understand the complex relationship between architecture and adversarial robustness. The dataset's scale, encompassing a wide range of WRN architectures, allows for a thorough exploration of the architectural space, which is a significant advancement over previous studies that have often been limited in scope. Furthermore, the authors have demonstrated the utility of NARes as a benchmark for neural architecture search algorithms, showing that it can be used to identify robust architectures. This is a crucial step towards developing more robust models through automated search techniques. The paper also provides valuable insights into the relationship between architecture and robustness, challenging existing principles and offering new perspectives on robust model design. For example, the finding that increasing the MACs budget is more beneficial than increasing the number of parameters is a significant observation that can inform future architectural designs. The authors also highlight the importance of stable accuracy and lower Lipschitz constants as indicators of robustness, which are valuable metrics for evaluating model performance under adversarial attacks. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making the dataset accessible to a wider audience. The authors have clearly articulated their methodology and findings, and the paper is well-organized and structured. The inclusion of pre-trained checkpoints and extensive metrics further enhances the dataset's accessibility and utility for further research. In summary, the creation of NARes and the accompanying analysis represent a significant contribution to the field of adversarial robustness, providing a valuable resource for researchers and facilitating further advancements in this area.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a valuable contribution, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the dataset's scope is limited to Wide Residual Networks (WRNs), which restricts its applicability to other types of neural network architectures. As explicitly stated in the paper, "we use the wide residual network (WRN) as the fundamental architecture of NARes" (Section 3.1). This focus on a single architecture family limits the generalizability of the findings to other architectures, such as ResNets, Transformers, or even more recent architectures like MLPs. This is a significant concern given the diverse landscape of neural network models used in practice, and the fact that different architectures may exhibit different robustness characteristics. The lack of diversity in the dataset means that the insights derived from it may not be applicable to other types of models, limiting the dataset's overall utility. Second, the evaluation is conducted solely on the CIFAR-10 dataset, which may not generalize to larger, more complex datasets like ImageNet. The paper states, "Every model was trained with the standard adversarial training (AT) by Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) for 100 epochs on the full CIFAR-10 training set" (Section 3.1). While CIFAR-10 is a widely used benchmark, it is a relatively simple dataset compared to real-world datasets. The robustness trends observed on CIFAR-10 may not translate directly to more complex datasets with higher resolution images and more diverse data distributions. This limitation is further compounded by the fact that the paper does not include any analysis of how the observed trends might change on different datasets. The lack of generalization to other datasets is a significant concern, as it limits the practical applicability of the dataset and the insights derived from it. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the practical implications of the findings for designing robust architectures. While the authors present insights such as "Compared to parameters, increasing MACs budget is preferred for AR" and "Stable accuracy consistently indicates the corresponding AR, while lower LIP is a necessary condition for AR" (Introduction), they do not offer concrete, actionable guidelines for designing robust architectures based on these findings. The discussion remains at a higher level of analysis, and the paper lacks a dedicated section or detailed discussion on how these findings can be directly translated into practical design principles. This lack of practical guidance limits the immediate impact of the dataset on the development of robust models. Fourth, the paper only considers one type of adversarial training (AT) in building the NARes dataset, specifically PGD. As stated in the paper, "Every model was trained with the standard adversarial training (AT) by Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)" (Section 3.1). However, there are many other AT methods, such as TRADES and MART, that could lead to different optimal architectures. The paper does not discuss the impact of different AT methods on the generalization of NARes, and it is unclear whether the architecture-robustness relationships observed with PGD AT would hold true when using other AT methods. This is a significant limitation, as different AT methods may lead to different optimal architectures, and the dataset's usefulness would be limited if it only reflects the characteristics of a single AT method. Fifth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of building and using the NARes dataset. While the paper mentions that "The entire training process cost approximately 13.1K GPU days, equivalent to 36 GPU years" and "The entire evaluation costs 2.9K GPU days ( ∼8 GPU years)" (Introduction, Section 3.2), it lacks more granular details like training time per architecture, memory requirements, and a comparison of computational costs with other datasets. This lack of information makes it difficult for researchers to assess the feasibility of using this dataset in their own work, especially those with limited computational resources. Finally, the paper does not compare NARes with other existing datasets on adversarial robustness. While the paper discusses existing neural architecture datasets and benchmarks for model robustness, it does not compare NARes with broader adversarial robustness benchmarks like RobustBench. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to position NARes within the broader landscape of adversarial robustness research and to highlight its unique contributions and limitations. These limitations, while not invalidating the paper's contribution, highlight areas where further research and development are needed to fully realize the potential of the NARes dataset. The confidence level for each of these identified weaknesses is high, as they are all directly supported by the paper's content and methodology.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses and enhance the value of the NARes dataset, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should expand the dataset to include other popular architectures beyond WRNs. This would significantly increase the dataset's utility and allow researchers to explore the interplay between different architectural choices and adversarial robustness. For example, the dataset could include a range of Transformer variants with varying numbers of layers, attention heads, and embedding dimensions, as well as other convolutional architectures like ResNets and VGGs. This would enable a more comprehensive understanding of how architectural factors influence robustness across different model families. Furthermore, the inclusion of diverse architectures would allow for more generalizable conclusions and facilitate the development of robust design principles applicable to a wider range of models. Second, the authors should perform a more rigorous analysis of how the observed trends in their dataset translate to larger, more complex datasets like ImageNet. This could involve training a subset of the architectures on ImageNet and comparing the robustness trends observed on CIFAR-10 with those on ImageNet. Specifically, the authors should investigate whether the correlations between stable accuracy, Lipschitz constant, and adversarial robustness observed on CIFAR-10 hold true on ImageNet. If the trends do not directly translate, the authors should investigate the reasons behind these discrepancies and provide guidance on how to adapt the findings to larger datasets. This could involve analyzing the impact of dataset complexity, image resolution, and the number of classes on the relationship between architecture and robustness. Such an analysis would significantly enhance the practical value of the dataset. Third, the paper should include a more in-depth discussion of the practical implications of the findings for designing robust architectures. The authors should provide concrete guidelines on how to leverage the dataset to identify robust architectural patterns. For example, they could analyze the dataset to identify specific architectural configurations that consistently exhibit high robustness across different adversarial attacks. They could also explore the relationship between architectural parameters and robustness, providing insights into which parameters are most critical for achieving robustness. This discussion should go beyond simply stating that stable accuracy and Lipschitz constant are correlated with robustness; it should provide actionable guidance on how to use these metrics to design more robust architectures. The authors should also discuss the limitations of their findings and suggest future research directions to address these limitations. Fourth, the authors should explore how the architecture-robustness relationships might change when using other adversarial training methods, such as TRADES or MART. This could involve retraining a subset of the architectures in NARes using these different methods and comparing the resulting robustness metrics. The authors should also discuss the potential implications of these differences for the use of NARes as a benchmark. If different AT methods lead to different optimal architectures, the authors should consider how this might impact the dataset's utility for NAS algorithms. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the computational cost associated with building and using the NARes dataset. This should include information on the training time, memory requirements, and the computational resources needed for evaluation. This information should be provided for each architecture and attack combination, allowing researchers to assess the feasibility of using the dataset in their own work. Furthermore, the paper should provide guidelines on how to efficiently use the dataset, such as how to parallelize the training and evaluation processes. Finally, the authors should compare NARes with other existing datasets on adversarial robustness, such as RobustBench, to demonstrate the advantages and disadvantages of NARes. A detailed comparison with existing benchmarks would help to position NARes within the broader landscape of adversarial robustness research and highlight its unique contributions and limitations. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly enhance the value and impact of the NARes dataset and address the identified limitations.

## questions
Several questions arise from my analysis of this paper, focusing on key uncertainties and methodological choices. First, how do the findings generalize to other datasets beyond CIFAR-10, particularly larger and more complex datasets like ImageNet? The paper's reliance on CIFAR-10 raises concerns about the applicability of the observed trends to real-world scenarios. Second, can the authors provide more insights into the relationship between stable accuracy, Lipschitz constant, and adversarial robustness? While the paper notes correlations, a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms would be valuable. Specifically, how can these metrics be used to guide the design of more robust architectures? Third, how can the dataset be used to develop new NAS algorithms for finding robust architectures? The paper demonstrates the utility of NARes as a benchmark, but more specific guidance on how to leverage the dataset for NAS would be beneficial. What specific search strategies are most effective, and what are the limitations of using NARes for NAS? Fourth, what is the specific motivation for creating a large-scale dataset on adversarial robustness? What are the limitations of existing studies that the authors aim to address with this dataset? The paper argues for a comprehensive investigation, but a more detailed explanation of the specific gaps in the current understanding of architecture's role in adversarial robustness would be helpful. Fifth, how can the authors demonstrate the generalizability of NARes to other architectures and datasets? The current focus on WRNs and CIFAR-10 limits the scope of the dataset. What steps can be taken to expand the dataset to include other architectures and datasets, and how would this impact the generalizability of the findings? Sixth, how do different adversarial training methods affect the generalization of NARes? The paper only considers PGD for adversarial training. How can the authors address the limitation of only considering one type of AT in building the dataset, and what are the implications of this limitation for the use of NARes as a benchmark? Seventh, what are the potential biases in the NARes dataset, and how can these biases be mitigated? The dataset is constructed using a specific training procedure and evaluation protocol, which may introduce biases that affect the generalizability of the findings. Finally, how does the computational cost of building and using NARes compare to other similar datasets? The paper provides some information on the total computational cost, but a more detailed analysis of the computational resources required to train and evaluate models on the dataset would be valuable. These questions aim to clarify key uncertainties and methodological choices, and to guide future research directions based on the NARes dataset.
