## summary
This paper introduces D2T2 (Decision Transformer with Temporal Difference via Next-State Guidance), a novel approach designed to enhance the performance of Decision Transformers (DTs) in stochastic environments. The authors begin by theoretically analyzing the limitations of standard DTs, demonstrating that their performance degrades in stochastic settings due to the increasing variance of returns-to-go (RTG) accumulated over time. To address this issue, D2T2 integrates temporal difference (TD) learning with the DT framework. Instead of using RTG as the supervision signal, D2T2 employs a learned value function to predict a desired next state, which then guides the DT's action selection. This approach aims to reduce the variance of the supervision signal and shorten the prediction horizon, thereby improving the stability and performance of the DT in stochastic environments. The method involves training a value function using Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) and then using this value function to generate a guiding vector that steers the DT towards high-reward regions. The authors empirically evaluate D2T2 on a variety of tasks, including the Tailgate and FrozenLake environments, as well as more complex benchmarks from the D4RL suite. The results demonstrate that D2T2 outperforms standard DT and other baselines in these stochastic environments, highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The paper provides a clear motivation for the proposed method, a detailed explanation of the methodology, and empirical evidence supporting its claims. The core contribution lies in the innovative integration of TD learning with the DT framework, offering a promising direction for improving the robustness of DTs in real-world, stochastic scenarios. The authors' approach of using a learned value function to guide the DT's action selection represents a significant departure from traditional RTG-based supervision, and the empirical results suggest that this approach is effective in mitigating the challenges posed by stochasticity. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and well-executed study that makes a valuable contribution to the field of offline reinforcement learning.

## strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper is exceptionally well-written, with a clear and logical progression of ideas. The authors effectively articulate the limitations of standard Decision Transformers (DTs) in stochastic environments, providing a solid theoretical foundation for their proposed method. The motivation for integrating temporal difference (TD) learning with the DT framework is clearly presented, and the rationale behind using a learned value function to guide the DT's action selection is well-explained. The proposed method, D2T2, is both simple and intuitive, making it relatively easy to understand and implement. The authors provide a detailed explanation of the methodology, including the use of Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) for value function estimation and the generation of the guiding vector. The empirical evaluation of D2T2 is another significant strength. The authors conduct experiments on a variety of tasks, including the Tailgate and FrozenLake environments, as well as more complex benchmarks from the D4RL suite. The results consistently demonstrate that D2T2 outperforms standard DT and other baselines in stochastic environments, providing strong empirical support for the proposed approach. The inclusion of experiments on both simple and complex environments adds to the credibility of the findings. Furthermore, the authors provide a theoretical analysis of the performance of DTs in deterministic environments, which helps to contextualize the limitations of standard DTs and the advantages of D2T2. The paper's focus on addressing the challenges posed by stochasticity in offline reinforcement learning is highly relevant and timely. The innovative integration of TD learning with the DT framework represents a significant contribution to the field, offering a promising direction for improving the robustness of DTs in real-world scenarios. The authors' approach of using a learned value function to guide the DT's action selection is a novel departure from traditional RTG-based supervision, and the empirical results suggest that this approach is effective in mitigating the challenges posed by stochasticity. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated, well-executed, and well-written study that makes a valuable contribution to the field of offline reinforcement learning.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, I am not entirely convinced by the FrozenLake experiments. The performance of all methods, including D2T2, is relatively poor, with success rates never exceeding 80% even under low stochasticity (p=0.4). This raises concerns about whether the environment, as set up, is truly representative of a stochastic challenge where the proposed method should excel. The authors do not provide a justification for the specific parameters of the FrozenLake environment, such as the grid size or the probability of slippage, and it is unclear if these parameters are optimal for showcasing the strengths of D2T2. Furthermore, the paper lacks a comparison against a simple, rule-based baseline, such as always moving right, which could provide a better understanding of the absolute performance level. This absence makes it difficult to assess whether the reported success rates are meaningful or simply reflect the inherent difficulty of the task. Second, while the paper demonstrates that D2T2 performs well in stochastic environments, the performance of D2T2 in the deterministic domains is very similar to that of DT. This is expected given the theoretical analysis of deterministic environments, but it also highlights a limitation of the proposed method. The paper does not explore the impact of stochasticity in the reward function, which is a crucial aspect of real-world scenarios. The authors' theoretical analysis primarily focuses on the variance of RTG due to stochastic transitions, but it does not explicitly address how stochastic rewards would affect DT's performance. I believe that experiments in which the transition function is deterministic, but the reward function is stochastic, would help to better understand the advantages of D2T2 over DT. Specifically, a more controlled experiment where the reward is stochastic but the transitions are deterministic would isolate the effect of reward stochasticity on the performance of both methods. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the conditions under which D2T2 offers a significant advantage. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's experiments do not isolate the effect of stochastic rewards, and the theoretical analysis primarily focuses on transition stochasticity. Third, the authors argue that the worse performance of DT in stochastic environments is due to the variance of RTG, but they fail to provide direct empirical evidence to support this claim. While the paper includes an experiment on the Tailgate Driving task where it compares DT with VDT (Value-conditioned Decision Transformer), which replaces RTG with a value function, this is only indirect evidence. The authors do not include experiments that directly manipulate the variance of RTG while keeping other factors constant. This lack of direct empirical evidence weakens the motivation for D2T2. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how the proposed method addresses the stitching problem, if at all, and how it compares to existing methods that explicitly tackle this issue. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper provides a theoretical argument and some indirect evidence, but lacks direct empirical support for the RTG variance argument. Fourth, the authors propose to use a learned value function to reduce the variance of RTG, but they fail to discuss the limitations of this approach. For example, if the value function is learned via Q-learning, there is no guarantee that it will be accurate, especially in complex environments. In such a case, it is possible that D2T2 will perform worse than DT. The paper does not include experiments that systematically vary the accuracy of the value function and analyze its effect on D2T2's performance. Additionally, the authors do not discuss the computational cost of learning the value function, and how this cost compares to the computational cost of training DT. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a discussion on value function limitations, a sensitivity analysis, and a computational cost comparison. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of D2T2. It would be helpful to understand how the computational cost of D2T2 compares to that of other methods, especially in terms of training time and memory requirements. Specifically, the paper should provide a breakdown of the computational cost associated with the temporal difference learning component and the decision transformer component, and how these costs scale with the size of the state and action spaces, as well as the length of the trajectories. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to understand the impact of the guiding vector dimension on the overall computational cost. The paper also does not discuss the sensitivity of D2T2 to hyperparameters. It would be helpful to understand how the performance of D2T2 is affected by different hyperparameter settings. For example, how does the performance vary with different learning rates for the temporal difference learning component and the decision transformer component? What is the impact of the discount factor on the performance of D2T2? A more detailed analysis of the sensitivity to these hyperparameters is needed to understand the robustness of the method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a computational complexity analysis and a hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to address the concerns regarding the FrozenLake environment, the authors should explore alternative state representations or environments that are more conducive to demonstrating the strengths of the proposed method. For instance, using a continuous state space approximation of the FrozenLake environment, or a different grid-world environment with more complex reward structures, could potentially highlight the advantages of D2T2 more clearly. Additionally, it would be helpful to compare the performance of the methods against a simple, yet effective, baseline for the FrozenLake environment, such as an optimal policy derived from dynamic programming. This would provide a clearer understanding of how well each method is performing relative to the optimal solution, and would help to determine if the poor performance is due to the limitations of the methods or the environment itself. Furthermore, it would be useful to investigate the sensitivity of the methods to different levels of stochasticity in the FrozenLake environment, as this could reveal the conditions under which D2T2 truly excels. Second, to further investigate the advantages of D2T2 in stochastic environments, it is crucial to conduct experiments where the transition function is deterministic, but the reward function is stochastic. This can be achieved by introducing noise to the reward function, such as adding a random variable to the reward at each step. This would allow for a more controlled analysis of the impact of reward stochasticity on the performance of both DT and D2T2. For example, one could consider a scenario where the reward is either +1 or -1 with some probability, or where the reward is a continuous value with some added noise. This would help to isolate the effect of reward stochasticity and provide a more nuanced understanding of the conditions under which D2T2 offers a significant advantage over DT. It would also be beneficial to explore different types of reward stochasticity, such as additive noise versus multiplicative noise, to see how each affects the performance of the methods. Third, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the relationship between RTG variance and DT performance. This could involve experiments where the variance of RTG is explicitly controlled, for example, by adding noise to the RTG or by using different methods to estimate it. The authors should also analyze how the variance of RTG affects the stitching ability of DT, and whether reducing the variance of RTG directly leads to better stitching. Furthermore, the authors should compare the performance of D2T2 with existing methods that explicitly address the stitching problem, such as those that use trajectory segmentation or other techniques to improve the consistency of stitched trajectories. This would help to clarify the specific advantages and disadvantages of D2T2 compared to other approaches. Fourth, to address the limitations of using a learned value function, the authors should conduct experiments where the accuracy of the value function is varied. This could be achieved by training the value function with different amounts of data, or by using different learning algorithms that produce value functions with varying degrees of accuracy. The authors should then analyze how the performance of D2T2 changes as the accuracy of the value function changes. This would provide a more complete understanding of the robustness of D2T2 to errors in the value function. Additionally, the authors should explore alternative methods for reducing the variance of RTG, such as using ensemble methods or other techniques for uncertainty estimation. This would provide a more comprehensive analysis of the different approaches for addressing the variance of RTG, and would help to identify the most effective methods for improving the performance of DT in stochastic environments. Fifth, the paper would benefit from a more thorough analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed D2T2 method. Specifically, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the computational cost associated with each component of the algorithm, including the temporal difference learning and the decision transformer. This analysis should consider how the computational cost scales with the size of the state and action spaces, the length of the trajectories, and the dimension of the guiding vector. Furthermore, it would be helpful to compare the computational cost of D2T2 with that of other state-of-the-art offline reinforcement learning methods. This comparison should include both training time and memory requirements. Finally, in addition to the computational complexity analysis, the paper should also include a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of D2T2 to hyperparameters. The authors should investigate how the performance of D2T2 is affected by different learning rates for the temporal difference learning component and the decision transformer component. It would be beneficial to provide a sensitivity analysis that shows how the performance varies with different learning rates, and to identify the optimal learning rate for each component. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of the discount factor on the performance of D2T2. It would be helpful to provide a plot that shows how the performance varies with different discount factors, and to identify the optimal discount factor for the method. This analysis should also consider the impact of other hyperparameters, such as the batch size and the number of training epochs. A more thorough analysis of the sensitivity to hyperparameters would help readers to better understand the robustness of the method and to apply it effectively in different settings.
