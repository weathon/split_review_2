## summary
This paper introduces Graph-Induced Sum-Product Networks (GSPNs), a novel probabilistic framework for graph representation learning. The core idea is to leverage the structure of message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) to construct a hierarchy of sum-product networks (SPNs). These SPNs are designed to capture the conditional dependencies between vertex attributes within a graph, enabling the model to answer probabilistic queries and learn from unlabeled data. The authors propose that by using SPNs, GSPNs can overcome some of the limitations of traditional deep graph networks (DGNs), such as their inability to handle uncertainty and missing data effectively. The method involves constructing a computational tree for each vertex in the graph, where each node in the tree is associated with an SPN. The parameters of these SPNs are learned through backpropagation by maximizing a pseudo log-likelihood objective. The parameters of sum units in a node's SPN are learnable transformations of the posterior probabilities of the sum units in the children's SPNs, creating a hierarchical probabilistic model. The paper presents experiments in three settings: scarce supervision, missing data, and graph classification. In the scarce supervision setting, the authors demonstrate that GSPNs can learn useful vertex embeddings from unlabeled data, which can then be used for downstream classification tasks. In the missing data setting, they show that GSPNs can effectively handle missing vertex attributes by computing marginal probabilities. Finally, in the graph classification setting, they show that GSPNs achieve competitive performance compared to other graph representation learning methods. The authors claim that GSPNs offer a more interpretable and tractable approach to graph representation learning compared to DGNs, while also providing the ability to handle uncertainty and missing data. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution by integrating probabilistic modeling with graph neural networks, offering a new perspective on graph representation learning.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The most significant strength lies in the originality of the proposed GSPN framework. The idea of combining probabilistic circuits, specifically sum-product networks, with message-passing graph neural networks is both novel and insightful. This integration allows the model to inherit the strengths of both approaches: the ability to handle uncertainty and perform probabilistic inference from SPNs, and the capacity to learn complex representations from graph data using MPNNs. This is a significant step forward in graph representation learning, as it addresses some of the limitations of traditional deep graph networks, which often struggle with uncertainty and missing data. The paper is also well-written and organized, providing a clear explanation of the background, related work, methodology, and experiments. The authors do a good job of motivating the need for a probabilistic approach to graph representation learning and clearly articulating the advantages of their proposed method. The use of appropriate notations and figures helps to illustrate the concepts and results, making the paper accessible to a broad audience. Furthermore, the authors conduct extensive experiments on various datasets and tasks, demonstrating the competitiveness of GSPNs with both neural and probabilistic baselines. The experiments cover a range of scenarios, including scarce supervision, missing data, and graph classification, which provides a comprehensive evaluation of the model's capabilities. The results show that GSPNs are not only competitive but often superior to the baselines in terms of performance, robustness, and efficiency. The ability of GSPNs to handle missing data and answer probabilistic queries is a particularly noteworthy achievement, as it opens up new possibilities for applying graph representation learning in real-world applications where data is often incomplete or uncertain. ## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should include a more detailed mathematical description of the GSPN architecture. This should include a step-by-step breakdown of the computations, starting from the input graph, through the message-passing layers, the construction and evaluation of the SPNs, and finally to the output. Specifically, the authors should provide equations that describe how the parameters of the SPNs are learned and how these parameters are used to compute the probabilistic queries. A clear explanation of how the GNN parameters and SPN parameters are jointly optimized during training is also necessary. This would greatly enhance the clarity and reproducibility of the work. Furthermore, a visual representation of the computational flow, perhaps in the form of a diagram, could complement the mathematical description and make the method more accessible. Second, to better evaluate the method's ability to approximate the true data distribution, the authors should conduct experiments on synthetic datasets where the ground truth distribution is known. This would allow for a more direct assessment of the model's performance in terms of likelihood estimation or other relevant metrics. For example, a synthetic dataset could be generated from a mixture of Gaussians or other well-understood distributions, and the GSPN's ability to recover these distributions could be evaluated. This would provide a more rigorous validation of the method's probabilistic modeling capabilities. Additionally, it would be beneficial to include a comparison with other probabilistic models, such as variational autoencoders or Gaussian mixture models, on these synthetic datasets to better understand the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. Third, to provide more empirical evidence for the claim that GSPNs can tractably answer probabilistic queries, the authors should include experiments that explicitly demonstrate the model's ability to perform tasks such as computing marginal probabilities of node or edge attributes, or performing conditional inference given partial observations. These experiments should compare the performance of GSPNs with existing probabilistic graph models, such as Markov Random Fields or Probabilistic Graphical Models, to highlight the advantages and limitations of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the authors should clarify the specific types of probabilistic queries that GSPNs can handle effectively and discuss any limitations in terms of query complexity or graph size. This would help to better understand the practical applicability of the proposed framework. Fourth, the authors should include a more thorough analysis of the model's sensitivity to hyperparameter choices. This should include a discussion of how the number of layers, the number of states in the categorical latent variable, and the choice of aggregation function affect the model's performance. The paper should also discuss the computational complexity of the proposed model, especially in relation to the size of the input graphs. While the paper mentions that the complexity is linear in the number of edges, it should provide a more detailed analysis of the memory requirements and the practical scalability of the model to large graphs. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the interpretability of the learned representations. While the model is probabilistic, it is not clear how the learned parameters and transformations can be interpreted in terms of the underlying graph structure and attributes. The paper should provide some analysis of the learned representations and discuss how they can be used to gain insights into the data. Finally, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with other probabilistic models for graphs. While the paper compares the proposed model with neural models, it should also compare it with other probabilistic approaches such as Bayesian networks and Markov networks. The paper should discuss the advantages and disadvantages of each approach in terms of their expressiveness, computational complexity, and ability to handle uncertainty and missing data. For example, the paper could discuss how the proposed model compares to a Bayesian network in terms of its ability to capture conditional dependencies between variables, or how it compares to a Markov network in terms of its ability to model the joint distribution over the graph. This comparison would help to contextualize the proposed model within the broader landscape of probabilistic graph models and highlight its unique contributions. In addition, the authors should provide more details on the SPN construction and parameterization, including the specific types of distribution units used in the base SPNs and the exact implementation of the learnable transformations. The optimization details should also be included in the main method section.
