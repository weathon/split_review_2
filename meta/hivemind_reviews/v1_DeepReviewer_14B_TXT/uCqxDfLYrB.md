## summary
This paper presents a comprehensive investigation into the scaling laws of time series foundation models (TSFMs), focusing on both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) performance. The authors explore how model parameters, computational resources, and dataset size impact the performance of TSFMs, specifically examining encoder-only and decoder-only Transformer architectures. Through extensive experimentation, they find that while scaling these factors generally improves both ID and OOD performance, certain architectural choices that enhance ID performance may hinder OOD scalability. The study also includes case studies of state-of-the-art models like Moirai and Chronos, comparing their scaling behavior to the baseline Transformer architectures. The core methodological approach involves training and evaluating a range of Transformer models with varying parameter counts, compute budgets, and dataset sizes on both ID and OOD time series data. The authors employ a power-law fitting methodology to characterize the scaling behavior of these models across different metrics, such as MAPE and log-likelihood loss. The main empirical findings reveal that encoder-only Transformers generally exhibit better scalability than decoder-only variants, and that while advanced architectures like Moirai and Chronos improve ID performance, they do not necessarily enhance OOD generalization. Based on these findings, the authors provide practical design principles for developing scalable TSFMs, emphasizing the importance of large datasets and computational resources for robust generalization. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution by empirically demonstrating the scaling laws of TSFMs in both ID and OOD settings, highlighting the trade-offs between ID and OOD performance, and providing practical guidelines for designing scalable TSFMs. The study's focus on OOD performance is particularly valuable, as it addresses a critical gap in the understanding of how TSFMs generalize to unseen data distributions. The paper's findings have important implications for the development of more robust and generalizable time series forecasting models, which are crucial for a wide range of real-world applications. However, the study also has limitations, particularly in its focus on univariate time series and Transformer-based architectures, which I will discuss in detail in the weaknesses section.

## weaknesses
Despite its strengths, this paper has several weaknesses that limit the generalizability and practical implications of its findings. First, the study primarily focuses on encoder-only and decoder-only Transformer architectures, which might limit the generalizability of the findings to other TSFM architectures. As the paper states, "For our baseline models, we selected two widely adopted architectures: the encoder-only Transformers and the decoder-only Transformers." (Section 2.2). The paper does not explore how the observed scaling laws might change when dealing with architectures that incorporate convolutional layers, recurrent layers, or attention mechanisms in different configurations. This lack of exploration into hybrid architectures or those with more complex attention mechanisms leaves a gap in understanding the broader landscape of TSFM scaling. The paper's conclusions about the trade-offs between ID and OOD performance might not hold for architectures that incorporate convolutional layers, recurrent layers, or attention mechanisms in different configurations. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world TSFMs employ diverse architectural designs. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper explicitly defines its scope as focusing on encoder-only and decoder-only Transformers, and the experiments are limited to these architectures and two specific Transformer-based models. (Section 3.1). The forecast horizon is not explicitly defined or varied in the experimental setup. The choice of context window and forecast horizon can significantly affect model performance, and it is unclear how these factors interact with the observed scaling laws. For instance, longer context windows might reveal different scaling patterns compared to shorter ones, and the optimal window length could vary depending on the specific time series characteristics. This is a critical omission, as the temporal context and prediction horizon are crucial factors in time series forecasting. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the experimental design focuses on scaling model parameters, compute, and dataset size, without systematically varying context window length or forecast horizon. Furthermore, hybrid architectures that combine these elements should be considered. For example, a model that uses convolutional layers for initial feature extraction followed by a Transformer encoder could be evaluated. Such an approach would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how different architectural choices impact scaling behavior and generalization capabilities. The study should also explore the impact of different pre-training strategies on the scaling laws, as this could significantly affect the observed performance trends. By including a more diverse set of architectures, the study can provide more robust and widely applicable guidelines for designing scalable TSFMs. Second, the study should investigate the impact of different context window lengths and forecast horizons on the scaling behavior of TSFMs. This could involve conducting experiments with different context window lengths and forecast horizons and analyzing how the scaling exponents change. For instance, do encoder-only and decoder-only architectures exhibit different sensitivities to these factors? Understanding these interactions is crucial for designing TSFMs that can effectively handle time series with varying characteristics. The analysis should also consider the computational cost associated with different context window lengths and forecast horizons, as this could impact the practical applicability of the proposed scaling laws. Third, to address the limitation of focusing solely on univariate time series, future work should investigate the scaling behavior of TSFMs in multivariate settings. This would involve analyzing how the number of input time series affects the scaling laws for both encoder-only and decoder-only architectures.
