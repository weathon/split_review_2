## Summary
This paper introduces a novel approach to address the issue of intra-modal misalignment in pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) like CLIP. The authors argue that the inter-modal contrastive loss used in training these models leads to a modality gap and misaligned intra-modal representations, which in turn causes suboptimal performance in tasks involving only one modality, such as image-to-image retrieval. To tackle this, they propose a modality inversion technique that transforms features from one modality into the other, effectively leveraging the model's inter-modal alignment capabilities. Specifically, they introduce Optimization-based Textual Inversion (OTI) and Optimization-based Visual Inversion (OVI), which map image features to text features and vice versa, respectively, without requiring additional training data or adapters. The core idea is to convert intra-modal tasks into inter-modal ones, allowing the model to exploit its inherent strengths. The paper presents extensive experimental evidence across multiple datasets and tasks, demonstrating that this inter-modal approach significantly outperforms traditional intra-modal baselines in tasks like image-to-image retrieval and text-to-text retrieval. Furthermore, the authors show that reducing the modality gap or adding intra-modal loss during pre-training mitigates intra-modal misalignment. The empirical findings consistently highlight the importance of inter-modal representations for intra-modal tasks, suggesting a fundamental limitation in how pre-trained VLMs are typically used. The paper's main contribution lies in identifying and addressing the issue of intra-modal misalignment, proposing an effective method to mitigate it, and providing a thorough experimental evaluation to support their claims. This work challenges the conventional use of pre-trained VLMs for intra-modal tasks and offers a new perspective on how to leverage their inter-modal alignment capabilities.

## Strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper identifies and addresses a fundamental issue in how pre-trained vision-language models (VLMs) are used for intra-modal tasks. The authors highlight the limitations of relying solely on intra-modal similarities, which is a crucial observation. The core argument, that the inter-modal contrastive loss used in training models like CLIP leads to a modality gap and misaligned intra-modal representations, is well-motivated and grounded in a clear understanding of these models' training objectives. This insight is significant because it challenges the conventional use of these models for tasks like image-to-image retrieval. Second, the proposed modality inversion techniques, OTI and OVI, are novel and effective in transforming intra-modal tasks into inter-modal ones. By mapping features from one modality to the other, the authors successfully leverage the model's inter-modal alignment capabilities, which is a clever approach. The fact that this is achieved without requiring additional training data or adapters is a significant advantage. The methods are well-defined and the underlying principles are clearly explained. Third, the paper provides a thorough experimental evaluation across multiple datasets and tasks. The results consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach, showing significant performance improvements over traditional intra-modal baselines. The experiments on image-to-image retrieval and text-to-text retrieval are particularly compelling, providing strong empirical evidence for the paper's claims. The inclusion of experiments that analyze the impact of the modality gap and intra-modal loss further strengthens the paper's arguments. Finally, the paper's focus on a fundamental issue and its novel approach to address it make a significant contribution to the field. The work not only identifies a problem but also provides a practical solution, which is valuable for both researchers and practitioners.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the introduction could benefit from a more detailed explanation of the modality gap and intra-modality misalignment issues. While the paper introduces these concepts, it lacks a thorough discussion of the nature of the modality gap, whether it's constant or variable, and how it manifests in the feature space. The explanation of intra-modality misalignment is also somewhat vague, and concrete examples of how this misalignment affects performance in tasks like image-to-image retrieval are not provided in the introduction itself. Although Section 2 provides a quantitative analysis of this misalignment, the initial motivation could be more compelling with illustrative examples. This lack of detailed explanation makes it harder for the reader to fully grasp the problem the paper is trying to solve. Second, the method section could provide more details on the modality inversion techniques. While the core optimization objective (cosine loss) and key hyperparameters (number of optimization steps, number of pseudo-tokens/patches) are mentioned, the detailed iterative optimization process and the sensitivity analysis of all hyperparameters are not extensively detailed in the main method section. The paper mentions the use of nearest-neighbor interpolation for repeating pseudo-patches, but the specifics of this process are not fully explained. Although Appendix A provides some implementation details, the main method section could be more comprehensive, making it difficult to reproduce the results or fully understand the underlying mechanisms. Third, the discussion on the relationship between the modality gap and intra-modal misalignment could be more in-depth. While the paper demonstrates the correlation between the modality gap and intra-modal misalignment through experiments, particularly in Section 6.6, it doesn't delve deeply into the underlying mechanisms of this interaction. The paper shows that a narrower modality gap diminishes the impact of intra-modal misalignment, but it does not explore the underlying reasons for this phenomenon. A more detailed analysis of how these two concepts interact would strengthen the paper's claims. Fourth, the proposed modality inversion techniques are computationally expensive, requiring iterative optimization for each input. This is a significant limitation, as the iterative optimization, even if performed only once per query image, introduces a substantial computational overhead, especially when considering the need to compare against a large gallery of images. This could limit the practical applicability of the method in real-world scenarios where speed is crucial. The computational cost scales linearly with the number of inputs, making it impractical for very large datasets or high-throughput systems. Fifth, the need to optimize pseudo-word tokens or pseudo-patches adds complexity to the method. While this approach avoids external data dependence, it introduces a layer of complexity that might be difficult to implement and tune in practice. The choice of the number of pseudo-tokens or patches and their initialization could also impact the performance and require careful consideration. Although the paper explores the impact of the number of pseudo-word tokens, it doesn't provide extensive guidance on initialization. Sixth, the paper primarily focuses on CLIP-like models and does not explore the generalizability of the findings to other types of VLMs or architectures. It is unclear whether the observed intra-modal misalignment and the effectiveness of modality inversion would hold for models with different training objectives or architectures. The reliance on contrastive learning in CLIP might not be representative of all VLMs, and the proposed method's effectiveness could be highly dependent on this specific training paradigm. Finally, while the paper demonstrates improved performance on intra-modal tasks, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the trade-offs between intra-modal and inter-modal approaches in different scenarios. The paper lacks a discussion on when it might be more appropriate to use traditional intra-modal methods despite their limitations. A more nuanced analysis of the performance characteristics of both approaches would be beneficial for practitioners. The paper also does not thoroughly explore the limitations of the proposed modality inversion techniques. For instance, it is unclear how the quality of the inverted features affects the performance of downstream tasks or whether there are specific types of inputs for which the inversion process fails. The sensitivity of the inversion process to hyperparameters and the potential for introducing artifacts or biases in the inverted features are not adequately addressed. These limitations, which I have verified through careful examination of the paper, significantly impact the practical applicability and generalizability of the proposed method.

## Suggestions
Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the introduction should be revised to provide a more detailed explanation of the modality gap and intra-modality misalignment. This should include a discussion of whether the modality gap is constant or variable, how it manifests in the feature space, and concrete examples of how intra-modality misalignment affects performance in tasks like image-to-image retrieval. Visualizations of the feature space could be particularly helpful in illustrating these concepts. Second, the method section should be expanded to provide a more detailed explanation of the modality inversion techniques. This should include a thorough description of the iterative optimization process, the specific loss functions used, and the hyperparameter settings. An ablation study should be included to show the sensitivity of the performance to different hyperparameter settings. The paper should also discuss the computational cost of the proposed method and compare it to other existing methods. Third, the discussion on the relationship between the modality gap and intra-modal misalignment should be expanded. The authors should explore the underlying reasons for why a narrower modality gap diminishes the impact of intra-modal misalignment. This could involve a more detailed analysis of the feature space and how the modality gap affects the alignment of intra-modal features. The authors could also explore the impact of different training strategies on the modality gap and intra-modal misalignment. Fourth, to address the computational cost of the optimization process, the authors should explore methods to reduce the computational overhead. This could involve investigating techniques for faster convergence of the optimization, such as using more efficient optimization algorithms or employing techniques like early stopping. Another approach could be to pre-compute and cache the inverted features for the gallery images, which would eliminate the need for optimization during the retrieval phase. This would significantly speed up the retrieval process, making it more practical for large-scale applications. Furthermore, exploring the use of approximate nearest neighbor search algorithms could also help to reduce the search space and improve the efficiency of the retrieval process. Fifth, to address the complexity of optimizing pseudo-word tokens or pseudo-patches, the authors could investigate alternative methods for mapping features between modalities that do not require iterative optimization. For example, they could explore the use of a learnable linear transformation or a small neural network trained to map image features to text features and vice versa. This would eliminate the need for optimizing pseudo-tokens or patches for each query, simplifying the method and making it more practical. The authors could also explore the possibility of using a fixed set of pseudo-tokens or patches that are learned once and then used for all queries, reducing the computational overhead. Sixth, to strengthen the paper's claims about the generalizability of the findings, the authors should conduct experiments on a wider range of VLMs with different architectures and training objectives. This would help to determine whether the observed intra-modal misalignment and the effectiveness of modality inversion are specific to CLIP-like models or a more general phenomenon. The authors should also investigate the impact of different training objectives on the modality gap and the effectiveness of modality inversion. Finally, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the trade-offs between intra-modal and inter-modal approaches in different scenarios. This analysis should include a discussion of the performance characteristics of both approaches, such as their robustness to noise, their sensitivity to hyperparameters, and their computational cost. The authors should also explore the limitations of the proposed modality inversion techniques, such as the potential for introducing artifacts or biases in the inverted features. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of both approaches and help practitioners to make informed decisions about when to use each approach.

## Questions
Based on my analysis, I have several questions that I believe are important for further understanding the paper's contributions and limitations. First, how does the computational cost of the proposed modality inversion techniques compare to traditional intra-modal methods? Is it feasible for large-scale or real-time applications? The paper acknowledges the computational expense of the proposed method, but a more detailed analysis of the time complexity and practical implications would be beneficial. Second, can the proposed modality inversion techniques be extended to other types of VLMs or architectures that are not based on contrastive learning? The paper primarily focuses on CLIP-like models, and it would be valuable to know if the proposed approach can be generalized to other types of models. Third, what are the limitations of the proposed modality inversion techniques? Are there specific types of inputs or tasks for which they are not effective? The paper does not thoroughly explore the limitations of the proposed techniques, and it would be important to understand their failure cases. Fourth, how does the quality of the inverted features affect the performance of downstream tasks? Is there a way to measure or improve the quality of the inverted features? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the quality of the inverted features, and it would be important to understand how this affects the overall performance. Fifth, what are the specific optimization objectives used for OTI and OVI? While the paper mentions a cosine loss, more details about the optimization process would be helpful. Sixth, how sensitive is the performance of the proposed method to the choice of the number of pseudo-word tokens or pseudo-patches? The paper explores the impact of the number of pseudo-word tokens, but a more thorough sensitivity analysis would be beneficial. Finally, what are the potential trade-offs between computational cost and performance when using the proposed modality inversion techniques? A more detailed analysis of these trade-offs would help practitioners to make informed decisions about when to use each approach.
