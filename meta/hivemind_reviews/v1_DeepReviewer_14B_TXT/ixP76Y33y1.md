## summary
This paper delves into the intriguing relationship between the intrinsic dimension of datasets and the generalization performance of deep neural networks, with a particular focus on the disparities observed between natural and medical imaging domains. The authors propose that the higher 'label sharpness' of medical image datasets, a measure of how rapidly the label changes with respect to changes in the input space, contributes to the faster growth of generalization error with intrinsic dimension compared to natural images. They introduce a theoretical framework that connects the intrinsic dimension of the data manifold, the label sharpness, and the generalization error, providing a scaling law that encapsulates these relationships. The core methodological approach involves estimating the intrinsic dimension of both the input data and the learned feature representations using methods like MLE and TwoNN, and calculating the label sharpness based on the Lipschitz constant of the ground truth labeling function. Empirically, the authors validate their theoretical claims by training various CNN architectures on a diverse set of natural and medical image datasets for binary classification tasks. They demonstrate that the generalization error increases more rapidly with intrinsic dimension for medical images than for natural images, and that this discrepancy is correlated with the higher label sharpness observed in medical image datasets. Furthermore, they explore the connection between label sharpness and adversarial robustness, finding that models trained on datasets with higher label sharpness are more vulnerable to adversarial attacks. The paper also extends the analysis to the intrinsic dimension of learned representations, showing that the dataset intrinsic dimension serves as an approximate upper bound for the representation intrinsic dimension. Overall, this work provides valuable insights into the factors influencing generalization performance in different imaging domains, highlighting the importance of considering both the intrinsic dimension and label sharpness of datasets when training deep learning models. The findings have implications for the design of more robust and generalizable models, particularly in the medical imaging domain where adversarial vulnerability is a significant concern.

## strengths
I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The introduction of the 'label sharpness' metric is a novel and insightful contribution, providing a quantitative measure to explain the observed differences in generalization behavior between natural and medical images. This metric, defined as the Lipschitz constant of the ground truth labeling function, offers a way to capture the complexity of the decision boundary in the input space. The authors' theoretical framework, which connects the intrinsic dimension, label sharpness, and generalization error, is well-motivated and rigorously derived. The resulting scaling law provides a valuable tool for understanding how these factors interact to influence model performance. The empirical validation of the theoretical claims is another strength of the paper. The authors conduct thorough experiments on a diverse set of datasets, including multiple medical imaging modalities and natural image datasets, using various CNN architectures. The consistency of the results across different datasets and models strengthens the validity of their findings. Furthermore, the exploration of the relationship between label sharpness and adversarial robustness is a significant contribution, highlighting the practical implications of their work for developing more secure deep learning models. The finding that higher label sharpness is correlated with lower adversarial robustness is particularly relevant in the context of medical imaging, where model reliability is crucial. Finally, the extension of the analysis to the intrinsic dimension of learned representations, and the demonstration that the dataset intrinsic dimension serves as an upper bound for the representation intrinsic dimension, provides a deeper understanding of the learning process and the role of intrinsic dimension in both data and representations.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's exclusive focus on CNN architectures is a significant limitation. As the authors themselves acknowledge, they only evaluate six CNN models: ResNet-18, -34, -50, and VGG-13, -16, -19. This narrow focus raises concerns about the generalizability of their findings to other types of neural networks, particularly transformer-based architectures, which have become increasingly prevalent in various domains, including medical imaging. The paper lacks any discussion or experimentation to support the claim that the observed relationships between intrinsic dimension, label sharpness, and generalization would hold for these alternative architectures. This is a critical gap, as different architectures may exhibit different learning behaviors and sensitivities to dataset properties. Second, the paper's restriction to binary classification tasks is another significant limitation. The authors explicitly state that they define binary classification tasks for each dataset, and the definition of label sharpness is tailored for binary labels. This focus on binary classification limits the applicability of the findings to real-world problems, which often involve multi-class scenarios. The paper does not address how the proposed label sharpness metric and the derived scaling laws would extend to multi-class settings. This is a crucial omission, as the relationships between intrinsic dimension, label sharpness, and generalization could be significantly different in multi-class problems, and the current analysis does not provide any insights into these potential differences. Third, the paper lacks practical guidance on how to leverage the theoretical findings to improve the performance of deep neural networks. While the authors establish a theoretical framework and validate it empirically, they do not provide concrete recommendations for model design or training strategies. The paper does not discuss how the observed relationships between intrinsic dimension, label sharpness, and generalization can be used to guide architectural choices, hyperparameter tuning, or data augmentation strategies. This lack of practical guidance limits the impact of the work, as it is not clear how practitioners can use the theoretical insights to develop more effective models or training procedures. Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with estimating the intrinsic dimension and label sharpness. While the authors mention that the computation of label sharpness takes less than 1 second in their specific setup, they do not provide a general analysis of the time complexity of the algorithms used for these estimations. This is a concern, as the computational cost of these estimations could be a limiting factor for large-scale datasets. The paper lacks a discussion of how the computational cost scales with the size of the dataset and the dimensionality of the data, and it does not explore potential optimizations or approximations that could reduce the computational burden. Finally, the paper does not explore the impact of data augmentation techniques on the intrinsic dimension and label sharpness of the datasets. The authors explicitly state that they resize and normalize images but do not use any data augmentation techniques. This is a significant omission, as data augmentation is a common practice in deep learning and can significantly alter the underlying data manifold. The paper does not investigate how data augmentation techniques, such as rotations, scaling, and cropping, affect the estimated intrinsic dimension and label sharpness, and it does not explore whether these augmentations can be used to improve the generalization performance of models. These limitations, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the generalizability and practical applicability of the findings. I have high confidence in these limitations, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the absence of crucial discussions and experiments.
