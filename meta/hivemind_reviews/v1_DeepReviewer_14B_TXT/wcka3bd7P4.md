## Summary
This paper introduces FROND (Fractional-Order Graph Neural Dynamical Network), a novel framework for continuous graph neural networks (GNNs) that leverages Caputo fractional derivatives to capture long-term dependencies and mitigate the oversmoothing problem. Unlike traditional continuous GNNs that rely on integer-order differential equations, FROND employs fractional derivatives, which have non-local properties, allowing for a more nuanced feature update process. The authors provide a theoretical foundation for their approach, including a non-Markovian random walk interpretation of the node feature updating process. They demonstrate that the fractional order, denoted by β, can be any positive real number, offering a flexible way to control the extent of memory in the feature updates. The core idea is that by using fractional derivatives, the model can move beyond the Markovian update mechanisms of conventional integer-order models, enabling more effective graph representation learning. The paper presents a detailed theoretical analysis, including a discussion of the Caputo fractional derivative and its properties, as well as a demonstration of how FROND can mitigate oversmoothing. The authors also provide a non-Markovian random walk interpretation of the node feature updating process, which helps to understand the behavior of the proposed method. Empirically, the authors validate the FROND framework by comparing the fractional adaptations of various established integer-order continuous GNNs, such as GRAND, on a variety of datasets. The experimental results show that FROND consistently improves the performance of these baseline models, demonstrating its potential as an effective extension to enhance traditional continuous GNNs. The authors conduct experiments on node classification tasks, showing that FROND achieves better performance than the integer-order counterparts. They also perform an ablation study on the selection of the fractional order β, demonstrating that the choice of β has a significant impact on the model's performance. The paper also explores the computational cost of the proposed method, showing that it can be slower than the integer-order counterparts due to the iterative nature of the fractional solvers. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field of graph neural networks by introducing a novel approach that leverages fractional calculus to address the oversmoothing problem and capture long-term dependencies. The theoretical analysis and empirical validation provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method, highlighting its potential for further development and application in various domains.

## Strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative application of fractional calculus to graph neural networks. The introduction of Caputo fractional derivatives to model feature updates in GNNs is a novel approach that addresses the limitations of traditional integer-order models, particularly the oversmoothing problem. This is a significant contribution, as it provides a new way to capture long-term dependencies in graph data, moving beyond the Markovian update mechanisms of existing methods. The theoretical analysis is another strong point, with the authors providing a clear explanation of the Caputo fractional derivative and its properties. The non-Markovian random walk interpretation of the node feature updating process is particularly insightful, as it provides a deeper understanding of the behavior of the proposed method. This theoretical grounding adds credibility to the proposed framework and helps to justify its effectiveness. Furthermore, the paper demonstrates the compatibility of FROND with existing integer-order continuous GNNs, showing that it can be used as an effective extension to enhance their performance. This is a valuable contribution, as it allows researchers to easily incorporate the proposed method into existing frameworks. The experimental results are also compelling, with the authors demonstrating that FROND consistently improves the performance of various established integer-order continuous GNNs across diverse datasets. This empirical validation provides strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method and highlights its potential for practical applications. The ablation study on the selection of the fractional order β is also a strength, as it demonstrates the impact of this parameter on the model's performance and provides insights into how to choose an appropriate value for it. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a wide audience. The authors provide clear explanations of the proposed method and its theoretical underpinnings, as well as detailed descriptions of the experimental setup and results. Overall, the paper presents a novel and well-motivated approach to graph neural networks, with strong theoretical foundations and compelling empirical validation. The introduction of fractional derivatives to address oversmoothing and capture long-term dependencies is a significant contribution to the field, and the paper's clear presentation and thorough analysis make it a valuable addition to the literature.

## Weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses need to be addressed. First, a significant limitation is the restriction of the proposed method to undirected graphs. The paper explicitly defines the graph as undirected in Section 2, stating, "We denote an undirected graph as  $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V}, \bW)$ without self-loops..." This assumption is crucial to the formulation of the random walk Laplacian, $\bL = \bI - \bW\bD^{-1}$, which relies on the symmetry of the weight matrix $\bW$. The lack of discussion or experimental validation on directed graphs is a major drawback, as many real-world networks, such as social networks with follower relationships or citation networks, are inherently directed. This limits the applicability of the proposed method to a subset of real-world problems. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is explicitly stated in the paper and is a fundamental assumption of the method. Second, the paper does not provide a thorough analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. While the supplementary material (Section A.3) provides some computational time analysis, it is not directly compared with existing integer-order continuous GNNs in the main paper. The paper mentions that F-GRAND can be slower than GRAND due to the iterative nature of the fractional solvers, but a more detailed comparison is needed to understand the practical implications of this increased computational cost. This lack of explicit comparison makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the proposed method, especially for large graphs. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the main paper lacks a direct computational cost comparison, and the supplementary material only provides limited analysis. Third, while the paper claims that FROND does not introduce additional training parameters to the backbones, the fractional order $\beta$ and the time horizon $T$ are hyperparameters that need to be tuned. The tuning of these parameters can be computationally expensive, and the paper does not provide sufficient guidance on how to select appropriate values for them. The ablation study in Section 4.4 provides some insights into the impact of $\beta$, but it does not offer a comprehensive analysis of the sensitivity of the method to these hyperparameters. This lack of guidance makes it difficult to use the proposed method effectively in practice. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does provide some insights into the selection of $\beta$, but the need for tuning and the lack of clear guidelines is a valid concern. Fourth, the paper's experimental section does not focus on demonstrating state-of-the-art results on the absolute largest available graphs. While the paper includes experiments on several datasets, the largest graph used in the main paper's node classification experiments is "CoauthorCS" and "Photo", which are moderate-sized graphs. The ogbn-arxiv dataset is mentioned, but the results are in the supplementary material (Table 7). This lack of prominent results on very large datasets in the main paper suggests that the method may not perform as well on such graphs. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does include results on a larger dataset in the supplement, but the main paper's focus is not on achieving state-of-the-art results on the largest graphs. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and potential directions for future research. While the paper discusses related work, it does not explicitly outline the limitations of its own approach in comparison. The paper primarily focuses on the strengths and experimental validation, with less emphasis on a comprehensive discussion of limitations and future directions. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a dedicated section discussing the limitations and future research directions in detail.

## Suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should extend the proposed method to handle directed graphs. This could involve exploring different formulations of the fractional derivative that are suitable for directed graphs, potentially by considering separate fractional derivatives for incoming and outgoing edges or by adapting the formulation to account for the asymmetry in the adjacency matrix. This extension would significantly broaden the applicability of the proposed method to a wider range of real-world problems. Second, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. This should include a direct comparison of the computational time and memory requirements of FROND with existing integer-order continuous GNNs. The analysis should also include a breakdown of the computational cost of each step, such as the fractional derivative calculation, the message passing, and the parameter updates. This would provide a clearer understanding of the scalability of the proposed method and its practical limitations. Third, the authors should provide more guidance on how to select appropriate values for the hyperparameters $\beta$ and $T$. This could involve developing heuristics or rules of thumb for choosing these parameters based on the characteristics of the graph and the task at hand. A more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the method to these hyperparameters would also be beneficial. This would help users to effectively apply the proposed method in practice. Fourth, the authors should include experiments on larger datasets in the main paper to demonstrate the scalability of the proposed method. This could involve using datasets such as OGBN-arxiv, OGBN-products, or other large-scale graph datasets. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method's performance and its ability to handle large graphs. Fifth, the authors should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and potential directions for future research. This could involve discussing the challenges of applying FROND to dynamic graphs or graphs with evolving structures, as well as the potential impact of the choice of the time horizon $T$ on the model's performance. This would provide a more balanced view of the proposed method and its potential for future development. Finally, the authors should explore techniques to reduce the computational cost of the fractional derivative calculation, such as using approximations or sparse matrix operations. This would make the proposed method more practical for large-scale applications. These suggestions are directly connected to the identified weaknesses and offer concrete ways to improve the paper's overall quality and impact.
