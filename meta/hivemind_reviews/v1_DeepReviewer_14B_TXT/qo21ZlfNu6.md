## Summary
This paper introduces a novel data extraction attack, termed 'neural phishing,' which demonstrates the vulnerability of large language models (LLMs) to targeted memorization and extraction of sensitive information. The core idea behind neural phishing is that an adversary can inject carefully crafted, benign-appearing 'poisoned' data into the training dataset, prompting the model to memorize specific secrets, such as credit card numbers or social security numbers. This is achieved by leveraging vague priors about the structure of user data, allowing the adversary to induce the model to learn and retain these secrets. The attack is evaluated across three phases: poisoning, fine-tuning, and inference, showcasing that the attack can achieve high success rates, particularly when the model is larger or overtrained. The authors demonstrate that even with limited prior knowledge of the secret's prefix, the attack can be effective, and that random perturbations of the secret's prefix can even increase the attack's success rate. The paper explores the impact of model size, training data, and the timing of the attack, revealing that larger models and longer training durations can lead to higher success rates. The authors also briefly touch upon potential defenses, such as deduplication, but acknowledge their limitations. The findings highlight a significant security risk associated with training LLMs on user data, as it reveals a new attack vector that can be used to extract sensitive information. The paper's primary contribution lies in identifying and demonstrating the feasibility of this novel attack, which has significant implications for the security and privacy of LLMs. The authors' experiments, while conducted on synthetic data, provide compelling evidence of the attack's potential impact. The paper's exploration of the factors influencing the attack's success, such as model size and training duration, offers valuable insights into the vulnerabilities of LLMs. Overall, this paper presents a timely and important contribution to the field, raising critical concerns about the security of LLMs and the potential for malicious exploitation.
