## summary
This paper introduces OMNIINPUT, a novel model-centric evaluation framework designed to assess the quality of AI/ML model predictions across the entire input space, including regions not typically covered by traditional, data-centric evaluation methods. The core idea is to move away from relying solely on pre-defined test sets and instead construct a test set by sampling from the model's own output distribution. This is achieved through an efficient sampling technique, specifically the Gradient Wang-Landau (GWL) algorithm, which generates representative inputs for different output values. These sampled inputs are then selectively annotated by human evaluators, allowing for the estimation of the model's precision and recall at various output thresholds, ultimately leading to a comprehensive precision-recall curve. The authors argue that this approach provides a more fine-grained comparison between models, particularly when their performance is similar on standard test sets. The framework's practicality is demonstrated through experiments on various popular models and training methods, revealing new insights into model performance and generalizability. The experiments include image classification tasks on MNIST and CIFAR-10, as well as a sentiment classification task using a language model. The results highlight the framework's ability to uncover differences in model behavior that are not apparent with traditional evaluation metrics. The authors also discuss the potential of OMNIINPUT to provide new insights for training more robust and generalizable models. The paper's main contribution lies in its shift from data-centric to model-centric evaluation, offering a fresh perspective on how we assess the performance of AI systems. By focusing on the model's output distribution, OMNIINPUT aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of a model's strengths and weaknesses, particularly in regions of the input space that are often overlooked. The authors also emphasize the importance of this approach for enhancing AI safety and reliability, as it allows for a more thorough examination of a model's behavior across all possible inputs. The framework's ability to reveal subtle differences in model performance and its potential to guide the development of more robust models are presented as key advantages. However, the paper also acknowledges several limitations, including the computational cost of the sampling process, the reliance on human annotation, and the current focus on binary classification tasks. These limitations are discussed as areas for future work, and the authors suggest potential solutions to address them. Overall, the paper presents a compelling and innovative approach to model evaluation, with the potential to significantly impact the field of AI research and development.

## strengths
The primary strength of this paper lies in its introduction of OMNIINPUT, a genuinely novel model-centric evaluation framework. This approach represents a significant departure from traditional data-centric methods, which rely on pre-defined test sets. By constructing a test set based on the model's own output distribution, OMNIINPUT offers a more comprehensive and fine-grained assessment of model performance. This is particularly valuable for identifying subtle differences between models that might not be apparent using standard evaluation metrics. The framework's ability to explore the entire input space, including regions that are not typically covered by traditional datasets, is a notable advantage. This is crucial for understanding a model's behavior in a more holistic way and for identifying potential weaknesses or biases that might otherwise go unnoticed. The use of the Gradient Wang-Landau (GWL) sampler is another technical innovation, allowing for efficient sampling of representative inputs across the output distribution. This is a key component of the framework, as it enables the generation of a diverse set of test inputs that are tailored to the specific model being evaluated. The experimental results, while limited in scope, demonstrate the practicality of OMNIINPUT and its ability to reveal new insights into model performance. The experiments on MNIST, CIFAR-10, and a language model show that the framework can be applied to different types of data and models, and that it can uncover differences in model behavior that are not apparent with traditional evaluation methods. The authors' emphasis on the importance of this approach for enhancing AI safety and reliability is also a significant contribution. By providing a more thorough examination of a model's behavior across all possible inputs, OMNIINPUT has the potential to help identify and mitigate potential risks associated with the deployment of AI systems. The paper's clear and concise presentation of the framework, along with its detailed explanation of the sampling and annotation processes, makes it accessible to a wide audience. The authors also provide a thoughtful discussion of the limitations of the framework and suggest potential avenues for future research. Overall, the paper presents a compelling and innovative approach to model evaluation, with the potential to significantly impact the field of AI research and development. The shift from data-centric to model-centric evaluation is a valuable contribution, and the framework's ability to provide a more comprehensive understanding of model behavior is a key strength.

## weaknesses
While the paper presents a compelling and innovative framework, several weaknesses limit its current impact and practical applicability. A primary concern, consistently raised by multiple reviewers, is the scalability of the OMNIINPUT framework. The paper explicitly acknowledges this limitation, stating in Section 2.1 that "To handle larger input spaces and/or more complicated models, more efficient and scalable samplers are required. However, it is beyond the scope of this paper and we leave it as a future work." This lack of scalability is a significant issue, as it restricts the framework's applicability to small-scale tasks and datasets. The current implementation, which relies on the Gradient Wang-Landau (GWL) sampler, may not be sufficient for large-scale models or high-dimensional input spaces. The paper does not provide a clear path forward for addressing this issue, nor does it discuss the computational complexity of the sampling process in detail. This makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the framework for real-world applications. The absence of a detailed analysis of the computational resources required by the framework, including memory usage, processing time, and hardware requirements, further exacerbates this concern. This lack of information makes it challenging to evaluate the practical feasibility of the framework, especially when compared to traditional evaluation methods. Another significant weakness is the reliance on human annotation for the evaluation process. As stated in Section 2.1, "For our classifiers, we designate a specific class as the target class. The (human) evaluators would assign a score to each sample within the same “bin” of the output distribution..." While the authors claim that the annotation effort is less than traditional methods, the process is still subjective and time-consuming. The paper does not provide sufficient details on the number of annotators involved, their expertise, or the specific instructions given to them. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the reliability and reproducibility of the evaluation results. The paper briefly touches upon the issue of human annotation ambiguity in Section 3.3, stating that "To assess the ambiguity in human labeling, we examine the variations in $r(z)$ when three different individuals label the same dataset (Fig. 3)." However, this is a limited analysis, and the paper does not provide a formal measure of inter-annotator agreement or discuss how this bias might affect the evaluation results. The reliance on human annotation introduces a new source of bias, contradicting the paper's claim in the introduction that "OMNIINPUT samples the representative inputs solely by the model itself, eliminating possible human biases introduced by the test data collection process." The paper also lacks a thorough comparison of the OMNIINPUT framework with existing evaluation methods. While the authors claim that their method is superior, they do not provide sufficient evidence to support this claim. The paper presents results of traditional methods in Section 3.1, but focuses on their inconsistencies rather than a direct comparison of evaluation metrics with OMNIINPUT. The lack of a rigorous comparison makes it difficult to assess the true value of the proposed framework. The paper does not include a dedicated experimental section comparing OMNIINPUT's results with standard evaluation metrics (e.g., accuracy, precision, recall on fixed test sets, AUC) on the same datasets. This makes it impossible to definitively prove the superiority of the proposed framework. Furthermore, the paper's focus on binary classification tasks limits its applicability. The experiments in Section 3 and 4 primarily focus on binary classification, and the paper does not provide a detailed explanation of how the framework can be extended to multi-class classification tasks. This lack of a clear strategy for handling multiple classes significantly restricts the practical applicability of the proposed method. The paper also lacks a detailed explanation of the sampling method used to obtain the output distribution. While the paper mentions the GWL sampler and provides citations, it lacks a detailed explanation of its implementation, parameters, and convergence properties within the paper itself. This makes it harder to assess the reliability of the sampled output distribution. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed explanation of the evaluation process, including the number of human evaluators involved, the specific instructions given to them, and the measures taken to ensure consistency and reliability of their judgments. The current description lacks sufficient detail to allow for reproducibility or to assess the potential for variability in the evaluation results due to differences in human interpretation. These weaknesses, which are all supported by direct evidence from the paper, significantly limit the current impact and practical applicability of the OMNIINPUT framework. The lack of scalability, the reliance on human annotation, the absence of a thorough comparison with existing methods, the focus on binary classification, the limited explanation of the sampling method, and the lack of detail in the evaluation process all represent significant areas for improvement.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made to the OMNIINPUT framework. First and foremost, the scalability limitations must be addressed. The authors should explore more efficient sampling techniques that can handle larger input spaces and more complex models. This could involve investigating alternative sampling algorithms or developing methods for approximating the output distribution in high-dimensional spaces. For example, they could explore the use of variational autoencoders or generative adversarial networks to learn a lower-dimensional representation of the input space, which could then be used for sampling. Additionally, the authors should consider techniques for parallelizing the sampling process or using distributed computing to improve scalability. A detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method and a comparison with existing evaluation techniques would also be beneficial. This would help to clarify the practical limitations of the framework and identify areas for future improvement. Second, the reliance on human annotation needs to be mitigated. The authors should explore alternative approaches to reduce the subjectivity and potential bias introduced by human evaluators. One possible approach is to use a combination of human and automated evaluation methods. For example, they could use a pre-trained model to generate pseudo-labels for a subset of the data, which could then be used to train a model to predict the quality of the outputs. This would reduce the reliance on human annotation and make the evaluation process more objective. Additionally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the inter-annotator agreement and discuss the potential impact of this agreement on the evaluation results. This would help to quantify the level of subjectivity in the evaluation process and identify potential sources of bias. The authors should also consider using multiple human annotators and averaging their scores to reduce the impact of individual biases. Furthermore, the paper needs a more rigorous comparison with existing evaluation methods to demonstrate the value of the proposed framework. The authors should select a few representative evaluation methods and compare their performance with the OMNIINPUT framework on a range of datasets and models. This comparison should include both quantitative and qualitative analysis. For example, the authors could compare the precision-recall curves obtained using the OMNIINPUT framework with those obtained using traditional evaluation methods. They should also discuss the strengths and weaknesses of each method and identify the scenarios where the OMNIINPUT framework is most effective. This would help to establish the practical value of the proposed framework and provide a clear understanding of its advantages and limitations. The authors should also extend the framework to handle multi-class classification tasks. This could involve exploring different strategies for handling multiple classes, such as a one-vs-all approach for each class or a more sophisticated method that can handle multiple classes simultaneously. The paper should also discuss the computational complexity of the proposed method, especially when dealing with high-dimensional output spaces. A theoretical analysis of the proposed framework, including its convergence properties and error bounds, would also be beneficial. To address the lack of detail in the sampling method description, the authors should provide a more comprehensive explanation of the GWL sampler, including its parameters and convergence properties. This would help to assess the reliability and robustness of the generated output distribution. The paper should also discuss the potential impact of the sampling method on the evaluation results. For example, if the sampling method is not able to explore the entire output space, the evaluation results may be biased. The authors should also explore different sampling methods and compare their performance. Finally, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of the evaluation process, including the number of human evaluators involved, the specific instructions given to them, and the measures taken to ensure consistency and reliability of their judgments. This would allow for reproducibility and a better assessment of the potential for variability in the evaluation results due to differences in human interpretation. By addressing these weaknesses, the OMNIINPUT framework can be significantly improved and its practical applicability can be enhanced.
