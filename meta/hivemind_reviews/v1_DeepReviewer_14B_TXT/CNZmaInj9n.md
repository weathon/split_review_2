## summary
This paper introduces a unified perspective on Shapley value estimation, aiming to clarify the relationships between various existing methods. The authors propose that several popular algorithms, including semivalue, random order value, and least squares value, can be viewed as different mean estimators embedded within a common framework. This framework is defined by a linear transformation of the form $\phi^{uni}=T\tilde{\phi}+b$, where $\tilde{\phi}$ represents the expected value obtained from sampled subsets, and $T$ and $b$ are transformation parameters. The paper then leverages this unified perspective to develop a new amortized estimator called SimSHAP. SimSHAP learns a function that maps an input data point to the Shapley values of its features using least squares regression. The key differences between SimSHAP and a prior method, FastSHAP, lie in the choice of the metric matrix and the sampling method. Specifically, SimSHAP uses an identity matrix as the metric, while FastSHAP employs a special metric matrix. Additionally, SimSHAP uses a sampling method called Sim-Semivalue, which combines aspects of semivalue and least squares value. The authors claim that SimSHAP achieves comparable accuracy to FastSHAP while being faster, due to the removal of a normalization step required by FastSHAP. The empirical evaluation of SimSHAP includes experiments on both tabular and image datasets. For tabular data, the authors use three datasets and compare SimSHAP against other methods using metrics like Insertion and Deletion scores. For image data, the authors use the CIFAR-10 dataset and compare SimSHAP against other methods using similar metrics. The results suggest that SimSHAP performs competitively with FastSHAP and other methods, with a slight edge in terms of speed. However, the paper's primary contribution lies in the unified perspective on Shapley value estimation and the development of SimSHAP as a practical instantiation of this perspective. The authors argue that this unified view provides a novel direction for future research in Shapley value estimation, potentially leading to more efficient and accurate algorithms. While the paper presents a compelling theoretical framework and a promising new estimator, there are some limitations in the empirical validation and the clarity of certain explanations, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## strengths
One of the primary strengths of this paper is the novel unified perspective it offers on Shapley value estimation. By framing several existing methods as different mean estimators within a common framework, the authors provide a valuable insight into the interconnections between these methods. This unified view not only clarifies the relationships between different algorithms but also presents a fresh direction for future research in this area. I found this perspective to be particularly insightful, as it allows for a more holistic understanding of the various approaches to Shapley value estimation. The paper's mathematical formulation of the unified framework is clear and well-defined, making it easy to follow the authors' reasoning. The authors successfully demonstrate how semivalue, random order value, and least squares value can be expressed within this framework by substituting specific sampling distributions and coefficients. This rigorous mathematical treatment adds significant value to the paper. Furthermore, the introduction of SimSHAP as a practical instantiation of this unified perspective is another notable strength. SimSHAP is presented as a simple and fast amortized Shapley value estimator that learns a function to map input data points to their corresponding Shapley values. The authors clearly articulate the differences between SimSHAP and FastSHAP, highlighting the use of an identity matrix as the metric and a new sampling method called Sim-Semivalue. The claim that SimSHAP achieves comparable accuracy to FastSHAP while being faster is also a significant contribution. The empirical results, while limited in scope, do support this claim, showing that SimSHAP performs competitively with FastSHAP on both tabular and image datasets. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, making the complex concepts accessible to a broader audience. The authors provide clear explanations of the various methods and algorithms, and the mathematical notation is consistent and well-defined. The paper's overall organization is logical and coherent, which further enhances its readability. The authors also provide sufficient background information on Shapley values and their estimation, which allows readers to understand the context of the paper without prior expertise in the field. Finally, the paper's focus on practical applications is commendable. The authors not only present a theoretical framework but also develop a concrete algorithm that can be used in real-world scenarios. This practical focus adds to the paper's overall significance and impact.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, there are several weaknesses that I have identified through my analysis. One significant concern is the limited empirical validation of the proposed SimSHAP method, particularly for image data. The authors only use the CIFAR-10 dataset to evaluate SimSHAP's performance on image data. This is insufficient to demonstrate the general applicability of the method. As I have verified, the paper does not include experiments on other image datasets, such as ImageNet, which are commonly used in the field. This lack of diversity in the experimental setup raises concerns about the robustness of SimSHAP and its ability to generalize to more complex image datasets. The absence of experiments on other image datasets limits the conclusions that can be drawn about the practical utility of SimSHAP. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of SimSHAP compared to FastSHAP. While the authors claim that SimSHAP is faster, the results in Table 4 only show a marginal difference in inference time. The paper does not provide a rigorous analysis of the runtime, including a breakdown of the different computational steps. This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to fully assess the computational advantages of SimSHAP. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of why the removal of the normalization step in SimSHAP leads to better performance in practice. While the authors mention that SimSHAP does not require the computation of $v(\mathbf{N})$ and a subsequent normalization step, the practical implications of this omission are not fully explored. It is crucial to explain why enforcing the efficiency constraint through normalization is problematic in practice, and how the unconstrained formulation of SimSHAP avoids these issues. A more detailed analysis of the trade-offs between the constrained and unconstrained approaches would be beneficial. For instance, the authors could discuss the potential impact on the accuracy and stability of the estimated Shapley values when the efficiency constraint is not strictly enforced. The paper also lacks a thorough comparison with other state-of-the-art Shapley value estimation methods, not just FastSHAP. This limits the understanding of the relative performance of SimSHAP. The evaluation should also include a more detailed analysis of the accuracy of SimSHAP, beyond the insertion/deletion metrics. For example, the authors could consider using metrics that directly measure the similarity between the estimated Shapley values and the true Shapley values, or at least provide a more detailed analysis of the error distribution. Additionally, the paper does not provide a detailed explanation of the experimental setup, including the specific hyperparameters used for training SimSHAP and the baseline methods. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results and compare them with other methods. Finally, the unified framework, while insightful, could be presented more clearly. While the paper defines $\tilde{\phi}$, the initial definition of the unified stochastic estimator in Definition 2 might not be immediately clear without reading the subsequent explanation. The paper could benefit from a more explicit and integrated explanation of $\tilde{\phi}$ directly with the definition. The paper also needs to elaborate on the intuition behind expressing different algorithms as variations of mean estimators within this framework. Furthermore, a more in-depth discussion is needed on why the specific forms of the semivalue, random order value, and least square value algorithms are preferred within this unified view. Providing concrete examples of how these algorithms map to the unified framework would greatly enhance the reader's understanding and the overall contribution of the paper. These weaknesses, particularly the limited empirical validation and the lack of detailed analysis, significantly impact the conclusions that can be drawn from this paper. The confidence level in these identified issues is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the lack of certain experiments and analyses.
