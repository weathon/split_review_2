## summary
This paper introduces ACTOR, an innovative agent designed to simulate human-like behavior in complex 3D environments. The core contribution lies in the agent's ability to achieve high-level, long-horizon goals through a perceive-plan-act cycle, guided by an internal value system akin to human decision-making. ACTOR leverages a tree search algorithm, incorporating both real-valued functions and language-based commands to evaluate potential actions. This approach allows the agent to actively search its behavior space, generating activity choices based on a hierarchical prior and evaluating these choices using customizable value functions. The agent's planning process is dynamic, enabling it to adapt to environmental changes and new language commands. The authors also present BehaviorHub, a large-scale dataset of simulated human behavior in 3D scenes, which includes 3D scans, human motion sequences, and corresponding plans. This dataset is generated automatically by leveraging existing resources of human motions and indoor scenes. The dataset is split into a training set and a dynamic test set, where the test set introduces dynamics changes. The experiments demonstrate that ACTOR exhibits more robust planning performance in BehaviorHub than two baseline methods. Furthermore, the authors show that BehaviorHub can be used as a training dataset for scene-aware motion generation and language-conditioned motion generation tasks. The paper's significance lies in its novel approach to simulating human behavior using LLMs and value-driven planning, as well as the creation of a large-scale dataset that can facilitate further research in this area. The integration of LLMs with a hierarchical planning framework and value functions represents a notable step towards more realistic and adaptable virtual agents. The paper's findings suggest that this approach can lead to more robust and adaptable virtual agents, with potential applications in various fields such as robotics, virtual reality, and video games.

## strengths
I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The core idea of using a value-driven mechanism to guide an LLM-based agent's planning process is both novel and compelling. The integration of real-valued functions and language-based commands within the value function allows for a more nuanced and human-like evaluation of potential actions. This approach moves beyond simple rule-based systems and allows the agent to make decisions based on a combination of quantitative metrics and qualitative preferences. The use of a tree search algorithm to explore the behavior space is also a significant strength, enabling the agent to plan for long-horizon goals and adapt to changes in the environment. The paper's empirical results, demonstrating ACTOR's superior performance compared to baseline methods on the BehaviorHub dataset, provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The creation of the BehaviorHub dataset is another major contribution. The dataset's scale and the inclusion of 3D scenes, human motion sequences, and corresponding plans make it a valuable resource for the research community. The automatic generation of the dataset, leveraging existing resources of human motions and indoor scenes, is a significant achievement that reduces the labor intensity of creating such a dataset. The fact that the dataset includes a dynamic test set, which introduces changes in the environment, is also a strength, as it allows for the evaluation of the agent's adaptability. Finally, the paper's demonstration of the dataset's utility for training models for scene-aware motion generation and language-conditioned motion generation tasks further highlights its value and potential impact.

## weaknesses
Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's writing could be improved to enhance clarity and reduce ambiguity. The term 'value,' as used in the abstract and introduction, is initially ambiguous. It could be interpreted as a value function in reinforcement learning or the value of objects in the scene. While the paper later clarifies that it refers to a 'value function' in the method section, the initial use in the abstract and introduction could lead to confusion. For example, the abstract states the agent is guided by its 'internal value,' and the introduction mentions 'value priorities' without immediate definition. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to understand the agent's motivation and planning process initially. Second, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with state-of-the-art MCTS algorithms. While the paper does compare different search algorithms, including MCTS, and shows its superior performance on the proposed benchmark, it does not delve into comparisons with specific state-of-the-art MCTS variants or provide a detailed analysis of computational complexity and convergence properties. The paper cites the original MCTS paper but does not explore how the proposed method differs from traditional MCTS algorithms in terms of computational complexity, convergence properties, and performance on similar tasks. This makes it difficult to assess whether the proposed method offers significant improvements over existing techniques. Third, the dataset generation pipeline, while impressive in its scale, is not fully automatic. The pipeline requires manual filtering and verification, which is labor-intensive and may introduce bias. The paper mentions using LLMs for initial filtering and refinement, but the final verification step relies on human judgment. This manual step can indeed be labor-intensive, potentially introduce bias, and limit scalability. The paper does not specify a clear, automated metric for this final verification stage. Fourth, the value function, while effective, is relatively simple and may not be generalizable to different scenarios. The paper describes two main types of value functions: real-valued functions (like shortest path) and language-based commands (using LLM prompts). While these are effective, they might be considered basic heuristics compared to more complex, learned value functions that could potentially capture a wider range of human behaviors and preferences. The generalizability of these specific functions to significantly different scenarios is a valid concern. Fifth, the paper lacks detailed information about the perception module. While it mentions using 'readily available models' and 'heuristic functions' to convert scene geometry and agent state into linguistic descriptions, it doesn't specify the exact models used, the types of simulated sensors, or the extent of scene understanding achieved. The provided example of the linguistic description suggests basic object recognition and state awareness but doesn't confirm more advanced perception capabilities. Finally, while the paper does mention the use of off-the-shelf models for motion generation and cites relevant papers, it lacks detailed explanations of the motion primitives used, how they are combined, and the mechanisms for ensuring smooth transitions. The paper also lacks a detailed explanation of the motion primitives used and how they are combined to form complex actions. While the cited papers likely contain these details, the current paper doesn't elaborate on them. The motion quality, while evaluated using FID and action recognition accuracy, could still be improved. The FID scores, while not terrible, suggest there's room for improvement. The reviewer's suggestion to consider more recent, high-quality motion generation models is relevant.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the paper should start by clearly defining the concept of 'value' as it is used in the context of the ACTOR agent. This definition should be precise and unambiguous, specifying whether it is a scalar, vector, or other mathematical object. The paper should then explain how this value is used in the agent's decision-making process, including how it is computed, updated, and used to guide the agent's actions. For example, if the value is a scalar reward, the paper should explain how it is derived from the environment and how it relates to the agent's goals. If it is a vector of preferences, the paper should explain how these preferences are represented and how they influence the agent's planning. The paper should also clarify the relationship between the agent's internal value representation and the external environment, explaining how the agent uses its perception of the environment to determine the value of different actions or states. Second, to address the concerns about the method's novelty and comparison to existing MCTS methods, the paper should include a detailed analysis of how the proposed method differs from traditional MCTS algorithms. This analysis should focus on the specific modifications or extensions made to the standard MCTS framework, such as the use of language-based commands or real-valued functions in the value functions. The paper should also provide a theoretical analysis of the method's computational complexity and convergence properties, comparing these properties to those of existing MCTS algorithms. Furthermore, the paper should include an empirical evaluation of the method's performance on a set of benchmark tasks, comparing its performance to that of state-of-the-art MCTS algorithms. This evaluation should include a variety of metrics, such as success rate, planning time, and computational cost, to provide a comprehensive assessment of the method's strengths and weaknesses. Third, to address the limitations of the dataset generation pipeline, a more robust and automated filtering mechanism should be developed. Instead of relying solely on manual verification, the authors could explore using a combination of automated metrics and a smaller-scale human review process. For example, they could train a classifier to identify and filter out low-quality plans based on features extracted from the generated text and motion data. This classifier could be trained on a small, manually verified subset of the data and then used to automatically filter the rest. This would reduce the labor intensity of the filtering process and improve the scalability of the dataset generation pipeline. Furthermore, the authors should investigate methods to quantify the uncertainty in the generated plans, which could be used to prioritize plans for manual review, focusing human effort on the most ambiguous cases. This would also help in identifying potential biases in the generation process. Fourth, to improve the generalizability of the value function, the authors should explore more sophisticated methods for modeling the value of actions. Instead of relying on simple heuristics, they could consider using a learned value function that is trained on a diverse set of human behaviors. This could involve using reinforcement learning techniques to learn a value function that can predict the long-term consequences of actions. The value function could also be made context-aware, taking into account the current state of the environment and the agent's goals. This would allow the model to adapt to different scenarios and tasks more effectively. Additionally, the authors should investigate methods to incorporate common-sense knowledge into the value function, which could help the model to reason about the value of actions in a more human-like way. This could involve using knowledge graphs or other forms of structured knowledge to represent common-sense facts about the world. Fifth, to address the lack of detail regarding the perception module, the authors should provide a more thorough description of its implementation. This should include a detailed explanation of the simulated sensors, the data processing pipeline, and the specific scene understanding capabilities. For example, the authors could describe how the agent perceives object affordances, such as whether a chair is suitable for sitting or a table for placing objects. It would be beneficial to include a discussion of the limitations of the perception module, such as its sensitivity to lighting conditions or occlusions. Furthermore, the authors should provide a quantitative evaluation of the perception module, such as metrics for object recognition accuracy or scene understanding completeness. Finally, regarding the motion generation module, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the motion primitives used and how they are combined to form complex actions. This should include a description of the motion representation, the control mechanism, and the techniques used to ensure smooth and realistic transitions between different motions. For example, the authors could describe how the system handles transitions between standing, walking, and sitting motions. It would be beneficial to include a quantitative evaluation of the motion quality, such as metrics for naturalness or smoothness. This could involve comparing the generated motions with human motion capture data or using metrics such as the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) or action recognition accuracy. The authors should also discuss the limitations of the motion generation module, such as its ability to handle complex environments or its sensitivity to changes in the environment. The authors should also consider exploring more advanced motion generation techniques, such as those based on diffusion models or transformers, to improve the realism and diversity of the generated motions. Specifically, they could investigate methods that explicitly model human-scene interactions, ensuring that the generated motions are not only realistic in isolation but also contextually appropriate within the 3D scenes.
