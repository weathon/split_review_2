## summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for quantifying the generalizability of experimental studies in machine learning. The authors define an experimental study as a set of experiments comparing alternatives under varying conditions, with a focus on factors that are allowed to vary, which they argue determines the scope of generalizability. They introduce the concept of an 'ideal study,' representing an exhaustive exploration of all possible experimental conditions, and contrast it with 'empirical studies,' which are limited samples of the ideal. The core contribution lies in a quantifiable definition of generalizability, defined as the probability that two empirical studies approximating the same ideal study yield similar results. This similarity is measured using a kernel-based metric applied to the rankings of alternatives produced by the experiments. The authors propose an algorithm to estimate the number of experiments required to achieve a desired level of generalizability, based on a linear relationship between the logarithms of sample size and the quantile of the distance metric. The paper presents case studies on categorical encoders and large language models, demonstrating the application of the framework and the estimation of the number of experiments needed for generalizable results. The authors argue that their framework provides a principled way to assess the robustness of experimental findings and to guide the design of studies that are both efficient and likely to produce generalizable results. The paper's significance lies in its attempt to formalize the often-neglected aspect of generalizability in machine learning experiments, offering a concrete approach to address the question of whether experimental results can be expected to hold under different conditions. By introducing a mathematical framework, the authors aim to move beyond qualitative assessments of generalizability and provide a more rigorous basis for experimental design and interpretation. The paper's approach is grounded in the idea that a well-designed study should produce consistent results across different experimental conditions, and that the number of experiments needed to achieve this consistency can be estimated using their proposed algorithm. The case studies, while limited in scope, provide initial evidence that the framework can be applied to real-world machine learning problems and can provide insights into the generalizability of experimental results. Overall, the paper makes a valuable contribution by formalizing the concept of generalizability in machine learning experiments and providing a practical algorithm for estimating the number of experiments needed to achieve a desired level of generalizability.

## strengths
This paper makes a significant contribution by introducing a formal framework for quantifying the generalizability of experimental studies in machine learning, a critical aspect often overlooked in practice. The core strength lies in the novel mathematical formalization of experimental studies, their results, and the concept of generalizability. The authors clearly define key terms, such as 'ideal study,' 'empirical study,' and 'allowed-to-vary factors,' providing a solid foundation for their framework. The distinction between ideal and empirical studies is particularly insightful, highlighting the gap between theoretical possibilities and practical limitations. The proposed definition of generalizability, based on the probability of obtaining similar results across different empirical studies approximating the same ideal study, is both intuitive and mathematically sound. The use of kernel-based metrics to measure the similarity between rankings of alternatives is a clever approach that allows for a flexible and nuanced assessment of generalizability. Furthermore, the development of an algorithm to estimate the number of experiments required to achieve a desired level of generalizability is a valuable practical contribution. This algorithm, based on a linear relationship between the logarithms of sample size and the quantile of the distance metric, provides researchers with a tool to design experiments that are both efficient and likely to produce generalizable results. The inclusion of case studies on categorical encoders and large language models demonstrates the applicability of the proposed framework to real-world machine learning problems. These case studies, while not exhaustive, provide concrete examples of how the framework can be used to assess the generalizability of experimental results and to estimate the number of experiments needed for robust conclusions. The paper is also well-written and organized, making it accessible to a broad audience of machine learning researchers and practitioners. The authors clearly define their terms, provide illustrative examples, and present their results in a logical and coherent manner. The paper's focus on the practical implications of generalizability, and the development of a concrete algorithm for estimating the required number of experiments, makes it a valuable contribution to the field. The authors have successfully translated a complex theoretical concept into a practical tool that can be used to improve the design and interpretation of machine learning experiments. The framework's ability to quantify generalizability, rather than relying on qualitative assessments, is a significant step forward in the field.

## weaknesses
While this paper presents a valuable framework, several limitations warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the reliance on the assumption that experimental conditions are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). This assumption, explicitly stated in the definition of empirical studies (Section 3.2.2), is a strong one and may not hold in many real-world scenarios. As the paper notes, the framework relies on the approximation that the empirical study results converge to the ideal study results under the i.i.d. assumption. However, violations of this assumption, such as temporal dependencies in data collection or correlations between experimental factors, could significantly impact the validity of the generalizability metric. The paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the implications of violating this assumption, nor does it offer any guidance on how to detect or mitigate such violations. This lack of discussion limits the practical applicability of the framework in real-world scenarios where the i.i.d. assumption is unlikely to hold perfectly. The paper's focus on ranking-based results also presents a limitation. While rankings are a common outcome in comparative studies, many experiments in machine learning involve continuous variables or more complex results, such as confusion matrices or ROC curves. The core of the framework, including the definition of experimental results, the choice of kernels, and the use of Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), is centered around rankings. The paper acknowledges this limitation in the limitations section, stating that "there are, of course, other possibilities, such as using the raw performance for the experimental results." However, it does not delve into specific methods or cite literature on handling continuous or complex outcomes within their framework. This limits the generality of the proposed approach and raises questions about its applicability to a broader range of machine learning experiments. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithm for estimating the number of experiments. While the authors mention that the algorithm is efficient, they do not provide a formal analysis of its time and space complexity. This is a significant omission, as the computational cost of the algorithm could be a limiting factor in its practical application, especially for large-scale experiments. A detailed analysis of the algorithm's complexity, including its dependence on the number of alternatives, experimental conditions, and the desired level of generalizability, would be necessary to assess its scalability. The absence of such an analysis makes it difficult to assess the algorithm's scalability for large-scale experiments. Furthermore, the paper's definition of generalizability, while mathematically sound, is defined for an 'ideal study' rather than directly for empirical studies. While the paper states that the goal is to understand the generalizability of empirical studies by approximating the ideal study, the definition of generalizability is explicitly tied to the probability distribution of the ideal study's results (Definition 4.1). This indirect approach raises questions about the practical interpretability of the generalizability metric. The paper also relies on the unknown probability distribution of the ideal study, which cannot be directly estimated in practice. While the authors propose methods to estimate it from empirical data, the reliance on this unobservable distribution introduces a degree of uncertainty. Finally, while the paper does explore the impact of different similarity thresholds in the experiments, it does not provide a principled method for selecting an appropriate threshold based on the specific context of the experiment. The choice of threshold introduces a degree of arbitrariness, and the paper should explore how different thresholds affect the conclusions about generalizability. The paper also does not explicitly address the potential for systematic biases in the experimental setup, which could lead to misleadingly high generalizability scores. These biases could arise from various sources, such as unrepresentative experimental conditions or flawed experimental design, and the paper should discuss how such biases might affect the generalizability metric and propose methods for detecting and mitigating them. These limitations, while not invalidating the paper's contributions, highlight areas where further research and development are needed to enhance the practical utility and theoretical soundness of the proposed framework. The confidence level for each of these identified weaknesses is high, as they are all supported by direct evidence from the paper's content and methodology.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, the paper should delve deeper into the practical implications of the i.i.d. assumption. Specifically, the authors should discuss how violations of this assumption might manifest in real-world experimental settings. For instance, if experiments are conducted on different datasets, the datasets might share underlying biases or correlations that violate the i.i.d. assumption. The authors could explore how to detect or mitigate such violations, perhaps by incorporating techniques from causal inference or domain adaptation. This could involve analyzing the experimental conditions for potential dependencies or using techniques to de-correlate the data. Furthermore, the paper should provide guidance on how to choose an appropriate similarity threshold. The authors could investigate the sensitivity of their generalizability metric to different threshold values and provide a principled method for selecting a threshold based on the specific context of the experiment. This could involve relating the threshold to the consequences of incorrect generalization, or to the variability observed in the experimental results. The authors should also consider the impact of the kernel choice on the generalizability metric, as different kernels may emphasize different aspects of the ranking similarity. Second, the paper should broaden its scope to include a wider range of experimental outcomes beyond rankings. While rankings are a common outcome in comparative studies, many experiments in machine learning involve continuous variables or more complex results. The authors could explore how their framework can be adapted to handle these types of outcomes. For example, they could investigate the use of different distance metrics that are suitable for continuous variables, such as the Earth Mover's Distance or the Wasserstein metric. They could also explore how to extend the framework to handle more complex results, such as confusion matrices or ROC curves, by defining appropriate distance metrics for these types of data. This would significantly increase the generality and applicability of the proposed framework. Additionally, the authors should provide concrete examples of how their framework can be applied to different types of experimental outcomes, demonstrating its versatility. Third, the paper needs a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithm. The authors should provide a formal analysis of the time and space complexity of the algorithm, including its dependence on the number of alternatives, experimental conditions, and the desired level of generalizability. This analysis should be supported by empirical evidence, demonstrating the scalability of the algorithm for different problem sizes. Furthermore, the authors should discuss potential optimizations that could be used to improve the efficiency of the algorithm, such as parallelization or approximation techniques. This would make the proposed framework more practical for large-scale experiments and would allow researchers to better assess the computational cost of applying the framework to their specific problems. Fourth, the paper should clarify the relationship between the 'ideal study' and the practical application of the framework to empirical studies. While the definition of generalizability is tied to the ideal study, the paper should provide more guidance on how the proposed metric can be used to guide the design and interpretation of empirical studies. This could involve providing a more concrete interpretation of the generalizability metric in the context of empirical studies and discussing how the metric can be used to assess the robustness of experimental findings. Fifth, the paper should address the potential for systematic biases in the experimental setup. The authors should discuss how such biases might affect the generalizability metric and propose methods for detecting and mitigating them. For example, if the experimental conditions are not representative of the target population, the generalizability metric might be misleadingly high. The authors could explore techniques for bias detection, such as sensitivity analysis or adversarial debiasing. They should also discuss how to design experiments that are less susceptible to bias, such as by using stratified sampling or randomized controlled trials. Finally, the paper should include a more thorough comparison with existing approaches to measuring generalizability, such as those based on statistical significance testing or resampling techniques. The authors should discuss the advantages and disadvantages of their approach compared to these alternatives, and provide a more detailed analysis of how their framework relates to concepts like statistical power and effect size. Specifically, it is unclear how the proposed kernel-based similarity measure relates to more traditional statistical measures of agreement or correlation, and whether the proposed framework can provide similar insights as these established methods. By addressing these limitations, the authors can significantly enhance the practical utility and theoretical soundness of their proposed framework.
