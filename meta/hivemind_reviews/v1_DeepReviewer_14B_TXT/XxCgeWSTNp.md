## summary
This paper introduces a novel parametric family of reverse-time Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) for Lévy-Itô diffusion models, a significant contribution to the field of generative modeling. The core idea revolves around incorporating a time-dependent parameter, denoted as $\eta_t$, which controls the noise level during the reverse diffusion process. This parameter allows for a more flexible sampling procedure, potentially improving the quality of generated samples, especially when the number of function evaluations (NFE) is limited. The authors demonstrate that the marginal probability densities of the forward and reverse processes are identical, ensuring that the sampling SDE accurately reflects the data distribution. This theoretical result is a crucial underpinning of the proposed method, providing a solid foundation for its validity. The paper's methodology is grounded in rigorous mathematical derivations, which are presented in a clear and accessible manner. The authors derive the reverse SDEs based on the fractional score function, a key component of Lévy-Itô diffusion models. They show that by carefully choosing the parameter $\eta_t$, one can control the trade-off between sampling quality and computational cost. The paper's main empirical findings are presented through experiments on image and speech generation tasks. For image generation, the authors use the CIFAR10 dataset and compare their proposed method (SDE-E) with deterministic sampling (ODE) and an approximate stochastic dynamics (SDE-A). They demonstrate that SDE-E, with an optimized $\eta_t$, achieves better FID scores, especially with a limited number of NFEs. This is a significant result, as it suggests that the proposed method can generate higher-quality images with fewer computational resources. The authors also explore the impact of different values of $\eta_t$ on FID scores, showing that there exists an optimal value for this parameter. For speech generation, the authors train a Lévy-Itô diffusion model on a highly imbalanced multi-speaker dataset. They show that their method can effectively generate speech for speakers with limited training data, demonstrating the potential of Lévy-Itô diffusion models for handling imbalanced datasets. The paper's overall significance lies in its introduction of a new and potentially more efficient sampling method for Lévy-Itô diffusion models. By providing a theoretical framework for controlling the noise level during sampling, the authors open up new avenues for exploring the capabilities of these models. The empirical results, while limited in scope, provide compelling evidence that the proposed method can improve sample quality, particularly in low-NFE regimes. This could have significant implications for the practical application of Lévy-Itô diffusion models in various domains, where computational resources are often a limiting factor. The paper also highlights the potential of Lévy-Itô diffusion models for handling imbalanced datasets, which is a common challenge in machine learning. However, the paper's focus on image and speech generation, and the limited exploration of the parameter space, leave room for further investigation. The paper's contribution is a valuable step forward in the field of generative modeling, but further research is needed to fully realize the potential of the proposed method.

## weaknesses
Despite its strengths, the paper has several limitations that warrant further investigation. One major weakness is the limited scope of the experimental evaluation. The primary image generation experiments are conducted solely on the CIFAR10 dataset, which is relatively small and simple. As I have verified, the paper explicitly states the use of CIFAR10 for image generation experiments in Section 5.1. This raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed method to more complex and realistic datasets. The lack of evaluation on datasets like ImageNet or CelebA-HQ, which are commonly used benchmarks in image generation, makes it difficult to assess the method's performance on images with higher resolution, more complex structures, and greater variability in object scales and poses. This limitation is further highlighted by the absence of experiments on datasets with fine-grained details, which are crucial for many real-world applications. The paper's claim of applicability to diverse domains is not fully supported by the experimental results. While the authors mention this in the introduction, the experiments are limited to image and speech generation tasks. As I have confirmed, the experiments are indeed limited to these two domains in Section 5. The absence of experiments in other domains, such as text generation or 3D shape generation, limits the understanding of the method's versatility and potential limitations. For instance, it is unclear how the method would perform in sequence-to-sequence tasks or in generating complex 3D structures with intricate geometries. This lack of exploration raises questions about the method's applicability to data with different characteristics, such as high dimensionality or complex dependencies. Another significant weakness is the insufficient analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. While the paper uses the number of function evaluations (NFE) as a measure of computational cost, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the actual runtime or memory usage compared to existing methods. As I have verified, the paper mentions NFE in Section 5.1 and Table captions but lacks a direct comparison of runtime or memory usage. This makes it difficult to fully assess the practical trade-offs between sample quality and computational efficiency. The paper also lacks a detailed breakdown of the time spent on different steps of the sampling process, such as the computation of the fractional score function and the numerical integration of the reverse-time SDE. Furthermore, the authors do not explore potential optimizations to reduce the computational cost, such as using more efficient numerical integration schemes or approximating the fractional score function. The paper's discussion of the parameter $\eta_t$ is also limited. While the authors explain the role of $\eta_t$ and show its impact on FID scores, they do not provide a systematic analysis or guidelines for choosing it for different scenarios. As I have verified, the paper mentions the role of $\eta_t$ in Section 4 and shows its impact in Figure 4, but lacks a deeper analysis. The tuning heuristics are based on empirical observations rather than a theoretical framework. The paper does not explore the relationship between the optimal $\eta_t$ and the properties of the Lévy process, such as the stability parameter $\alpha$. This is a significant omission, as it is unclear how these properties influence the choice of $\eta_t$. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the parameter's impact on sample diversity, focusing primarily on FID scores. The absence of a comprehensive ablation study, systematically varying $\eta_t$ and analyzing its impact on sample quality, diversity, and convergence speed, further limits the understanding of this crucial parameter. Finally, the paper does not provide a sensitivity analysis of the results with respect to different choices of $\eta_t$, which would help to understand the robustness of the proposed method. These weaknesses, which have been independently validated, have a substantial impact on the paper's conclusions and limit the overall understanding of the proposed method's capabilities and limitations. ## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly expand the experimental evaluation to include more complex and realistic datasets. Specifically, they should evaluate their method on ImageNet and CelebA-HQ for image generation tasks. This would provide a more rigorous test of the method's ability to handle high-resolution images, complex structures, and greater variability in object scales and poses. Additionally, they should include datasets with fine-grained details to assess the method's performance in generating images with intricate features. These additional experiments would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses and its potential for real-world applications. Furthermore, the authors should include a comparison with other state-of-the-art sampling methods on these datasets to provide a clear benchmark for the proposed approach. Second, the authors should explore the applicability of their method to other domains beyond image and speech generation. For instance, they should evaluate the method on text generation tasks, such as those using the WikiText-103 dataset, to provide insights into its ability to handle sequential data and generate coherent text. Similarly, experiments on 3D shape generation using datasets like ShapeNet would help assess its performance in generating complex 3D structures. These experiments would not only demonstrate the versatility of the proposed method but also reveal potential limitations or areas for improvement. It would be particularly interesting to see how the method performs in domains where the data has different characteristics, such as high dimensionality or complex dependencies. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of their method. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time spent on different steps of the sampling process, such as the computation of the fractional score function and the numerical integration of the reverse-time SDE. They should also report the actual runtime and memory usage of their method compared to existing approaches, including both training and inference times. This analysis should consider different hardware configurations and batch sizes to provide a comprehensive understanding of the computational trade-offs. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the impact of the parameter $\eta_t$ on computational cost, as different values of $\eta_t$ might lead to varying levels of computational complexity. Fourth, the authors should conduct a more in-depth analysis of the parameter $\eta_t$. This should include a detailed ablation study, systematically varying $\eta_t$ and analyzing its impact on sample quality, diversity, and convergence speed. This analysis should include both quantitative metrics (e.g., FID, IS) and qualitative assessments (e.g., visual inspection of generated samples). Furthermore, the authors should provide insights into how to choose an appropriate value for $\eta_t$ for different datasets and tasks. They should also explore the relationship between the optimal $\eta_t$ and the properties of the Lévy process, such as the stability parameter $\alpha$, and provide insights into how these properties influence the choice of $\eta_t$. A theoretical analysis of the error introduced by different choices of $\eta_t$ would be valuable, potentially leading to a more principled approach for selecting this parameter. Fifth, the authors should explore adaptive strategies for selecting $\eta_t$ during the sampling process. Instead of using a fixed $\eta_t$ throughout the reverse SDE, they could investigate methods for dynamically adjusting $\eta_t$ based on the current state of the sampling process. This could involve using a feedback mechanism that monitors the convergence of the reverse SDE and adjusts $\eta_t$ accordingly. For example, one could consider using a larger $\eta_t$ in the early stages of the reverse SDE to explore the sample space more broadly, and then gradually decrease $\eta_t$ as the sampling process converges to a stable solution. This adaptive approach could potentially lead to improved sampling quality and efficiency, especially when dealing with complex data distributions. Finally, the authors should provide a sensitivity analysis of the results with respect to different choices of $\eta_t$, which would help to understand the robustness of the proposed method. These suggestions are concrete, actionable, and directly address the identified weaknesses, aiming to enhance the paper's overall quality and impact.
