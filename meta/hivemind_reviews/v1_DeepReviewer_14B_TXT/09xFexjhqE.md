## Summary
This paper introduces AutoLoRa, a novel framework designed to enhance the robust fine-tuning of pre-trained models, specifically addressing the challenge of divergent gradient directions when optimizing for both adversarial and natural objectives. The core idea revolves around disentangling these objectives by employing a low-rank (LoRa) branch to optimize the natural objective, while the feature extractor (FE) is optimized for the adversarial objective. This disentanglement aims to stabilize the optimization process, which is often unstable in traditional robust fine-tuning methods. The authors also propose automated scheduling strategies for learning rates and loss term scalars, further improving the stability and effectiveness of the fine-tuning process. Empirically, the paper demonstrates that AutoLoRa achieves state-of-the-art adversarial robustness across various downstream tasks, outperforming existing methods like vanilla Robust Fine-Tuning (RFT) and TWINS. The method is presented as a practical solution for converting pre-trained models into adversarially robust models without extensive hyperparameter tuning. The authors motivate their approach by highlighting the issue of gradient divergence, where the gradients of the adversarial and natural objectives point in different directions, leading to unstable optimization and sensitivity to hyperparameters. They propose that the LoRa branch addresses this issue by separating the optimization paths for the natural and adversarial objectives. The automated scheduling of learning rates and loss term scalars is inspired by techniques used in adversarial attack generation and graduated optimization, respectively. The experimental results show that AutoLoRa consistently achieves higher robust test accuracy compared to the baselines, and the ablation studies provide insights into the impact of different components of the proposed method. The paper also includes an analysis of the gradient similarity between the natural and adversarial objectives, showing that AutoLoRa achieves higher similarity, which is correlated with improved robustness. Overall, the paper presents a practical and effective approach to robust fine-tuning, with a focus on disentangling optimization objectives and automating hyperparameter scheduling. However, as I will discuss in detail, the paper lacks a strong theoretical foundation and has some methodological and experimental limitations that need to be addressed.

## Strengths
The paper presents several notable strengths. First, the core idea of disentangling the natural and adversarial objectives using a low-rank branch is a novel approach to addressing the gradient divergence issue in robust fine-tuning. This is a practical and intuitive way to tackle the instability often observed in these methods. The use of a LoRa branch, while not entirely new in itself, is cleverly applied here to separate the optimization paths, which is a key contribution of the paper. Second, the automated scheduling of learning rates and loss term scalars is a significant practical advantage. This reduces the need for manual hyperparameter tuning, making the framework more accessible and easier to use in real-world applications. The authors draw inspiration from existing techniques, such as the automatic step size scheduler in AutoAttack and graduated optimization, but their specific adaptation and integration into the fine-tuning process is a valuable contribution. Third, the empirical results are compelling. The paper demonstrates consistent improvements in adversarial robustness across multiple datasets and model architectures, outperforming existing methods like vanilla RFT and TWINS. The ablation studies provide further evidence of the effectiveness of the proposed method and its different components. The analysis of gradient similarity also provides some insight into why AutoLoRa is more effective, showing that it achieves higher similarity between the gradients of the natural and adversarial objectives. Finally, the paper is generally well-written and easy to follow. The authors provide a clear motivation for their work, explain their proposed method in detail, and present their experimental results in a clear and concise manner. The inclusion of an appendix with additional details is also helpful for reproducibility. These strengths make the paper a valuable contribution to the field of adversarial robustness, offering a practical and effective approach to robust fine-tuning.

## Weaknesses
Despite its strengths, the paper suffers from several significant weaknesses that undermine its overall impact. The most prominent issue is the lack of a strong theoretical foundation. While the authors provide an intuitive explanation for why their method works, they do not offer any formal proofs or theoretical analysis to support their claims. Specifically, the paper lacks a theoretical analysis of why the low-rank branch effectively mitigates the issue of divergent optimization directions. The authors propose that the LoRa branch addresses the gradient divergence between natural and adversarial objectives, but there is no formal analysis or proof to support this claim. The paper would benefit from a more rigorous exploration of the underlying mechanisms, such as an analysis of the gradient space and how the LoRa branch alters the optimization landscape. Without this, the effectiveness of the approach remains somewhat empirical and lacks a strong theoretical foundation. This is evident in the lack of convergence analysis, and the absence of any insights into the optimization landscape and how the low-rank branch affects the gradient flow. The paper also lacks a theoretical analysis of why the proposed method works. The authors do not provide any analysis of the convergence properties of the proposed method, nor do they offer any insights into the optimization landscape and how the low-rank branch affects the gradient flow. A theoretical analysis of the gradient similarity and how the LoRA branch mitigates the divergence would be beneficial. This lack of theoretical depth is a major limitation, as it makes it difficult to fully understand why the method works and how it might be improved. The paper also fails to provide a theoretical justification for the choice of a low-rank branch. While the intuition of separating the natural and adversarial objectives is clear, it is not clear why a low-rank branch is the optimal choice for this disentanglement. What are the properties of the low-rank branch that make it suitable for this task? Are there other types of branches that could achieve similar or better results? A theoretical justification for the choice of a low-rank branch is needed. The paper also lacks a theoretical analysis of why the proposed scheduling strategies work. The proposed scheduling strategies are based on empirical observations, but there is no theoretical justification for why these specific strategies are effective. For example, why is a step size scheduler for adversarial attack enhanced by using the scheduler for the learning rate? What are the underlying dynamics that make this scheduling effective? The paper relies on empirical success and analogies to existing methods without providing a formal theoretical basis. Furthermore, the paper argues that the issue of gradient divergence impedes obtaining adversarial robustness, but the authors do not provide a theoretical analysis of why this is the case. It is not clear why the divergence of gradients would necessarily lead to a decrease in adversarial robustness. A more rigorous explanation, possibly involving the geometry of the loss landscape or the properties of the optimization trajectory, is needed to support this claim. For example, are there specific conditions on the loss function or the feature space that exacerbate this divergence and lead to poor robustness? The paper also does not provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the low similarity in gradient directions is the primary cause of poor robustness in existing methods. While the authors present a correlation between gradient similarity and robust accuracy, this does not establish a causal relationship. It is possible that other factors, such as the specific optimization algorithm or the choice of hyperparameters, also contribute to the observed robustness. The paper needs to provide more compelling evidence that the gradient divergence is the main bottleneck in achieving robustness. The comparison between the proposed method and existing methods may not be entirely fair, as AutoLoRa introduces additional parameters through the LoRa branch. While the authors claim parameter efficiency, the LoRa branch still introduces new parameters that are not present in the baseline models. This difference in model capacity could contribute to the observed performance gains, making it difficult to isolate the impact of the proposed optimization strategy. A more rigorous comparison would involve controlling for the number of parameters or providing an ablation study that quantifies the contribution of the LoRa branch. Finally, the paper could benefit from a clearer and more structured presentation. Some sections are dense and could be streamlined for better readability. The description of the automated scheduling of loss term scalars is not sufficiently detailed, making it difficult to understand the exact mechanism and its impact on the overall performance. The paper would benefit from a more detailed explanation of the specific algorithms used for scheduling. These weaknesses, taken together, significantly limit the paper's contribution and raise questions about the generalizability and robustness of the proposed method.

For example, they could investigate whether the low-rank branch introduces any regularization effects that contribute to the improved robustness. This would help to determine whether the low-rank branch is indeed the optimal choice for disentangling the optimization process. This could involve analyzing the gradient space and how the LoRA branch alters the optimization landscape. This would help to move beyond empirical observations and provide a more solid theoretical basis for the proposed method. The analysis should also investigate the impact of the LoRa branch on the loss landscape, examining whether it leads to a smoother or more stable optimization surface. Second, the paper needs a more rigorous analysis of the proposed scheduling strategies. This could involve analyzing the dynamics of the optimization process and how the learning rate and loss term scalars affect the convergence behavior. For example, the authors could investigate whether the proposed scheduling strategies help to avoid local minima or saddle points. It would also be beneficial to compare the performance of the proposed scheduling strategies with other scheduling methods, such as adaptive learning rate methods. This would help to determine whether the proposed strategies are indeed effective and whether they offer any advantages over existing methods. It would also be beneficial to compare the performance of AutoLoRa with and without the LoRa branch, while keeping the number of parameters constant, to isolate the effect of the proposed optimization strategy. Fourth, to establish a causal link between gradient similarity and robustness, the authors should conduct experiments that directly manipulate the gradient directions. For example, they could explore techniques for explicitly aligning the gradient directions during training, such as using gradient projection or regularization methods. If these techniques lead to improved robustness, it would provide stronger evidence that the gradient divergence is indeed the primary cause of poor robustness in existing methods. Furthermore, the authors should investigate whether the observed correlation between gradient similarity and robustness holds across different datasets and model architectures. This would help to determine the generalizability of their findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between gradient similarity and adversarial robustness. Finally, to improve the clarity and presentation of the paper, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the automated scheduling of loss term scalars. This should include a clear description of the specific algorithms used for scheduling, as well as a discussion of the rationale behind the chosen scheduling strategy. For example, the authors could provide a step-by-step explanation of how the scalars are adjusted during training, and how these adjustments affect the balance between the natural and adversarial objectives. Furthermore, the authors should consider restructuring some of the denser sections to improve readability. This could involve breaking down long paragraphs into smaller, more digestible chunks, and using more visual aids, such as diagrams and flowcharts, to illustrate the key concepts. A clearer presentation would make the paper more accessible to a wider audience and enhance its overall impact.
