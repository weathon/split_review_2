## summary
This paper delves into the theoretical underpinnings of non-convex pairwise stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in the presence of heavy-tailed gradient noise. The authors tackle a significant gap in the learning theory for this setting by establishing stability-based learning guarantees. They achieve this by first linking $\ell_1$ on-average model stability to generalization error in the general non-convex setting. Then, they refine these bounds by introducing sub-Weibull gradient noise, effectively removing the need for bounded gradient assumptions. Furthermore, under the Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition, they derive sharper error bounds for both generalization error and excess risk. The analysis is extended to minibatch pairwise SGD, where they derive the first stability-based near-optimal generalization and optimization bounds, which align with empirical observations. The core methodological approach revolves around algorithmic stability analysis, specifically focusing on $\ell_1$ on-average model stability. The authors derive generalization bounds by leveraging this stability notion and then refine these bounds under the assumption of sub-Weibull gradient noise. The introduction of sub-Weibull noise is a key methodological contribution, as it allows for the removal of the bounded gradient assumption, making the results more broadly applicable. The paper also makes use of the PL condition to derive sharper bounds, which is a common technique in non-convex optimization. The extension of the analysis to minibatch SGD is another significant methodological contribution, as it provides a more practical perspective on the algorithm's performance. The main empirical findings of the paper are limited to the theoretical results, as there is a notable absence of experimental validation. The theoretical results are presented in the form of several theorems and corollaries, which provide bounds on the generalization error, excess risk, and optimization error. These bounds are expressed using big-O notation, which hides constant factors and makes it difficult to directly apply them in practice. The overall significance of the paper lies in its theoretical contributions to the understanding of non-convex pairwise SGD under heavy-tailed noise. The paper provides a comprehensive theoretical framework for analyzing the generalization and optimization properties of this algorithm, which is a valuable contribution to the field. However, the lack of experimental validation and the complexity of the derived bounds limit the practical applicability of the results. The paper's contributions are novel in the sense that they address a gap in the learning theory for non-convex pairwise SGD with heavy-tailed noise, and the results are significant in that they provide a theoretical foundation for understanding the behavior of this algorithm. However, the practical implications of these results are not fully explored due to the absence of empirical validation and the complexity of the bounds.

## weaknesses
The most significant weakness of this paper is the complete absence of experimental validation. While the theoretical results are interesting and novel, there is no empirical evidence to support their practical relevance. The paper lacks a dedicated "Experiments" section, and there are no tables or figures presenting experimental results on real-world datasets. This absence of empirical validation makes it difficult to assess how well the derived bounds capture the behavior of pairwise SGD in realistic settings. It is unclear whether the assumptions made in the analysis, such as the sub-Weibull gradient noise and the PL condition, hold in practice. Without empirical evidence, the theoretical findings remain somewhat abstract, and their practical impact is hard to gauge. This is a critical limitation, as it prevents the reader from fully understanding the practical implications of the theoretical results. The lack of experimental validation is a recurring concern, as all four reviewers independently identified it as a major weakness. The confidence level in this weakness is high, as it is directly observable from the absence of an "Experiments" section and any presentation of experimental results. Another significant weakness is the reliance on the Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition. The paper explicitly states the PL condition as Assumption 3.9, and several key theorems (4.6, 4.8, 4.9, and 4.11) rely on this assumption. The PL condition is a strong assumption that may not hold for many non-convex loss functions encountered in practice. This limitation restricts the applicability of the theoretical results to a specific class of problems. The paper does not include a detailed discussion about the limitations of the PL condition or potential relaxations. This is a critical oversight, as it limits the scope of the analysis and makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the results. The confidence level in this weakness is high, as it is directly observable from the explicit statement of the PL condition as an assumption and its use in several key theorems, coupled with the lack of discussion about its limitations. Furthermore, the theoretical results, while novel, are complex and difficult to apply directly in practice. The derived bounds, expressed using big-O notation, involve multiple nested terms and constants that are difficult to estimate or interpret in real-world scenarios. For example, Theorem 4.2 states: "$\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{\substack{i,j\in[n],i\neq j}}\mathbb{E}\left[\Vert w_{T}-w_{T}^{\prime}\Vert\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\left((\beta n)^{-1}L T^{\frac{1}{2}}\log T\right)$". The constant factor within the $\mathcal{O}$ notation is not specified, and the parameters $\beta$, $L$, and $T$ are not always easy to estimate in practice. This makes it hard to translate the theoretical insights into practical guidelines for hyperparameter tuning or algorithm design. The constants hidden within the big-O notation may have a significant impact on the actual performance, but these are not explicitly addressed, limiting the practical utility of the results. The confidence level in this weakness is high, as it is directly observable from the use of big-O notation in the theorems and the presence of abstract parameters. The paper also lacks concrete guidance on how to choose the step size and batch size in practice. The theoretical results provide bounds on the convergence rate and generalization error, but they do not provide explicit formulas for selecting these parameters. The authors should provide practical recommendations for choosing these parameters based on the theoretical analysis and empirical observations. For example, the authors could provide a rule of thumb for selecting the step size based on the smoothness of the loss function and the heavy-tailed parameter of the gradient noise. Similarly, the authors could provide guidance on how to choose the batch size based on the size of the dataset and the computational resources available. This practical guidance would make the paper more accessible to practitioners and facilitate the adoption of the proposed algorithm in real-world applications. The confidence level in this weakness is high, as it is directly observable from the absence of practical guidance on parameter selection. Finally, the paper does not adequately discuss the limitations of the theoretical analysis or potential directions for future research. While the authors provide a comparison with existing results, it is not clear how these results can be used to guide the design of practical algorithms. The paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the theoretical analysis and potential directions for future research. This could include exploring alternative assumptions or analysis techniques that could lead to more practical bounds. For example, they could investigate the possibility of deriving bounds that are less sensitive to the constants hidden within the big-O notation, or explore the use of different stability measures that may be more suitable for pairwise learning.
