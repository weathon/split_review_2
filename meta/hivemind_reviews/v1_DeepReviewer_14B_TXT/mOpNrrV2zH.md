## summary
This paper introduces CBGBench, a novel benchmark designed to unify and evaluate structure-based drug design (SBDD) methods. The core idea behind CBGBench is to frame various SBDD tasks, such as de novo molecule generation, linker design, and scaffold hopping, as a graph completion problem. This approach allows for a standardized evaluation framework, enabling a more direct comparison of different generative models. The authors have curated a diverse set of evaluation metrics, encompassing chemical properties, interaction patterns, and geometric validity, to provide a comprehensive assessment of model performance. The benchmark includes a modular codebase, which facilitates the integration of various SBDD methods and their extension to new tasks. The experimental results presented in the paper highlight the strengths and limitations of current SBDD methods, offering valuable insights for future research directions. Specifically, the paper demonstrates the applicability of CBGBench to a range of tasks, including de novo generation, linker design, fragment growing, side chain decoration, and scaffold hopping. The results show that while current methods perform reasonably well on de novo generation and linker design, there is still room for improvement in tasks like fragment growing and scaffold hopping. The paper also emphasizes the importance of considering multiple evaluation metrics, as models that perform well on one metric may not necessarily excel on others. The authors acknowledge the limitations of relying solely on computational metrics and express their intention to explore the use of AI to assist in more accurate metric calculation in future research. Overall, CBGBench represents a significant step towards standardizing the evaluation of SBDD methods and provides a valuable resource for the drug design community. By unifying various SBDD tasks under a common framework and providing a comprehensive set of evaluation metrics, this benchmark has the potential to accelerate the development of more effective and reliable AI-driven drug design tools. The paper's contribution lies not only in the creation of the benchmark itself but also in the detailed analysis of the results, which offers insights into the current state of the art and highlights areas where further research is needed. The authors' commitment to a modular and extensible codebase also ensures that CBGBench can be easily adapted and extended as the field of SBDD continues to evolve.

## strengths
I find the primary strength of this paper to be its introduction of a unified benchmark, CBGBench, for structure-based drug design. This is a significant contribution as it addresses the need for a standardized evaluation framework in a field characterized by diverse tasks and evaluation metrics. The paper's approach of framing various SBDD tasks as a graph completion problem is innovative and allows for a more direct comparison of different generative models. This unified perspective is a major advantage, as it enables researchers to assess the performance of models across multiple tasks using a consistent methodology. The inclusion of a wide range of evaluation metrics, encompassing chemical properties, interaction patterns, and geometric validity, is another notable strength. This comprehensive approach ensures that models are evaluated from multiple perspectives, providing a more complete picture of their performance. The modular codebase is also a significant asset, as it facilitates the integration of various SBDD methods and their extension to new tasks. This flexibility is crucial for the continued development and adaptation of the benchmark as the field evolves. The paper's detailed analysis of the experimental results is also commendable. The authors do not simply present the results; they delve into the strengths and weaknesses of each method, providing valuable insights into the challenges of SBDD. This analysis is particularly useful for identifying areas where current methods excel and where further research is needed. The paper's clear and concise writing style makes it easy to follow, and the figures and tables are well-presented and informative. Overall, I believe that CBGBench has the potential to become a valuable tool for the drug design community, and this paper provides a solid foundation for its continued development and adoption.

## weaknesses
While I appreciate the contributions of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, although the paper claims to include CNN-based methods, my analysis reveals that the inclusion is not as comprehensive as it could be. While methods like LiGAN and VoxBind are mentioned and their results are presented, the paper does not fully explore the potential of voxel-based representations, which are a key aspect of many CNN-based approaches. The paper's focus seems to be primarily on graph-based methods, with CNN-based methods being somewhat of an afterthought. This is evident in the limited discussion of voxelization strategies and the lack of exploration of different CNN architectures. This is a significant limitation because voxel-based methods offer a distinct approach to capturing spatial relationships, and their limited representation in the benchmark restricts the scope of the evaluation. The paper's classification of methods in Table 1 also does not fully capture the nuances of different CNN architectures, further highlighting this issue. This lack of comprehensive coverage of CNN-based methods undermines the benchmark's claim to be a truly unified evaluation framework. Secondly, I have serious concerns about the paper's reliance on computational metrics without wet-lab validation. The paper acknowledges this limitation in the "Conclusion and Limitation" section, stating that "many of the metrics are obtained through computational chemistry methods. These computational functions are often considered unable to accurately reflect the chemical properties of molecules, such as the calculated SA (Synthetic Accessibility) and AutoDock Vina Energy." However, I believe this issue is more significant than the paper suggests. The synthetic accessibility score (SA), for instance, is a crucial factor in determining the practical viability of a designed molecule. While computational methods can provide an estimate of SA, they often fail to capture the complexities of real-world synthesis. Similarly, the binding affinity predicted by AutoDock Vina, while useful, is an approximation that can differ significantly from experimental results. The paper's reliance on these computational metrics without any form of experimental validation raises concerns about the real-world applicability of the benchmark. This is particularly problematic because the ultimate goal of SBDD is to design molecules that can be synthesized and tested in the lab. If the computational metrics do not accurately reflect these real-world properties, the benchmark's value is significantly diminished. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper itself acknowledges the limitations of computational metrics, and the potential for discrepancy between computational predictions and experimental results is well-documented in the literature. Finally, I find the lack of detailed guidelines for the practical implementation of CBGBench to be a significant weakness. While the paper mentions a "modular codebase" and "standardized training and evaluation protocols," it does not provide the actual code or detailed instructions on how to set up the benchmark, preprocess input data, handle edge cases, or integrate new models. This lack of clarity makes it difficult for other researchers to adopt and extend the benchmark. The paper's claim of facilitating a more systematic evaluation of AI-driven drug design methods is undermined by the difficulty in reproducing and extending the benchmark. This is a crucial issue because the widespread adoption of the benchmark is essential for its long-term impact on the field. Without clear guidelines and a readily available codebase, the benchmark's potential to unify and standardize SBDD evaluation is severely limited. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does mention the existence of a codebase, but the lack of detail in the paper itself makes it difficult to assess its completeness and usability.

## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I propose several concrete and actionable suggestions. Firstly, to enhance the benchmark's coverage of CNN-based methods, I recommend a more comprehensive exploration of voxel-based representations. This would involve adapting the current framework to handle 3D grid inputs and outputs, which would require careful consideration of grid resolution, voxelization strategies, and the appropriate evaluation metrics for these methods. For example, metrics like voxel-wise accuracy or intersection-over-union (IoU) could be used to assess the quality of the generated voxel grids. Furthermore, the benchmark should include a more diverse set of CNN architectures, such as those using transposed convolutions for generative tasks. This would not only broaden the scope of the benchmark but also provide valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of different modeling approaches. The paper should also provide a more detailed discussion of the voxelization process, including the specific parameters used and their impact on the results. This would allow other researchers to reproduce the results and adapt the benchmark to their specific needs. Secondly, to address the limitations of relying solely on computational metrics, I strongly suggest incorporating experimental validation as a key component of the benchmark. This could involve selecting a subset of generated molecules and testing their binding affinity using biophysical assays, such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR) or isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). Additionally, the synthetic accessibility of the generated molecules should be evaluated by attempting to synthesize them in the laboratory. This would provide a more realistic assessment of the practical utility of the generated molecules and help identify methods that are not only computationally efficient but also practically viable. The inclusion of experimental validation would significantly increase the credibility and impact of the benchmark. The paper should also discuss the challenges associated with experimental validation and provide guidance on how to overcome these challenges. This would make the benchmark more accessible to researchers who may not have access to advanced experimental facilities. Finally, to improve the practical implementation of CBGBench, I recommend providing detailed guidelines and documentation. This should include step-by-step instructions on how to set up the benchmark, preprocess input data, train and evaluate models, and integrate new methods into the framework. The documentation should also include examples of how to use the benchmark with different models and datasets. Furthermore, the authors should consider providing a modular codebase that allows researchers to easily extend and customize the benchmark for their specific needs. This would make the benchmark more accessible and encourage its widespread adoption within the drug discovery community. The codebase should be well-documented and easy to use, with clear instructions on how to install and run the benchmark. The authors should also provide a mechanism for users to report bugs and suggest improvements, ensuring that the benchmark remains up-to-date and relevant to the needs of the community. By addressing these issues, the authors can significantly enhance the value and impact of CBGBench as a unified and standardized evaluation framework for SBDD.
