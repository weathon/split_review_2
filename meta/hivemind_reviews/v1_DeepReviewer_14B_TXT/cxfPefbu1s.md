## Summary
This paper introduces a novel framework for addressing algorithmic fairness by focusing on the data generating process, a perspective that shifts away from solely outcome-based fairness considerations. The authors draw inspiration from John Rawls's concept of 'pure procedural justice,' arguing that fairness should be embedded within the very procedures that generate data and drive decision-making. The core idea is to decouple 'objectionable' components of the data generating process from 'neutral' ones. This is achieved through the use of reference points and a value instantiation rule, which ensure that predictions are based only on the neutral components, thereby preventing what the authors term 'disguised procedural unfairness.' The framework is motivated by the observation that many existing fairness interventions, while addressing direct discrimination, may inadvertently alter neutral aspects of the data generating process, leading to unintended and potentially unfair outcomes. The authors define 'objectionable components' as any problematic aspects of the data generating process, which can include edges in the causal graph or segments of paths, and 'neutral components' as those that are not problematic. The proposed method involves identifying these components, configuring reference points to benefit the least advantaged individuals, and applying a value instantiation rule to ensure that only neutral components influence predictions. The paper presents a theoretical analysis of the framework, demonstrating its properties and limitations. Empirically, the framework is evaluated through experiments on both simulated and real-world datasets, including a linear model and the UCI Adult dataset. The results of these experiments are used to illustrate the problem of disguised procedural unfairness and to validate the effectiveness of the proposed solution. The paper's main contribution lies in its unique perspective on algorithmic fairness, its formalization of procedural fairness using causal concepts, and its practical framework for mitigating unfairness in data generating processes. By focusing on the procedures that generate data, the authors aim to provide a more robust and equitable approach to algorithmic decision-making. The paper's emphasis on preserving the integrity of neutral components while addressing objectionable ones is a significant departure from many existing fairness methods, which often alter all aspects of the data generating process. Overall, the paper presents a compelling argument for considering procedural fairness and offers a practical framework for achieving it, though it also reveals several limitations that warrant further investigation.

## Weaknesses
Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, independently validated by multiple reviewers, is the framework's reliance on a known and accurate causal graph. The paper explicitly states, 'Given a causal graph that represents the underlying data generating process...' (Section 4), highlighting its dependence on a fully specified causal model. This assumption is a significant limitation, as obtaining a complete and accurate causal graph is often challenging in real-world scenarios, especially for complex systems with multiple interacting factors. The paper does not adequately address the practical challenges of obtaining such a graph or the sensitivity of the framework to errors in the causal structure. The framework's reliance on a fully specified DAG means that it cannot directly handle situations where causal relationships are uncertain, latent, or involve feedback loops. This limits its applicability in complex, real-world settings where causal structures are often partially known or disputed. The paper lacks a discussion of how the framework would perform with an incomplete or incorrect causal graph, which is a critical omission given the method's dependence on this assumption. This limitation is not merely theoretical; it has significant practical implications, as the effectiveness of the framework is heavily dependent on the accuracy of the specified causal graph. Any errors in the causal structure could lead to incorrect identification of objectionable and neutral components, potentially undermining the fairness goals. My analysis confirms that the paper does not include any sensitivity analysis or discussion of how the framework would perform with an incomplete or incorrect causal graph. This is a significant weakness that undermines the practical applicability of the framework. This lack of practical guidance is a significant limitation, as the identification of objectionable components is a crucial step in applying the framework. ## Suggestions

First, the authors should explicitly address the limitations imposed by the reliance on a fully known causal graph. This could involve exploring methods for handling uncertainty in the causal structure, such as using techniques from causal discovery or robust causal inference. Specifically, the authors could investigate how the framework performs when the causal graph is misspecified or when there are unobserved confounders. It would be valuable to analyze the sensitivity of the proposed method to different causal structures and to provide guidelines on how to mitigate the impact of errors in the assumed causal graph. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the framework to variations in the causal assumptions. This would enhance the practical applicability of the framework in scenarios where the data generating process is not fully known. Second, the authors should provide more detailed practical guidance on how to implement the framework. This could involve developing a more structured approach for identifying and defining 'objectionable components' and 'neutral components.' For example, they could explore methods for quantifying the degree of association between features and protected attributes, or use causal discovery algorithms to identify potential pathways of discrimination. This would provide a more principled way to determine which components of the data generating process should be considered objectionable. Furthermore, the authors could investigate the use of domain expertise and stakeholder input to guide the classification process, ensuring that the framework is both technically sound and socially relevant. This could involve developing a systematic protocol for eliciting and incorporating expert knowledge about the data generating process and its potential biases. The authors should also provide concrete examples of how to identify and classify components in complex, real-world scenarios, such as a hiring process. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the framework. This should include a formal analysis of the time and space complexity, as well as an empirical evaluation of the framework's performance on large-scale datasets. This would help to assess the scalability of the method and identify potential bottlenecks. The authors could also consider exploring approximations or optimizations to improve the computational efficiency of the framework, especially for high-dimensional data or complex causal graphs. This could involve investigating the use of optimization algorithms to find the optimal configuration of reference points and instantiation rules that maximize fairness while minimizing the loss of predictive accuracy. Fourth, to improve the intuitive understanding of 'disguised procedural unfairness,' the authors should provide more concrete examples and a more detailed explanation of how it manifests in real-world scenarios. This could include case studies or examples from specific domains, such as lending, hiring, or healthcare. These examples should illustrate how seemingly neutral processes can inadvertently perpetuate unfairness and how the proposed framework can address these issues. The authors should also consider expanding their empirical evaluation to include a wider range of datasets and scenarios. This could include datasets with more complex causal structures, as well as datasets from different domains. This would help to demonstrate the generalizability of the framework and its ability to address diverse fairness challenges. Finally, the authors should provide a more thorough discussion of the ethical implications of using reference points to modify the data generating process. This could include a discussion of the potential for unintended consequences and the need for transparency and accountability in the use of the framework. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly enhance the practical applicability and impact of their work.
