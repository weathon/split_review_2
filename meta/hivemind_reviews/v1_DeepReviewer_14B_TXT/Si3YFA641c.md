## summary
This paper introduces R-EDL, a refined version of Evidential Deep Learning (EDL) designed to address two specific limitations inherent in the traditional EDL framework. The authors pinpoint the rigid setting of the prior weight parameter, which is typically fixed to the number of classes, and the variance-minimized regularization term in the loss function as the primary sources of these limitations. They argue that these settings can lead to overconfident predictions and hinder the model's ability to accurately quantify uncertainty. To overcome these issues, R-EDL proposes treating the prior weight as an adjustable hyperparameter and deprecating the variance-minimized regularization term. Instead, R-EDL directly optimizes the expectation of the Dirichlet distribution. The core idea is to provide a more flexible and accurate uncertainty estimation by allowing the model to better balance the proportion and magnitude of evidence when making predictions. The authors present a theoretical analysis of how the prior weight influences the evidence-to-confidence mapping, demonstrating that a fixed prior weight can lead to counter-intuitive outcomes. They also explain how the variance-minimized regularization term encourages the Dirichlet distribution to approach a Dirac delta function, which exacerbates overconfidence. Empirically, the paper demonstrates that R-EDL outperforms traditional EDL and other state-of-the-art methods on various uncertainty estimation tasks, including out-of-distribution (OOD) detection and image classification, across several datasets. The experimental results show that R-EDL achieves improved performance in terms of both classification accuracy and uncertainty estimation, particularly in challenging scenarios such as few-shot learning and noisy data. The paper also includes an ablation study to assess the impact of the proposed modifications, showing that both relaxing the prior weight setting and deprecating the variance-minimized regularization term contribute to the improved performance of R-EDL. The authors also analyze the effect of the hyperparameter λ, which is related to the prior weight, on the model's performance. Overall, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of uncertainty estimation by identifying and addressing specific limitations within the EDL framework. The proposed R-EDL method offers a simple yet effective way to improve the accuracy and reliability of uncertainty estimates, which is crucial for deploying deep learning models in safety-critical applications. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, providing a clear and detailed explanation of the proposed method and its theoretical underpinnings. The comprehensive experimental evaluation further strengthens the credibility of the proposed method, demonstrating its effectiveness across a variety of tasks and datasets. However, the paper also has some limitations, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

The paper acknowledges that R-EDL is a generalization of EDL, and the differences between the two methods are relatively small. While the experimental results show that R-EDL does perform better than EDL, the improvements are not always dramatic. ## suggestions
To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more in-depth theoretical analysis of why relaxing the prior weight and deprecating the variance-minimized regularization term leads to the observed performance gains. While the paper provides some mathematical analysis of the prior weight's influence and explains why minimizing variance can lead to overconfidence, a more detailed analysis, potentially with visualizations, would be beneficial. For example, the authors could investigate how the prior weight affects the shape of the Dirichlet distribution and how this, in turn, influences the uncertainty estimates. Similarly, a theoretical analysis of the variance-minimized regularization term could shed light on why its deprecation leads to better calibration. This could involve examining the gradients of the loss function and their impact on the learned parameters. This would not only enhance the theoretical contribution of the paper but also provide a more solid foundation for the proposed method. Second, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive exploration of the hyperparameter space. While the paper includes a parameter analysis for λ, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the interaction between hyperparameters or a thorough sensitivity analysis across various datasets and tasks. This could involve a grid search or a more sophisticated hyperparameter optimization technique. The results of this analysis should be presented in a clear and concise manner, perhaps through tables or plots, to demonstrate the robustness of the proposed method and provide practical guidance for practitioners. Additionally, the paper should discuss the computational cost associated with tuning these hyperparameters and how this cost compares to other uncertainty estimation methods. Third, the authors should include a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of R-EDL compared to traditional EDL and other uncertainty estimation methods. This should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for both training and inference, compared to standard EDL and other uncertainty estimation techniques. This should also include a discussion of how the adjustable hyperparameter for the prior weight impacts computational overhead. Furthermore, it would be useful to explore the potential for approximations or optimizations that could mitigate any increased computational burden, making R-EDL more practical for real-world applications with limited resources. Fourth, the authors should expand the experimental evaluation to include more challenging datasets. Specifically, the paper should include experiments on datasets with higher dimensionality, such as those found in medical imaging or remote sensing, where the feature space is significantly larger and more complex. Additionally, the performance of R-EDL should be evaluated on datasets with varying levels of noise and class imbalance, which are common in real-world applications. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses and its ability to handle the complexities of real-world data. Finally, the authors should broaden the scope of the paper by investigating the applicability of R-EDL to other domains beyond image classification. For example, in natural language processing, R-EDL could be used for tasks such as sentiment analysis or text classification, where uncertainty estimation is crucial. Similarly, in time-series analysis, R-EDL could be applied to tasks such as anomaly detection or forecasting, where accurate uncertainty quantification is essential. This would not only broaden the impact of the paper but also provide valuable insights into the generalizability of the proposed method across different types of data and tasks.
