## Summary

This paper proposes Meta-Rewarding, an iterative self-improvement method that adds a third role—meta-judge—to the self-rewarding framework. The meta-judge evaluates the model's own judgments (not just its responses), generating preference pairs for training both the actor and the judge simultaneously. Starting from Llama-3-8B-Instruct, the method improves AlpacaEval 2 LC win rate from 22.9% to 39.4% and Arena-Hard from 20.6% to 29.1% over four iterations, outperforming a Self-Rewarding baseline equipped with the same length-control mechanism.

## Strengths

1. **The meta-rewarding framework yields clear improvements over a strong baseline.** On AlpacaEval 2 LC win rate, Meta-Rewarding reaches 39.44% at iteration 4 vs. 35.49% for Self-Rewarding + LC (Table 1, ~4% gap). On Arena-Hard, it reaches 29.1% vs. 27.3% (Table 2). These results demonstrate that adding judge training via the meta-judge provides non-trivial gains beyond what actor-only training achieves.

2. **The judge's agreement with GPT-4 improves meaningfully, particularly on self-generated responses.** On Self-Chosen Pairs (agreement without ties), Meta-Rewarding rises from 61.03% (seed) to 79.33% at iteration 4 (Table 4). This exceeds Self-Rewarding + LC's 78.42% at the same iteration, confirming that the meta-judge refines the model's own judging capability.

3. **Principled handling of known confounders.** The paper introduces a length-control mechanism ($\rho$ parameter) that selects shorter responses among top-scoring ones, and carefully addresses positional bias in the meta-judge via bidirectional evaluation with weighted scoring (Eq. 1–3). Table 5 shows that without length-control ($\rho=0$), responses become excessively verbose and LC win rate suffers.

4. **Broad coverage of improvement.** The method improves LC win rate in 17 out of 18 AlpacaEval categories (Figure 4, labeled Figure 3 in text), including reasoning-heavy categories (science, literature, gaming), demonstrating that gains are not limited to narrow skills.

5. **Transparent reporting of limitations.** The paper explicitly documents meta-judge degradation (Table 6), score saturation (Figure 5), and the decision to halt judge training after iteration 2. This honesty is commendable and allows future work to target these specific bottlenecks.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **The meta-judge degrades rapidly, forcing early termination of judge training — a core design failure.** After only two iterations, the meta-judge exhibits severe bias: 97.68% preference for higher-scored judgments and 68.11% positional bias (Table 6). The authors respond by dropping judge training entirely in iterations 3 and 4, switching to actor-only DPO. While this is transparently reported, it means the method's key differentiator—simultaneous training of actor and judge via meta-rewards—operates for only half the training process. The paper does not propose any mitigation (debiasing, regularization, early-stopping rule), leaving the method brittle and unlikely to scale to more iterations. The paper's own ablation (Table 5, dashes for low-$\rho$ Meta-Rewarding runs) also suggests instability.

2. **Different length-control parameters are used for the two methods in the main comparison, making the comparison less clean.** The main results (Table 1) use $\rho=0.4$ for Meta-Rewarding but $\rho=0.1$ for Self-Rewarding + LC (confirmed by Table 5). Since $\rho$ controls the trade-off between score-based selection and length preference, and different values produce different win rates (e.g., Self-Rewarding at $\rho=0.3$ achieves 35.83% vs. 35.49% at $\rho=0.1$), some portion of the observed gap may reflect the length-control policy rather than the meta-judge itself. The dashed entries in Table 5 for Meta-Rewarding at $\rho=0, 0.3, 0.35$ prevent a controlled comparison at matched $\rho$ values.

3. **Key comparisons are mentioned in text without supporting tables or controlled conditions.** (a) The SPPO baseline (38.77% LC win rate) is cited in prose (line 281) but absent from the main tables, despite being a stronger comparison than some included baselines. (b) MT-Bench results are described in a single sentence (line 298) with no table provided, making the claim that the method "does not sacrifice multi-turn ability" unsupported. These omissions weaken the evidential completeness of the paper.

### Minor

1. **No confidence intervals for the headline AlpacaEval 2 results.** While Arena-Hard includes 95% CIs (Table 2), AlpacaEval 2 — where the strongest claim resides (39.44% vs. 35.49%) — reports only point estimates. Given that the Arena-Hard CIs overlap between Meta-Rewarding and Self-Rewarding + LC at several iterations (e.g., Iter 4: 29.1% [-2.3, +2.1] vs. 27.3% [-2.0, +2.2]), the absence of variance estimates for AlpacaEval makes it harder to assess whether the headline gap is robust to evaluation noise.

2. **The judge evaluation relies primarily on agreement with GPT-4, which measures convergence to a proxy rather than ground-truth judgment quality.** The paper does include a human correlation experiment (Open Assistant), but acknowledges that "this improvement is not sustained over later training iterations" (line 340), and the human correlation scores are not shown in a table. The judge agreement numbers in Table 4 show that at iteration 4, Meta-Rewarding's advantage over Self-Rewarding + LC on Self-Chosen Pairs agreement-without-ties is only 79.33% vs. 78.42% — a 0.91% gap. On GPT-4 Chosen Pairs, the gap is similarly narrow (59.54% vs. 53.12%, but the Self-Rewarding baseline is noisy across iterations). The claim of "significant improvement in judging" is somewhat overstated given these numbers and the proxy nature of the evaluation.

3. **The initial SFT step uses human-annotated data (EFT/Open Assistant).** While the paper is transparent about this (line 208) and the baseline uses the same procedure, the framing of "no human supervision" (abstract, conclusion) conflates "no human feedback during iterative self-play" with "no human data at all." The method's self-improving iterations genuinely use no human data, which is the fair claim, but the abstract's phrasing overreaches slightly.

### Trivial

- The abstract reports the seed model's LC win rate as 22.9%, while a footnote in Table 1 reports their own evaluation as 24.57% and explains this is due to a different inference template. The values are for two slightly different conditions, but the presentation could confuse readers.
- Table 5 contains dashes ("-") for Meta-Rewarding win rates at $\rho=0, 0.3, 0.35$. While the paper explains these runs produced degenerate outputs, providing the actual (low) win rates would be more informative.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An experiment continuing judge training into iterations 3 and 4 (perhaps with explicit debiasing or regularization of the meta-judge) would clarify whether the observed saturation is inherent or addressable.
- Reporting judge-human Spearman correlations in a table rather than only in prose would strengthen the claim of improved judging ability.
- A controlled comparison at matched $\rho$ values (e.g., both methods at $\rho=0.3$) would isolate the contribution of the meta-judge from the length-control policy.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from this set of reviews is the tension between the method's promise and its documented fragility. The meta-judge demonstrably provides useful signal for two iterations (yielding gains over the baseline), but then degenerates into near-deterministic bias. The fact that judge *ability* continues to improve in iterations 3 and 4 (Table 4) even *without* explicit judge training suggests that actor improvements indirectly benefit the judge—a spillover effect the paper does not discuss. This raises an intriguing possibility: perhaps the judge training is most valuable early on to "jump-start" the judge, after which actor improvements alone suffice to carry judging ability further. If true, the method could be simplified by explicitly budgeting when to apply meta-judge training.
