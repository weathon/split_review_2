## Summary
This paper proposes "label sharpness" ($K_\mathcal{F}$), a measurable dataset property quantifying how similar images can be while having different labels. It derives scaling laws connecting dataset intrinsic dimension, label sharpness, and generalization error, showing that higher $K_\mathcal{F}$ in medical datasets may explain their steeper generalization scaling compared to natural images. The paper also links $K_\mathcal{F}$ to adversarial robustness and connects dataset intrinsic dimension to representation intrinsic dimension, supported by experiments across 6 models and 11 datasets.

## Strengths
1. **Operationalizes label sharpness as a measurable dataset property.** The paper defines $K_\mathcal{F}$ via Eq. (1) and provides a practical estimator $\hat{K}_\mathcal{F}$ (Eq. 3) computable in under one second on 1000 samples. Prior work (Bahri et al., 2021) treated this as an unknown constant; this paper makes it empirically estimable, enabling quantitative analysis of domain discrepancies. The finding that medical datasets have roughly 2.5× higher $K_\mathcal{F}$ than natural ones (Figure 1) is a concrete, reproducible observation.

2. **Derives a generalization scaling law incorporating $K_\mathcal{F}$ and connects it to observed domain differences.** Theorem 1 (building on Bahri et al.) gives $L = O(K_L\max(K_f, K_\mathcal{F}) N^{-1/d})$, and Theorem 2 argues $K_f \simeq K_\mathcal{F}$ for large $N$, yielding $L \simeq O(K_L K_\mathcal{F} N^{-1/d})$. The paper then uses this to explain the steeper scaling for medical data observed in prior work (Konz et al., 2022): medical datasets' higher $K_\mathcal{F}$ predicts faster growth of log loss with intrinsic dimension. Figure 2 shows a clear visual separation between domains consistent with this account.

3. **Establishes a theoretical and empirical link between $K_\mathcal{F}$ and adversarial robustness.** Theorem 4 derives $\hat{R}(f) = \Omega(1/K_\mathcal{F})$, and experiments using FGSM attacks across six models and all datasets show positive Pearson correlations between attack loss penalty and $K_\mathcal{F}$ (Table 1, e.g., $r=0.77$ for ResNet-18 at $\epsilon=1/255$). This substantiates the observation that medical models are typically more vulnerable to adversarial attack.

4. **Unifies dataset intrinsic dimension and representation intrinsic dimension scaling laws.** Theorem 4 (representation scaling law) and Theorem 5 ($d_r \lesssim d$) reconcile two previously separate lines of research, supported by empirical validation in Figure 5 showing $d$ systematically bounds $d_r$ across all model–dataset pairs.

### Major
1. **The main scaling-law experiment does not cleanly isolate the effect of $d$ from confounding factors.** Figure 2 plots $\log L$ against $d$ pooling all (model, dataset, $N$) triplets together. Points with the same $d$ come from different $N$ values, and points with the same $N$ appear at different $d$. The observed separation between domains could be partially driven by differences in $N$ across datasets, differences in $K_\mathcal{F}$, or other unmeasured factors. The paper states (line 151) that "models trained on datasets with similar $d$ and of the same size $N$ tend to perform much worse if the data is medical images," but the main figure does not explicitly control for $N$, and the supporting analysis (footnote 1) is deferred to the appendix. Per-dataset scaling plots showing $\log L$ vs. $\log N$ with predicted slopes $-1/d$ would provide a much cleaner test of Eq. (2) and the claimed role of $K_\mathcal{F}$.

2. **Theorem 2 ($K_f \to K_\mathcal{F}$) is central but unverified in the main text.** The claim that the trained model's Lipschitz constant converges to the labeling function's Lipschitz constant is strong and not obvious for overparameterized neural networks—it depends on implicit bias, architecture, and training procedure. The proof is deferred to the appendix, and no empirical validation (e.g., spectral norm estimates of $K_f$ vs. $\hat{K}_\mathcal{F}$) is provided. If this theorem does not hold approximately for the models studied, the simplified scaling law $L \simeq O(K_\mathcal{F} N^{-1/d})$ lacks justification, and the model-dependent $K_f$ term cannot be removed.

### Minor
1. **The adversarial robustness analysis is correlational with limited data points.** The evidence linking $K_\mathcal{F}$ to adversarial vulnerability is based on cross-dataset correlations across 11 datasets. The Pearson correlations vary substantially across architectures (e.g., $r=0.18$ for ResNet-34 at $\epsilon=8/255$), and some of the variation is unexplained. While the paper does not claim strict causality, the theoretical bound (Theorem 4) suggests a causal relationship that the experiments only partially support.

2. **Theorem 5 ($d_r \lesssim d$) is acknowledged as a rough estimate but presented as a formal theorem.** The paper transparently notes this limitation (line 281: "While a rough estimate"), but the result is essentially an algebraic consequence of assuming both scaling laws hold with the same $L$, rather than a substantive theorem. This does not invalidate the empirical observation in Figure 5 (which shows the relationship holds in practice), but it inflates the theoretical contribution.

3. **Only FGSM attacks are used for adversarial robustness evaluation.** While FGSM is a standard baseline, stronger attacks (PGD, CW) could produce different correlations. The paper does not acknowledge this limitation explicitly.

4. **The $\hat{K}_\mathcal{F}$ estimator (Eq. 3) is the maximum over sampled pairs and could be sensitive to a single near-identical opposite-class pair.** The paper uses $M=1000$ and reports confidence intervals, but the estimator's variance across random subsets is not discussed. Additionally, the estimator is defined for binary classification only, which limits generality.

5. **The claim that "no existing theoretical explanation" exists (line 4) is somewhat overstated.** Konz et al. (2022) already observed and documented the domain discrepancy; the paper's contribution is proposing $K_\mathcal{F}$ as *a* factor, not the *first* explanation.

## Novel Insights
The most novel observation emerging across the reviews is not present in the paper itself: the $\hat{K}_\mathcal{F}$ estimator for binary labels reduces to $1 / \min_{x_j,x_k: y_j\neq y_k} \|x_j - x_k\|$, making it literally the inverse of the minimum distance between opposite-class points in the sample. This interpretation clarifies both the strength of $K_\mathcal{F}$ (it directly captures the "hardness" of the nearest decision boundary) and its vulnerability (it can be dominated by a single near-duplicate opposite-class pair, making it a maximum-type statistic with potentially high variance). The paper could usefully discuss this interpretation explicitly.
