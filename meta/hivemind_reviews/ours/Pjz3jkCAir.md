## Summary

This paper introduces CONFIDE, a method that combines a learned context encoder with a known PDE functional form to estimate coefficient functions in a zero-shot manner from observed spatio-temporal data. The approach uses an autoencoder to compress observed signals, a coefficient estimator to output PDE parameters, and a finite-difference-based functional loss to train without requiring ground-truth coefficients. Experiments on four PDE families (constant-coefficient, Burgers', FitzHugh-Nagumo, Navier-Stokes) show large prediction improvements over FNO, U-Net, Neural-ODE, and DINo, and also demonstrate generalization to out-of-distribution coefficients and initial conditions.

## Strengths

- **Zero-shot generalization across unseen coefficient distributions is convincingly demonstrated.** The OOD coefficient experiment (Table 1, Burgers' OOD Coefficients) shows CONFIDE achieving prediction MSE 0.0074 ± 0.0100, substantially lower than Neural-ODE (0.0423), FNO (0.9646), and Unet (0.0096). This directly validates the central claim that the method can handle test signals generated by coefficients outside the training distribution.

- **Consistently superior prediction across all four PDE families.** Table 1 reports CONFIDE achieving the lowest end-of-horizon prediction MSE on every setup: constant coefficients (0.0023 vs next-best Unet 0.0160), Burgers' (0.0008 vs Unet 0.0016), FitzHugh-Nagumo (0.0083 vs CONFIDE-0 0.0845), and Navier-Stokes (0.0033 vs CONFIDE-0 0.0173). The performance gaps are large and consistent.

- **Accurate coefficient estimation with quantitative evidence.** For the constant-coefficient setup, Figure 2b reports an R² of 0.93 between estimated and true coefficient values. Low coefficient estimation errors are reported across setups in Table 1 (e.g., 0.0095 ± 0.0131 for constant coefficients, 7.5×10⁻³ ± 1.23×10⁻² for FitzHugh-Nagumo-2D).

- **Large (44×) training speed advantage over integration-based methods** is concretely shown in Section 4.5: CONFIDE trains in 3.6 seconds versus 159.2 seconds for Neural-ODE on a pendulum ODE toy example, validating the computational benefit of the finite-difference approach.

- **Ablation study validates the initial-conditions-aware autoencoder design.** Section 4.6 reports that adding the initial condition as decoder input yields ~35% improvement in both signal prediction and parameter estimation over the no-autoencoder or no-initial-conditions variants, providing clear empirical support for the architectural choice.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **How the coefficient estimator network outputs function-valued coefficients is underspecified.** The paper's general PDE form (Eq. 1) allows coefficients p_l(x,t,u) that can depend on space, time, and the solution. For Burgers' equation, b(u) = −u is a function of u. The paper describes the output as "a vector p̂" (line 150) but never explains how a function-valued coefficient is represented — whether as a field over the grid, as parameters of a basis expansion, or as a learned embedding that is evaluated pointwise. The functional loss (Eq. 4) evaluates ||F(p̂, u(x,τ))||² at spatio-temporal points, which implicitly requires p̂ to be evaluable on the grid, but the mechanism is never stated. This is a genuine clarity gap: a reader cannot reproduce the approach for PDEs with genuinely function-valued coefficients (as opposed to the constant-coefficient and scalar-parameter cases that dominate the experiments). The paper should state explicitly how the network parameterizes functions of (x,t,u).

- **The adaptation of FNO and U-Net baselines to the multi-context setting is not explained.** The paper reports that "All approaches observe the first 50 time points and predict the next 150" (line 376), but FNO and U-Net are typically designed to map an initial condition to a solution under a fixed operator. When the operator (PDE coefficients) varies across samples, these models would need modification to handle the varying dynamics. The paper does not describe how FNO and U-Net consume the observed time series — whether by concatenation, channel stacking, or some other conditioning mechanism. Without this, the large performance gaps (e.g., CONFIDE 0.0033 vs FNO 4.4223 on Navier-Stokes) cannot be properly interpreted: they may reflect an architectural mismatch rather than a fundamental advantage of CONFIDE. (Note: CONFIDE-0, which also uses the context encoder but lacks the PDE form, outperforms FNO/U-Net too, which partially mitigates this concern, but the baseline adaptation remains unclear.)

### Minor

- **The "Coefficient estimation error" metric (Table 1, lines 384–397) is never defined.** For constant coefficients, it is presumably MSE between estimated and true scalar values. For Burgers' equation, where one coefficient (b(u) = −u) is a function, it is unclear whether the error is computed pointwise over the spatio-temporal grid, integrated over the domain, or measured on a derived quantity. The paper should state the metric explicitly for each setup.

- **The OOD coefficient experiment reveals a limitation that is acknowledged but underexplored.** The paper honestly states (line 441) that CONFIDE "learns to output coefficients only in the range of the coefficients in the train set, it projects the observed signal to the range of coefficients in the train set so that it best describes the observed signal." This means the estimated coefficients for OOD data are systematically biased toward the training range, yet the prediction error stays low. The paper does not analyze this trade-off — how far the estimated coefficient is from the true value when the true a is outside U[1,2], or whether the model is learning a robust but non-identifiable mapping. Since coefficient explainability is a stated benefit, this warrants deeper investigation.

- **The Burgers' coefficient estimation figure (Figure 4b) caption could mislead.** The caption says CONFIDE "accurately estimates the spatio-temporal dynamics of the coefficient," and the panel shows a field over (x,t). The ground-truth coefficient b(u) = −u is a function of u only — but when evaluated at the solution u(x,t), the product naturally varies with x and t. This is not an error (the estimate is correct), but the caption could be read as implying the coefficient itself has spatio-temporal structure independent of u, which is not the case.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from the reviews — which extends beyond what the paper explicitly discusses — is the tension between prediction accuracy and coefficient identifiability in the OOD setting. The paper shows that CONFIDE can achieve low prediction MSE (0.0074) even when it is forced to output coefficients in the training range (U[1,2]) for data generated with coefficients outside that range (U[2,4]). This suggests the model is learning a family of forward maps parameterized by the context embedding, and different coefficient values within the training range can produce similar predictions for held-out dynamics. The implication is that coefficient explainability and predictive accuracy may trade off in the OOD regime: the model can extrapolate predictions well, but the inferred coefficients may not reflect the true generative parameters. This is a practically important point that deserves explicit study.

The combination of a learned context encoder with a finite-difference-based functional loss is a meaningful contribution.

**Importance of research question:** The problem of inferring PDE coefficients from data with varying dynamics is highly relevant to scientific modeling, where each observational instance (e.g., each battery cell, each patient) may have different physical parameters.
