## Summary
This paper identifies "perturbation synergy" (mutual dependence between perturbation regions) as a bottleneck for adversarial transferability in black-box attacks, and proposes Adversarial Perturbation Dropout (APD). APD uses Grad-CAM++ to locate attention hotspots, then at each iteration creates multiple "dropped" versions of the adversarial image where perturbations in square regions around those hotspots are removed. Gradients from all dropped variants are averaged to update the perturbation. The method is designed as a plug-in for existing iterative attacks (MI-FGSM, DIM, TIM, SIM, AAM) and reports consistent improvements of 10–19% on ImageNet across multiple source/target model combinations, including ensemble attacks and defended models.

---

## Strengths
1. **Well-motivated mechanism.** The paper identifies perturbation synergy as a concrete obstacle to transferability and provides an empirical anchor (Figure 1b): selectively removing perturbations that the source model attends to but the target does not causes a larger drop in ASR than random removal. This motivates breaking synergy rather than spreading perturbations further. The connection to dropout as a regularization technique is conceptually coherent.

2. **Large and consistent gains across diverse settings.** APD improves MI-FGSM by 12.7% on average in single-model attacks (Table 1), and boosts DIM, TIM, SIM, AAM, and AA-TI-DIM by 10–12%. In ensemble attacks (Table 2) the gain averages 15.62%. On defended models and diverse architectures (Table 3), APD-AA-TI-DIM beats AA-TI-DIM by 2.6% on defenses and by up to 13.3% (ViT-B/16). These are non-trivial improvements over already-strong baselines.

3. **CAM-guided dropout justified over random dropout.** Figure 4 shows that CAM-based region selection consistently outperforms random selection across all four source models, validating the design choice. The rationale — that random dropping may miss attention hotspots or fail to break synergy among multiple hotspots within a block — is clearly explained.

4. **Plug-and-play integration.** APD is demonstrated as a general wrapper — it improves every baseline it is applied to (MI, DIM, TIM, SIM, AAM, AA-TI-DIM) without modifying their internal procedures. This is genuine evidence of practical utility.

5. **Thorough hyperparameter ablations.** The study of β (Figure 5) and number of centers/scales (Figure 6) shows clear trends and saturation behavior, establishing that the chosen defaults (β=27, ~4 centers, ~7 scales) are reasonable and the method is not pathologically sensitive.

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
None.

### Minor

1. **CAM dependency limits the source-model scope.** APD requires Grad-CAM++ at each iteration, which is defined for architectures with spatial feature maps (standard CNNs). All source models in the experiments are CNNs (Inc-v3, Inc-v4, IncRes-v2, Res-101). While this matches the practice of many transferability papers, the paper claims APD can be "seamlessly integrated" (lines 32, 228) without acknowledging this architectural limitation. A brief discussion of applicability to non-CNN source models (e.g., ViT-based sources) and what would be needed to extend APD to them is missing.

2. **Disparate results on lightweight models left undiscussed.** In Table 3, APD improves AA-TI-DIM by 13.3% on Seq2d and 11.3% on ViT-B/16, but only 1.8% on MnasNet. The paper reports this number without analysis. Readers would benefit from a brief discussion: is this a property of lightweight architectures, of the MnasNet-specific attention patterns, or of some other factor? Understanding where APD's mechanism fails is as informative as where it succeeds.

3. **Dropout operation could be more precisely specified.** The paper describes $x_{tjk}^{drop}$ as "the image generated by dropping the perturbations located at the j-th center point with scale kβ from $x_t^{adv}$" (line 133). The natural interpretation is that the accumulated perturbation is set to zero in that square region (effectively restoring those pixels to near-clean values), but the paper does not state this explicitly. While the method is implementable from the description, adding one sentence of precision would eliminate ambiguity.

4. **Hyperparameter β tuned on the test set.** The ablation of β (Figure 5) selects β=27 based on performance against the same target models used in the final evaluation tables. A separate validation split would be methodologically cleaner. This is common practice in the adversarial attack literature and does not invalidate the results, but it is worth noting as a limitation on the claimed optimality.

5. **Figure 1(b) motivation is qualitative.** The motivating experiment on selective vs. random noise removal is described only via a bar chart image without reporting the exact ASR values in the text. The paper states "Selective Noise Removal can induce a more significant decrease" (line 24) but the reader cannot evaluate the magnitude. Reporting the actual percentages would strengthen the empirical motivation.

### Trivial
None.

## Suggestions
1. Add one sentence making the dropout operation explicit: "Dropping perturbations at a region means setting the accumulated adversarial perturbation in that square region to zero, effectively restoring those pixels to their clean values."
2. Include a brief discussion of the MnasNet result — why does APD provide minimal gain on lightweight architectures?
3. Acknowledge the CAM dependency on CNN source models as a limitation in the conclusion or a dedicated limitations paragraph. Note what would be needed to extend the method (e.g., using attention maps from ViT's self-attention layers).
4. Report the exact ASR values from Figure 1(b) in the text so the motivating experiment is quantitatively interpretable.

---

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a clear, empirically supported contribution: it identifies perturbation synergy as a transferability bottleneck, proposes a principled dropout mechanism to address it, and demonstrates large, consistent improvements across a broad experimental landscape (5+ baselines, 4 source models, 7 target models, defended models, diverse architectures). No verified weakness threatens the core claims. The limitations (CAM dependency on CNNs, undiscussed failure cases on MnasNet, minor precision issues) are addressable and do not undermine the paper's value.
