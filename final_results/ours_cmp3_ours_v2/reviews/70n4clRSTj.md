## Summary

**SpookyBench** is a synthetic benchmark that tests whether Video-VLMs can perceive information encoded purely through temporal (motion) patterns when individual frames contain only structured noise. The benchmark has three categories (Words, Object Images, Dynamic Scenes) generated by algorithms that create motion-defined forms. Humans achieve >98% accuracy, but all 15 tested Video-VLMs — spanning 2B–78B parameters, open- and closed-source — score 0%. Fine-tuning on 400 SpookyBench videos still yields 0% test accuracy. The paper frames this as evidence that current models are "time-blind."

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated gap.** The paper correctly identifies that existing temporal benchmarks (TemporalBench, TVBench, VidHalluc) allow spatial shortcuts, meaning performance on them conflates spatial and temporal abilities. SpookyBench cleanly eliminates this confound by design.

- **Striking negative fine-tuning result (Section 4.4).** Fine-tuning InternVL2.5-8B and Qwen2-VL-7B on 400 SpookyBench videos for 10 epochs still yields 0% test accuracy. This rules out the "out-of-distribution" explanation and points toward a genuine architectural limitation.

- **Comprehensive model coverage.** 15 models spanning 2B to 78B parameters, including closed-source (GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, Gemini 2.0 Flash), video-specialized (TimeChat, VideoGPT+, InternVideo2.5), and general-purpose VLMs. The breadth makes the 0% result harder to dismiss as a quirk of one architecture.

- **Frame-rate control (Section 4.3).** Testing humans and models across 1–30 FPS shows that temporal sampling density does not explain the gap — humans need ≥20 FPS but models fail at every rate.

- **Clear and reproducible benchmark design.** Algorithms 1 and 2 are fully specified. The noise generation, motion parameters, and SNR metrics are well-documented. The dataset is synthetic and can be regenerated arbitrarily.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Framing mismatch between what is tested and what is claimed.** The paper claims to test "temporal reasoning" and "temporal understanding" (Title, abstract, introduction Line 13). What SpookyBench actually tests is **motion-defined form perception** — detecting coherent motion vectors in noise, segmenting regions based on motion boundaries, and recognizing shapes/text/objects from those motion-defined regions. This is a low-level perceptual capability (figure-ground segregation via motion cues), not temporal reasoning about event order, causality, or long-range dependencies. An architecture could be capable of temporal reasoning while being unable to detect pattern boundaries in noise via motion. The paper's conclusions about "fundamentally time-blind architectures" (Section 6) and "significant implications for real-world applications" (Section 1) are broader than what the benchmark's task actually measures. The benchmark itself remains useful as a diagnostic, but the claims need to be reframed to match what it tests.

- **No diagnostic analysis of why models fail.** The paper reports 0% with 0.0 standard deviation across all models but provides only cursory qualitative output descriptions ("models acknowledged the instruction but still failed," "fine-tuned models mimicked training examples" — Section 5). Critical unanswered questions: (1) Do models detect the coherent motion at all? Probing ViT token embeddings across frames would clarify whether failure is at the motion perception stage or later. (2) What is the distribution of failure types — how often do models output "I cannot see anything" vs. confabulating wrong answers vs. describing noise artifacts? (3) Is the 0% partially an artifact of exact-match evaluation? Reporting softer metrics (BLEU, ROULE, or human evaluation of outputs) would distinguish between "models detect something but can't verbalize it" and "models detect nothing." Without this analysis, the paper cannot distinguish between genuine architectural inability and limitations of the evaluation protocol.

### Minor

- **Fine-tuning experiment is underreported (Section 4.4).** The paper does not report training accuracy (could models overfit the 400 training videos?), learning curves, validation loss, or the fine-tuning objective (next-token prediction on captions? classification head?). These details are critical for interpreting the negative result — whether models failed to learn anything at all, or learned the training distribution but couldn't generalize.

- **Human evaluation is undersized for precise claims.** Six participants (Section 4.2) is sufficient to establish the *qualitative* finding that humans can do the task — all six score >91% on every category. But the paper uses "98.0% ± 0.6" as a precise quantitative anchor against which models' 0% is contrasted. With n=6, confidence intervals are wide (Table 3 shows Dynamic Scenes accuracy ranging from 91.2% to 99.0% across annotators). A larger sample would substantiate the precise accuracy claim.

- **Dynamic Scenes category is very small (57 videos, 12.6% of dataset, per Section 3.3).** Performance estimates on this category have high variance. More videos would improve reliability.

- **SNR threshold analysis (Section 3.3.2) is confusing.** The text describes a binary threshold effect where accuracy jumps from ~0% to 85.7% at 2.5 dB SNR, mentions "Prompts performed best (40% accuracy)," and references Chain-of-Thought prompting. It is unclear whether these results are from humans or models. If from models, the ~0% below threshold and 85.7% above contradict the paper's main finding that all models score 0%. If from humans, the mention of prompting strategies is misplaced. Figure 4's table shows binary 0%/100% accuracy, which does not match the 85.7% figure in the text.

- **No classical CV baselines.** The paper does not test whether pre-neural methods (frame differencing, optical flow + template matching) can solve the task. If a simple motion-detection algorithm succeeds, it clarifies the bottleneck is in learned representations rather than task difficulty. If even classical methods fail, the benchmark may be too hard as a diagnostic.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Correlation with real-world temporal tasks.** The paper would be strengthened by showing whether performance on SpookyBench predicts performance on natural temporal tasks (temporal grounding, event ordering, anomaly detection in real videos). Currently the practical significance is asserted but not demonstrated.

- **Parameter ablation.** The paper does not justify why specific velocity (v) and noise density values were chosen, or whether results are sensitive to these choices.

- **Graduated difficulty variants.** Adding easier conditions (higher SNR, partial spatial cues) would produce a more informative failure spectrum than the binary 0% vs. 98% divide.

- **Human study demographics.** The paper would benefit from reporting participant screening criteria (normal/corrected vision, familiarity with such stimuli) and viewing conditions.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding — beyond the 0% result itself — is that fine-tuning on the exact task distribution does not help at all (Section 4.4). This goes beyond "models haven't seen data like this" and suggests the bottleneck is architectural: current frame-sampling, spatial-feature-first video architectures may lack the representational capacity to extract any information from motion-defined patterns when individual frames carry no spatial signal. This is distinct from typical failures on temporal benchmarks, where models at least achieve above-chance performance. However, the paper does not follow through on this insight with diagnostic experiments (probing, representation analysis, simpler baselines) that would clarify *where* the bottleneck lies. The insight is therefore suggestive rather than proven.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the paper's scope.** Change "temporal reasoning" to "motion-defined form perception" or "temporal pattern perception" throughout. This aligns claims with the actual task and makes the contribution more precise and defensible.

2. **Diagnose the failure.** The single most impactful addition would be probing whether ViT encoders register the coherent motion at all (e.g., frame-to-frame embedding similarity analysis, lightweight probes on internal representations). This would transform the paper from "models fail" to "here is where and why they fail."

3. **Report softer metrics and sample model outputs.** Show BLEU/ROUGE scores, or at minimum provide qualitative examples of model outputs with failure mode categorization. This addresses concerns about exact-match ceiling effects.

4. **Expand the human evaluation or soften the quantitative claim.** With n=6, replace "98.0% ± 0.6" with a more qualitative claim ("all six participants achieved >91% on every category") or increase the sample.

5. **Add classical baselines.** Frame-differencing, optical flow + template matching, or even a 3D ResNet would clarify whether the task is solvable at all by non-LLM methods.

6. **Clarify the SNR threshold analysis (Section 3.3.2).** Make explicit whether the data is from humans or models, and resolve the inconsistency between the 85.7% figure in the text and the binary 0%/100% in Figure 4's table.

7. **Report training accuracy and learning curves for the fine-tuning experiment (Section 4.4).** This would clarify whether models failed to learn anything or learned the training set but couldn't generalize.

8. **Add more human participants.** Even 20 participants would substantially improve the statistical reliability of the human baseline.
