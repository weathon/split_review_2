# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper investigates how three LLM compression paradigms—quantization, distillation, and pruning—affect the reasoning capabilities of Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), specifically DeepSeek-R1 and its distilled variants. The authors conduct performance benchmarking across four reasoning datasets (AIME 2024, FOLIO, Temporal Sequences, MuSiQue) and apply mechanistic interpretability (difference of means + attribution patching) to identify which weight matrices are most important for reasoning and how compression shifts importance patterns. The paper presents three main findings: (1) parameter count affects knowledge retention more than reasoning capability, (2) the MLP up-projection in the final layer of distilled LRMs is among the most important components, and (3) current quantization methods over-compress final-layer modules and MLP gate projections—protecting 2% of weights can improve accuracy by 6.57%. The scope covers three compression paradigms across multiple model families (Llama, Qwen) at various scales (7B-671B), making it the most comprehensive empirical study of LRM compression to date. However, the work has several significant methodological limitations: the attribution patching method relies on an untested first-order approximation, key comparisons are confounded by uncontrolled variables, statistical rigor is uneven, and some central claims are overstated relative to the evidence presented.

## Strengths
1. **Comprehensive empirical scope.** The paper systematically benchmarks three compression paradigms (quantization, distillation, pruning) across a diverse set of model families (Llama, Qwen) and scales (7B-671B), covering both standard accuracy metrics and knowledge-intensive evaluation. This is the broadest empirical study of LRM compression to date and provides a useful reference point for the field.

2. **Novel application of mechanistic interpretability to compression.** Adapting difference of means and attribution patching to study compression effects on weight importance is a creative methodological contribution. The fine-grained, per-module analysis (seven linear components per layer across all layers) is more granular than prior layer-wise analyses (e.g., Venhoff et al., 2025) and represents a genuine step forward in understanding how compression alters model internals.

3. **Actionable practical finding.** The discovery that protecting only ~2% of weights (final-layer MLP modules) yields 6.57% accuracy improvement over baseline 3-bit AWQ is practically significant. This mixed-precision insight can be directly incorporated into compression toolkits and offers a clear, low-cost improvement path for practitioners.

4. **Empirical validation of importance scores.** The paper validates its weight-importance rankings by selectively quantizing individual components and showing that higher-ranked components cause larger accuracy drops (Table 3). This validation loop—discovery through interpretability, confirmation through targeted intervention—strengthens the credibility of the findings.

5. **Cross-model consistency.** The observation that the final-layer up-projection is the most important component holds across both Llama-8B and Qwen-7B distilled models, suggesting the finding may have some generality across architectures. Similarly, the quantization over-compression pattern is observed for both AWQ and GPTQ methods.

## Weaknesses
### W1. Attribution patching relies on untested first-order approximation (Major)

The importance score $I_{m\ell}^c$ in Equation (2) uses a first-order Taylor approximation $(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{m\ell}^c)^\top \frac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf{a}_{m\ell}} \mathcal{L}$ to estimate the causal effect of shifting a module's activation along the steering direction. This approximation is theoretically valid only when the model's input-output map is locally linear in the activations—a strong assumption for transformer LRMs with layer normalization, GeLU activations, attention softmax, and residual connections creating highly nonlinear compound effects. The paper does not validate this approximation against ground-truth activation patching (directly intervening on activations), nor does it discuss the expected error magnitude. If the approximation is poor, the importance rankings that form the basis of all three contribution claims could be unreliable. **Fix:** Add a validation experiment comparing attribution-patching rankings against actual activation patching on a subset of layers; report rank correlation. Bound the claims accordingly if the approximation error is non-negligible.

### W2. Knowledge vs reasoning claim is confounded by cross-architecture comparison (Major)

The paper's first key finding—"weight count has a greater impact on knowledge memorization than reasoning"—is primarily supported by comparing Llama-70B versus Qwen-32B on MuSiQue (knowledge) versus reasoning benchmarks. This comparison confounds architecture family (Llama vs Qwen), pre-training data distribution, tokenization, distillation recipe, and parameter count simultaneously. The observed MuSiQue gap could arise from any of these factors. The paper acknowledges that "the smaller parameter count of Qwen puts itself at a disadvantaged position" but does not provide within-family comparisons (e.g., Llama-8B vs Llama-70B) to isolate parameter count as the causal factor. **Fix:** Add within-family MuSiQue comparisons. If computationally feasible, also evaluate at iso-parameter settings (e.g., compare Llama-32B with Qwen-32B). Bound the claim to: "Within each model family, smaller models show proportionally larger drops on knowledge-intensive tasks than on reasoning tasks."

### W3. Headline SOTA and "6.57% improvement" claims lack proper baselines (Major)

The abstract states that protecting 2% of weights can "greatly surpass the state-of-the-art," but the paper does not define what SOTA refers to. Table 4 only compares 3-bit AWQ with and without selective protection. The "state-of-the-art" is not a specific published method or result but appears to be the unprotected 3-bit AWQ baseline itself. Without external SOTA comparisons on the exact same models and datasets, this wording is misleading. The "gains of up to 23.17%" (Section 5.2) compares the protected model against individual baseline entries in Table 1, but these baselines span different compression methods and model families—cherry-picking the best comparison inflates the perceived improvement. **Fix:** Replace all unqualified "state-of-the-art" wording with specific comparative statements (e.g., "improving over standard 3-bit AWQ by 6.57% average accuracy"). Compare against published SOTA mixed-precision quantization methods, if any exist for these models.

### W4. Selective protection validation does not control for quantization error magnitude (Major)

Section 5.2 interprets the 6.57% improvement from restoring final-layer MLP weights to 16-bit as validating that these modules are "overly compressed" by AWQ. However, an alternative explanation is that final-layer MLP modules have wider numerical ranges or more outlier activations, incurring larger quantization error under any method. The improvement may primarily reflect error reduction rather than mechanistic importance. Without per-module quantization error (MSE) reporting and a control experiment using randomly selected weights at the same 2% budget, the causal attribution is ambiguous. **Fix:** Report per-module quantization MSE. Add a control: protect 2% of randomly selected weights and compare the improvement. If the random-control improvement is substantially smaller, the mechanistic importance interpretation is strengthened.

### W5. Baseline statistical rigor is uneven (Major)

The original R1 and dynamically quantized R1 models († rows in Table 1) are evaluated with a single pass, while all other models use three-run averages. These single-run results are used as baselines for the paper's central comparisons (e.g., "2.51-bit R1 reaches close-to-R1 performance"). Without variance or confidence intervals, it is impossible to determine whether a 3.4-point AIME difference (76.7 vs 73.3) is meaningful or noise. The paper also does not report per-seed results anywhere, making it impossible to assess run-to-run variability for any model. **Fix:** Report single-run results for all † models alongside instruction that variances are unavailable. Use cautious comparison language throughout. If computationally feasible, run at least 2-3 seeds on the 671B models to establish variance bounds.

### W6. 1_up anomaly undermines the rank-importance validation (Major)

Table 3 shows that quantizing 1_up—the lowest-ranked up-projection layer—causes the largest single-component accuracy drop on AIME 2024 (from 42.2 to 6.7, a 35.5-point drop, compared to 32_up's 22.2-point drop). The paper acknowledges this exception but does not explain it. This anomaly directly contradicts the claim that "component rank generally correlates with the accuracy drop" and suggests either (a) the importance metric has systematic blind spots for early layers, (b) importance is task-specific in ways not captured by the aggregated RI, or (c) the validation method has confounds (e.g., 1_up may have extreme weight ranges causing disproportionate quantization error). **Fix:** Provide explicit analysis of the 1_up anomaly. Report weight statistics (range, outliers, quantization MSE) for early vs late layers. If the anomaly cannot be explained, add a clear caveat that the importance ranking is less reliable for early-layer components and should be validated on a per-layer basis before being used for compression decisions.

### W7. The decision to zero out importance increases hides compensatory dynamics (Major)

Section 2.3 states that when visualizing importance shifts, only decreases in RI are shown because "any increase in relative importance necessarily compensates for decreases elsewhere." While mathematically correct for a normalized score, this choice discards information about compensatory dynamics—e.g., whether after quantization, certain middle-layer modules increase in relative importance, indicating the model is routing around damaged components. Understanding these compensatory patterns could be as valuable for compression design as knowing which modules lose importance. **Fix:** Show full shift maps (increases and decreases) in the appendix. In the main text, explicitly discuss whether any systematic compensatory patterns were observed and what they imply.

### W8. Causal language about distillation overstates the evidence (Minor)

Section 4.3 claims "distillation effect is quite powerful in transforming a non-reasoning LLM into an LRM" and that important weights "are primarily the result of distillation with SFT." However, the evidence only shows that importance patterns differ between base and distilled models. This is consistent with either (a) distillation introducing new reasoning-specific weight specializations or (b) fine-tuning on reasoning data eliciting latent capabilities already encoded in the base model's weights. The paper's current phrasing implies causation without evidence. **Fix:** Replace causal language with correlational wording (e.g., "The importance shift is consistent with distillation introducing reasoning-related specialization"). Add a control: compare with a model fine-tuned on non-reasoning data to see if importance patterns are data-specific or task-specific.

### W9. Abstract makes unsupported SOTA claim (Minor)

The abstract closes with "greatly surpassing the state-of-the-art," which is unverifiable without a clear definition of the SOTA baseline. This wording is likely to trigger reviewer concern and may need to be removed or precisely scoped even in a revised version. **Fix:** Replace with specific, bounded phrasing: "...outperforming unprotected 3-bit AWQ by 6.57% average accuracy on R1-Distill-Llama-8B. Comparisons with published SOTA mixed-precision methods remain as future work."

### W10. Conclusion is too brief and lacks limitations (Minor)

The three-sentence conclusion makes an unsupported generalization claim ("These findings generalize across both R1 and non-R1 models") without presenting the Appendix J evidence in the main text, and does not acknowledge any of the study's limitations. A strong conclusion should summarize validated claims, state bounded limitations, and prioritize future work. **Fix:** Expand the conclusion as suggested in the annotation on Page 9. Add a dedicated Limitations subsection before or within the conclusion.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

The paper addresses an important and timely research question—how compression affects reasoning in large reasoning models—with a commendably broad empirical scope covering three compression paradigms across multiple model families. The application of mechanistic interpretability to compression analysis is novel and yields practically actionable insights (e.g., protecting final-layer MLP modules). 

However, the score is constrained by several significant methodological concerns. The core interpretability pipeline (attribution patching) relies on an untested first-order Taylor approximation, and the validation experiment (Table 3) contains an unexplained anomaly (1_up) that weakens confidence in the importance rankings. The paper's most striking claims—that parameter count affects knowledge more than reasoning, and that selective protection outperforms SOTA—are supported by confounded comparisons and unsubstantiated SOTA claims, respectively. Statistical rigor is uneven (single-run baselines for the most important comparisons). 

These issues are fixable through additional validation experiments and more cautious framing. The paper's strengths (broad scope, novel methodology, actionable findings) provide a solid foundation, but the current evidence does not yet support the full strength of the claims being made. With targeted revisions addressing the major weaknesses identified above, this work could become a valuable reference for the LRM compression community.
