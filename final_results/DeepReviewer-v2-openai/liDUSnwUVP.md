## Summary

This paper presents HG-DCM (History-Guided Deep Compartmental Model), a framework for early-stage pandemic forecasting that integrates historical outbreak data with a deep learning parameter predictor and a compartmental (DELPHI) model. The core idea is to train a neural network across multiple biologically distinct pandemics (Ebola, SARS, Dengue, seasonal influenza) so that when a novel pathogen emerges (COVID-19), the model can leverage learned priors about parameter dynamics rather than relying solely on sparse current-outbreak data. The method uses a modified ResNet (without Batch Normalization) to extract embeddings from time series, combines them with epidemiological/demographic metadata, and predicts the 12 time-varying parameters of the DELPHI compartmental model. An IVP solver then generates cumulative case forecasts. Experiments on early COVID-19 forecasting (2-8 week training windows, 12-week forecast horizon, 258 locations) show that HG-DCM reduces overshooting and improves median MAE over DELPHI and a CNN baseline.

**Strengths:** The cross-disease transfer paradigm is a novel and well-motivated approach to the cold-start problem. The architecture preserves compartmental interpretability while benefiting from neural network flexibility. The constructed multi-pandemic dataset is a valuable resource. The ablation studies (DELPHI, CNN, T-DCM) provide useful isolation of the historical data and mechanistic modeling contributions.

**Weaknesses:** The empirical evaluation is limited — only 2 external baselines on 2 locations for the main comparison, with an unexplained Mean MAE anomaly at the 4-week setting. The core assumption that biologically distinct pandemics share universal dynamics is insufficiently defended. Several methodological details (loss function stability at zero values, \(\beta\) selection, BN-removal validation) are not fully specified. The limitations section omits important issues (testing bias, dataset imbalance, single-disease evaluation). Novelty claims ("first study," "new paradigm") cannot be verified without external literature search (deferred).

## Strengths
**S1. Novel and well-motivated cross-disease transfer paradigm.** The core idea of training a deep compartmental model across multiple biologically distinct pandemics to create transferable priors for novel pathogen forecasting is innovative and addresses a genuine unmet need. The paper correctly identifies that standard compartmental models fail in the cold-start phase, and the proposed solution — learning a shared mapping \(f\) from early-stage signals to transmission parameters — is principled and grounded in epidemiological intuition. This framing provides a clear research contribution regardless of the specific empirical outcomes.

**S2. Architecture preserves epidemiological interpretability.** Unlike purely black-box deep learning models, HG-DCM predicts the 12 interpretable parameters of the DELPHI compartmental model (infection rate, median day of action, death rate, etc.). This allows the authors to analyze parameter distributions and show that HG-DCM produces more conservative estimates than DELPHI (Figure 5). The interpretability advantage is a concrete strength that goes beyond forecasting accuracy metrics.

**S3. Multi-pandemic dataset as a contribution.** The authors compiled a diverse historical pandemic dataset spanning COVID-19, Ebola, SARS, Dengue, and seasonal influenza, with country-level metadata. The paper acknowledges this was assembled because no public database existed. If released, this dataset could become a useful benchmark for the epidemiological forecasting community, enabling future work on cross-disease transfer learning. The data augmentation strategies (window-shift for past pandemics, masking for current) are technically sound and well-justified.

**S4. Targeted ablation study.** The ablation design (DELPHI alone, CNN alone, T-DCM without historical data) cleanly separates the contributions of historical guidance, mechanistic structure, and deep learning. The comparison between HG-DCM and T-DCM (same architecture, different training data) directly demonstrates that historical data drives the performance gain, not the architectural complexity. This is stronger evidence than a simple "our method beats baseline" comparison.

**S5. Clear motivation and writing structure.** The introduction builds a compelling narrative from the practical importance of early pandemic forecasting, through the cold-start problem, to the proposed solution. The "human epidemiologist intuition" analogy (Page 1, line 11) is effective for conveying why historical knowledge should help. The paper is generally well-organized and easy to follow.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Limited baseline comparison undermines SOTA claims (Major).** The headline claim of outperforming "state-of-the-art methods" rests on a comparison against only 2 external baselines (GradABM, EiNNs), tested on only 2 locations (US and Massachusetts). For the US, GradABM has zero results, and for Massachusetts, EiNNs is missing at the 2-week setting (Table 1). EiNNs actually outperforms HG-DCM in 3 of 8 settings (US 4-week, MA 6-week, MA 8-week by median). The paper justifies this limited comparison by citing lack of reproducible code from the COVID-19 Forecast Hub, but this means the external validation is too narrow to support strong comparative claims. The ablation comparisons against DELPHI and CNN are stronger but insufficient to establish SOTA status.

**W2. Unexplained Mean MAE anomaly at 4-week setting (Major).** In Table 2, HG-DCM's Mean MAE at 4 weeks (110,452.4) is approximately 6× larger than at 2 weeks (18,602.6) and 15× larger than at 6 weeks (7,112.5). Meanwhile, the Median MAE shows a monotonic improvement. This discrepancy suggests that at 4 weeks, a small number of locations produce catastrophic failures, inflating the mean. The paper does not discuss or explain this anomaly, and the narrative claims "HG-DCM consistently outperforms DELPHI" without mentioning this counterintuitive pattern. This undermines confidence in the model's stability.

**W3. Core assumption of universal pandemic dynamics insufficiently defended (Major).** The paper's central premise — that biologically distinct pandemics (SARS, Ebola, Dengue, seasonal influenza) share universal macroscopic dynamics — is stated as fact with a single citation [Viboud et al.]. Dengue is vector-borne with seasonal patterns driven by mosquito ecology, Ebola spreads through contact with bodily fluids, and influenza has strong winter seasonality. The paper does not provide evidence that these share the same transmission decay patterns or response-to-intervention shapes. If this assumption is weak, cross-disease transfer could introduce systematic bias rather than useful regularization. The experiments only test on COVID-19, so they do not validate transferability to other novel pathogens.

**W4. Loss function has mathematical and indexing ambiguities (Major).** The MAPE term in Eq. 3-4 divides by \(C_{ij}\) (actual cumulative cases), which can be zero at early time points, producing undefined values. The paper states "negative daily case counts are set to zero" but does not address zero cumulative cases. Additionally, the index \(j=0\) is not defined — is it the first day cumulative cases exceed 100? If so, \(C_{i0} \approx 100\), which partially mitigates the zero-division issue, but this is not stated. The \(\beta\) weighting in Eq. 5 (\(L = L_P + \beta L_C\)) is not explained — how \(\beta\) is selected (cross-validated? set to 1?) is not discussed. These issues affect reproducibility.

**W5. Empirical validation is retrospective and single-disease (Major).** The method is only validated on COVID-19 case forecasting. To demonstrate that cross-disease transfer generalizes, the authors would need to either (a) hold out one disease from training and test on it, or (b) validate on a future outbreak. The current design (training on Ebola/SARS/Dengue/Influenza + early COVID, testing on later COVID) does not test whether the method works for truly novel pathogens — it tests whether historical pandemics help forecast the same pandemic they are training on. This is a weaker form of generalization.

**W6. Missing reproducibility details for the dataset (Major).** The constructed multi-pandemic dataset is a claimed contribution, but the description lacks critical details: exact inclusion/exclusion criteria are vague ("more than 100"), no dataset size statistics are reported (number of locations per pandemic, time series lengths), no train/validation/test splitting strategy is described, and the harmonization process (different reporting frequencies, standards, regional aggregations) is not explained. Without these details, the dataset cannot be reproduced or evaluated for potential biases (e.g., influenza dominance).

**W7. Overclaiming in novelty and contribution framing (Moderate).** The abstract claims HG-DCM "establishes a new paradigm," and the introduction asserts "this is the first study" for cross-disease temporal transfer. Without external literature verification (deferred in this run), these claims are unsubstantiated. Even within the paper's own framing, the claim is weakened by the limited evaluation. The discussion section makes broader claims about "a necessary shift in perspective" and "data-centric generalization" that extrapolate beyond the evidence — the paper does not directly test whether data diversity matters more than architecture depth.

**W8. Limitations section is too narrow (Moderate).** The Limitations section (Section 4) only discusses data granularity and lack of mortality data. It omits several critical issues: (a) testing/reporting bias confounds early case counts across countries, (b) dataset imbalance (influenza dominates), (c) single-disease (COVID-19 only) evaluation limits generalization claims, (d) no discussion of whether the model can be updated in real-time as a pandemic unfolds, (e) no discussion of spatial generalization — whether patterns learned from one location's metadata transfer to another. A more comprehensive limitations section would strengthen the paper by showing awareness of these issues.

**W9. Parameter inference section contains an error and overclaim (Minor).** The sentence "HG-DCM predicted a lower infection rate, median day of action, median day of action, and death rate" contains a repeated phrase ("median day of action"). The word "realistic" in "produces more conservative and realistic estimates" is an unsupported judgment — we don't know the ground truth parameters for COVID-19. The Wilcoxon test p-values should also be interpreted cautiously because the 258 locations are not independent (spatial correlation in pandemic dynamics).

**W10. BN removal lacks empirical validation (Minor).** The removal of Batch Normalization layers is presented as a key architectural insight, but no ablation experiment (with-BN vs. without-BN) is reported to support the claim. The paper also does not discuss alternatives like Layer Normalization or Group Normalization, which are batch-size independent and could address the same cross-domain batch statistics problem.

## Score
**Final Score: 5.5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper proposes a novel and well-motivated cross-disease transfer paradigm for early pandemic forecasting, with a principled architecture that combines deep learning with interpretable compartmental modeling. The constructed multi-pandemic dataset and targeted ablation studies are valuable contributions. However, the empirical validation is substantially limited — only 2 external baselines on 2 locations, an unexplained Mean MAE anomaly at the 4-week setting, and single-disease (COVID-19 only) evaluation that does not fully test the cross-disease generalization claim. The core assumption of universal pandemic dynamics across biologically distinct diseases (SARS, Ebola, Dengue, Influenza) is insufficiently defended. Several methodological details (loss function stability, hyperparameter selection, dataset splitting) lack specification, harming reproducibility. Novelty claims ("first study," "new paradigm") cannot be verified without external literature search, which was unavailable in this run. The paper has clear potential but requires substantial strengthening of the empirical evidence and more careful claim-bounding before it meets the bar for a top-tier venue. Score emphasizes research value and novelty as primary dimensions, with validity concerns limiting the current assessment.
