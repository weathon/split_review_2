## Summary
This paper presents ESS-Flow, a training-free method for controlled generation with pretrained flow-based generative models. The core idea is to reformulate conditional generation as Bayesian inference in the source (latent) space of the generative model, where the prior is a standard Gaussian. By applying Elliptical Slice Sampling (ESS) directly in this source space, the method avoids gradient and Jacobian computations entirely, requiring only forward passes through the generative model and the observation potential function. This gradient-free property is valuable for scientific applications involving non-differentiable simulations, quantization, or discrete constraints (e.g., molecular design with categorical atomic types, space-group symmetry targeting). The method also supports a multi-fidelity extension using importance-weighting across ODE discretization levels. Experiments on materials property targeting (using FlowMM on the MP-20 dataset) and protein backbone structure prediction (using Chroma) demonstrate that ESS-Flow achieves competitive or superior property-targeting accuracy while maintaining structural realism, particularly when compared to gradient-based optimization methods like D-Flow and PnP-Flow, and sampling-based methods like DAPS. The paper is well-structured, the motivation is clear, and the technical derivation (Jacobian cancellation leading to source-space inference) is mathematically sound. Key limitations include the method's reliance on the prior adequately covering the target distribution and the computational cost of MCMC sampling requiring many forward ODE evaluations.

## Strengths
**S1 — Clean and principled methodological connection.** The paper identifies a mathematically elegant link: performing inference in the Gaussian source space of flow-based models via elliptical slice sampling, with the Jacobian terms cancelling out. This is a clever insight that avoids the computational expense and differentiability requirements of existing source-space approaches. The derivation from Eq. (2) to Eq. (3) is sound, and the resulting algorithm (Algorithm 1) is straightforward to implement.

**S2 — Genuinely gradient-free inference.** ESS-Flow demonstrably handles non-differentiable potentials (binary space-group indicators, discrete atomic types) where gradient-based methods like D-Flow, PnP-Flow, and even DAPS (which requires score approximations) cannot operate natively. This is a meaningful practical advantage for scientific applications involving external simulators, quantum chemistry calculations, or discrete optimization constraints.

**S3 — Strong empirical results on materials property targeting.** On the bulk modulus, shear modulus, and band gap targeting tasks, ESS-Flow achieves substantially lower mean absolute errors (8.99 GPa, 10.53 GPa, 1.85 eV respectively) compared to the best competitor (DAPS: 39.14 GPa, 84.33 GPa, 3.90 eV). The histograms in Figure 3 confirm that ESS-Flow samples concentrate near the target values while competing methods show much broader distributions.

**S4 — Honest limitation disclosure.** The paper clearly states that ESS-Flow is most effective when the target distribution is not overly collapsed relative to the prior, and acknowledges that it is not suitable for tasks like noiseless image inpainting. This candid assessment helps practitioners understand when the method is applicable and avoids overclaiming.

**S5 — Applicability to scientific domains with realistic observation models.** The protein structure prediction experiment uses a realistic distance cutoff (6 Å) and noise model, going beyond the simplified evaluation in prior work (Levy et al., 2024). The ELBO and clash count metrics provide a multi-faceted evaluation that reveals the trade-off between data fidelity and structural realism across methods.

## Weaknesses
**W1 — Unfair baseline comparison due to continuous approximation confound (Major).** 
In the materials experiments, D-Flow and PnP-Flow are forced to use a continuous approximation for atomic types (softmax with τ=0.1, Eq. 5), while ESS-Flow and DAPS operate on the true discrete representation. This confound makes it impossible to attribute ESS-Flow's superior performance solely to the gradient-free MCMC approach — part of the gap may arise from the approximation error imposed on the gradient-based methods. The paper's claim that ESS-Flow "outperforms all other methods significantly" conflates two distinct advantages: (a) the ability to handle discrete variables natively and (b) the efficacy of ESS as a sampling mechanism. The paper should include an ablation where ESS-Flow is evaluated under the same continuous approximation to disentangle these factors, and the comparison language should acknowledge this asymmetry more transparently. 
*Evidence anchor: Page 1 - Section 5.1 Material Generation, Eq. (5).*

**W2 — Missing MCMC convergence diagnostics and burn-in analysis (Major).**
As an MCMC method, ESS-Flow inherits all standard convergence challenges: burn-in requirements, autocorrelation, effective sample size monitoring, and multi-chain diagnostics. The paper provides a Proposition 1 (geometric convergence) but does not report any empirical convergence diagnostics for the experiments. Which ESS chain lengths were used? Was burn-in discarded? What was the effective sample size for the reported property estimates? How many independent chains were run? Without this information, the reliability of the reported means and standard deviations cannot be assessed. This is particularly concerning because the paper notes that "moderate numbers of function evaluations" are used, which may be insufficient for chain convergence in high-dimensional problems (e.g., protein structures with ~1764 dimensions).
*Evidence anchor: Page 1 - Section 4.1 (Algorithm 1), Page 1 - Section 6 (Conclusion NFE remark).*

**W3 — Multi-fidelity importance weighting fails for sharp targets (Major).**
The multi-fidelity extension (Section 4.2) uses importance weighting to combine coarse and fine ODE discretizations. However, the paper's own experimental results show that effective sample sizes drop to 0.1% and 1.0% for the band gap and stability tasks, which have sharper target distributions. This indicates that the simple importance weighting approach is practically useless for most realistic settings where posteriors are concentrated. The paper acknowledges this as a "shortcoming" but still presents the method as a contribution. Either a more robust multi-fidelity approach (e.g., delayed acceptance, tempered transitions) should be developed, or the importance-weighting method should be presented with clear applicability criteria (e.g., effective sample size > 10% required for reliable estimation).
*Evidence anchor: Page 1 - Section 5.1.1, Page 1 - Section 4.2 (Eq. 4).*

**W4 — Insufficient protein experiment sample size and metric confounding (Major).**
The protein backbone prediction experiment uses only 10 samples per method. With such a small sample size, the reported means and standard deviations have high Monte Carlo error, and the min RMSD values are dominated by sampling noise. Furthermore, the ELBO metric is computed using Chroma's own evaluation function — since ESS-Flow explicitly enforces the Chroma prior, it has an inherent advantage on this metric that may reflect prior enforcement rather than genuine structural quality. Independent structure quality metrics (e.g., MolProbity score, Ramachandran statistics) would provide a more objective comparison. The paper should increase the sample count (≥50 recommended) and report bootstrap confidence intervals.
*Evidence anchor: Page 1 - Section 5.2 Protein Structure Prediction, Table 4.*

**W5 — Proposition 1 convergence guarantee is existential, not practical (Minor).**
The geometric convergence bound from Natarovskii et al. (2021) depends on unverifiable assumptions (the pullback potential must be bounded away from 0 and ∞ on compact sets with regular tail behavior) and includes a dimension-dependent term (1 + ‖z‖₂) that can be very large for high-dimensional problems. The constants c and β are not specified in terms of tractable problem parameters. This limits the practical value of the theoretical guarantee. The paper should complement this with empirical diagnostics (trace plots, autocorrelation functions, ESS per dimension) from the actual experimental settings.
*Evidence anchor: Page 1 - Section 4.1, Proposition 1.*

**W6 — Velocity field preservation claim needs computational cost qualification (Minor).**
The paper states that ESS-Flow "preserves the pretrained velocity field" as an advantage over guidance-based methods, without acknowledging that this comes at the cost of many forward ODE passes (MCMC iterations × per-iteration forward evaluations). Guidance-based methods require only a single modified ODE pass, which may be orders of magnitude cheaper. This trade-off should be explicitly discussed and quantified (e.g., wall-clock time comparison) to help readers make informed method selection.
*Evidence anchor: Page 1 - Introduction paragraph 5 (line 72), Section 5.1 Material Generation.*

**W7 — Low uniqueness rates suggest potential mode collapse (Minor).**
ESS-Flow's S.U.N.T. rates show substantially lower uniqueness (U.N.) than DAPS across multiple tasks (e.g., 46.1% vs 80.8% for bulk modulus, 30.5% vs 74.6% for shear modulus). While high property attainment is desirable, this combination could indicate that ESS-Flow collapses to a small set of modes satisfying the target rather than broadly exploring the posterior. A multimodality diagnostic and diversity metric should be reported to differentiate genuine posterior concentration from sampling inefficiency.
*Evidence anchor: Page 1 - Table 3 (S.U.N.T. rates).*

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper introduces a clean and well-motivated methodological contribution (gradient-free source-space inference for flow-based models using elliptical slice sampling) and demonstrates promising empirical results on materials property targeting. The core idea of leveraging the Gaussian source prior for gradient-free posterior sampling is technically sound and addresses a genuine need in scientific applications involving non-differentiable simulators. However, the experimental comparison is confounded by the continuous approximation imposed on gradient-based baselines, making the extent of ESS-Flow's advantage unclear. The lack of MCMC convergence diagnostics undermines reproducibility, and the multi-fidelity contribution is shown to be ineffective for concentrated target distributions. The protein structure prediction experiment is underpowered (only 10 samples) and uses a potentially biased metric (ELBO from the prior model itself). The paper's novelty cannot be fully assessed without external literature verification (deferred in this run). With fairer baselines, proper MCMC diagnostics, and larger-scale experiments, the paper could potentially reach 7-8/10.

**Key weaknesses limiting the score:**
1. Confounded baseline comparison in materials experiments (continuous approximation vs. native discrete handling).
2. Missing MCMC convergence diagnostics and burn-in reporting (essential for any MCMC-based method).
3. Insufficient protein experiment sample size and potentially biased evaluation metric.
4. Multi-fidelity importance-weighting approach is shown to fail for sharp target distributions (0.1% ESS).
