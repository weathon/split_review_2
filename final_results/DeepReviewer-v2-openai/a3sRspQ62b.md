## Summary
This paper presents FourierFlow, a frequency-aware generative framework for turbulence modeling built on conditional flow matching. The authors identify two key limitations of standard generative models for turbulence: spectral bias (underrepresentation of high-frequency components) and common-mode noise (attention mechanisms diluting localized variations). FourierFlow addresses these through three architectural and optimization innovations: (1) a Salient Flow Attention (SFA) mechanism that amplifies local relative variations while suppressing common-mode signals, (2) a Frequency-guided Fourier Mixing (FFM) branch with learnable frequency-dependent weighting and adaptive fusion, and (3) a masked auto-encoder (MAE) representation alignment loss that implicitly biases the generator toward high-frequency features.

The method is evaluated on compressible Navier-Stokes (Mach 0.1 and 1.0) and shear flow benchmarks at 128×128 resolution, with comparisons against 12 baselines spanning surrogate models, diffusion models, and flow matching variants. FourierFlow achieves the lowest MSE, nRMSE, and Max_Err across all three scenarios. Additional generalization experiments on out-of-distribution viscosity parameters and long-horizon rollouts suggest improved robustness compared to the authors' own surrogate baseline.

The paper addresses an important problem and offers a principled combination of techniques. However, several issues affect the validity of the claims: the theoretical analysis is derived for diffusion SDEs while the method uses flow matching (deterministic ODE); experiments lack variance reporting and statistical significance; the attention scope assignment contains an apparent contradiction; and generalization comparisons omit top-performing generative baselines. These limitations should be addressed before the work can be fully assessed.

## Strengths
**1. Well-motivated problem and clear identification of generative model limitations.** The paper makes a convincing case that spectral bias and common-mode noise are real obstacles for applying generative models to turbulence. The two-limit framing (spectral bias + common-mode noise) is intuitive and grounded in both signal processing theory and attention mechanism analysis. This provides a clean motivation for the architectural components.

**2. Thoughtful combination of complementary mechanisms.** The three proposed components (SFA for spatial saliency, FFM for spectral emphasis, MAE alignment for implicit regularization) are conceptually complementary and target different aspects of the identified limitations. The adaptive fusion gate between SFA and FM branches is a sensible design that allows per-location balancing of spatial and frequency information.

**3. Comprehensive baseline coverage.** The paper evaluates against 12 baselines spanning four paradigm families (autoregressive surrogates, multi-step surrogates, next-step generative + rollout, multi-step generative), which provides a broad picture of where FourierFlow stands relative to existing approaches. The inclusion of recent generative models (STDiT, SiT, PDEDiff) and neural operators (FNO, FFNO, OFormer, DPOT) strengthens the empirical grounding.

**4. Generalization experiments beyond in-distribution testing.** The OOD viscosity experiments and long-horizon rollout analysis (even if limited to a surrogate comparison) demonstrate awareness that real-world turbulence modeling requires robustness beyond standard test-set evaluation. The long-rollout stability up to hundreds of steps is a practically relevant result.

**5. Clean writing and well-structured figures.** The paper is generally well-organized, with clear figures (Figure 1 spectral bias visualization, Figure 3 framework overview, Figure 2 architecture comparison) that communicate the key ideas effectively. The dual-branch architecture is communicated with appropriate level of detail in the equations.

## Weaknesses
**W1 [Critical] — Theoretical framework does not match the method (theory-framework mismatch).** Section 4 derives spectral bias for the diffusion SDE ($dx_t = g(t)dw_t$) with SNR analysis based on accumulated Wiener noise variance (Lemmas 1-3). However, FourierFlow uses Conditional Flow Matching (Section 2.3), which is a deterministic ODE framework trained with $x(t) = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1$ and velocity matching loss — no Wiener process, no cumulative noise variance. The SNR argument that high frequencies are corrupted earlier technically applies to diffusion, not to flow matching. The paper does not explain why this spectral bias transfers to the flow matching setting, nor does it provide an equivalent theoretical analysis for ODE-based transport. This creates a fundamental disconnect between the motivation and the method. *(See Annotation 10)*

**W2 [Critical] — No variance reporting or statistical significance.** Table 1 reports only single-point estimates (MSE, nRMSE, Max_Err) without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or number of seeds. Across a 12-model comparison with parameter counts spanning 12.4M--169M, the reader cannot assess whether reported improvements are statistically reliable. For example, FourierFlow's Shear Flow MSE (0.5811) vs. STDiT (0.5908) is a ~1.6% difference that may lie within noise range. The "outperforming the second-best method by approximately 20% on average" claim is not verifiable without variance. *(See Annotation 11)*

**W3 [Major] — Attention scope assignment appears reversed in SFA.** The text states that $\text{Attn}_1$ focuses on "more localized structures" while $\text{Attn}_2$ captures "broader background context." However, Eq. (4) defines $\text{Attn}_1$ with a full softmax over all positions (global), while Eq. (5) constrains $\text{Attn}_2$ to a local neighborhood $\mathcal{N}(i)$ (local). This is the opposite of the claimed assignment. If the scope assignment is indeed reversed, then the differential attention $\text{Attn}_1 - \lambda \text{Attn}_2$ would suppress local features rather than background — contradicting the stated goal of amplifying salient local structures. *(See Annotation 8)*

**W4 [Major] — Common-mode loss defined but never used.** Section 2.2 defines $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cm}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cm}}^{\text{freq}}$ as explicit regularization losses for common-mode noise. Yet the total training objective in Section 3.3 is $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CFM}} + \gamma \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{Align}}$ with no mention of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{cm}}$. If common-mode suppression is achieved purely through the SFA architectural inductive bias (not through loss regularization), this should be stated explicitly. The current presentation leaves readers uncertain about whether a key theoretical component is actually implemented. *(See Annotation 7)*

**W5 [Major] — Generalization comparisons use only a self-comparison surrogate.** The OOD and long-horizon experiments (Section 5.4, Figures 7-8) compare FourierFlow against "the SOTA surrogate baseline" — which is FourierFlow's own architecture trained without generative modeling. This is a self-comparison that demonstrates the advantage of flow matching training over surrogate training for the same architecture, but it does not show whether FourierFlow generalizes better than competing generative models (e.g., STDiT, CFM, Diffusion). The claim "strong generalization capabilities" is therefore not yet supported against independent baselines. *(See Annotation 13)*

**W6 [Major] — Spectral bias claim lacks quantitative spectral metric.** The paper claims "strong evidence" for spectral bias (Section 1, Point 1) but only provides a visual figure (Figure 1) without any quantitative spectral error metric (e.g., high-wavenumber MSE, spectral energy ratio, or log-spectral distance). A visual demonstration on one sample is insufficient to establish a general claim about generative model limitations, and it cannot be used to compare spectral bias severity across methods. *(See Annotation 4)*

**W7 [Major] — Conclusion is too short and lacks critical reflection.** The two-sentence conclusion (Section 6) merely restates the title and claims "superior accuracy, physical consistency, and generalization" without any numerical summary, limitation acknowledgment, or future direction. This leaves the reader without a consolidated takeaway and gives the impression that the authors did not critically examine their own work's boundaries. *(See Annotation 14)*

**W8 [Major] — Baseline comparison not parameter/FLOPs-matched.** The baseline parameter counts range from 12.4M (2D FNO) to 169M (STDiT). The "20% improvement" is averaged across all baselines, but many baselines are much smaller models. Without capacity-matched or throughput-matched comparisons, it is unclear whether gains come from the proposed method or simply from having more capacity in critical components. *(See Annotation 12)*

**W9 [Minor] — Incomplete sentence defining a core concept.** In Section 1, Point (2): "More critically, highly nonlinear and high-frequency variations in localized turbulence regions" is a sentence fragment without a main verb. This occurs at the exact point where "common-mode components" are introduced, undermining clarity of the core definition. *(See Annotation 5)*

**W10 [Minor] — Notation inconsistency in FM branch.** Equation (8) uses $n$ in the exponent $\|\xi\|^n$ while the text refers to $\eta$ (initialized as 1). These should be consistent. It is also unclear whether $\eta$ remains fixed or is learned during training. *(See Annotation 9)*

**W11 [Deferred] — Novelty verification cannot be completed in this run.** Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode (external paper search unavailable), the novelty of SFA, FFM, and MAE alignment for turbulence modeling cannot be verified against the literature. While the combination appears novel, the individual components (differential attention, AFNO-based Fourier mixing, REPA-style representation alignment) are each adapted from existing work. How much of the performance gain is attributable to novel assembly vs. known techniques cannot be determined without external literature comparison. A manual literature verification is recommended before publication decisions.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

The paper addresses a timely and important problem (generative turbulence modeling) and proposes a technically sound architecture with three complementary components. The empirical evaluation covers a broad set of baselines and includes useful OOD and long-rollout analyses.

However, two critical weaknesses prevent a higher score. **(1)** The theoretical framework (Section 4) is derived for diffusion SDEs while the method uses flow matching (deterministic ODE), creating a fundamental disconnect between the claimed motivation and the actual mechanism. Without an equivalent analysis for the flow matching setting, the spectral bias premise is not formally grounded for FourierFlow. **(2)** The complete absence of variance or significance reporting across all experiments means the claimed improvements — some as small as ~1.6% — cannot be assessed for statistical reliability.

Additional major issues (reversed attention scope assignment, unused common-mode loss, generalization comparisons against only a self-baseline, lack of quantitative spectral metrics) further reduce confidence in the conclusions as currently presented. These issues are individually fixable through clarifications and additional experiments, but their combined effect on the paper's validity is substantial.

**Final Score: 5/10**

*Rating rationale: The paper demonstrates a well-motivated architectural effort but has fundamental validity gaps in theoretical grounding and statistical rigor that prevent the claims from being fully supported. Revision addressing the theory-framework mismatch (or explicitly acknowledging it), adding variance reporting, correcting the attention scope description, and expanding generalization comparisons to include top generative baselines would significantly strengthen the paper.*
