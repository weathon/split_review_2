## Summary
This paper introduces OptMerge, a data-free model merging method for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), and presents a new benchmark for evaluating model merging across five MLLM capabilities (VQA, Geometry, Chart, OCR, Grounding) as well as across modalities (vision, audio, video). The core methodological contribution is an optimization-based merging approach that builds on WUDI Merging (Cheng et al., 2025) with three key extensions: low-rank SVD denoising of task vectors, SGD-based optimization for LoRA-tuned models, and mean initialization of the merged vector. The benchmark includes expert models fine-tuned on InternVL2.5 (full fine-tuning) and Qwen2-VL (LoRA), with comprehensive evaluation using 10 merging algorithms. The paper reports that OptMerge achieves competitive or superior performance compared to existing merging methods and, in some settings, approaches or exceeds mixture training performance.

**Primary contributions:**
- A structured benchmark for MLLM merging with five capability categories, two base architectures, and released expert checkpoints.
- OptMerge, an optimization-based merging method with low-rank denoising and tailored strategies for full vs. LoRA fine-tuning.
- Extensive experiments showing that data-free merging can be competitive with multi-task training, and that modality merging can combine vision, audio, and video capabilities into a unified model.

**Key strengths:** comprehensive experimental comparison across 10 methods, exploration of both capability and modality merging, theoretical analysis of merging-fine-tuning interactions (Theorem 3.1), and publicly released models/code.

**Key weaknesses:** overclaimed novelty regarding the "first" benchmark and "first" theoretical explanation, incomplete statistical evidence for central claims about surpassing mixture training, notation ambiguities in the core optimization equations (Eq. 2-3), validation-set dependency that partially contradicts the "data-free" characterization, and the absence of a limitations section.

## Strengths
**1. Comprehensive benchmark construction.** The paper constructs a well-structured benchmark covering five distinct MLLM capabilities (VQA, Geometry, Chart, OCR, Grounding) with at least 100k training samples per task, spanning both full fine-tuning (InternVL2.5) and LoRA fine-tuning (Qwen2-VL) scenarios. The release of expert model weights and evaluation code is a valuable contribution that will benefit the model merging community.

**2. Broad empirical comparison.** The paper evaluates 10 model merging methods across capability merging, modality merging, real HuggingFace checkpoints, and general QA benchmarks. This is one of the most comprehensive empirical studies of MLLM merging to date, providing useful insights about which methods perform well under which conditions (e.g., TSV for modality merging, OptMerge for capability merging).

**3. Theoretical analysis of fine-tuning and merging interaction.** Theorem 3.1 provides a formal upper bound connecting fine-tuning hyperparameters (learning rate, iterations) with merging error, decomposing the error into convergence residual, cross-task interference, and curvature terms. While the proof is deferred to the appendix, the result offers a useful conceptual framework for understanding why moderate fine-tuning yields better merging outcomes.

**4. Practical computational efficiency.** Table 7 demonstrates that OptMerge reduces GPU memory from 240GB (mixture training) to 2.62GB for InternVL2.5-1B and from 256GB to 21.97GB for Qwen2-VL-7B, with corresponding speedups. This practical advantage is significant for the community.

**5. General task integration.** Table 10 shows that merging specialized models via OptMerge substantially outperforms individual expert models on integrated benchmarks (e.g., 91.89 vs 76.54 on ScienceQA), demonstrating emergent capability integration from merging — a practically important finding.

**6. Ablation analysis with clear component contributions.** Table 4 provides a clean ablation showing the individual and combined contributions of SGD, mean initialization, and low-rank approximation, revealing that the components are tightly coupled and must be used together.

## Weaknesses
**1. Overclaimed novelty and insufficiently qualified "first" statements (Major).** The paper contains several absolute novelty claims that need qualification. The abstract states "no benchmark exists for model merging research that clearly divides the tasks of MLLM training and evaluation," yet the paper itself cites AdaMMS (Du et al., 2025b) and UQ-Merge (Qu et al., 2025) which propose MLLM merging evaluation protocols. The Remark after Theorem 3.1 claims it is "the first theoretical explanation" of the fine-tuning-merging relationship — a strong claim that requires literature verification not provided in the paper. The contribution list claims "the first model merging benchmark" without qualifying against prior MLLM merging evaluation work. These overclaims reduce credibility and invite reviewer pushback. **Fix:** Replace absolute "first" and "no benchmark" statements with precisely scoped claims, acknowledging prior evaluation frameworks and specifying the novel dimensions of this benchmark.

**2. Statistical evidence insufficient for central empirical claims (Major).** The paper repeatedly states that model merging "potentially surpasses mixture training," but the evidence is mixed and lacks statistical rigor. On InternVL2.5 (Table 2), OptMerge (57.44) is actually below mixture training (57.66). On Qwen2-VL, the comparison is against Qwen2-VL-Instruct, which was trained with different data and objectives — not a matched mixture training baseline. No variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests are reported anywhere in the main tables. Without multi-seed runs and proper statistical testing, the claimed superiority of merging over multi-task training is not established. **Fix:** (a) Report mean ± std over at least 3 seeds, (b) add a matched mixture training baseline for Qwen2-VL, (c) replace "surpasses" with more cautious language like "closely matches or, in some settings, approaches the performance of mixture training."

**3. Notation ambiguity and dimensional inconsistency in core equations (Major).** The core methodological contribution, Eq. (3), suffers from unclear dimensions. The text states that task vector $\tau_{i,l}$ is an $m \times n$ matrix (line 77), and Eq. (2) decomposes $(\tau_{i,l} - \bar{\tau}_l)$ via SVD into $U \Sigma V^\top$ with $U \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times r}$, $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times r}$, $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times r}$. However, Eq. (3) uses $( \Sigma_{1:k} V_{1:k}^\top )^\top$ as a right-multiplying factor, and it is unclear how the resulting dimensions align. The variable $\mathbf{x}_{i,l}$ (the fine-tuning data) is referenced but never formally defined in the main text. **Fix:** Provide explicit tensor shapes for each term, add a dimension table for Eq. (3), and formally define $\mathbf{x}_{i,l}$ before using it to motivate the loss.

**4. "Data-free" claim contradicted by validation-set-dependent hyperparameter selection (Major).** The paper consistently describes OptMerge as "data-free" and operating "without requiring data" (abstract, introduction, conclusion). However, Section 5.1 states that the scaling coefficient $\lambda$ is determined by "searching within the range [0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0, 1.5]" — this requires a validation set and forward passes through the merged model. While the core optimization (Eq. 3) is data-free, the overall pipeline is not. **Fix:** Clearly separate the data-free optimization step from the validation-dependent $\lambda$ selection, acknowledge the need for a small validation set, and report sensitivity to $\lambda$ to show robustness.

**5. Missing limitations and incomplete conclusion (Major).** The paper has no limitations section. The conclusion does not discuss when OptMerge might fail, what assumptions it relies on, or what the open challenges are. Notably: Theorem 3.1 assumes convex losses (PL condition, L-smoothness) which do not hold for neural networks; the benchmark only covers models sharing the same base architecture; modality merging results (Table 5) show TSV outperforming OptMerge, which is not acknowledged. **Fix:** Add a dedicated limitations subsection discussing convexity assumptions, architecture constraints, and the modality merging gap.

**6. Modality merging results do not support OptMerge superiority claim (Minor).** Table 5 shows TSV Merging achieving 67.34% average vs. OptMerge's 67.00%. The text refers generically to "the best merging method" outperforming online composition, but the narrative framing around OptMerge as the best method across all settings is inconsistent with this result. **Fix:** Explicitly acknowledge that TSV Merging performs best for modality merging and provide a hypothesis about why orthogonalization helps more than denoising when merging across modalities.

**7. Missing mixture training baseline in general tasks table (Minor).** Table 10 shows OptMerge outperforming individual experts on integrated benchmarks, but the mixture training baseline from Table 2 is not included. Without this comparison, the claim that merging "surpasses mixture training" remains unverified for general tasks. **Fix:** Add mixture training results to Table 10 or acknowledge their absence as a limitation.

**8. "First" theoretical claim needs verification (Verification).** The claim that Theorem 3.1 provides "the first theoretical explanation" of fine-tuning's effect on merging should be verified against the model merging theory literature (e.g., linear mode connectivity, Task Arithmetic analysis in Ilharco et al., 2023, Wortsman et al., 2022). Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode in this review, external literature verification was not performed; the authors should self-verify this claim. **Fix:** Either provide a brief literature comparison showing no prior bound exists, or qualify the claim (e.g., "to our knowledge, the first explicit bound connecting learning rate and iterations to merging error").

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:**
The paper presents a practically useful benchmark and a sensible merging method (OptMerge) with comprehensive empirical evaluation. The benchmark construction, release of expert model weights, and extensive comparison across 10 merging methods represent a genuine contribution to the MLLM merging community. However, the paper is weakened by several issues that affect its scientific reliability and presentation:

- **Research value (moderate-high):** The benchmark and systematic comparison are valuable resources. The finding that merging can approach multi-task training performance is practically important.
- **Novelty (moderate):** The OptMerge method combines existing techniques (SVD denoising, SGD regularization, mean initialization) in a novel pipeline, but each component has been studied in prior merging work. The "first" claims for the benchmark and theory are overstated.
- **Validity (moderate):** Central claims about surpassing mixture training are not statistically supported — the evidence is mixed and lacks variance reporting. The "data-free" characterization is contradicted by validation-dependent lambda selection.
- **Reproducibility (moderate):** The notation ambiguities in Eq. (3) and incomplete dimension specifications reduce reproducibility. However, the release of code and checkpoints partially mitigates this.
- **Presentation (fair):** The paper is clearly written but overclaims in several places, the related work lacks critical analysis, and there is no limitations section.

The overall score of 6/10 reflects that the paper has solid empirical contributions and practical value, but the scientific presentation needs strengthening in claim accuracy, statistical evidence, and self-critical analysis before it reaches the rigor expected for top-tier venues.
