## Summary
This paper presents an information-theoretic framework for optimizing experimental designs to distinguish between two competing hypotheses about probabilistic neural coding in early sensory areas: the likelihood coding hypothesis (exemplified by probabilistic population codes) and the posterior coding hypothesis (exemplified by neural sampling codes). The authors derive analytic expressions for the "information gap" (Δ^info)—the expected decoder performance difference when extracting mismatched probabilistic content—measured as the KL divergence between the true posterior and a task-marginalized surrogate posterior. Through extensive simulations with Poisson and gain-modulated Poisson neural populations, they show that the information gap accurately predicts empirical decoder performance differences across diverse task settings, contrast levels, and neural models. The framework is then used to map information gap landscapes over the two-dimensional task parameter space (prior separation d and prior width σ), identifying strategic "sweet spots" that maximize discriminative power between the two hypotheses. An empirical validation on the Allen Brain Observatory Visual Coding dataset confirms that single-context designs yield zero information gap, motivating the need for multi-context optimized designs. The work bridges computational theory and experimental neuroscience by providing a principled, optimizable metric for designing experiments that can resolve a fundamental debate about Bayesian computation in the brain.

## Strengths
1. **Principled and mathematically grounded framework.** The information gap Δ^info provides a closed-form, interpretable objective for experimental design, derived from first principles of Bayesian decision theory and information theory. The derivation of optimal (Bayes-optimal) decoders for mismatched probabilistic content—Eq. (2) for decoding posteriors from likelihood-coding populations and Eq. (5) for decoding likelihoods from posterior-coding populations—is technically elegant and captures the core asymmetry between the two coding hypotheses.

2. **Clear operationalization of a longstanding question.** The paper translates the abstract debate about likelihood vs. posterior coding into a concrete, falsifiable experimental prediction: under the optimal task design, likelihood and posterior decoders will show systematically different performance, with the direction of the difference identifying the underlying neural code. This operationalization is valuable progress for the field.

3. **Comprehensive simulation validation.** The theoretical predictions are validated across multiple dimensions: two neural population models (Poisson, gain-modulated Poisson), three contrast levels (high, medium, low), two coding hypotheses, and multiple task parameter sets. The convergence analyses (Fig. 3) and scatter validations (Fig. 4) provide complementary evidence that the framework's predictions are reliable.

4. **Practical task optimization with actionable output.** The information gap landscapes (Fig. 5-6) directly inform experimental design by mapping discriminative power across the parameter space. The identification of strategic "sweet spots" (e.g., d ≈ 30°, σ ≈ 20° for low contrast) provides concrete, actionable recommendations for experimental neuroscientists planning multi-context studies.

5. **Honest treatment of limitations and extensions.** The authors acknowledge the need for generative models, sufficient training data, and the asymmetry in discriminability between the two hypotheses. They also discuss extensions to mixed coding hypotheses and imperfect priors, showing awareness of the framework's boundary conditions.

## Weaknesses
1. **Missing quantitative validation metrics (Major).** The simulation validation (Section 3, Fig. 4) relies on visual scatter-plot agreement ("remarkable agreement") without reporting standard goodness-of-fit statistics (R², slope, mean absolute error) across the 12 subplot conditions. This omission makes it impossible for readers to assess whether agreement degrades in specific parameter regimes—for example, where the fixed-point iteration for Eq. (5) might converge slowly or where discretization effects are strongest. *Action: Report R² and slope (with confidence intervals) for each subplot in Fig. 4; add residual analysis across the (d, σ) parameter space.*

2. **Unspecified criterion for "strategic sweet spots" (Major).** The paper identifies optimal task parameters (asterisks in Fig. 5) using the notion of "sufficient discriminative signal" without operationalizing this threshold (Page 7, Section 4.1). Experimentalists cannot reproduce the selection without knowing whether it requires Δ^info_lik > 0.05 nats, >80% of maximum, or some statistical power criterion. *Action: Define the precise optimization objective used to select the asterisk positions; report the implied trade-off quantitatively.*

3. **Lack of positive control in empirical validation (Major).** The Allen Brain Observatory analysis (Section 5) confirms a null result (Δ^info ≈ 0) consistent with the theory, but does not include a positive control condition where Δ^info is known to be non-zero. Without this, the null finding could alternatively arise from insufficient decoder sensitivity, suboptimal decoder architecture, or limited neural coverage—rather than from the absence of discriminable information. *Action: Add a positive control (e.g., decoding under two contrast levels where Δ^info should differ) or explicitly caveat the null result's interpretability as suggested in the annotation on Page 9.*

4. **Limited discussion of discretization sensitivity (Major).** The derivation (Section 2) assumes discretized observations $x \in \{x_i\}$, but real orientation stimuli are continuous. The paper mentions extension to continuous observations via "numerical methods" in the Discussion but provides no guidance on discretization granularity, bin boundary placement, or the bias-variance tradeoff of different discretization schemes. This gap reduces the framework's immediate applicability for experimentalists. *Action: Add a brief analysis or reference showing how discretization resolution affects Δ^info estimates; recommend bin width ≤ tuning curve width/4.*

5. **Under-specified limitations section (Minor).** The limitations section (Page 9) is only three sentences and omits several practical concerns that affect real-world adoption: (a) required trial counts for stable decoder training are quantified in Fig. 3 but not distilled into a rule of thumb; (b) sensitivity of optimal designs to misspecification of the generative model parameters is not discussed; (c) convergence properties and uniqueness guarantees for the fixed-point iteration in Eq. (5) are not addressed. *Action: Expand limitations to include data requirements, model sensitivity analysis, and fixed-point convergence guarantees as detailed in the annotation on Page 9.*

6. **Abstract lacks quantitative anchoring (Minor).** The abstract (Page 1) describes simulation validation qualitatively without reporting any specific performance metric (e.g., correlation coefficient, prediction error). Adding one bounded quantitative result would substantially improve reader confidence and citation impact. *Action: Add a sentence such as: "Across simulated populations, the information gap predicts decoder performance differences with near-unity slope (R² > 0.95)."*

7. **Null-case diagnostic logic not explicitly stated in Section 2 (Minor).** The decoding framework (Page 2) describes how asymmetric decoder performance reveals the coding hypothesis, but does not explicitly discuss the null case (Δ^info ≈ 0), which is critical for understanding when experimental designs lack discriminative power. This null case is later demonstrated empirically (Section 5), but flagging it earlier would strengthen the logical foundation. *Action: Add a sentence in Section 2 noting that Δ^info ≈ 0 corresponds to uninformative designs.*

8. **Fixed-point equation (Eq. 5) lacks convergence analysis (Verification).** The implicit equation for $\ell_{jk}^*(\theta)$ is solved "up to a multiplicative constant" using fixed-point iteration, but the paper does not discuss uniqueness of the fixed point, convergence guarantees, or stopping criteria. This is technically important because the information gap for posterior-coding populations depends on this solution. *Action: Add convergence analysis in Appendix A.1; report iteration count and tolerance.*

9. **Novelty verification deferred (Deferred — not a paper weakness).** External literature search was unavailable during this review. Therefore, claims about the framework's novelty relative to prior work on optimal experimental design in neuroscience (e.g., Watson & Pelli 1983; Lewi et al. 2006; Machens et al. 2005) cannot be independently verified. This assessment is deferred for manual literature review. The paper's self-citation of these prior works is appropriate but their relationship to the current contribution should be examined by readers with full literature access.

## Score
**Final Score: 7/10**

The paper presents a mathematically rigorous and well-validated framework for a genuinely important question in computational neuroscience (distinguishing likelihood vs. posterior neural codes). The theoretical derivation of the information gap is elegant, the simulation validation is thorough across multiple neural models and contrast conditions, and the task optimization landscapes provide actionable experimental guidance. These constitute a substantive methodological contribution.

The score is primarily limited by: (1) missing quantitative fit metrics for the simulation validation, making it difficult to assess whether theory-experiment agreement degrades in specific regimes; (2) undefined optimization criteria for the strategic "sweet spots," reducing reproducibility of the headline recommendations; (3) no positive control in the Allen dataset analysis, weakening the sole empirical validation; (4) insufficient discussion of discretization sensitivity and practical implementation details that affect real-world deployment; and (5) deferred novelty verification due to literature search unavailability in this review run.

All identified weaknesses are fixable with additional quantitative reporting, expanded discussion, and positive control experiments. The core theoretical contribution is sound and the framework has clear potential to guide future experimental work on probabilistic neural representations.
