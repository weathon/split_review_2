## Summary

This paper presents **FASTer**, a unified framework for efficient autoregressive Vision-Language-Action (VLA) modeling, consisting of two components: FASTerVQ (a neural action tokenizer) and FASTerVLA (an autoregressive policy built upon the tokenizer). FASTerVQ introduces a non-uniform action patchifier that groups action dimensions by physical semantics, followed by a transformer-based residual vector quantization (RVQ) encoder-decoder trained with time-domain and frequency-domain L1 losses. FASTerVLA employs block-wise autoregressive decoding (BAR) and a lightweight action expert to achieve faster inference while maintaining task performance.

The experiments span 9 benchmarks across 5 robot embodiments (simulated and real), comparing against both diffusion-based (π0, Diffusion Policy) and autoregressive (π0-FAST, OpenVLA, VQ-VLA) baselines. The key reported results are: (1) FASTerVLA achieves 97.9% success rate on LIBERO, surpassing prior SOTA; (2) FASTerVQ achieves higher Valid Reconstruction Rate (VRR) under multiple error tolerances compared to FAST and alternatives; (3) FASTerVLA reduces inference latency to 112ms on single-arm tasks (vs. π0's 176ms and π0-FAST's 197-556ms). The framework shows cross-backbone compatibility across PaliGemma, Qwen2.5, and InternVL3.5.

**Overall assessment:** The paper addresses a well-motivated problem (action tokenization efficiency for autoregressive VLAs) and proposes a technically sound solution. However, several claims around novelty ("first systematic analysis"), causal attribution (tokenizer quality → downstream performance), and scope (generalization to "multimodal generation") are overstated or insufficiently evidenced. The experimental evaluation is broad but has fairness-of-comparison concerns and missing statistical rigor. With revisions to bound claims, improve baseline transparency, and add missing details, the paper could make a solid contribution.

## Strengths
**1. Well-motivated problem and clear framing of requirements.** The paper identifies a genuine bottleneck in autoregressive VLA models: the trade-off between action token count (inference latency) and reconstruction fidelity. The four requirements (compression, reconstruction quality, 2D structural modeling, flexibility) provide a clear design rationale that guides the method development.

**2. Technically sound and practical method design.** FASTerVQ's action patchifier — grouping action dimensions by physical semantics before applying RVQ — is a practical and effective design choice. The two-dimensional partitioning (uniform temporal, non-uniform action-dimension grouping) addresses both temporal redundancy and the heterogeneous distribution across different action channels (e.g., gripper state vs. end-effector position). The hybrid time-frequency reconstruction loss (L1 + DCT-L1) is well-motivated for robot action data that contains both high-frequency noise and low-frequency trends.

**3. Block-wise Autoregressive (BAR) decoding is a principled approach to speed up inference.** The observation that action codes across different dimensions are only weakly coupled provides justification for parallel token prediction within blocks. The coarse-to-fine decoding order (codebook-wise before temporal) aligns naturally with the RVQ structure. This approach meaningfully reduces the number of autoregressive steps (from N to N/B) while maintaining consistency.

**4. Broad experimental evaluation across multiple embodiments and benchmarks.** The paper evaluates on 9 benchmarks spanning 5 robot embodiments, including both simulated environments (LIBERO, VLABench, GalaxeaManisim) and real-world platforms (xArm, R1Lite bimanual and whole-body). This breadth strengthens the claims about cross-embodiment generalization and practical applicability.

**5. Cross-backbone compatibility is demonstrated.** Showing that FASTerVLA works across PaliGemma, Qwen2.5, and InternVL3.5 backbones — and notably improving InternVL3.5-2B by 17.3% — demonstrates the tokenizer's flexibility and practical value for the community.

**6. Valid Reconstruction Rate (VRR) is a useful evaluation metric.** The introduction of VRR to capture functional fidelity (whether reconstruction errors are within task-relevant tolerances) is a practical contribution over raw L1/L2 reconstruction loss, which can be dominated by sensor noise.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Baseline fairness and comparison transparency (Severity: Major)**
The paper reports strong results against numerous baselines, but it is not clear which comparisons are fair (same initialization, data, and compute budget) and which are taken from published numbers. Section 4.1 states that baselines and FASTer are "initialized from checkpoints pretrained on large-scale robotics data (e.g., from pi0-FAST)," but Table 1 includes baselines like Diffusion Policy, Octo-Base, and OpenVLA that likely use their original published results. Methods compared in Figure 4 (P0, Fast, FASTer) appear to share the same backbone, which is a fairer comparison — but this distinction is not clearly marked in Table 1. Without transparent reporting of which results were reproduced under matched conditions vs. cited from literature, the claimed improvements may partially reflect differences in training protocols rather than methodological advantage. **Required action:** Add explicit markers in the results tables (e.g., † for re-implemented baselines) and clarify the comparison protocol for each baseline in a dedicated "Fairness of Comparison" subsection.

**W2. Overclaimed novelty and insufficiently bounded contribution statements (Severity: Major)**
Several novelty and SOTA claims are not appropriately scoped. (a) Contribution C3 claims "the first systematic analysis of action tokenization for VLAs" — this is a strong "first" claim that is contestable given existing work (VQ-VLA, FAST, prior action tokenization analyses). Without external literature verification (unavailable in this run), this claim must be downgraded. (b) The conclusion states "autoregressive modeling can be fast, transferable, and scalable for multimodal generation" — the paper evaluates only robot action generation, not general multimodal generation, making this an overclaim. (c) The abstract claims "surpassing previous state-of-the-art VLA models in both inference speed and task performance" without specifying the comparison scope. **Required action:** Replace "first" with "to our knowledge, a systematic analysis"; remove the multimodal generation overclaim; and bound SOTA claims to evaluated settings.

**W3. VRR metric aggregates physically heterogeneous quantities (Severity: Major)**
The VRR metric (Eq. 4) uses a single L1 threshold across the entire action vector, but the action vector mixes translation (meters), rotation (radians), and gripper state (binary). The text acknowledges different physical interpretations for sigma across component types but the formula applies a single L1 norm across all dimensions combined. A translation error of 0.01m and a rotation error of 0.01 rad are not physically comparable, yet they contribute equally to the L1 sum. This aggregation may obscure cases where translation is accurate but rotation is poor. **Required action:** Define per-component VRR with separate thresholds for translation, rotation, and gripper, or clarify that the action vector is normalized to a common scale before computing L1. Report both per-component and aggregate VRR.

**W4. Missing implementation details affecting reproducibility (Severity: Major)**
Several key design choices are underspecified: (a) The action patchifier's padding strategy (zero-padding? learned?) and the mapping from patchifier parameters (m, h, n, d) to encoder output dimensions (C_h, C_a) are not provided. (b) The lightweight action expert's architecture (number of layers, hidden dimension, parameter count) and its integration with the backbone (cross-attention? shared states?) are described only qualitatively. (c) The BAR mechanism's block formation strategy in the 3D code tensor and position encoding for replicated ⟨BoBlk⟩ tokens are not specified. **Required action:** Provide an appendix table with patchifier parameters per embodiment, clarify the action expert architecture with exact dimensions, and detail the BAR block formation and position encoding strategy.

**W5. Causal link between tokenizer quality and downstream performance is asserted without direct evidence (Severity: Major)**
Section 4.2 states that baseline tokenizers' reconstruction errors cause the "LLM backbone [to learn] inherently erroneous action codes, which constrain overall performance." While this is a plausible claim, it is presented as an established causal relationship without a controlled experiment. The correlation between tokenizer VRR and downstream policy success rate is suggestive but not sufficient for causal attribution — confounders such as token length, codebook size, and training stability could independently affect both. **Required action:** Add an ablation where the same downstream VLA is trained with tokenizers of varying VRR while controlling for token count and codebook size. Alternatively, rephrase to an evidence-consistent correlation claim.

**W6. Conclusion lacks limitations section and overgeneralizes (Severity: Moderate)**
The conclusion (Section 5) is only a single short paragraph that restates contributions qualitatively and adds an unsupported claim about "multimodal generation." There is no discussion of limitations, failure cases, or scope boundaries. This omission is notable for a systems paper with broad empirical claims. **Required action:** Add a dedicated limitations paragraph covering: (a) the VRR metric's aggregation issue, (b) the limited number of embodiments evaluated (5), (c) the reliance on a single pretrained checkpoint for most comparisons, and (d) potential failure modes (e.g., high-frequency tasks, precision-critical manipulation).

**W7. Inconsistent reference to Figure 1 as "preliminary experimental results" (Severity: Moderate)**
The introduction (Page 1) states "our preliminary experimental results, as illustrated in Figure 1, indicate that existing tokenization methods fail to comprehensively satisfy these principles." Figure 1 is a high-level framework diagram, not an experimental result figure. This misleads readers into thinking Figure 1 contains quantitative evidence that it does not. **Required action:** Replace the reference to Figure 1 with a reference to Section 4.2 where actual experimental comparisons are presented.

**W8. Efficiency comparison limited to a single GPU configuration (Severity: Minor)**
Inference latency is measured only on an RTX 5090 using PyTorch. Results may not generalize to other hardware (e.g., edge devices, older GPUs, or TensorRT-optimized deployments). The paper does not report peak memory usage, which is important for deployment feasibility. **Required action:** Add peak GPU memory and latency on at least one additional hardware configuration (e.g., RTX 3090 or edge GPU).

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Scoring rationale:** The score prioritizes research value, novelty, and validity as primary dimensions. The paper demonstrates solid technical engineering and broad empirical evaluation, which are strengths. However, several major weaknesses reduce confidence below the threshold for a strong accept:

- **Novelty (weight: high):** The core ideas (patchified RVQ for action, block-wise decoding) are incremental improvements over existing tokenization methods (FAST, VQ-VLA, chunk policies). The claimed "first systematic analysis" is contestable. Novelty contribution is moderate — solid engineering but lacking a conceptual breakthrough. Score contribution: 5/10.
- **Research value / significance (weight: high):** Action tokenization efficiency is practically important for deploying autoregressive VLAs. The broad evaluation across 5 embodiments demonstrates practical value. However, the lack of transparent baseline fairness and the missing causal evidence for tokenizer→performance link weaken the significance claims. Score contribution: 6/10.
- **Validity / soundness (weight: high):** Several validity concerns exist: the VRR metric's aggregation across heterogeneous units, the causal overclaim about tokenizer errors propagating to policy performance, and the unclear baseline fairness. The method description is reproducible only with substantial guessing. These concerns are fixable but currently present. Score contribution: 5/10.
- **Empirical quality (weight: medium):** The evaluation breadth is commendable, but the lack of statistical significance reporting (confidence intervals, multi-seed variance for most results), the single-GPU latency measurement, and the missing controlled ablation linking tokenizer quality to policy performance are notable gaps. Score contribution: 6/10.

**Overall:** The paper is a solid engineering contribution with broad experiments, but the overstated claims, reproducibility gaps, and several methodological weaknesses prevent a higher score. The core technical ideas are reasonable and with major revisions addressing the fairness, claim-bounding, and reproducibility issues, the paper could reach 7-8/10.
