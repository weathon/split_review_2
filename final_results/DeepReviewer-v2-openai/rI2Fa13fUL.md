## Summary
This paper proposes Generative Trajectory Policies (GTP), a new policy class for offline reinforcement learning. The core idea is to unify diffusion models, flow matching, consistency models, and related approaches under a continuous-time ODE framework, and then learn the full ODE solution map as a policy. The authors identify three practical challenges — computational cost, training instability, and objective misalignment — and address them via: (1) a closed-form score approximation that replaces expensive ODE solver rollouts with single-step perturbations, and (2) an advantage-weighted generative objective for policy improvement. Empirical results on D4RL benchmarks show that GTP achieves strong performance, particularly on AntMaze tasks, with few inference steps. The paper is well-structured and provides both theoretical motivation and empirical validation. However, several concerns about novelty relative to prior work (especially Consistency Trajectory Models), the gap between theoretical claims and practical implementation, and overclaiming in the abstract and conclusion temper the overall contribution.

## Strengths
1. **Unified theoretical framing.** The paper presents a clean, unified ODE perspective that connects diffusion models, flow matching, consistency models, CTMs, shortcut models, and mean flows through the lens of the flow map $\Phi(x_t, t, s)$. This synthesis is pedagogically valuable and provides a structured design space for generative policy design.

2. **Practical algorithmic contributions.** The two key adaptations — the closed-form score approximation (Theorem 1) and advantage-weighted generative objective (Theorem 2) — address genuine challenges when deploying trajectory-based generative models in offline RL. The score approximation in particular provides a computationally efficient alternative to on-the-fly ODE solving, and the ablation study shows it yields practical benefits in training time and stability.

3. **Strong empirical results on AntMaze.** GTP achieves notable improvements on the challenging AntMaze tasks, both in the BC setting (66.3 average vs. 44.1 for C-BC) and the full RL setting (80.6 average vs. 69.6 for D-QL and 78.3 for QGPO). The perfect score on antmaze-umaze is a strong result.

4. **Inference efficiency.** With K=5 sampling steps, GTP matches the inference speed of consistency-based methods while outperforming them in policy quality, demonstrating progress toward resolving the expressiveness-efficiency trade-off.

5. **Clear exposition.** The paper is well-organized, with careful definitions of the flow map, parameterization, and loss functions. Algorithm 1 provides a concise pseudocode summary.

## Weaknesses
1. **Novelty overlap with CTMs is understated.** The core GTP architecture — parameterizing the full flow map $\Phi(x_t, t, s)$ and training it with both an instantaneous flow loss and a trajectory consistency loss — is directly inherited from Consistency Trajectory Models (CTMs, Kim et al., 2024). Section 3.4 acknowledges this but frames it as CTMs being "a special case" of the unified framework, whereas from a contribution perspective, GTP's core dual-loss design is CTM's design adapted for RL. The paper's actual novel contributions are the score approximation (which replaces ODE solver rollouts with closed-form perturbations) and the advantage-weighted objective. These are meaningful adaptations, but the paper should more clearly delineate what is inherited versus what is new. This concern is documented in annotations on Page 1 (Sections 3.4 and Contribution paragraph).

2. **Discrepancy between theoretical consistency loss and practical implementation.** The theoretical Trajectory Consistency Loss (Eq. 6) enforces multi-step composition: $\Phi(x_t, t, s) \approx \Phi(\Phi(x_t, t, u), u, s)$. The practical implementation (Eq. 17) does not implement this composition. Instead, it uses a direct perturbation target $\tilde{a}_u = a + u \cdot z$ obtained from the original sample $a$, bypassing the student network's intermediate prediction. This reduces to a noise-conditional agreement loss that resembles consistency training rather than true trajectory consistency. While the score approximation (Theorem 1) provides asymptotic justification, the naming and framing as "Trajectory Consistency Loss" is misleading. This is detailed in the annotation on Page 1 (Section 4.3, Actor Training).

3. **Theorem 1's theoretical guarantee has hidden variance issues.** The score approximation replaces the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[x|x_t]$ with the sample $x$ itself. Theorem 1 bounds the bias as $O(h^p)$ under Lipschitz and zero-stability assumptions, but does not address the variance introduced by the surrogate — the solver states conditioned on $x_t$ will have additional variance proportional to $\text{Var}[x|x_t]$. For large $t$ (high noise), this conditional variance is large, and the propagated states may be noisier than the bound suggests. The theorem's asymptotic equivalence ($h \to 0$) is correct, but for finite step sizes used in practice, the practical behavior depends on the variance term, which is not discussed. This is documented in the annotation on Page 1 (Section 4.1, Theorem 1).

4. **Overclaiming in abstract and conclusion.** The abstract claims "perfect scores on several notoriously hard AntMaze tasks," but only antmaze-umaze achieves a perfect 100.0 in the full RL setting (Table 2). The other AntMaze tasks (antmaze-ud: 81.9, antmaze-mp: 83.3, antmaze-md: 94.2, antmaze-lp: 53.5, antmaze-ld: 71.0) are not perfect scores. Similarly, the conclusion states GTP "outs prior generative and offline RL methods" without qualifying that on several individual Gym tasks (e.g., halfcheetah-medium: 53.9 vs C-AC 69.1; halfcheetah-medium-replay: 50.8 vs C-AC 58.7), GTP underperforms specific baselines. These overstatements undermine the paper's objectivity and may trigger reviewer scrutiny. Documented in annotation on Page 1 (Abstract).

5. **Theoretical justification vs. practical implementation gap in value guidance.** Theorem 2 derives the optimal policy as $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{BC}} \exp(\eta A)$. The practical weights in Eq. (14) truncate negative advantages to zero: $w = \exp(\eta \cdot \max(0, A)/\text{std}(A))$. This effectively changes the target distribution from the theoretically derived $\pi^*$ to a rectified version that only up-weights positive-advantage actions while completely ignoring negative-advantage actions (rather than down-weighting them proportionally). While the truncation is motivated by stability, the paper should discuss this gap and its implications for the policy improvement guarantee. This is documented in the annotation on Page 1 (Section 4.2).

6. **Single-task ablation study.** The ablation experiments (Table 3) are conducted only on hopper-medium-expert. The score approximation and variational guidance may behave differently on tasks with different reward structures (e.g., sparse-reward AntMaze) or action dimensionalities. Without multi-task ablation evidence, the generality of the findings is limited. This is documented in the annotation on Page 1 (Section 5.3).

7. **Limited statistical rigor in evaluation.** Gym tasks are evaluated over only 10 episodes per seed, which is a modest sample size, especially for stochastic environments. Within-seed variance is not reported alongside the across-seed variance. The paper would benefit from either longer evaluation rolls, explicit confidence intervals, or a justification that 10 episodes is sufficient for the environments tested. This is documented in the annotation on Page 1 (Section 5, Experiment Setup).

8. **Missing computational comparison.** The paper claims inference efficiency (K=5 sampling steps) but does not provide wall-clock inference time comparisons against baselines (Diffusion-QL at K=5 vs. Consistency-AC at K=2 vs. GTP at K=5). Training time is reported only for one ablation setting. A practical reader would benefit from concrete speed comparisons.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper presents a practically motivated and empirically strong approach for generative policies in offline RL, with a clean unified theoretical framing. The two key adaptations — score approximation and advantage-weighted training — are well-reasoned and yield competitive results, particularly on AntMaze tasks. However, the score is tempered by the following factors:

- **Novelty concerns (primary):** The core dual-loss architecture for learning the full flow map $\Phi(x_t, t, s)$ is directly inherited from CTMs (Kim et al., 2024). The paper's claim of a "new policy paradigm" overstates the novelty relative to this prior work. The genuine contributions — the score approximation and the advantage-weighted objective — are practically valuable but represent adaptations of existing techniques rather than fundamentally new principles. This concern applies at the research-value level.

- **Theory-practice gap:** The trajectory consistency loss as implemented (Eq. 17) does not match the theoretical formulation (Eq. 6), and Theorem 1's guarantee does not fully address the variance introduced by the sample-based surrogate. These gaps weaken the theoretical foundation that the paper emphasizes as a key strength.

- **Empirical scope limitations:** Ablation studies are single-task, evaluation episodes are limited, and the claimed "state-of-the-art" is not uniformly supported across individual tasks. These issues reduce evidential strength but are addressable in revision.

- **Strengths that support the score:** The unified OED framing provides a valuable pedagogical lens; the score approximation is practically effective; the AntMaze results are genuinely strong; and the work clearly identifies and addresses the expressiveness-efficiency trade-off in generative policies for offline RL.

The paper is a solid, well-executed contribution that addresses an important problem, but the novelty and rigor concerns noted above prevent a higher score. The work would benefit from more circumspect novelty claims, multi-task ablations, and a transparent discussion of the gap between theoretical framing and practical implementation.
