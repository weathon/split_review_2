## Summary
This paper presents a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models two inverse music information retrieval tasks: Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR, score→performance) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT, performance→score). The key idea is to disentangle note-level score content (pitch, rhythm) from global performance style via separate latent spaces, enabling bidirectional supervision between the two tasks. The framework uses a Seq2Seq formulation that avoids fine-grained note-level alignment, and introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module to generate style embeddings from score content alone, mimicking a pianist's interpretive ability.

The method is evaluated on the ASAP dataset (paired) plus unpaired score/performance collections, with the ATEPP dataset used for out-of-distribution evaluation of disentanglement. Results show competitive composite scores on APT (MUSTER E_avg=12.48, best among compared systems) and competitive EPR performance (alignment rate 92.27% with PSR). Subjective listening tests indicate favorable ratings. Disentanglement is verified through performer/composer identification experiments and style transfer evaluations.

However, the paper has several notable weaknesses: (1) the claimed content-space alignment between score and performance encoders lacks any explicit loss term; (2) APT pitch accuracy (E_p=3.08) is worse than all baselines, yet is described as "comparable to SOTA"; (3) the PSR module shows a clear quality gap compared to oracle-style rendering that is not discussed as a limitation; (4) the two-stage PSR training pipeline has no end-to-end feedback to the rendering decoder; (5) three listed contributions overlap, with C3 being a consequence of C1 rather than independent. Overall, the paper presents a promising direction for joint EPR/APT modeling but requires several clarifications and tightened claims before publication. Novelty verification is deferred due to unavailable external literature retrieval in this run.

## Strengths
1. **Novel problem formulation**: Jointly modeling EPR and APT as dual tasks with shared representations is a well-motivated and underexplored direction in MIR. The paper correctly identifies that independent treatment of these inverse tasks is suboptimal and proposes a unified architecture that can leverage cross-task supervision.

2. **Methodological integration of key components**: The combination of disentangled content-style representation, Seq2Seq formulation without note-level alignment, and a diffusion-based style generation module is technically solid and represents a non-trivial engineering contribution. The use of masked autoencoding objectives for unpaired data is a sensible extension.

3. **Thorough evaluation scope**: The paper goes beyond basic task metrics and includes disentanglement verification through performer/composer identification (Table 4), style transfer listening tests (Figure 5), and PSR era-clustering analysis (Figure 4). This multi-faceted evaluation provides reasonable evidence for the claimed disentanglement.

4. **Competitive composite results on APT**: The MUSTER E_avg of 12.48 is the best among compared systems, driven by substantially improved onset and offset accuracy. This suggests that joint training with EPR benefits timing-related aspects of transcription.

5. **Alignment-free EPR demonstration**: Showing that EPR can be formulated as a Seq2Seq task without note-level alignment while achieving competitive accuracy (alignment rate 92.27%) is a practically relevant contribution that could simplify EPR training pipelines and reduce preprocessing overhead.

6. **Open science practices**: The paper provides demo pages and commits to code release, which supports reproducibility and community adoption.

## Weaknesses
### Major Weaknesses

**W1. Content-space alignment is stated but not enforced (Page 4 - Section 3.2/3.3)**

The framework claims that score content features $z_x$ and $z_y$ are "encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption), yet the total objective (Eq. 6) contains no explicit alignment or consistency loss between these two representations. The only supervision comes from the EPR and APT cross-entropy losses, which provide indirect and potentially weak alignment signals. Without a direct loss (e.g., cosine similarity, L2 distance, or contrastive loss), the two content encoders may produce arbitrarily different embedding distributions. This is a core mechanistic issue: if $z_x$ and $z_y$ are not aligned, style transfer (using $z_x$ with a style vector $z_s$) may suffer because the performance decoder $g_Y$ is trained on $z_y$ distributions, not $z_x$ distributions. The paper must either add an explicit alignment loss or provide empirical evidence that alignment emerges from task losses alone (e.g., nearest-neighbor overlap between $z_x$ and $z_y$ spaces). This is a must-address issue for publication.

**W2. APT pitch accuracy is inferior to all baselines; selective reporting risk (Page 7 - Table 1, Section 5.1)**

The pitch edit distance ($E_p = 3.08$) is the *worst* among all 7 compared methods, yet the text states "our model achieves performance comparable to the state-of-the-art APT system." While the composite MUSTER E_avg (12.48) is indeed the best — driven by strong onset/offset deviations — a reader relying on the narrative alone would not know that pitch accuracy degraded. This selective emphasis on the composite score while obscuring a clear weakness in pitch prediction undermines scientific objectivity. The paper should present a balanced interpretation: joint modeling benefits timing precision at the cost of pitch fidelity, and discuss why this trade-off occurs (e.g., shared content encoder may not be optimal for both tasks). The claim "comparable to SOTA" should be replaced with a precise description of where the method excels and where it underperforms.

**W3. PSR module's two-stage training creates a quality gap that is not discussed as a limitation (Page 5 - Section 3.4, Page 7 - Table 2, Section 5.3)**

The PSR module is trained on frozen $z_s$ from the joint model, with no end-to-end feedback from the rendering decoder $g_Y$. This creates a distribution mismatch: the DDPM is trained to match $q(z_s|y)$ (style encoder posterior), but at inference, $z_s$ is sampled from the DDPM's reverse process. Table 2 shows a clear quality gap between Ours (Target) and Ours (PSR) — velocity MAE increases from 10.33 to 15.24 (47% relative increase), velocity KL from 1.76 to 2.67 (52%). The paper presents PSR as a contribution but does not discuss this degradation as a limitation or propose mitigation. At minimum, the text should explicitly quantify the PSR-vs-Target gap and suggest joint fine-tuning as future work.

**W4. Contribution claims C1 and C3 overlap significantly (Page 2 - Introduction)**

C1 claims "a unified transformer-based model for joint EPR and APT" while C3 claims "a Seq2Seq formulation of EPR without note-level alignment." The Seq2Seq formulation is an architectural property of the unified model in C1, not an independent contribution. Listing them separately inflates the contribution count. C3 should be merged into C1 as a sub-point ("using a Seq2Seq formulation that eliminates note-level alignment"), or repositioned as an empirical finding ("demonstration that EPR can be effectively learned without alignment") if supported by dedicated ablation. As currently written, a reviewer may view the three-claim list as two genuine contributions plus one derivative claim.

**W5. Related work does not adequately distinguish from closest prior APT method (Page 3 - Section 2.2)**

Section 2.2 states that the paper "adopts a similar Seq2Seq framework" from Beyer & Dai (2024) for APT but does not specify what is adopted versus newly designed. Table 1 shows that Beyer & Dai (2024) achieves better pitch accuracy (E_p=2.73 vs 3.08) and missing notes (8.40 vs 8.43), while the proposed method improves onset/offset. Without a clear delineation of technical differences, the APT novelty resides primarily in the shared encoder architecture rather than in transcription-specific innovation. The paper should state explicitly: which components are inherited, which are modified, and how the joint training affects APT performance compared to running Beyer & Dai's system independently.

### Minor Weaknesses

**W6. Abstract lacks quantitative anchors (Page 1 - Abstract)** — It describes "competitive performance" and "effective disentanglement" without any numbers, reducing its informational value for rapid screening.

**W7. Subjective evaluation underpowered (Page 7 - Section 4.3/5.1)** — Only 11 participants, no reported inter-rater reliability, and incomplete statistical protocol. The claim "highest ratings across all attributes" should be softened.

**W8. Genre-alignment hypothesis untested (Page 2 - Introduction)** — The PSR module is motivated by a style-genre alignment hypothesis, but the evaluation uses era clustering (Figure 4) which is a coarse proxy, not a direct test of genre-style compatibility.

**W9. Conclusion overclaims for style transfer and PSR (Page 10 - Section 6)** — Findings (c) and (d) stretch beyond the evidence: only 3 samples in style transfer evaluation, and PSR evaluation uses era clustering rather than direct stylistic appropriateness measurement.

**W10. EPR evaluation metrics rely on alignment despite claiming alignment-free advantage (Page 7 - Section 4.3)** — The metrics (alignment/insertion/missing rates) require note-to-note alignment, creating an asymmetry where alignment-free training is evaluated via alignment-dependent measurement.

**W11. Disentanglement verification lacks leakage quantification (Page 8 - Section 5.2)** — Table 4 shows Style→Comp accuracy of 77.46% but this could partly reflect content-correlated composer information. No quantitative leakage metric (e.g., content prediction from $z_s$) is reported.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale**: The paper addresses a well-motivated and timely problem (joint EPR/APT modeling) with a technically competent architecture. However, several issues reduce confidence: (1) the core content-alignment mechanism lacks explicit loss enforcement (W1), (2) selective reporting of APT results obscures a clear pitch-accuracy weakness (W2), (3) the PSR module's quality gap is not discussed as a limitation (W3), and (4) contribution claims overlap (W4). The evaluation scope is commendable but the subjective component is underpowered. Novelty assessment is deferred due to unavailable external retrieval in this run. With the identified major weaknesses addressed (especially W1 and W2), the paper has potential for a higher score.
