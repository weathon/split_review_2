## Summary
This paper presents TokenCount, a training-free object counting framework that repurposes the Segment Anything Model (SAM) without architectural modifications or auxiliary models. The method consists of two components: (1) a **probabilistic prompt generation** stage that models object-location likelihood via cosine similarity between the exemplar and image embeddings, followed by temperature-scaled softmax normalization and multinomial sampling with iterative spatial expansion; and (2) an **output token-based verification** stage that leverages SAM's decoder output tokens with a geometric similarity metric (Triangle Similarity–Sector Similarity, TS-SS) to distinguish positive from negative instances. The method is evaluated on FSC-147 and CARPK benchmarks, reporting MAE of 16.25 (FSC-147 few-shot), 18.57 (FSC-147 one-shot), and 4.68 (CARPK). The core idea—directly using SAM's output tokens as discriminative representations for counting—is novel and well-motivated. However, the paper suffers from significant overclaiming in its narrative (asserting broad superiority not supported by the data), incomplete reproducibility details, and a high RMSE (133.11 on FSC-147) that suggests unreliable per-image counting with heavy-tailed error distribution. The TS-SS metric's advantage over simpler alternatives is marginal (~0.23 MAE difference) and lacks statistical validation.

**Novelty verdict**: Deferred manual verification (Retrieval-Disabled Mode active; external literature comparison unavailable in this run). The core claims—using SAM output tokens for verification and probabilistic iterative prompt generation—appear technically novel within the SAM-only training-free counting space, but a comprehensive literature comparison is needed to confirm overlap with CLIP/DINO-based and hybrid approaches.

## Strengths
1. **Well-motivated core idea**: The insight that SAM's output tokens—which integrate semantic and positional information through bidirectional cross-attention—can be directly used as discriminative representations for object counting is conceptually elegant. This bypasses the need for external similarity computation in the image embedding space, which has been a key limitation of prior training-free approaches.

2. **Training-free deployment**: The method requires no fine-tuning or additional trainable modules, using SAM's original architecture as-is. This is a genuine practical advantage for scenarios where annotated counting data is unavailable or expensive to obtain.

3. **Principled prompt generation**: The probabilistic prompt generation (Eq. 1) replaces heuristic grid or superpixel methods with a principled probability distribution derived from SAM's own embedding. The iterative expansion with probability updates (decreasing positive regions to zero, reducing negative regions) is a clean mechanism for balancing coverage and efficiency.

4. **Competitive results on CARPK**: On the CARPK aerial parking lot dataset, the method achieves MAE 4.68, outperforming most training-based approaches. This demonstrates that the approach can excel when the foundation model's segmentation capabilities are well-matched to the target domain's visual characteristics (consistent viewpoint, clear object boundaries, predictable scales).

5. **TS-SS metric rationale**: While the improvement over simpler metrics is small, the reasoning that output tokens jointly encode semantic (angular) and positional (magnitude) information—and that a hybrid geometric metric is needed to capture both—is technically sound and well-articulated in Section 3.2.

6. **Ablation coverage**: The ablations cover verification metric choice, iteration/prompt trade-offs, and embedding space comparison (image embedding vs. output token). These provide useful insight into the method's behavior and support the design choices.

## Weaknesses
### W1. Systematic overclaiming of performance superiority (Major)
The abstract and quantitative analysis repeatedly claim "superior accuracy" and "outperforming existing training-based and training-free methods." This is not supported by the evidence in Table 1. On FSC-147, the method's MAE (16.25) is worse than six training-based methods (LOCA: 10.79, PseCo: 13.05, SAFECOUNT: 14.32, BMNet+: 14.62, and Counting-DETR: 16.79 is barely worse). The selective comparison against Counting-DETR (16.79) while ignoring stronger baselines creates a misleading narrative. On CARPK, the claim "outperforming all supervised methods" is true only within the reported table, but TFCAC (training-free with DINO encoder, MAE 4.39) beats the proposed method (4.68). The claim should be bounded to "competitive among training-free SAM-only methods" with explicit acknowledgment of remaining gaps.

**Recommendation**: Revise abstract and Section 4.2 to report the full range of baseline performance, acknowledge stronger results explicitly, and bound claims to the "SAM-only training-free" subcategory. Specify that the method does not outperform the leading training-based approaches on FSC-147.

### W2. High RMSE and unaddressed error distribution (Major)
The method's RMSE on FSC-147 (133.11) and one-shot setting (135.86) is the highest among all SAM-only methods (TFOC: 132.16, C-Anything: 131.24) and dramatically higher than training-based methods (LOCA: 56.97) and SAM+α methods (TFCAC: 56.33). This indicates that when the method makes errors, they are large—suggesting occasional catastrophic failures (missing entire dense clusters or double-counting large regions). The paper never discusses this pattern. A practitioner relying on per-image counts would face unacceptable uncertainty.

**Recommendation**: Add a dedicated error analysis subsection. Stratify MAE and RMSE by object-density quartiles. Report the 95th percentile absolute error. Discuss the failure modes: are high-RMSE images concentrated in specific categories, density ranges, or viewpoint conditions? Propose a confidence-calibration mechanism for per-image predictions.

### W3. Incomplete reproducibility and experimental reporting (Major)
Several critical experimental details are missing or underspecified:
- **FSC-147 exemplar selection**: The paper specifies exemplar selection for CARPK (12 random from training) but not for FSC-147. This is essential because FSC-147 has a standard 3-exemplar protocol used by all compared baselines.
- **No variance reporting**: MAE and RMSE are reported as point estimates without standard deviations or confidence intervals. For CARPK, the random selection of 12 training-set exemplars introduces variance that must be quantified (3-5 random trials at minimum).
- **Hyperparameter tuning**: The t-scale (0.066) and verification threshold (300) are said to be chosen on "validation experiments," but no validation set is described. Are these tuned on FSC-147 and fixed for CARPK, or tuned per dataset?
- **NMS threshold**: The IoU threshold for mask NMS is not reported, yet it directly affects the final count.
- **Token verification threshold = 300**: It is unclear whether this is in TS-SS units, normalized TS-SS, or another scale. The threshold value should be interpretable.

**Recommendation**: Provide a complete reproducibility checklist specifying: exemplar count and selection method for each dataset, NMS IoU threshold, validation set definition, and verification threshold interpretation. Add multi-run variance for all main results.

### W4. TS-SS metric improvement is marginal and unvalidated (Moderate)
Table 3 shows TS-SS (MAE 16.25) versus cosine similarity (16.48) and L2 distance (16.53). The improvement over cosine similarity is 0.23 MAE points (~1.4% relative). Without variance estimates, it is impossible to determine whether this difference is statistically significant or simply noise. Additionally, the TS-SS metric from Heidarian & Dinneen (2016) was designed for document clustering, not visual token comparisons, and the transferred motivation (semantic ≈ angular, positional ≈ magnitude) lacks empirical validation (e.g., ablation showing that the angular and magnitude components contribute independently).

**Recommendation**: (a) Report 95% confidence intervals for each metric's MAE via bootstrapping. (b) Add an ablation that decomposes TS-SS into its TS and SS components individually. (c) Provide a correlation analysis between TS-SS scores and ground-truth objectness to validate the metric's suitability for this task. (d) If the gain remains marginal, consider adopting cosine similarity as the simpler default and discuss the TS-SS result as a suggestive improvement needing further validation.

### W5. Probabilistic prompt generation: confounded ablation and missing oracle analysis (Moderate)
Table 4 varies both iterations (8, 16, 32) and prompts-per-iteration (32, 64, 128) simultaneously, confounding their effects. The non-monotonic result (16 iterations + 128 prompts beats 32 + 128) suggests complex interactions with the early stopping mechanism (IoU > 0.95, patience=5) that are not explained. Without isolation of variables, practitioners cannot determine the optimal configuration. Furthermore, the best configuration uses ~1438 prompts per image on average, raising efficiency questions.

**Recommendation**: (a) Add a fixed-total-prompt ablation (e.g., ~1000 total prompts) with different (iterations × prompts-per-iteration) splits to isolate iteration effects. (b) Provide an oracle analysis using ground-truth object locations to estimate the upper bound of prompt-coverage-based recall, separating coverage failures from verification failures.

### W6. PCA analysis has contradictory captions and limited evidence (Minor)
Figure 5's captions are internally inconsistent: one caption (line 253) describes column 2 as "well-separated" for image embeddings and column 3 as "overlap," while another (line 254) states the opposite. The figure layout must be corrected. Additionally, only two example images are shown, and the fraction of variance explained by the first two PCA components is not reported, making it unclear whether the apparent separation is genuine or a dimensionality-reduction artifact.

**Recommendation**: Fix the caption to match the actual figure layout. Report variance-explained percentages. Compute silhouette scores or inter/intra-class distance ratios over many random image samples to quantify separability quantitatively.

### W7. Related work organization and positioning (Minor)
Section 2.1 reads as a chronological list rather than a technical taxonomy. Strongest related baselines (e.g., TFCAC, PseCo) are mentioned but their precise technical differences from the proposed method are not articulated. The section would benefit from organizing methods by their technical approach to similarity computation (image-embedding cosine, external encoder fusion, output-token reuse) rather than by chronology.

### W8. Qualitative Analysis section lacks depth (Minor)
Section 4.3 makes broad claims ("robustness," "stable performance," "diverse real-world scenarios") supported by only two example visualizations. No failure-case analysis, category-stratified breakdown, or density-sensitivity analysis is provided.

### W9. Discussion section has contradictory statements (Minor)
The Discussion claims "superior computational efficiency compared to state of the art" in the same paragraph that says "This limitation affects scalability for real-time applications and large datasets." These statements are contradictory. The final sentence's referent ("This limitation") is ambiguous—it could refer to the small-object limitation, the efficiency issue, or both.

### W10. Conclusion adds unsupported claims (Minor)
The conclusion states "Experimental results validate the effectiveness of our method, demonstrating performance equal to or better than existing methods." This overstates the evidence, as several training-based methods significantly outperform the proposed approach on FSC-147. The conclusion should state validated findings precisely: "On FSC-147, our method achieves MAE 16.25, competitive with several training-based approaches while requiring no training. On CARPK, it achieves MAE 4.68, outperforming most training-based methods."

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Rationale**: 
This paper presents a technically interesting and well-motivated idea—repurposing SAM's output tokens for training-free object counting. The core insight (that output tokens jointly encode semantic and positional information, and can serve as discriminative representations) is conceptually novel and the probabilistic prompt generation is a principled alternative to heuristic methods. The ablation studies are reasonably thorough in scope.

However, the paper's impact is significantly undermined by three factors:
1. **Systematic overclaiming** (abstract, introduction, and quantitative analysis assert superiority not supported by the evidence). The method does not outperform leading training-based approaches on FSC-147 and trails TFCAC on both datasets, yet the narrative reads as if it achieves state-of-the-art.
2. **High RMSE values** (133.11 on FSC-147, 135.86 one-shot) indicate unreliable per-image predictions with heavy-tailed errors—a critical practical limitation that is never discussed.
3. **Incomplete reproducibility** (missing exemplar selection protocol for the main benchmark, no variance reporting, underspecified hyperparameters) weakens the contribution's usability as a reproducible baseline.

The research value lies primarily in demonstrating the feasibility of the output-token approach, which could inspire future work. The practical value is currently limited by the high error variance. With major revisions addressing the overclaiming, error analysis, and reproducibility gaps, the paper could be significantly strengthened. The novelty, while promising, requires external literature verification that could not be performed in this run (Retrieval-Disabled Mode).

**Post-Revision Target**: 6-7/10 — achievable if the authors address the overclaiming (W1), add error distribution analysis (W2), complete reproducibility details (W3), and provide statistical validation for the TS-SS metric (W4). The upper bound is constrained by the method's inherent RMSE limitation relative to training-based approaches.
