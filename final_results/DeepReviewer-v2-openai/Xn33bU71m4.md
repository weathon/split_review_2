## Summary

This paper evaluates nine open-source mid-sized LLMs (CodeLlama, Llama2, DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-V2, Qwen2.5, Qwen3, WizardCoder, Phi4, Gemma3) on two reverse engineering tasks — name recovery and type inference from stripped binaries. The authors develop an automated evaluation pipeline with placeholder-based input normalization and auxiliary-LLM-driven response extraction, and report experiments on a dataset of 40,000 functions across four architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM 32-bit, MIPS 32-bit) at multiple optimization levels. The main findings are that zero-shot F1 scores are uniformly low (<0.1 for name recovery, <0.03 for type inference), and fine-tuning yields inconsistent improvements (up to 0.33 for variable name recovery, but negligible for type inference).

**Core strengths:** The paper addresses a timely and practical question — how to select LLMs for binary reverse engineering — and puts forward a systematic benchmarking framework. The multi-architecture, multi-optimization dataset is a useful resource.

**Core weaknesses:** The manuscript has significant factual inconsistency (an untraceable F1=0.37 claim), major reproducibility gaps (LoRA hyperparameters, auxiliary LLM identity, compiler details omitted), and missing statistical rigor (no variance estimates, no significance tests). The novelty claim of "first systematic evaluation" cannot be verified without external literature access (Retrieval-Disabled Mode). The conclusion lacks a limitations section and bounding of claims. These issues collectively reduce confidence in the reported results and limit the paper's scientific impact.

## Strengths
1. **Timely and practical research question.** The paper asks a fundamentally important question for the security community: how to select LLMs for binary reverse engineering tasks when multiple models are available. Given the rapid proliferation of open-source LLMs, a principled comparison is valuable for practitioners and researchers alike.

2. **Multi-dimensional evaluation scope.** The benchmark spans four CPU architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM 32-bit, MIPS 32-bit), four optimization levels (O0-O3), two input representations (decompiled code and assembly), and two task types (name recovery and type inference). This breadth provides a more comprehensive picture than prior single-architecture or single-task studies.

3. **Standardized evaluation pipeline.** The automated pipeline using placeholder-based symbol normalization and auxiliary-LLM-based response extraction addresses a genuine challenge — LLM output format variability — that prior works have not systematically solved. The approach enables fair comparison across models with different instruction-following capabilities.

4. **Transparent negative results.** The paper honestly reports that all evaluated models perform poorly (most F1 scores <0.1 in zero-shot settings) and that fine-tuning gains are inconsistent. Reporting negative results with this level of detail is valuable for the field, helping to calibrate expectations and avoid wasted effort.

5. **Practical findings on model selection.** The observations that (a) model size within the same family does not predict performance, and (b) training data composition can matter more than architecture, are actionable insights for researchers selecting LLMs for security tasks.

6. **Case study analysis.** Table 8 provides concrete examples of where fine-tuning helps (e.g., recovering `_stack_chk_fail` and OpenSSL function names), which helps readers understand the qualitative nature of the improvements beyond numeric scores.

## Weaknesses
### W1 (Major) — Untraceable F1=0.37 claim contradicts reported data
The Introduction (Page 1, line 11) and Abstract state that fine-tuned DeepSeek-R1 achieves "an F1 score of 0.37." However, **no value of 0.37 appears in any result table** (Tables 2-6) or figure (Figures 1-2). The highest fine-tuned F1 for DeepSeek-R1 in the reported tables is 0.17 (Table 3, variable name recovery at O0). The 0.37 claim is a factual inconsistency that cannot be verified by the reader. If it refers to a specific setting reported only in the appendix, it must be explicitly cross-referenced. **Impact:** This error undermines trust in the reported results. Scientific conclusion: the paper's central empirical narrative is factually non-reproducible from the evidence presented. **Fix:** Correct to match reported data or add explicit traceable reference.

### W2 (Major) — Missing variance, confidence intervals, and significance tests
All F1 scores in Tables 2-6 are reported as single-point values without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests. For a benchmarking paper whose core contribution is a systematic comparison, this is a serious methodological gap. Differences of 0.01–0.03 F1 between models or optimization levels cannot be assessed as meaningful vs. noise. The paper itself acknowledges randomness (Reproducibility statement: "the number will be similar, but not identical"), yet does not quantify it. **Impact:** The head-to-head comparisons that motivate the paper (e.g., which model to select) have no statistical grounding. **Fix:** Report mean±std over ≥3 seeds; add paired significance tests for key comparisons.

### W3 (Major) — LoRA fine-tuning details are critically underspecified
Section 3.3 mentions LoRA and cites QLoRA (Dettmers et al.), but provides **none of the following**: LoRA rank (r), alpha scaling, target modules (which layers), quantization precision, optimizer, learning rate, batch size, training epochs, sequence length, hardware configuration, or whether hyperparameters were consistent across all 9 models. Table 7 reports "time spent" but no training configuration. **Impact:** The fine-tuning experiments cannot be reproduced or assessed for fairness. Different models may have used different settings, invalidating the comparison. **Fix:** Provide a complete configuration table in the appendix.

### W4 (Major) — Auxiliary LLM for response extraction is not identified
Section 3.2 uses an "auxiliary LLM" to normalize model outputs but never specifies which model, at what size, with what prompt, or with what accuracy. **Impact:** This creates an unquantified confound — the reported F1 scores may partly reflect the auxiliary model's extraction errors rather than the target LLMs' capabilities. The evaluation pipeline cannot be reproduced. **Fix:** Specify the auxiliary LLM, include the prompt template, and report extraction accuracy on a held-out validation set.

### W5 (Major) — x86-64-only main-text results vs. general claims
Section 4.4 presents results only for x86-64 in the main text, deferring ARM/MIPS/x86-32 to the appendix. Yet the title, abstract, and conclusion make general claims without architecture qualifiers. **Impact:** Readers cannot verify that the main conclusions hold across all architectures. If ARM or MIPS results differ, the paper's headline finding would be architecture-dependent. **Fix:** Add a sentence confirming cross-architecture consistency or report a compact summary for all architectures in the main text.

### W6 (Major) — Dataset lacks crucial provenance details
Section 4.1 does not specify compiler version, exact compilation flags, or the distribution of functions across source projects. **Impact:** Replication and generalizability assessment are hindered. Functions from a few GNU projects may share structural patterns, and results may not transfer to other software or compilers. **Fix:** Provide compiler version, CFLAGS per optimization level, and a project-level breakdown table.

### W7 (Major) — Type inference metric strictness may conflate inability with measurement
Type inference uses exact string matching, penalizing equivalent types (e.g., `unsigned int` vs `uint32_t`). Finding 2 ("fine-tuning has limited impact on type inference") may partly reflect metric strictness rather than model inability. **Impact:** The paper's negative conclusion on type inference is not robustly established. **Fix:** Add a relaxed matching evaluation and discuss the gap between strict and relaxed scores.

### W8 (Major) — Generic contribution claims
The three listed contributions ("Automatic LLM Experiments," "Extensive Evaluation," "Uncovering Characteristics") describe activities rather than specific scientific findings. They do not allow readers to identify what new knowledge was gained. **Impact:** Reduces the paper's perceived scientific contribution. **Fix:** Rewrite as evidence-grounded findings (e.g., "We find that zero-shot LLMs achieve F1<0.1 on all tasks; fine-tuning improves variable name recovery up to 33× but type inference stagnates; model size does not predict performance").

### W9 (Major) — Conclusion lacks limitations and claim bounding
The single-paragraph conclusion does not discuss any limitations, does not bound its claims to the tested models/settings, and provides vague future directions. **Impact:** For a benchmarking paper, omitting limitations is a significant weakness that reduces scholarly credibility. **Fix:** Add explicit limitations (model size range, architecture coverage, metric choices, fine-tuning scope) and specific next-step research directions.

### W10 (Moderate) — Related works section is a flat citation list
The second related-work paragraph ("Moreover, in terms of related reverse engineering works...") reads as a laundry list without analytical comparison axes. **Impact:** Does not help readers understand the novelty gap relative to prior work. **Fix:** Replace with a comparison table organized by task coverage, model type, dataset size, and evaluation methodology.

### W11 (Minor) — Binaries background paragraph is tutorial-level
The "Binaries" paragraph explains basic compilation concepts that are standard knowledge in the target audience. **Impact:** Wastes limited space; results are deferred to the appendix while basic material occupies main-text real estate. **Fix:** Condense to 1-2 sentences.

### W12 (Minor) — Fine-tuning time causal claim is speculative
Finding 7 claims that "poor template design" caused DeepSeek-V2's 3× longer training time, but the evidence is a single uncontrolled observation on CodeLlama. **Impact:** Weakens the paper's scientific rigor. **Fix:** Qualify the claim or add controlled experiments.

### Novelty (Deferred)
The paper claims "the first systematic, large-scale evaluation of multiple LLMs" on these tasks. Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode in this run, external literature verification is unavailable. This claim requires manual verification. The Related Works section acknowledges several prior studies that cover overlapping tasks; the claim's validity depends on whether any prior work already performed cross-model comparisons under a unified framework.

### Reproducibility
The paper states results are reproducible with "identical scripts" but omits critical details: auxiliary LLM identity, LoRA hyperparameters, compiler version/flags, random seeds, and GPU hardware configuration. The single-seed design without variance reporting means results are inherently unrepeatable with statistical confidence. Reproducibility is rated as **low** in the current manuscript state.

## Score
**Final Score: 4/10**

**Scoring rationale:**

The paper addresses a timely and practically important question, and the multi-architecture benchmarking effort represents real engineering investment. However, the manuscript in its current form suffers from several major issues that significantly reduce its scientific value and trustworthiness:

1. **Factual inconsistency (W1):** The central empirical claim of F1=0.37 is untraceable in the reported data. This is a fundamental reporting error that undermines the paper's core narrative.

2. **Reproducibility failures (W3, W4, W6):** The evaluation pipeline, fine-tuning setup, and dataset are critically underspecified. Another research group cannot replicate the results without substantial guesswork.

3. **Statistical invalidity (W2):** All comparisons lack variance estimates and significance testing, making it impossible to assess whether reported differences are meaningful.

4. **Metric confound for type inference (W7):** The strict type matching conflates measurement strictness with model ability, weakening the paper's negative conclusion on type inference.

5. **Generic contributions (W8):** The contribution statements describe methodology rather than presenting falsifiable scientific findings, reducing the paper's perceived novel contribution.

On the positive side, the benchmark design covers important dimensions (4 architectures, 4 optimization levels, 2 tasks, 9 models), the standardized evaluation pipeline addresses a real need, and the honest reporting of uniformly negative results is valuable. With major revisions — particularly correcting the factual error, adding variance reporting, specifying all experimental details, and bounding claims appropriately — the paper could become a useful reference for the community.

The score of 4/10 reflects that while the underlying research question is sound and the experimental effort is substantial, the current presentation and methodological rigor require major improvements before the paper meets the standard for publication in a top venue. The paper is not fatally flawed, but its weaknesses are widespread and centrally impact the trustworthiness of its reported findings.
