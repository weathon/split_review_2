# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper presents CREDO (Creativity–Reality Evaluation with Decoupled Ontology), a process-level framework for evaluating creativity in human-LLM collaborative learning. The framework introduces two core components: (1) the Innovation Tracing Atlas (ITA), which decomposes multi-turn student–LLM dialogues into cognitive steps and attributes learner vs. model contributions at the turn level, and (2) an instruction-tuned evaluator based on DeepSeek-32B with LoRA fine-tuning that jointly predicts 1–5 scores across four expert-elicited creativity dimensions (Interdisciplinary Innovation, Problem Reframing, Risk-Driven Innovation, Resource Integration Efficiency) and generates textual rationales. 

The empirical study uses a curated dataset of 1,273 cleaned dialogues from 81 undergraduates across multiple STEM domains. The fine-tuned evaluator achieves a Quadratic Weighted Kappa of 0.728 against expert judgments (reaching ~90% of the human inter-rater ceiling of 0.81) and demonstrates robust attribution capability with a macro-average F1 of 0.84 for distinguishing original student ideas from LLM-influenced content.

**Overall assessment:** The paper addresses an important and timely problem — interpretable, process-level creativity assessment in LLM-assisted learning — with a thoughtful framework and generally solid empirical execution. The main contributions are the CREDO evaluation dimensions tailored to human-AI collaboration and the ITA-based attribution methodology. Key weaknesses include a misalignment between the training loss (cross-entropy, which ignores ordinal structure) and the evaluation metric (QWK, which penalizes by distance), insufficient uncertainty quantification on the headline result, and several gaps in reporting that affect reproducibility and evidence completeness. With revisions addressing these issues, the paper has the potential to make a meaningful contribution to the fields of AI in education and creativity assessment.

## Strengths
**1. Timely and well-motivated problem.** The paper addresses a genuinely important challenge: how to evaluate creativity in educational settings where students increasingly collaborate with LLMs. The "governance paradox" (embracing LLMs while needing to assess genuine student ability) is clearly articulated, and the limitations of traditional creativity assessment tools (TTCT, think-aloud protocols) in LLM-mediated contexts are convincingly argued.

**2. Thoughtful framework design (CREDO).** The four CREDO dimensions (Interdisciplinary Innovation, Problem Reframing, Risk-Driven Innovation, Resource Integration Efficiency) are well-motivated operationalizations of creativity constructs that are specifically relevant to human-AI collaboration. Table 1 provides a clear and systematic comparison between classical creativity dimensions and the proposed dimensions, with explicit identification of why each classical dimension fails under LLM collaboration. The grounding in established educational theories (Bloom's Taxonomy, PISA 2022 framework) strengthens construct validity.

**3. High-quality dataset and rigorous annotation protocol.** The curated dataset of 1,273 dialogues with multi-stage preprocessing (including semantic coherence screening, PII anonymization, and standardization) sets a high bar for data quality. The annotation protocol — expert calibration, double-blind review, arbitration for disagreements >1 point, and reporting of inter-rater reliability (Cohen's Weighted Kappa = 0.81, Cronbach's Alpha = 0.86) — is methodologically rigorous and provides a strong gold standard.

**4. Strong empirical results on the core task.** The fine-tuned evaluator achieves QWK = 0.728, substantially outperforming both GPT-4 (0.513) and the untuned DeepSeek-32B (0.342). The attribution validation experiment (macro F1 = 0.84 for distinguishing learner vs. LLM contributions) provides direct quantitative evidence supporting the core claim of attribution capability — a concern explicitly raised by an area chair that the authors directly addressed.

**5. Transparency and reproducibility orientation.** The paper explicitly scopes its claims to the studied tasks and domains, includes a dedicated limitations section, and commits to releasing code, data, and evaluation scripts. This is commendable and raises confidence in the research's integrity.

**6. Human-in-the-loop iterative improvement.** The iterative optimization process (identifying lower consistency on Risk-Driven Innovation, re-evaluating 17 high-disagreement samples, refining the scoring manual, retraining) demonstrates methodological maturity and responsiveness to data-driven quality issues.

## Weaknesses
### Major Weaknesses

**W1. Training–evaluation metric misalignment (ordinal structure ignored).** The supervised objective in Eq. (1) uses standard cross-entropy (CE) for score prediction, treating each of the four CREDO dimensions independently and all classification errors equally. However, the core evaluation metric, Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK), explicitly penalizes errors by their distance on the ordinal scale (predicting 4 vs. 5 is penalized less than 1 vs. 5). This creates a fundamental misalignment between training and evaluation. The paper states scores are "modeled via ordinal or 5-way classification per dimension" — the ambiguity between ordinal regression and flat classification matters because only ordinal-aware losses (e.g., cumulative link models, ordinal hinge loss, or ranking-based objectives) would align the training signal with QWK. The authors should either adopt an ordinal-aware loss, or explicitly state that CE was used despite the misalignment and discuss the potential impact. [Page 1 - Section 3.3.1, Eq. (1)]

**W2. Human performance ceiling is not directly comparable.** The paper establishes the human expert inter-rater reliability (QWK = 0.81) as a "Human-Level Performance Ceiling" and reports the model achieves "nearly 90%" of this ceiling (0.728/0.81 ≈ 0.899). However, the human QWK of 0.81 was computed from the full annotated dataset of 1,273 dialogues, while the model QWK of 0.728 is reported only on the test set of 128 dialogues. These estimates are not directly comparable: they are computed on different samples with different variance properties, and the human ceiling on the specific test subset may differ substantially from the full-dataset estimate. Neither value is accompanied by confidence intervals, bootstrap estimates, or standard errors, making it impossible to assess whether the gap between 0.728 and 0.81 is statistically significant. [Page 1 - Sections 3.2.3 and 4.1]

**W3. Semantic coherence filter may introduce selection bias.** The preprocessing pipeline removes dialogues where "the similarity for three consecutive pairs of utterances fell below a threshold of 0.15" measured by Sentence-BERT cosine similarity. This filter risks systematically excluding the most creative and exploratory dialogues — precisely those where students pursue divergent ideas, make conceptual leaps, or explore novel directions. The paper does not report how many dialogues were removed at this stage, compare removed vs. retained dialogues on any dimension, or justify the 0.15 threshold. If creative divergence is filtered out, the resulting dataset may be biased toward conventional, linearly progressing dialogues, which would weaken the validity of the creativity assessment framework. [Page 1 - Section 3.1.2, Data Cleaning Step 3]

**W4. Attribution experiment sample description is ambiguous and lacks inter-rater reliability.** The attribution validation reports using "200 randomly sampled dialogues from the test set" — but the test set has only 128 dialogues total. This suggests the 200 may refer to utterances rather than dialogues, but the text is unclear. Additionally, while the paper reports expert agreement on the final CREDO scores (Kappa = 0.81), it does not report inter-rater reliability for the attribution-level annotation task (classifying utterances as Original/Developed/Restated Idea). If the two experts disagree significantly on this intermediate classification, the gold-standard labels are noisy and the reported F1 = 0.84 may be an underestimate (or overestimate) of true model capability. [Page 1 - Section 4.2.2, Table 3]

**W5. Qualitative case study lacks comparative evidence.** The case study of Student 0018 is purely descriptive, presenting the ITA visualization and narrative interpretation without comparing model-predicted scores to expert scores for that specific student, without showing the model-generated rationale text, and without explaining how the ITA trace supports the specific score values. This weakens the claim that "the model's internal reasoning logic aligns with that of human experts" — a claim that requires explicit comparison of model and expert reasoning, not just a narrative description of a student's cognitive trajectory. [Page 1 - Section 4.3, Microscopic Case Study]

### Minor Weaknesses

**W6. CREDO acronym and full name misalignment.** The full name "Creativity–Reality Evaluation with Decoupled Ontology" does not clearly map to the framework's actual content. The phrase "Decoupled Ontology" is never defined or explained in the paper, and "Reality Evaluation" is ambiguous. The dimensions (Interdisciplinary Innovation, Problem Reframing, etc.) do not obviously relate to "Decoupled Ontology." The acronym appears forced, and the paper would benefit from either a clearer full name or an explanation of the terminology. [Page 1 - Section 1.4]

**W7. ITA node classification lacks reliability evidence.** The paper introduces ITA with three node types (Origination Nodes, Development Nodes, Scaffolding Support) but does not report inter-rater reliability for this intermediate classification task, does not clarify whether ITA labeling is done by human experts or the automated model, and does not provide annotation guidelines or examples. Without this information, the attributional foundation of the framework cannot be independently assessed. [Page 1 - Section 3.2.2]

**W8. Dataset discipline distribution not reported.** The paper states data comes from 81 undergraduates across "multiple domains" but does not report the distribution of academic disciplines or topic areas. With only two example prompts provided (both STEM), readers cannot assess domain coverage. Per-student dialogue counts are also missing, making it impossible to check for participant-level confounds. [Page 1 - Section 3.1.1]

**W9. Introduction narrative is overly generic.** The first paragraph of the introduction discusses creativity assessment generally without mentioning LLMs or the paper's specific focus until the third paragraph. This delays the reader's understanding of the paper's specific contribution and makes the opening seem disconnected from the core contribution. [Page 1 - Introduction, Section 1 opening]

**W10. Missing confidence intervals for main results.** The headline QWK of 0.728 is reported as a point estimate without confidence intervals, bootstrap estimates, or any measure of uncertainty. Given that this result drives the "90% of human ceiling" claim and is central to the paper's contribution, the absence of uncertainty quantification is a significant gap. [Page 1 - Section 4.2.1, Table 2]

**W11. KD teacher performance not reported.** The knowledge distillation framework uses a full-parameter fine-tuned teacher to guide the LoRA student, but the teacher's absolute performance is never reported. Without this, readers cannot assess the teacher-student capacity gap or whether KD effectively bridges it. [Page 1 - Section 3.3.2]

**W12. Missing variance on self-reported limitations.** The limitations paragraph is commendably transparent but omits discussion of: (a) potential Hawthorne effects (students knew they were recorded, potentially inflating creativity behaviors), (b) LLM version stability during the two-week collection window, and (c) the absence of uncertainty quantification on the main result. [Page 1 - Section 5, Discussion/Limitations]

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a timely and practically important problem with a thoughtfully designed framework (CREDO) and generally solid empirical execution. The strengths in framework design, dataset curation, annotation rigor, and core empirical results are substantial. However, the score is moderated by several significant weaknesses that affect the confidence in the reported results:

- The training-evaluation metric misalignment (CE vs. QWK, W1) suggests the model may not be optimized for what it claims to measure.
- The incomparable human-ceiling estimation (W2) and absence of confidence intervals (W10) mean the headline "90% of human ceiling" claim is not statistically grounded.
- The potential selection bias from the semantic coherence filter (W3) raises concerns about whether the dataset is representative of creative human-LLM dialogues.
- The ambiguous attribution experiment sample (W4) and missing ITA reliability evidence (W7) leave gaps in the evidence chain connecting the proposed methodology to the claimed attribution capability.

These weaknesses are fixable with additional analyses, reporting clarifications, and methodological tightening, but in their current form, they reduce confidence in the quantitative claims. The paper's novelty — shifting from outcome-focused to process-level creativity assessment with explicit human-LLM attribution — is conceptually valuable but cannot be fully assessed without external literature comparison (deferred due to retrieval unavailability in this review run).
