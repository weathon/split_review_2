## Summary
This paper presents a mechanistic interpretability study of how language models (LMs) retrieve entities bound in-context. The authors challenge the prevailing view that LMs rely solely on a positional mechanism (retrieving entities based on their position in the sequence) by demonstrating that this mechanism becomes noisy and unreliable for middle positions when many entity groups are present. They identify two additional mechanisms—a lexical mechanism (retrieving a target entity via its bound counterpart) and a reflexive mechanism (retrieving a target entity through a direct self-referential pointer)—and show through interchange interventions that LMs employ a mixture of all three. The paper formalizes this mixture as a causal model combining a Gaussian positional term with one-hot lexical and reflexive terms, achieving 0.95 Jensen–Shannon similarity with the LM's next-token distribution on synthetic binding tasks. The findings are validated across nine models (2B–72B parameters) from three families and ten binding tasks, and are shown to generalize to inputs interleaved with free-form filler text. The work contributes a richer understanding of in-context entity binding and offers a mechanistic explanation for the "lost-in-the-middle" effect.

## Strengths
1. **Clear and important research question.** The paper addresses a fundamental question in mechanistic interpretability: how do LMs retrieve bound entities? By identifying the failure modes of the positional mechanism in longer contexts, the work fills a genuine gap in prior literature that had only evaluated entity retrieval in very short settings (n=2-3).

2. **Rigorous experimental methodology.** The use of interchange interventions (activation patching) is well-suited to the causal abstraction framework. The counterfactual design to distinguish three mechanisms is clever and carefully thought out, and the additional validation in Section 3.4 to disentangle the reflexive pointer from the answer entity shows strong methodological discipline.

3. **Cross-model and cross-task validation.** Testing nine models across three families (Llama, Gemma, Qwen) and ten binding tasks provides convincing evidence that the three-mechanism mixture is a general phenomenon rather than an artifact of a specific architecture or template.

4. **Formal causal model with high predictive accuracy.** The combined causal model (Gaussian positional + one-hot lexical + one-hot reflexive) achieving JSS of 0.95 on synthetic data provides a compact, interpretable summary of the observed dynamics. The ablation analysis cleanly demonstrates the necessity of all three components.

5. **Extension to more naturalistic settings.** Section 5's experiments with interleaved filler text show that the findings are not an artifact of the artificial templatic format, and the connection to the "lost-in-the-middle" effect gives the work broader relevance to long-context reasoning in LLMs.

6. **Open science commitment.** The authors release code and data, which facilitates reproducibility and future research.

## Weaknesses
1. **Missing limitations discussion (Major).** The paper has no dedicated limitations section or paragraph, which is a significant omission for a mechanistic interpretability paper. Key unacknowledged limitations include: (a) the templatic binding tasks (repetitive "A loves B" patterns) may not capture the complexity of entity binding in natural text; (b) all entities are distinct person names and concrete objects (no abstract entities, synonyms, or ambiguous references); (c) the causal model M is trained on only one model-task combination (gemma-2-2b-it, music task); (d) testing covers up to n=20 groups and up to ~10K padding tokens, which is still far from real-world long-context scenarios (100K+ tokens). The conclusion should explicitly bound the scope of findings.

2. **Lack of statistical rigor in core intervention experiments (Major).** The main discovery evidence (Figures 2, 3) is presented without confidence intervals, error bars, or variance estimates. Section 4 reports that "All CIs are < 0.02" for the causal model fitting, but the foundational intervention experiments that establish the U-shaped reliability curve are not accompanied by similar uncertainty quantification. Without this, readers cannot assess whether the observed pattern is robust to random entity selection, model seed, or trial variance. At minimum, Figure 2 should include confidence bands or error bars on the bar/line charts.

3. **Unresolved confound in the reflexive mechanism validation (Major).** Section 3.4 convincingly shows that the patched signal at layer ℓ is a pointer rather than the answer entity itself. However, the experiment does not distinguish between a pointer to the entity *token* and a pointer to the entity's *position* (token index). If the reflexive mechanism actually encodes a positional index that points to where the target entity token resides, then it is functionally similar to a position-based retrieval mechanism, albeit operating at a finer granularity. The authors should test this by placing the target token at a different position in the counterfactual and checking whether the reflexive mechanism retrieves the token or the position. This would substantially strengthen the claim that the reflexive mechanism is distinct.

4. **Unclear mapping between proposed mechanisms and prior "lookback" framework (Moderate).** The Prior Studies paragraph mentions the "lookback" mechanism (Prakash et al., 2025) but does not explicitly state how the three proposed mechanisms relate to it. The reader cannot tell whether the positional mechanism IS the lookback mechanism, whether the three mechanisms are sub-components of it, or whether they are alternatives. This weakens the novelty positioning. An explicit sentence mapping (e.g., "Within the lookback framework, the positional, lexical, and reflexive signals serve as three different key types for addressing the bound representation") would resolve this.

5. **Insufficient detail on the free-form text experiment design (Moderate).** Section 5 introduces 1,000 "entity-less" filler sentences but does not describe how they are generated or validated. If fillers are generated by an LM, distributional artifacts could affect entity binding. If they are manually written, the sample size may be insufficient. The paper should include generation details in the main text or appendix and report a basic validation (e.g., manual inspection or automated check that no entity names appear in filler sentences).

6. **Ambiguous definition of the lexical and reflexive causal variables (Moderate).** In Section 3.1, the lexical mechanism is defined as L := q (query entity) and the reflexive as R := t (target entity). Since both q and t are entity tokens, the formal definitions of the intermediate variables L and R are nearly identical at the symbolic level. The mechanisms differ only in their output operation (query group vs. self-dereference), not in the variable type. A clearer representational distinction—e.g., L stores the *lexical identity* (a token embedding) while R stores a *position pointer* (an index or address)—would make the causal models more analytically distinct and prevent confusion.

7. **Limited main-text evidence for cross-model and cross-task generalization (Minor).** The paper's central claim is that the three-mechanism mixture is a general account of entity retrieval across models and tasks. Yet the primary results (Figure 2, 3, 5) are from gemma-2-2b-it on the music task, with other models and tasks cited only to the appendix. A compact summary table or a sentence quantifying the JSS range across models in the main text would significantly strengthen the in-line argument.

8. **No ablation of the intervention layer selection (Minor).** The paper selects layer ℓ for interventions based on a localization analysis (Section 3.3, Figure 2 left). However, it does not explore how sensitive the results are to the exact choice of ℓ ± 1 or ℓ ± 2. Reporting a sensitivity analysis (or at minimum stating the range of layers that give qualitatively similar results) would improve methodological rigor.

9. **Speculative "lost-in-the-middle" claim without direct test (Minor).** Section 5 concludes by suggesting that the mechanism mixture provides a "mechanistic explanation of the lost-in-the-middle effect." While plausible, this is stated as a claim rather than a hypothesis. The paper does not test whether disabling the lexical mechanism (e.g., via ablation) directly increases the lost-in-the-middle degradation, nor does it correlate the positional mechanism's noise profile with actual accuracy drops. A targeted intervention experiment would turn this speculation into evidence.

10. **Novelty verification is deferred (Deferred).** Due to external retrieval being unavailable in this run (paper_search API disabled), novelty claims cannot be independently verified against the existing literature. The extent to which the lexical and reflexive mechanisms have been implicitly observed or reported in prior work (e.g., in the cited Prakash, Feng, Dai, and Wu papers) could not be assessed. Authors should ensure that their claims of discovering new mechanisms are carefully scoped against the full set of prior mechanistic interpretability findings on entity binding. Manual literature verification is recommended before final submission.

## Score
**Final Score: 7/10**

**Rationale:** The paper makes a meaningful contribution to mechanistic interpretability by identifying and validating two additional entity retrieval mechanisms (lexical and reflexive) beyond the prevailing positional view. The experimental methodology is thorough, the cross-model validation is commendable, and the formal causal model provides a compact summary of the dynamics. However, the paper has several notable weaknesses that prevent a higher score: (a) the lack of statistical rigor (no confidence intervals or error bars) in the core intervention experiments undermines confidence in the quantitative patterns; (b) the missing limitations discussion is a significant omission for a paper making general claims about LM internals; (c) the unresolved confound in the reflexive mechanism validation (token pointer vs. position pointer) needs clarification; and (d) the speculative "lost-in-the-middle" explanation is presented without direct causal testing. Additionally, novelty verification could not be independently conducted in this review due to external retrieval being unavailable. The paper is well-executed within its scope but would benefit from addressing these concerns before publication.
