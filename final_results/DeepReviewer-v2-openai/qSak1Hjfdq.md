## Summary
This paper tackles the problem of lifelong vision-and-language navigation (VLN) across multiple scenes and diverse illumination conditions (normal, low-light, overexposure, scattering). The authors formalize this as the All-Day Multi-Scenes Lifelong VLN (AML-VLN) problem. They propose Tucker Adaptation (TuKA), a parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that represents multi-hierarchical navigation knowledge as a fourth-order tensor and uses Tucker decomposition to decouple shared knowledge (core tensor + encoder/decoder) from scene-specific and environment-specific expert vectors. A Decoupled Knowledge Incremental Learning (DKIL) strategy is introduced to consolidate shared subspaces via elastic weight consolidation while constraining task-specific experts via consistency and orthogonal losses. The AllDayWalker agent built on top of TuKA is evaluated on a self-constructed benchmark of 24 scenarios (5 simulated scenes × 4 environments + 2 real-world scenes × 2 environments). Results show consistent improvements over LoRA-based continual learning baselines in success rate (SR avg 65% vs. best baseline 52%) and forgetting rate (F-SR avg 11% vs. 18%). Additional experiments on 30-task scaling and 6 unseen scenarios demonstrate generalization capability.

**Novelty assessment (deferred — external literature verification unavailable in this run):** The core idea of using a high-order tensor with Tucker decomposition for multi-factor knowledge decoupling in VLN adaptation is novel from a methodological standpoint. However, the relationship to existing tensor factorization approaches in continual learning (e.g., Tucker decomposition for multi-task learning, tensor-based adapters) and the specific technical novelty of TuKA versus MoE-LoRA hybrids cannot be fully assessed without external literature verification. Manual verification is required before final novelty judgment.

## Strengths
1. **Well-motivated problem formulation.** The AML-VLN setting (lifelong VLN across scenes and illumination conditions simultaneously) is practically relevant and clearly defined. The catastrophic forgetting demonstration in Figure 2 effectively motivates the need for multi-scene lifelong adaptation.

2. **Technically interesting methodological core.** Using a fourth-order tensor with Tucker decomposition to explicitly decouple scene factors and environment factors is a creative application of tensor factorization to the VLN adaptation problem. The dimensional alignment trick (selecting rows from factor matrices to reduce the tensor to a 2D weight matrix) elegantly bridges the gap between high-order tensor representation and standard LLM fine-tuning.

3. **Comprehensive evaluation design.** The benchmark of 24 scenarios with both synthetic degradations (atmospheric scattering, low-light, overexposure) and real-world data provides a structured testbed. The forgetting rate metrics (F-SR, F-SPL, F-OSR) appropriately measure catastrophic forgetting beyond average performance. The 30-task scaling experiment and 6 unseen-scenario generalization test add robustness evidence.

4. **Consistent empirical advantage.** AllDayWalker achieves the highest average SR (65%) and lowest F-SR (11%) across all baselines on the 24-task benchmark. The improvement over SD-LoRA (52% SR, 18% F-SR) and BranchLoRA (44% SR, 36% F-SR) is substantial and consistent across most individual tasks.

5. **Reproducibility-friendly.** Code and video demos are publicly released. The architecture builds on the open-source StreamVLN agent with a clearly specified backbone (Qwen2-7B, CLIP vision encoder).

## Weaknesses
### W1. Critical formula error: undefined loss term in Eq (9) [Major]
The total loss in Eq (9) includes $\mathcal{L}_{sk}$, which is never defined in the paper. The three regularization terms defined in the method section are $\mathcal{L}_{ewc}$ (Eq 4, elastic weight consolidation), $\mathcal{L}_{co}$ (Eq 7, expert consistency), and $\mathcal{L}_{es}$ (Eq 8, expert subspace orthogonal constraint). The term $\mathcal{L}_{sk}$ appears without definition or equation reference, making the loss function incompletely specified. **Impact:** A reader implementing the method cannot determine what $\mathcal{L}_{sk}$ represents — it may refer to the EWC loss $\mathcal{L}_{ewc}$, but this is not stated. The navigation loss weight $\lambda = 1 - (\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 + \lambda_3) = 0.5$ is also aggressive (regularization equals task loss in weight) without justification. **Fix:** Replace $\mathcal{L}_{sk}$ with $\mathcal{L}_{ewc}$ (or define it explicitly), and report how $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3$ were selected. *(Page 1 - Section 3.3, Eq 9)*

### W2. Formula error in Eq (8): normalization by squared norm [Major]
Eq (8) defines row normalization as $\text{Norm}(U) = (U[i,:]) / \|U[i,:]\|_F^2$. Standard unit-vector normalization uses $\|U[i,:]\|_F$ (first power), not the square. If the squared norm is used, the resulting rows have norm $1/\|U[i,:]\|_F$ rather than unit length, which is unconventional and could cause training instability. Additionally, the orthogonality constraint $\|\tilde{U}^3(\tilde{U}^3)^T - I\|_F^2$ enforces full-matrix orthonormality across *all* expert rows, whereas the text states the goal is to make the *new* expert orthogonal to previous ones — a significant discrepancy between the stated intent and the mathematical formulation. **Fix:** Correct to $\text{Norm}(U)[i,:] = U[i,:] / \|U[i,:]\|_F$, and modify the loss to enforce pairwise orthogonality only for the currently active expert. *(Page 1 - Section 3.3, Eq 8)*

### W3. Missing statistical evidence: no variance or significance testing [Major]
All reported results (Tables 1-5, Figure 7) are single-point estimates without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests. VLN navigation results are known to have high variance due to random seeds, episode sampling, and initialization. The forgetting rates include anomalous negative values (e.g., F-SR = -3% for T14, -4% for T20), where AllDayWalker's sequential performance exceeds the joint-training oracle — a finding that is not discussed and could indicate evaluation noise or a flawed baseline definition. Without variance information, the core claim that AllDayWalker "consistently outperforms" baselines cannot be rigorously verified. **Fix:** Report all metrics as mean±std over ≥3 seeds, add paired significance tests (e.g., Wilcoxon) against best baselines, and explain the negative forgetting rates. *(Page 1 - Section 5.1-5.2, Tables 1-2)*

### W4. Parameter fairness not verifiable [Major]
The paper states parameter counts are "comparable" across methods, but TuKA's low-rank configuration ($r_1=r_2=8$, $r_3=64$, $r_4=64$, $M=7$, $N=4$) yields a core tensor of $8\times8\times64\times64 = 262,144$ parameters per layer, plus 704 expert row parameters, plus $U^1$ and $U^2$ factor matrices. In contrast, baseline LoRA uses $r=6$ ($2\times d_{model}\times6$ per layer). The total parameter disparity across methods is substantial, yet no parameter count table is provided. Without this, TuKA's performance gains could partly reflect higher model capacity rather than the tensor decomposition design. **Fix:** Add a table reporting total trainable parameters for each method per layer and overall, and discuss whether a higher-rank LoRA baseline would close the gap. *(Page 1 - Section 5.1, Implementation Details)*

### W5. Benchmark design confounds [Major]
The 24-task AML-VLN benchmark has several design issues: (a) Results appear to use a single fixed task order — the paper notes "the order of tasks is randomized" but does not specify whether reported numbers are averaged across multiple orders; (b) Environment types are imbalanced — scattering and overexposure appear only in simulation (5 scenes), while real-world scenes only have normal and low-light, meaning the environment experts $U^4$ for scattering and overexposure see less diverse training data; (c) The real-world validation is limited to 2 scenes with 2 environments, yet the contribution list claims "additional real-world deployments also validate the superiority." **Fix:** (a) Average results over ≥3 random task orders; (b) Analyze whether environment expert quality correlates with training diversity; (c) Qualify the real-world claim with the actual scope. *(Page 1 - Section 5.1, Figure 6)*

### W6. Overclaiming and writing precision issues [Minor]

- **Abstract overclaim:** "consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines" on a self-constructed benchmark with primarily LoRA-based comparisons — this should be bounded to "outperforms existing LoRA-based and test-time adaptation methods on the proposed AML-VLN benchmark." *(Page 1 - Abstract)*
- **Contribution 3 ambiguity:** "additional real-world deployments" implies more extensive testing than the 2-scene, 2-environment validation reported. *(Page 1 - Section 1, Contribution list)*
- **Vague inspiration:** "Inspired by human lifelong learning and continual evolution" is an unsupported rhetorical device that does not link to any specific design choice. *(Page 1 - Section 1, paragraph 2)*
- **Motivation logic gap:** The claim that MoE-LoRA's "two-dimensional matrix form" fundamentally cannot represent multi-hierarchical knowledge conflates mathematical form with representational capacity — higher-rank matrices can encode complex factor interactions without explicit tensor decomposition. A more precise justification is needed. *(Page 1 - Section 1, paragraph 3)*

### W7. Missing limitations section [Major]
The conclusion contains no discussion of limitations, failure cases, or practical constraints. Key omissions include: the sim-to-real gap for synthetic degradations, the reliability of CLIP-based expert retrieval under visually similar conditions, the memory/compute overhead of higher-order tensors vs. LoRA baselines, and the scope of real-world validation. A dedicated limitations paragraph is essential for scientific completeness. *(Page 1 - Section 6, Conclusion)*

### W8. Novelty verification deferred [Note]
Due to external literature retrieval being unavailable in this run, novelty claims cannot be verified against prior work. The core tensor decomposition approach is methodologically interesting, but its relationship to existing tensor-based adapter work (e.g., tensor-train decomposition for model compression, Tucker decomposition in multi-task learning) and its differentiation from MoE-LoRA hybrids require manual literature verification before a final novelty judgment can be established.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a practically relevant problem (lifelong VLN under diverse scene and illumination conditions) with a technically interesting methodological contribution (high-order tensor adaptation with Tucker decomposition). The empirical results show consistent improvements over LoRA-based baselines on a structured 24-scenario benchmark. However, the score is constrained by several major weaknesses that affect reproducibility and validity confidence:

- **W1 (undefined loss term) and W2 (normalization error)** are concrete mathematical errors that must be corrected before the method can be reliably reproduced. These are fixable but currently compromise the technical completeness.
- **W3 (no statistics)** and **W4 (parameter fairness)** significantly weaken the core empirical claim. Without variance estimates or controlled parameter budgets, the reported performance advantage cannot be fully attributed to the proposed method.
- **W5 (benchmark confounds)** and **W7 (missing limitations)** reflect insufficient scientific rigor in evaluation design and reporting completeness.
- **Novelty assessment is deferred** pending external literature verification; if the tensor decomposition approach turns out to overlap substantially with existing tensor-based adaptation work, the contribution value would further decrease.

The paper's strengths — a well-motivated problem, a creative technical approach, and promising initial results — suggest potential for a higher score after addressing the identified reproducibility and rigor issues. The most impactful revision would be: (1) fix all formula errors, (2) add multi-seed variance and significance tests, (3) report explicit per-method parameter counts, and (4) add a limitations section.
