# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper introduces **random set stability**, a new framework for deriving worst-case generalization bounds over data-dependent random sets produced by stochastic optimization algorithms. The core idea is to replace the intractable mutual information (IT) terms that appear in existing topological and fractal generalization bounds with a stability parameter $\beta_n$ that is more interpretable and empirically estimable. The authors establish three main theoretical results: (1) an expected worst-case generalization bound expressed in terms of the random set stability parameter and a Rademacher complexity term (Lemma 3.4); (2) IT-free versions of intrinsic dimension bounds (Theorem 4.3); and (3) IT-free versions of topological complexity bounds based on weighted lifetime sums and positive magnitude (Theorem 4.4). These results are supported by experiments using ViT on CIFAR-100 and GraphSAGE on MNISTSuperpixels.

The paper addresses a genuine need in learning theory: existing worst-case bounds for data-dependent sets rely on mutual information terms that are computationally intractable and theoretically poorly understood. Replacing these with a stability-based parameter is a conceptually sound direction. However, the practical impact is limited by several factors: the stability parameter $\beta_n$ itself remains challenging to estimate accurately; the experimental validation uses ADAM and 0-1 loss which do not satisfy the theoretical assumptions; the bound's rate ($\beta_n^{1/3}$) is slower than classical $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})$; and the empirical bounds in Table 1 are 5--15x larger than actual generalization gaps. The work is a solid theoretical contribution with clear positioning relative to prior art, but the gap between theory and practical applicability requires further bridging.

## Strengths
**S1. Well-motivated problem with clear positioning.** The paper identifies a genuine weakness in existing worst-case generalization bounds for data-dependent random sets — the reliance on intractable mutual information (IT) terms — and proposes a principled alternative based on algorithmic stability. The positioning relative to the prior work of Foster et al. (2019) (hypothesis set stability without algorithmic randomness) and Simsekli/Birdal/Andreeva lines of work (topological bounds with IT terms) is clear and logically coherent. The paper demonstrates awareness of its place in the literature.

**S2. Novel conceptual contribution: random set stability.** The notion of random set stability (Assumption 3.1) that incorporates algorithmic randomness $U$ is a meaningful extension of both uniform argument stability (Definition 2.1) and hypothesis set stability (Definition 2.2). The use of data-dependent selections (Definition 3.1) provides a rigorous way to talk about "for all $w \in \mathcal{W}_{S,U}$" when $\mathcal{W}_{S,U}$ is random. Lemma 3.2 shows this new assumption is implied by classical uniform argument stability, which connects the framework to a large body of existing theory.

**S3. Clean recovery of classical results.** Lemma 3.4 with $J=1$ recovers algorithmic stability bounds (Corollary 3.5) and with $J=n$ recovers Rademacher complexity bounds for fixed hypothesis sets (Corollary 3.6). This interpolation between two classical regimes demonstrates the framework's generality and provides a unifying perspective on seemingly distinct generalization bound families.

**S4. Concrete IT-free topological bounds.** Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 provide explicit generalization bounds using upper box-counting dimension, $\alpha$-weighted lifetime sums, and positive magnitude — all without mutual information terms. These are the first bounds of their kind and make the topological complexity approach amenable to empirical evaluation for the first time. The authors explicitly acknowledge the slower $\beta_n^{1/3}$ rate as a deliberate trade-off, which is an honest admission.

**S5. Clear exposition of technical machinery.** The definitions and assumptions are stated precisely, and the logical flow from background (Section 2) to main results (Section 3) to applications (Section 4) is well-structured. The examples (1.1--1.4) illustrating how the random set formalism captures diverse settings are helpful for intuition.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Assumption 3.1 (random set stability) has subtle verifiability gaps (major).** The assumption requires that for *any* data-dependent selection $\omega$ of $\mathcal{W}_{S,U}$, there *exists* a map $\omega'$ satisfying certain conditions. The existence of such $\omega'$ is asserted to follow from "mild measure-theoretic conditions" (citing Molchanov, 2017), but these conditions are never specified. For practitioners, it is unclear how to verify Assumption 3.1 for a given algorithm without essentially proving uniform argument stability (Definition 2.1) for each iterate and then applying Lemma 3.2, which gives a potentially loose bound ($\beta_n = L \sum \delta_k$ scales linearly with trajectory length $T$). The paper's main theoretical contribution thus rests on an assumption whose verifiability is only partially addressed.

**W2. Corollary 3.3 contains a suspicious exponent simplification (major).** The expression for $\beta_n$ in Corollary 3.3 includes a term $k^{(G+1)/(G+1)} = k^1$, which means the sum $\sum k = T(T+1)/2$ and the smoothness parameter $G$ cancels out entirely. This is either a typo (the intended exponent may have been $k^{(G+1)/G}$) or the $G$-Lipschitz assumption on $\nabla\ell$ is irrelevant for this bound. Either way, the formula as written is misleading and should be corrected or clarified. This issue directly affects the claimed $\mathcal{O}(T^2/n)$ stability rate, which depends on this summation.

**W3. Optimistic estimation of $\beta_n$ undermines empirical validation (major).** The paper acknowledges that the empirical estimation of $\beta_n$ is optimistic because the supremum over $\mathcal{Z}$ is approximated using only $M=500$ held-out points. However, the implications of this optimism for the empirical results (Table 1) are not discussed quantitatively. Since $2J\beta_n$ appears additively in the bound (Eq. 8), underestimating $\beta_n$ by even a small factor can substantially change the bound. The claim that bounds "provide meaningful guarantees" because they "remain below 100% accuracy" is not rigorous — an optimistic bound that is always below the true bound is not a valid guarantee. The paper should either provide a finite-sample correction for the $\beta_n$ estimation or explicitly treat the empirical results as exploratory rather than confirmatory.

**W4. Theory-empirics gap (major).** The theoretical results make specific assumptions: Lipschitz loss (Assumption 4.1), bounded loss in $[0,B]$ (Assumption 4.2), and algorithmic randomness $U$ that is independent of $S$. The experiments, however, use ADAM (not the SGD analyzed in Corollary 3.3), 0-1 loss (which is not Lipschitz), and do not verify Assumption 4.1 for the models used. The empirical results therefore cannot be interpreted as validating the theory; at best they show that quantities inspired by the theory correlate with generalization gaps under certain conditions. This gap between theoretical assumptions and experimental practice should be explicitly discussed and ideally addressed with additional experiments under the assumed conditions.

**W5. The claimed unified form of prior bounds (Eq. 5) is unverifiable (major).** The paper asserts that existing worst-case bounds all take the form $\sqrt{(\mathbf{C}(\mathcal{W}_{S,U}) + \text{IT} + \log(1/\zeta))/n}$, but provides no concrete theorem numbers or equation citations to support this claim. The cited works (Simsekli et al., 2020; Birdal et al., 2021; Dupuis et al., 2023, 2024; Andreeva et al., 2024) use substantially different mathematical frameworks (PAC-Bayesian, fractal, information-theoretic) with different scaling behaviors. Without explicit references, readers cannot verify this claimed unification, which is central to the paper's motivation. This is a weakness in scholarly rigor.

**W6. Bounds are loose and scale suboptimally (moderate).** The bounds in Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 scale as $\beta_n^{1/3}$ times topological complexity terms, yielding a rate of $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/3})$ when $\beta_n = \Theta(1/n)$, compared to the classical $\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})$ for fixed hypothesis sets (Corollary 3.6). The empirical results in Table 1 confirm substantial looseness: bounds are 5–15x larger than actual worst-case generalization gaps (e.g., 104.43 vs 10.24 for ViT). While the authors acknowledge this as a deliberate trade-off, the practical utility of bounds that are an order of magnitude larger than the quantity they aim to bound is questionable. The paper does not discuss regimes where the bounds could be expected to be tight.

**W7. Notation inconsistency (minor).** The paper uses $\mathbb{Z}^n$ (blackboard Z) in Eq. (3) for the data space, but $\mathcal{Z}$ (calligraphic Z) everywhere else (Eqs. 1-2). Lemma 3.4 introduces $\mu_S$ as a distribution over data, but $S$ was previously distributed as $\mu_z^{\otimes n}$. These inconsistencies, while minor, create confusion in a paper already dense with notation.

**W8. Narrow experimental scope (moderate).** Only two model-dataset combinations (ViT + CIFAR-100, GraphSAGE + MNISTSuperpixels) are tested, with only two learning rates and two batch sizes each. The claim of "practically relevant settings" is overstated given this narrow scope. The correlation between $\mathbf{E}^1$ and generalization gap also varies substantially across $n$ values, with $r$ dropping to 0.28 and 0.37 for GraphSAGE at large $n$, raising questions about the stability of the reported relationships.

**W9. Novelty depends on unverifiable comparison (deferred).** Because external literature retrieval was not available in this run, novelty assessment is deferred to manual verification. However, based on the manuscript's own description, the closest prior works (Foster et al., 2019; Andreeva et al., 2024) already studied worst-case bounds for data-dependent sets. The specific novelty of this paper lies in (a) incorporating algorithmic randomness $U$ into the stability framework and (b) eliminating IT terms. Both are incremental but meaningful contributions to the existing literature, provided the technical issues in W1 and W2 are resolved.

**W10. Reproducibility concerns (moderate).** The paper states "implementation will be made available upon publication" but does not provide code or pseudocode for the main algorithms (stability estimation, topological complexity computation) in the main text. Algorithm 1 is mentioned but appears only in the appendix. Key experimental details (architecture specifications, exact training hyperparameters, data preprocessing) are deferred to Appendix C with only brief references in the main text. Given the complexity of the quantities being estimated, the lack of in-text algorithmic descriptions makes independent reproduction difficult.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper introduces a conceptually meaningful framework (random set stability) that addresses a genuine weakness in existing worst-case generalization bounds — the reliance on intractable mutual information terms. The theoretical development is rigorous in its formalization, and the recovery of classical results (Corollaries 3.5, 3.6) demonstrates the framework's generality. However, the score is limited by several factors:

1. **Research value is moderate**: The central improvement (replacing IT terms with β_n) trades one difficult-to-estimate quantity for another. While β_n is more interpretable, its empirical estimation remains challenging and optimistic, as acknowledged by the authors. The resulting bounds scale as n^{-1/3} and are 5–15x larger than the actual generalization gap in experiments, limiting practical applicability.

2. **A key technical result has an unresolved issue**: Corollary 3.3's β_n expression contains an exponent that simplifies to k^1, making the G-Lipschitz assumption irrelevant. This needs correction or clarification before the results can be fully trusted.

3. **The empirical validation does not match the theoretical assumptions**: Experiments use ADAM (not SGD), 0-1 loss (not Lipschitz), and optimistic β_n estimates. This gap prevents the experiments from serving as a true validation of the theory.

4. **Novelty is incremental but solid**: The incorporation of algorithmic randomness U into set stability and the removal of IT terms from topological bounds are meaningful steps forward from Foster et al. (2019) and Andreeva et al. (2024). The contribution is well-positioned within existing literature, though external verification of novelty claims is deferred due to retrieval limitations in this run.

The paper has clear strengths in its theoretical framing and would benefit from (a) correcting the exponent issue in Corollary 3.3, (b) providing quantitative bounds on the β_n estimation error, (c) conducting experiments under assumptions matching the theory (e.g., using SGD and Lipschitz loss), and (d) expanding the empirical scope to more model-dataset combinations. With these revisions, the score could potentially rise to the 6–7 range.
