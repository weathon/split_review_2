## Summary

This paper proposes HiSo, a Hessian-informed zeroth-order (ZO) federated optimization method that integrates diagonal Hessian approximations into the scalar-only communication framework pioneered by DeComFL. The core idea is to precondition ZO gradient estimates using a learned global diagonal Hessian matrix $H_r$, which is updated via an RMSProp-style exponential moving average of squared update magnitudes. Since $H_r$ is reconstructed from scalar representations that are already transmitted, it incurs no additional communication overhead.

The paper makes three main contributions:
- **C1 (Generalized framework):** A generalized scalar-only communication FL framework that decouples the dimension-free property from the specific choice of ZO-SGD, enabling integration of more advanced optimizers.
- **C2 (HiSo algorithm):** A Hessian-informed ZO method that uses a diagonal Hessian approximation for preconditioning while preserving scalar-only communication.
- **C3 (Theory):** Convergence analysis showing that under a "well-approximated Hessian" condition combined with low-effective-rank structure, HiSo achieves a rate $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\zeta/mR})$ independent of model dimension $d$ and Lipschitz constant $L$. This generalizes DeComFL's analysis and extends it to multiple local updates ($\tau > 1$), which was an open question.

Empirically, on LLM fine-tuning tasks (SST-2, QQP, SQuAD) with OPT-125M to OPT-2.7B models, HiSo achieves 1.4–5.4x speedup in communication rounds over DeComFL while maintaining the same per-round cost.

**Novelty assessment (deferred):** Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode in this review run, external literature verification was not available. Novelty comparisons against prior Hessian-informed ZO methods and FL adaptive optimizers are deferred for manual verification. The paper's core technical novelty lies in combining diagonal Hessian preconditioning with scalar-only communication, which appears to be a natural but non-trivial extension of existing ideas.

## Strengths
**1. Well-motivated problem and clean solution.** The paper identifies a genuine tension in federated ZO optimization: curvature information could accelerate convergence, but transmitting it breaks the dimension-free paradigm. HiSo's resolution — learning a diagonal Hessian approximation from quantities already being communicated — is elegant and practical. The observation that the scalar representation framework can support algorithms beyond ZO-SGD (Section 3.3) is a useful conceptual contribution that may enable future work.

**2. Solid theoretical analysis with meaningful novelty.** Theorem 1 provides a convergence bound in the $H_r^{-1}$-weighted norm, and the corollaries cleanly separate the roles of the well-approximated condition and the low-effective-rank structure. The extension to $\tau > 1$ (multiple local updates, Corollary 3) resolves an open limitation in DeComFL's analysis, and the dimension-independent rate $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\zeta/mR})$ under the whitening assumption is genuinely interesting.

**3. Strong empirical validation across multiple tasks and model scales.** Experiments span three LLM benchmarks (SST-2, QQP, SQuAD) and three model sizes (OPT-125M to OPT-2.7B), with consistent results. The comparison against both ZO-FL baselines (FedZO, DeComFL) and first-order FL methods (FedAvg, FedAdam, FedYogi, FedAdagrad) is comprehensive. The communication cost reporting (KB per client) is transparent and enables direct comparison.

**4. Clear exposition of the scalar representation mechanism.** The explanation of how ZO gradients decompose into scalars $(g, s)$ and how this enables dimension-free reconstruction (Section 3.1-3.2) is pedagogically well-structured. Figures 1-3 support the textual description effectively.

**5. Reproducibility-conscious design.** The algorithm description in Section 4.3 and Appendix D provides sufficient detail for implementation. The use of shared random seeds and the model-reset mechanism are clearly specified.

## Weaknesses
**W1 — Theoretical headline result depends on an unverifiable condition (Major).** The paper's central theoretical claim — convergence independent of dimension $d$ and Lipschitz constant $L$ — is conditional on the "well-approximated Hessian" condition (Definition, Eq. 17). As the authors honestly acknowledge (Page 8, Remarks): "it is hard to determine if this approximation holds in the context of LLMs." This means the strongest advertised result rests on an assumption that cannot be empirically verified for the target application. When the condition fails, HiSo's performance "degenerates into DeComFL" (same page), which has dimension-dependent convergence. The abstract and introduction should more prominently communicate this caveat rather than leading with the dimension-free claim as a headline. *Evidence: Page 1 Abstract, Page 8 Remarks paragraph.*

**W2 — Definition of "well-approximated condition" is logically circular (Major).** The formal definition in Eq. (17) states that $H$ is well-approximated if $\text{Tr}(H^{-1/2}\Sigma H^{-1/2}) \leq \zeta$, where $\zeta$ is defined in Eq. (16) as exactly $\text{Tr}(H^{-1/2}\Sigma H^{-1/2})$. This makes the condition always true by identity — any $H$ satisfies $\text{Tr}(H^{-1/2}\Sigma H^{-1/2}) \leq \text{Tr}(H^{-1/2}\Sigma H^{-1/2})$. The intended meaning appears to be that $\zeta \ll d$ (the trace is much smaller than dimension), but this is not what is written. This circularity undermines the formal definition and needs correction. *Evidence: Page 7, Eq. (16)-(17). See annotation #9.*

**W3 — "Ascent direction" terminology is inconsistent with algorithm's use of subtraction (Major).** Section 4.1 repeatedly uses "ascent direction" to describe $\Delta x_{r,k}^{(i)}$, but Algorithm 1 (line 11) and Eq. (8) apply it as $x_{r,k+1} = x_{r,k} - \eta \Delta x$, making $\Delta x$ a descent direction. This is not just a terminology issue — it creates confusion about the sign conventions in the least-squares formulation (Eq. 5-6), which minimizes $\|\nabla f_i - \Delta x\|$ but should minimize $\|\nabla f_i + \Delta x\|$ if $\Delta x$ is to approximate the negative gradient. *Evidence: Page 4, Section 4.1 heading and Eq. (5)-(6). See annotation #7.*

**W4 — Missing direct validation that learned H approximates true Hessian (Major).** The MNIST CNN experiment (Section 6) shows that the learned diagonal Hessian H has a long-tail distribution "consistent with the low effective rank assumption." This is suggestive but not sufficient to establish that H is a good approximation of the true Hessian. The paper claims "our strategy effectively approximates relevant Hessian structure" (Page 8), but no comparison against ground-truth Hessian (not even for a small CNN where it is computationally feasible) is provided. A simple correlation analysis between H's entries and the true diagonal Hessian for the CNN model would significantly strengthen the empirical case. *Evidence: Page 8, MNIST experiment description. See annotation #12.*

**W5 — Learning rate in Theorem 1 is non-constructive (Major).** The condition $\eta \leq \min(\frac{\beta_\ell}{mL}, \frac{1}{8\rho_k}, \frac{\beta_\ell}{4(\tau-1)}) \sqrt{\frac{1}{L(d+2)}}$ depends on $\rho_k$, which involves the unknown Hessian $\Sigma_{r,k}$ and the learned $H_r$. Since $\rho_k$ is not a known quantity, this condition cannot be used to set the learning rate in practice. The corollaries resolve this under the well-approximated condition (which is itself unverifiable), but this means the parameter-free guarantee of Theorem 1 does not translate into a practical learning rate schedule. *Evidence: Page 8, Theorem 1. See annotation #10.*

**W6 — Speedup metric in Table 2 compares against DeComFL's best accuracy, not convergence to HiSo's own optimum (Minor).** Measuring speedup as "rounds for HiSo to match DeComFL's best accuracy" is informative but incomplete. Since HiSo achieves higher final accuracy than DeComFL on most tasks (Table 3), the metric understates HiSo's true convergence cost. Reporting both the matching-rounds metric and HiSo's own convergence rounds to its saturation point would give a fuller picture. *Evidence: Page 9, Table 2 and accompanying text. See annotation #14.*

**W7 — Communication savings vs. first-order methods conflates paradigm-level and algorithm-level claims (Minor).** The "up to 90 million times communication savings" compared to first-order methods (contribution list) is a property of the ZO scalar-only paradigm, not specific to HiSo's algorithmic innovation. DeComFL achieves the same orders-of-magnitude savings. The contribution should separate the paradigm-level saving (ZO vs. FO) from HiSo-specific saving (HiSo vs. DeComFL within ZO). *Evidence: Page 1, contribution bullet 4. See annotation #4.*

**W8 — Hessian update Eq. (12) has timing ambiguity (Minor).** The description says Hessian is updated "at the beginning of one communication round with $\tau$-local update steps," but the equation uses $\Delta x_{r,0}$ (the first local step) while appearing after Algorithm 1 (which uses $\tau$ steps per round). It is unclear whether the Hessian update uses the first local step, the last, or an aggregate across all $\tau$ steps. This ambiguity could lead to implementation divergence. *Evidence: Page 5, Eq. (12) vs. surrounding text. See annotation #8.*

**W9 — Model-reset reconstruction cost not quantified (Minor).** The paper states the server stores historical scalars as "negligible extra memory" (Page 3), which is correct for memory. However, clients that rejoin after many missed rounds must replay all missed gradient scalars to reconstruct the current model. The computational cost of this replay scales linearly with the number of missed rounds, which could be significant for straggler-prone FL deployments. This cost should be quantified or bounded. *Evidence: Page 3, downlink communication description. See annotation #11.*

**W10 — Limited discussion of limitations and failure modes (Minor).** The paper does not include a dedicated limitations section. Key practical concerns are only briefly mentioned in passing: (a) the well-approximated condition "is hard to determine" (Page 8), (b) momentum extension is deferred (footnote 2), (c) the impact of the smoothing parameter $\mu$ on bias is not analyzed. A concrete limitations paragraph would improve completeness. *Evidence: Scattered across Pages 5, 8.*

**W11 — Unsupported claim about Adam's use of second-order information (Minor).** The introduction cites Kingma & Ba (2015) — Adam — as evidence that "second-order information can significantly accelerate convergence." Adam uses first-order gradient moments, not Hessian information. This reference is misleading and should be replaced with citations to actual Hessian-informed methods (e.g., Ye et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2025). *Evidence: Page 1, Introduction paragraph 2. See annotation #3.*

## Score
**Final Score: 6.5/10**

*Rationale:* The paper addresses a well-motivated problem and presents a clean algorithmic idea (HiSo) with a solid theoretical analysis and convincing empirical results across multiple LLM benchmarks. The generalization of DeComFL's framework and the extension to $\tau > 1$ are genuine contributions.

However, the score is tempered by several issues: (1) the headline theoretical result (dimension-free convergence) is conditional on a "well-approximated condition" that the authors acknowledge is unverifiable for LLMs; (2) the formal definition of this condition is circular as written (Eq. 17); (3) the "ascent direction" terminology conflicts with the algorithm's actual use of descent updates; (4) the empirical validation of the Hessian approximation quality is indirect at best; (5) the learning rate in Theorem 1 is non-constructive; and (6) several communication savings claims conflate paradigm-level and algorithm-level contributions.

The paper would benefit from: fixing the circular definition, adding direct Hessian approximation validation for the CNN case, clarifying the descent/ascent terminology, adding a limitations section, and more precisely scoping the theoretical claims in the abstract and introduction. These issues are fixable and do not invalidate the core contribution.

**Recommendation:** The paper has merit and addresses an important problem. I recommend acceptance conditional on addressing the major issues (W1-W5), particularly the circular definition and the terminology inconsistency.
