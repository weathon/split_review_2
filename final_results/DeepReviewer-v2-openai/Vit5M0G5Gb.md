## Summary
This paper presents a theoretical framework analyzing saddle-to-saddle learning dynamics as a mechanism for the dynamical simplicity bias — the tendency of neural networks to learn progressively more complex solutions during gradient descent training. The authors study a general architecture class defined by Eq. (1), covering fully-connected, convolutional, and attention-based layers. The theoretical contributions are threefold: (1) proving the existence of embedded fixed points (Theorem 1) where narrower network solutions are saddle points in wider networks; (2) establishing invariant manifolds (Theorem 3) that constrain the network to effectively narrower behavior; and (3) analyzing how timescale separation — driven by either data distribution (linear case) or initialization (quadratic case) — causes gradient descent trajectories to approximately follow these invariant manifolds, producing stage-like learning with plateaus. Testable predictions about the effects of width, data spectrum, and initialization on plateau structure are provided and validated on synthetic benchmarks.

The paper is ambitious in scope, aiming to unify observations across linear networks, ReLU networks, convolutional networks, quadratic networks, and linear self-attention. The fixed-point and invariant-manifold results are rigorous and broadly applicable. However, the dynamical analysis (Section 5) is restricted to two-layer networks with homogeneous polynomial activations, while the title and abstract imply broader generality. The empirical validation is limited to simplified synthetic settings with small numbers of singular values, and novelty verification against existing saddle-to-saddle literature cannot be completed in this run due to unavailable external paper search. Overall, this is a theoretically substantive paper with valuable insights, but the gap between the general theoretical claims and the specific dynamical analysis needs careful scoping, and the empirical support requires strengthening.

## Strengths
1. **Ambitious theoretical unification.** The paper attempts to provide a unified theoretical account of dynamical simplicity bias across several major architecture families (FC, conv, attention). This high-level ambition — seeking common mechanisms rather than architecture-specific explanations — is commendable and addresses an important gap in the literature.

2. **Rigorous fixed-point and invariant-manifold analysis.** Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 are presented with clear mathematical statements and proof sketches. The extension of the Fukumizu & Amari (2000) fixed-point hierarchy (cases iii-iv in Theorem 1) to activations with homogeneity or linear-additivity properties is a genuine technical contribution. The invariant manifold result (Theorem 3) connecting weight-space relationships to effective network width is elegant and provides a clear geometric picture of why simpler solutions can persist during training.

3. **Clear operational definition of simplicity.** The paper defines "simple" in terms of the number of effective units (hidden neurons, kernels, or attention heads) required to express a solution. This is concrete and architecture-aware, and it connects naturally to the invariant manifold framework: each constraint on the weights reduces the effective width by one unit. This is more operational than prior notions of simplicity in the deep learning theory literature.

4. **Distinction between data-induced and initialization-induced dynamics.** The identification of two distinct mechanisms for timescale separation — one driven by the singular value spectrum of the data (linear case) and one driven by random initialization differences (quadratic case) — is insightful and yields testably different predictions (e.g., the effect of width on plateau length differs between linear and quadratic architectures).

5. **Testable predictions.** Section 6 provides several parameterized predictions about how width, data power-law exponent, initialization structure, and initialization scale affect learning dynamics. The fact that these predictions are qualitatively confirmed in synthetic simulations (Figure 2) demonstrates that the theory has falsifiable consequences, which is a strength for a theoretical paper.

6. **Honest discussion of limitations.** Section 7 openly discusses several open questions — the exhaustiveness of fixed points, the extension to deep networks, the conditions for saddle-to-saddle dynamics, and the role of permutation symmetry in non-feedforward architectures. This intellectual honesty strengthens the paper's scientific credibility.

## Weaknesses
1. **Scope mismatch between claims and technical results (Major).** The title and Abstract claim a framework "across neural network architectures" and a "universal mechanism." However, the core dynamical analysis (Section 5) is restricted to two-layer networks where the activation function $\phi(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{u})$ is a homogeneous polynomial in the weights — specifically, linear or quadratic. The general architecture class defined by Eq. (1) is used only for the fixed-point and invariant-manifold results (Sections 3-4), which are rigorous but do not by themselves guarantee saddle-to-saddle *dynamics*. The paper acknowledges this gap for tanh networks (Section 7), stating they "probably do not have saddle-to-saddle dynamics," which directly contradicts the "universal" framing. A more precise scoping of claims — distinguishing between the geometry results (broadly applicable) and the dynamical results (proven for specific activation classes) — is essential before publication. *(See annotation on Abstract, Introduction P3)*

2. **Dynamics analysis is approximate and lacks rigorous error control (Major).** Theorem 4 analyzes the linearized system (10), which is obtained by dropping $O(\epsilon^2)$ terms from Eq. (9) under small initialization. The most interesting dynamics — saddle escape — occurs precisely when this approximation breaks down (i.e., when weights grow to $O(1)$). The paper argues that after escape, the dynamics again linearizes around the new saddle (Eq. 12), but the transition between these regimes is heuristic. No bound on the approximation error over the full trajectory is provided. Similarly, Proposition 5's analysis of quadratic dynamics uses a scalar analogy ($\dot{v}_i = v_i^2$) that does not capture the coupled vector-valued dynamics of Eq. (14), where directional alignment with the eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yZ}$ plays a critical role. *(See annotations on Theorem 4, Proposition 5)*

3. **Empirical validation is limited to simplified synthetic settings (Moderate).** All experiments use small synthetic datasets with controlled singular values (e.g., $D=3$, power-law spectra, small $H$). While this is common for theoretical papers, the paper makes strong claims about "simplicity bias across architectures" and the effects of "data distribution" — but never tests these predictions on real-world datasets (e.g., MNIST, CIFAR, or language modeling tasks). The "linear self-attention" experiments use a simplified model where softmax is removed, leaving uncertainty about whether the predicted effects (e.g., width shortening plateaus) persist in the full attention mechanism. Without real-data validation, the practical significance of the predictions remains unclear. *(See annotations on width effect, data distribution)*

4. **Lack of statistical rigor in experimental results (Moderate).** Figure 2 shows individual loss curves without error bars, confidence intervals, or multi-seed replication. Given that initialization randomness is a core driver of the quadratic-case dynamics (Proposition 5), it is essential to show that the reported curves are representative rather than cherry-picked. The claim about "to our knowledge, this regime has not previously been observed" (large low-rank initialization) requires multiple independent replications to establish reliability. *(See annotations on initialization structure)*

5. **Related-work positioning is insufficiently detailed (Moderate).** The Related Work section is a single paragraph that cites the Fukumizu & Amari line and promises more detail in Appendix A (which is removed). It does not explicitly differentiate this paper's contributions from recent saddle-to-saddle analyses: Jacot et al. (2022) analyze nested saddles in deep linear networks; Berthier (2023) and Pesme & Flammarion (2023) study saddle escape dynamics. What exactly does the current framework add that these prior works did not provide? The paper claims "invariant manifolds and dynamics" as the novel contribution, but the relationship to these prior dynamical analyses needs clear articulation. Without the Appendix, reviewers cannot verify the completeness of the literature positioning. *(See annotation on Related Work)*

6. **Self-attention formulation obscures the linearization assumption (Moderate).** Equation (2) presents standard softmax attention, but the paper's dynamical analysis applies to "linear self-attention" (softmax removed). The transition from the general formulation to the tractable linearized model is not explicitly discussed until Section 5.2, and the main text does not clarify that softmax is neither linear nor quadratic nor homogeneous — meaning the core dynamical theorems do not directly apply to standard attention without modification. This could mislead readers who are primarily interested in transformer dynamics. *(See annotation on Eq. 2)*

7. **The quadratic-case intuition relies on an oversimplified scalar analogy (Minor).** The claim that quadratic dynamics produces timescale separation is supported only by the scalar ODE $\dot{v}_i = v_i^2$ as intuition (Eqs. 15-16). The actual dynamics (Eq. 14) involves coupled evolution of $v_i$ and $\mathbf{u}_i$ through $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yZ}$. The scalar simplification suppresses the directional competition between units that could arise when multiple units align with different eigenvectors of $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{yZ}$, potentially weakening the claimed timescale separation. A more rigorous norm-based argument (e.g., showing $d\|\theta_i\|^2/dt \propto \|\theta_i\|^4$ for small $\theta_i$) would strengthen the theoretical foundation. *(See annotation on quadratic scalar analogy)*

8. **"Distance to invariant manifold" conjecture is stated without definition or validation (Minor).** Section 6 conjectures that "the distance from the initial weights to invariant manifolds associated with low effective width determines the strength of feature learning." This is potentially significant but is stated without a concrete metric or any supporting evidence. The paper does not define how to compute this distance, test its correlation with plateau duration, or separate its effect from other factors (e.g., initialization scale). Without operationalization, the conjecture is not falsifiable. *(See annotation on initialization scale)*

9. **Novelty verification cannot be completed in this run (Deferred).** Due to the unavailability of external paper search (Retrieval-Disabled Mode), novelty judgments relative to the extensive saddle-to-saddle literature (Jacot 2022, Berthier 2023, Pesme & Flammarion 2023, Fukumizu & Amari line, and recent self-attention dynamics papers) cannot be independently verified. The paper's claims about being the first to establish invariant manifolds and the data-induced vs initialization-induced distinction are plausible but require manual literature verification before final acceptance. *(See runtime retrieval status)*

10. **Certain overclaims in framing (Minor).** Phrases such as "reconstructs the network's own architecture" (Section 7) and "universal mechanism" (Introduction) overstate what the theory actually demonstrates. The learning process does not reconstruct the architecture; it progressively utilizes the available width. More careful wording would improve scientific accuracy without diminishing the paper's contributions. *(See annotation on Discussion summary)*

## Score
**Final Score: 6.5/10**

This score prioritizes research value and theoretical contribution as the primary dimensions, consistent with the scoring policy stated in the review instructions. The paper presents genuinely interesting theoretical insights (embedded fixed points, invariant manifolds, and timescale separation mechanisms) and generates testable predictions that are qualitatively confirmed in simulations. The strengths are: (i) a clean operational definition of simplicity in terms of effective units; (ii) rigorous fixed-point and invariant-manifold analysis for a broad architecture class; and (iii) an insightful distinction between data-driven and initialization-driven dynamics.

The score is tempered by the following factors: (1) a significant scope mismatch between the broad claims (universal mechanism, general class of architectures) and the actual dynamical analysis (restricted to two-layer networks with homogeneous polynomial activations); (2) the dynamical analysis relies on approximations whose error is not rigorously bounded; (3) empirical validation is entirely on small synthetic problems without real-data verification; (4) insufficient statistical rigor (no multi-seed error bars); and (5) novelty cannot be independently verified in this run due to retrieval unavailability. The weaknesses are primarily in claim scoping and empirical support, which are fixable revisions, not fatal flaws. The underlying theoretical machinery is sound and potentially impactful.

**Decision rationale:** The paper's core theoretical results (Theorems 1 and 3) are solid contributions that advance our understanding of the loss landscape geometry across architectures. The dynamical analysis (Theorems 4 and Proposition 5) provides a plausible mechanism for stage-like learning, albeit under restricted conditions. The main risk is overclaiming generality. With more precise claim scoping, quantitative plateau measures, and ideally one real-data demonstration, the paper could be significantly strengthened. The deferred novelty verification means the final assessment of contribution magnitude over prior saddle-dynamics work requires manual literature review.
