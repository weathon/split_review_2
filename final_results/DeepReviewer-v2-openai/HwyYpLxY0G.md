## Summary

This paper proposes the Aligned Scoring Rule (ASR), a method for designing proper scoring rules that align with human preferences in textual information elicitation. Building on the ElicitationGPT framework of Wu & Hartline (2024), ASR optimizes over the space of separate proper scoring rules—weighted combinations of single-dimensional proper scoring rules for each rubric dimension—to minimize the mean squared error between the scoring rule output and a reference score (instructor score or LLM-Judge score). The optimization is convex with only 6m variables (six per rubric dimension) and linear constraints, making it efficiently solvable. Experiments on two peer-grading datasets (22 assignments, 516 reviews) show that ASR substantially improves alignment (lower MSE, higher Pearson/Spearman correlation) compared to non-aligned baselines including the constant baseline and the V-shaped scoring rules used in ElicitationGPT (AV and MV).

The paper addresses a relevant and timely problem—making proper scoring rules preference-aligned rather than just truthful—and the convex optimization approach is technically sound and interpretable. However, the paper has several significant weaknesses: (1) overclaims and imprecise language in key claims (e.g., "outperforms previous methods," "high correlation" for r=0.554), (2) limited empirical evaluation with only two small-scale baselines and no comparison against directly competing LLM-grading methods, (3) a strong Know-it-or-not assumption that restricts generalizability without sufficient discussion, (4) missing reproducibility details (train/test splits, optimization procedure, regression coefficients), and (5) the absence of a Limitations section. Novelty assessment is deferred due to external literature verification being unavailable in this run (Retrieval-Disabled Mode).

## Strengths
1. **Well-motivated problem**: The paper identifies a genuine gap in proper scoring rule design—truthfulness alone does not ensure alignment with human preferences. This is a worthwhile research direction with clear practical relevance for peer grading, content evaluation, and any setting where textual reports need to be scored in a preference-aligned yet strategyproof manner.

2. **Clean and tractable optimization formulation**: Casting the alignment problem as minimizing MSE over separate proper scoring rules results in a convex optimization problem with only 6m variables and linear constraints (Program 2, Corollary 3.4). This makes the approach computationally efficient, interpretable, and guaranteed to find a global optimum—a significant advantage over black-box or non-convex alternatives.

3. **Interpretability through convexity**: The separate scoring rule formulation with learned per-dimension weights provides a natural mechanism for identifying which rubric dimensions are most important for alignment (as shown in the Appendix C case demonstration). This interpretability is valuable for understanding which aspects of textual quality the scoring rule emphasizes.

4. **Empirical improvement over non-aligned baselines**: The experimental results (Table 1) show that ASR dramatically reduces MSE compared to the constant baseline and ElicitationGPT with AV/MV scoring rules (e.g., MSE 1.73 vs 9.54 for EGPT(AV) under instructor score alignment). While the baseline comparison set is limited (see Weaknesses), the magnitude of improvement suggests the optimization is meaningfully improving alignment.

5. **Rigorous properness foundation**: By building on the Wu & Hartline (2024) reduction framework, the paper inherits provable properness guarantees and adversarial robustness (Theorems 3.2, 3.3). The paper correctly notes that optimization over separate scoring rules preserves these guarantees because weighted sums of proper scoring rules remain proper.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Overclaiming and imprecise language in key claims (Severity: Major)**

Several central claims in the paper are overstated relative to the supporting evidence. (a) The Abstract states ASR "outperforms previous methods in aligning with human preference," but the experimental comparison only includes a constant baseline and non-aligned ElicitationGPT—neither of which is a method designed for alignment. No prior alignment-optimized proper scoring rule exists in the comparison set. (b) The Introduction (line 64) claims LLM-as-Judge "often align closely with human judgments," yet Figure 3 shows only a Pearson correlation of 0.554 (R² ≈ 0.307), which is moderate at best. The paper later (line 244) repeats that there is "a high correlation" (r=0.554), conflating statistical significance with practical alignment strength. (c) The Grading with LLMs paragraph (lines 98-104) claims "more favorable results" without any direct experimental comparison against the cited embedding-similarity or direct-prompting baselines. These issues collectively reduce the paper's objectivity and scientific credibility. *Fix:* Replace "outperforms previous methods" with concrete scope-bound language (e.g., "improves alignment compared to non-aligned proper scoring rule baselines"). Downgrade "high correlation" to "moderate positive correlation." Remove or downgrade the "more favorable results" claim unless direct comparisons are added.

**W2. Limited experimental evaluation and baseline comparability (Severity: Major)**

The experimental evaluation has three related limitations. First, only two baselines are compared: a constant score and non-aligned ElicitationGPT (AV and MV). Neither is designed for alignment optimization, so the comparison primarily demonstrates that optimizing for alignment works—which is expected. The paper should compare against alternative alignment methods, such as directly prompting an LLM to output a score (the Schneider et al. approach), or using embedding similarity (Kwiatkowski et al.). Second, the Spearman correlation evaluation protocol is changed relative to Wu & Hartline (2024) (footnote 3) without a dual-reporting validation: EGPT baselines are evaluated at the individual-review level rather than the original student-average level. This could artificially deflate their performance. Third, no statistical significance tests, confidence intervals, or variance estimates are reported for any metric in Table 1. *Fix:* Add at least one LLM-grading baseline, report both evaluation protocols for Spearman correlation, and add standard errors or bootstrapped confidence intervals for all metrics.

**W3. Strong Know-it-or-not assumption with insufficient discussion of limitations (Severity: Major)**

Assumption 2.2 restricts the agent's posterior belief to {0, 1, p_i} (the prior), motivated by the observation that the dataset's textual reports express either a clear position or no information. This is a strong restriction with three significant implications that are not adequately discussed. (1) The ternary report space {0, 1, ⟂} cannot capture graded confidence (e.g., "70% likely correct"), which limits applicability to domains where agents express uncertainty. (2) The mapping ⟂→p_i is convenient for properness but may harm alignment: if a peer reviewer genuinely lacks information, being scored at the prior mean may be misaligned with human judgment. (3) The paper does not discuss whether Assumption 2.2 holds outside the specific peer-grading dataset or how the approach would be extended to continuous reports. No Limitations section exists anywhere in the manuscript. *Fix:* Add a dedicated Limitations subsection acknowledging these constraints and discussing potential extensions to continuous report spaces.

**W4. Missing reproducibility details (Severity: Major)**

Several critical implementation details are absent. (a) Train/test split methodology is not specified—is evaluation performed per-assignment, per-submission, or per-review? (b) The optimization procedure (line 211 states "gradient descent algorithm over samples") does not specify which constrained optimization technique is used (projected gradient descent? interior-point? Lagrangian methods?). The convex constraints in Program 2 are not simple box constraints. (c) The "nearly-identity linear fit" claim (line 256, Figure 4) is not backed by numerical regression coefficients, confidence intervals, or R² values. (d) The number of summary points m per assignment is not reported; this is important because it determines the dimensionality of the optimization (6m variables). (e) The LLM model version for oracles is mentioned as "Gemini-2.5 series" but without a specific version identifier, making exact reproduction impossible. *Fix:* Add all missing details to the main text or appendix: train/test split protocol, optimization algorithm with constraint handling, regression coefficients with CIs, distribution of m across assignments, and exact model identifiers.

**W5. No comparison of LLM oracle error against the non-inversion condition (Severity: Minor)**

Theorem 3.2 guarantees properness when the QA oracle is non-inverting (Definition 3.1: Pr[ŕ_i ≠ r_i | R] ≤ 1/2). However, the paper never empirically estimates this probability for the actual LLM oracle used. If the oracle inverts reports more than half the time—which is plausible for ambiguous rubric points—the properness guarantee does not apply, and ASR may not be truthful. *Fix:* Conduct a human evaluation on a subset of reviews to estimate the error rate of the QA oracle, or at minimum discuss this as an unverified condition.

**W6. Missing Limitations and broader impact discussion (Severity: Minor)**

The paper has no Limitations section and no Broader Impact or Ethics statement. For a method that involves LLM-based evaluation of peer reviews—which could have real consequences for students' grades and learning outcomes—the absence of a discussion about potential biases (LLM bias against non-native speakers, reward hacking by strategic agents, fairness across student populations) is a significant omission. *Fix:* Add a Limitations subsection covering the points in W3 above, and include a brief Broader Impact discussion addressing fairness concerns.

**W7. Introduction narrative could be sharper (Severity: Minor)**

The introduction currently has four substantial paragraphs before stating the paper's goal. The first paragraph (line 62) introduces proper scoring rules at the abstract numerical level without bridging to the textual elicitation problem. The peer-grading example (line 63) uses a numerical prediction framing (students predict homework correctness) rather than the textual review setting that the paper addresses. The narrative could be restructured to: (1) open with the textual elicitation challenge, (2) state why properness matters for LLM-based scoring, (3) identify the alignment gap, and (4) preview ASR. *Fix:* Restructure as suggested in the Introduction annotation recommendations.

## Score
**Final Score: 5.5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a well-motivated problem (aligning proper scoring rules with human preferences) and proposes a clean, convex optimization approach that is computationally efficient and interpretable. The empirical results show substantial improvement over non-aligned baselines in MSE and correlation metrics. However, the score is limited by four factors: (1) the paper's central claims are repeatedly overstated beyond what the evidence supports (e.g., "outperforms previous methods" when only non-aligned baselines are compared, calling a 0.554 correlation "high"), which reduces scientific objectivity; (2) the experimental evaluation is too narrow—only two baselines are compared, both of which are expected to perform poorly since neither was designed for alignment; (3) the strong Know-it-or-not assumption and the absence of a Limitations section leave important generalizability questions unaddressed; and (4) missing reproducibility details (train/test split, optimization algorithm, regression coefficients) prevent independent verification. Novelty assessment is deferred due to external literature verification being unavailable in this run.

The paper has clear potential with revisions. The core idea—optimizing proper scoring rules for alignment via convex MSE minimization—is solid and practically useful. With tightened claims, expanded baselines, added reproducibility details, and a candid Limitations discussion, the paper could become a stronger contribution.
