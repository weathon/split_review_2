## Summary
This paper presents AUTO-RT, a reinforcement learning framework for automatic jailbreak strategy exploration in large language models (LLMs). The core idea is to formulate red-teaming prompt generation as a sequential decision process that discovers attack strategies (abstract prompt patterns) rather than individual fixed templates. The framework introduces two technical components: (1) **Dynamic Strategy Pruning (DSP)**, which terminates redundant or inconsistent exploration branches early to focus computational budget on high-potential strategies, and (2) **Progressive Reward Tracking (PRT)**, which uses a downgraded (weaker safety-aligned) version of the target model to shape sparse binary success/fail signals into a graded reward, guided by a novel **First Inverse Rate (FIR)** metric for selecting the appropriate downgrade model. Experiments across 16 white-box and 2 black-box LLMs show that AUTO-RT consistently improves attack success rates over RL and imitation learning baselines, with absolute gains of +1.5pp to +42.0pp depending on the target model, while also improving semantic diversity and defense generalization diversity. The paper makes a solid empirical contribution to automated red-teaming, though several methodological choices and metric design decisions require clarification and additional validation.

## Strengths
1. **Novel problem formulation.** The paper makes a conceptual contribution by distinguishing between exploitability and severity in red-teaming, and formulating red-teaming as a strategy-level exploration problem rather than individual prompt optimization. This is a meaningful reframing that addresses a real limitation of existing template-based approaches.

2. **Effective technical design.** The hierarchical decomposition of the attack model into a trainable strategy generator ($AM_\theta^s$) and a fixed rephrasing model ($AM^r$) is a clever way to enable RL-based optimization at the strategy level while keeping query instantiation tractable. The combination of DSP and PRT is technically sound, with clear motivation for each component addressing specific sparse-reward challenges.

3. **Robust empirical evaluation.** The paper evaluates across 16 white-box models spanning diverse families (Llama, Mistral, Yi, Gemma, Qwen, Vicuna) and 2 black-box models (70B+ scale). The evaluation includes three complementary dimensions (effectiveness, efficiency, diversity) with multiple metrics per dimension. The ablation study isolates the contributions of DSP and PRT.

4. **Practical significance.** Automated red-teaming is an important problem for LLM safety. The paper's approach of discovering diverse, high-exploitability vulnerabilities has direct practical value for safety auditing, and the framework operates in both white-box and black-box settings with only text output required.

5. **FIR metric.** The proposed First Inverse Rate metric for selecting the downgrade model is a principled approach to balancing informativeness and alignment. The empirical demonstration that FIR-based selection consistently yields optimal attack performance is compelling.

## Weaknesses
### W1. Abstract performance claim is unanchored (Major)
The abstract states "improves success rates (by up to 16.63%)" without specifying the baseline, target model, or condition. Scanning Table 1, gains range from +1.50pp (Qwen 2.5 14B over RL) to +42.00pp (Gemma 2 2B over RL), making the 16.63% figure untraceable. **Impact:** The central quantitative claim of the paper is not verifiable from the provided data. **Fix:** Replace with a bounded statement reporting the full improvement range across models, or explicitly state the specific comparison that yields 16.63%.

### W2. Top-100 ASR metric introduces selection bias and potential test-set leakage (Major)
The primary effectiveness metric $\text{ASR}_{\text{st}}$ averages only the "top 100 strategies with the highest ASR on $\mathcal{T}_{\text{st}}$" — the *test* toxicity set is used to select which strategies to evaluate. This creates two issues: (a) the evaluation set is used for selection, which can overestimate true generalization, and (b) the metric captures only peak capability rather than average strategy quality. A method that produces 5 good and 95 poor strategies could score similarly to one producing 100 moderately effective strategies. **Impact:** The reported ASR values may not reflect the true quality of the learned strategy distribution. **Fix:** Report complementary average-over-all-strategies ASR, and clarify whether the top-100 selection uses a held-out validation set (not test set).

### W3. Reward shaping's containment assumption lacks quantitative validation (Major)
PRT relies on the assumption that the unsafe region of the target model is "fully contained within" that of the downgrade model (Figure 2). The paper states this as an empirical observation ("most cases with $R_{\text{TM}'}=0$ also yield $R_{\text{TM}}=0$") but provides no numerical containment rate. Without knowing the rate of misleading signals ($R_{\text{TM}'}=1$ but $R_{\text{TM}}=0$), the effectiveness of PRT cannot be properly evaluated. **Impact:** If the containment assumption fails even moderately, PRT could systematically waste exploration budget on strategies that only fool the weaker model. **Fix:** Report containment rates and confusion matrices for the downgrade model across different target model families.

### W4. Non-trainable rephrasing model creates an unvalidated bottleneck (Major)
The strategy rephrasing model $AM^r$ (which instantiates concrete attack queries from strategies) is not optimized and uses a frozen Vicuna-7B. The quality of the entire framework depends on $AM^r$ faithfully translating abstract strategies into effective queries, but the paper provides no validation of rephrasing quality (e.g., human evaluation, semantic consistency scores, or ablation with different $AM^r$ backbones). **Impact:** Poor rephrasing could attenuate or distort the strategy signal, reducing the effectiveness of strategy-level RL optimization. **Fix:** Add a human or automated evaluation of rephrasing fidelity, and justify the design choice to keep $AM^r$ frozen.

### W5. Sparse-reward challenges are asserted without diagnostic evidence (Major)
Section 2.3.1 attributes exploration difficulty to "overwhelming safe signals" and "heightened sparsity in strategy-level optimization" but provides no empirical diagnostics (reward histograms, constraint satisfaction rates over training, or comparisons to random exploration baselines). **Impact:** The paper's technical contributions (DSP and PRT) are motivated by these challenges, but without demonstrated evidence, the causal link between diagnosed problems and proposed solutions remains unsubstantiated. **Fix:** Add reward distribution analysis and constraint drift curves for the pure RL baseline.

### W6. Human-based comparison lacks protocol clarity (Moderate)
Table 3 compares AUTO-RT with AutoDAN, Human Templates, and Past-Tense attacks but shows a much lower AUTO-RT ASR (38.38%) than the main results table (up to 56.40%). The paper does not explain whether the model sets, toxicity sets, and evaluation protocols are identical between Table 1 and Table 3. Without this clarification, readers cannot reconcile the discrepancy. **Fix:** Clearly state the evaluation protocol differences between the two tables, or report per-model breakdowns.

### W7. Conclusion lacks specificity and omits limitations (Moderate)
The conclusion paragraph is only two sentences and uses vague language ("significantly improves") with no quantified results or boundary conditions. It presents an entirely positive summary without mentioning any limitations (downgrade model dependence, computational cost, rephrasing quality risk). **Impact:** Reduces scientific credibility and provides no guidance for future work. **Fix:** Add quantified findings, explicit limitations, and a forward-looking direction.

### W8. Penalty value for DSP is not analyzed (Minor)
DSP uses a penalty signal $C(f_i, c_i)$ with value -0.5 (from Figure 1), but the paper does not report sensitivity analysis or justify this choice. The theoretical guarantee references "sufficiently small" penalties without specifying the condition in implementation terms. **Fix:** Add penalty sensitivity analysis and clarify the relationship to reward scale.

### W9. Related work structure is suboptimal (Minor)
The related work is organized by feedback type (textual vs numerical) rather than by positioning relative to the paper's contributions (strategy-level vs prompt-level, exploitability-aware vs severity-focused). This makes it harder for readers to understand the paper's differentiated position. **Fix:** Restructure around strategy-level vs prompt-level axes.

### W10. Downgrade model construction details are underspecified (Minor)
The construction of $\mathcal{A}$ (toxic data for downgrading) is described in one sentence with no information about dataset size, filtering thresholds, quality control, or diversity checks. **Impact:** Affects reproducibility and assessment of downgrade model quality. **Fix:** Report dataset statistics, filtering criteria, and qualitative examples.

## Score
**Final Score: 6.5/10**

**Scoring rationale:** The paper addresses an important problem (automated red-teaming for LLMs) with a novel and technically sound strategy-level exploration framework. The conceptual distinction between exploitability and severity is well-motivated, and the hierarchical decomposition (strategy generator + rephrasing model) is a clever design. The empirical evaluation is broad, covering 16 white-box and 2 black-box LLMs with complementary metrics.

However, the score is tempered by several material weaknesses that reduce the current level of confidence in the results: (1) the primary ASR metric uses a top-100 selection on the test set, introducing selection bias and potential leakage; (2) the reward shaping method's core containment assumption is stated but not quantitatively validated; (3) the non-trainable rephrasing model creates an unverified bottleneck; (4) the sparse-reward challenges that motivate the technical contributions are asserted without diagnostic evidence; (5) the abstract's central performance claim is untraceable to the presented data. These issues are fixable with additional analysis and clarifications, but they currently limit the paper's evidentiary strength.

The paper would benefit from a targeted revision focused on: (a) reporting complementary all-strategy ASR, (b) quantifying containment rates for the downgrade model, (c) adding diagnostic data for the sparse-reward challenges, (d) validating the rephrasing model's quality, and (e) tightening claims in the abstract and conclusion.
