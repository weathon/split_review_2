# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper re-evaluates claims that programmatic policies (expressed in domain-specific languages) generalize better out-of-distribution (OOD) than neural policies in reinforcement learning. The authors make three main contributions: (C1) a re-evaluation showing that the reported OOD generalization gap in TORCS, KAREL, and PARKING largely stems from uncontrolled experimental confounds (reward shaping, observation design) rather than representational differences; (C2) a framework distinguishing *expressivity* (whether a policy class contains a generalizing solution) from *discoverability* (whether training can find it), and an argument that standard neural architectures fail expressivity when tasks require working memory that scales with input size; (C3) a proof-of-concept where FUNSEARCH with an LLM synthesizes a BFS program that provably generalizes OOD on a wall-sparse Karel maze.

The paper tackles an important and timely question. The re-evaluation is a valuable service to the community, and the expressivity/discoverability framework provides useful conceptual clarity. However, the paper's empirical evidence has significant gaps: (a) selection bias in the TORCS evaluation (only successful seeds are counted for generalization), (b) ambiguous comparison metrics in PARKING, (c) critical underspecification of the proof-of-concept experiment, and (d) over-claiming in the abstract and conclusion relative to what the data actually show. Additionally, the memory-capacity argument conflates theoretical universality of RNNs with practical limitations of gradient-based training, weakening the theoretical contribution. Novelty/comparison conclusions are deferred due to external retrieval being unavailable in this run.

## Strengths
1. **Important and timely research question.** The paper addresses a fundamental question in RL: whether programmatic policies truly generalize better OOD than neural policies, or whether observed advantages are artifacts of uncontrolled experimental factors. This question has significant implications for how the field designs and evaluates policy representations.

2. **Valuable negative result via re-evaluation.** The TORCS re-evaluation convincingly shows that using a cautious reward function (β=0.5) substantially improves neural OOD generalization. This demonstrates that the reported gap in prior work was partly due to reward shaping favoring the speed-insensitive programmatic policies, not inherent representational superiority. This negative result is scientifically useful.

3. **Conceptual framework (expressivity vs. discoverability).** The distinction between *expressivity* (does the policy class contain a generalizing solution?) and *discoverability* (can the search process find it?) provides a clean analytic lens for comparing representations. This framework can guide future experimental design and help researchers avoid conflating representation capacity with training success.

4. **Memory-scaling insight.** The paper correctly identifies that OOD generalization in tasks requiring instance-scaled working memory (e.g., general pathfinding) poses a fundamental expressivity challenge for fixed-capacity neural architectures. The information-theoretic argument (Ω(log |V|) bits for vertex indexing) is a principled lower bound.

5. **Honest discussion of limitations.** The discussion acknowledges that PARKING remains challenging for both representations and that discoverability is domain-dependent—this intellectual honesty strengthens the paper's credibility.

## Weaknesses
### W1 (Major) — Selection bias in TORCS evaluation undermines the headline claim
The TORCS experiment (Section 4.1, Table 1) evaluates OOD generalization for DRL (β=0.5) only on the subset of seeds that "learned how to complete laps" on the training track: 13/30 (43%) for G-TRACK-1 and 4/15 (27%) for AALBORG. The reported OOD generalization rates (76%, 69%, 100%, 100%) are conditional on this successful subset. When computed over all seeds (including those that failed to learn), the generalization rates drop to ~33% and ~27% respectively. By contrast, NDPS generalizes OOD on 3/3 seeds (100%). The paper states "neural policies matched programmatic ones in generalization" and "can match or exceed the OOD generalization of programmatic policies" (Abstract), but the unconditional comparison shows NDPS substantially outperforming DRL. This selection bias is a critical flaw that directly undermines the paper's central negative result.

**Required revision:** Report unconditional OOD generalization rates over all seeds; add a statistical comparison (e.g., binomial test or bootstrap confidence interval) between NDPS and DRL; revise all text claiming "matched" or "can match or exceed" to accurately reflect the selection bias; explicitly discuss why 57% (G-TRACK-1) and 73% (AALBORG) of DRL seeds failed to learn the training task.

### W2 (Major) — Proof-of-concept is critically underspecified
The FUNSEARCH experiment (Section 5) is the paper's key evidence that programmatic representations can express instance-scaling memory. However, it is described in only 6 lines: (a) the "wall-sparse version of KAREL's Maze (Figure 7)" is not shown or described in the provided manuscript (appendix removed); (b) only 3 synthesis runs are reported without success rate, failure cases, or sensitivity analysis; (c) the reward function for FUNSEARCH is not specified; (d) the experiment uses an LLM (Qwen 3-Coder) within FUNSEARCH, which introduces a neural component into the supposedly "programmatic" solution, blurring the claimed distinction; (e) there is no baseline comparison (e.g., does FUNSEARCH + LLM also find a neural policy implementing BFS?).

**Required revision:** Provide full details of the wall-sparse maze environment; report synthesis statistics (number of attempts, success rate, failure modes, sensitivity to hyperparameters); add a baseline comparing against FUNSEARCH finding a neural BFS implementation; discuss the implications of using an LLM within the programmatic synthesis pipeline.

### W3 (Major) — Conclusion and abstract overstate findings relative to evidence
The abstract claims neural policies "can match or exceed the OOD generalization of programmatic policies" and the conclusion states "neural policies can generalize as well as programmatic ones." These statements are too strong given the evidence: (a) in TORCS, only conditional generalization rates are reported (see W1); (b) in KAREL, the "PPO with a_{t-1}" baseline drops from 0.59 to 0.04 on HARVESTER (93% drop)—a failure case that is not discussed; (c) in PARKING, the comparison is ambiguous, with two metrics supporting different conclusions. The conclusion also omits the discoverability caveat acknowledged earlier in the paper, creating an overly optimistic narrative.

**Required revision:** Scope all summary claims to match the evidence: "In TORCS and KAREL, with specific training modifications, a subset of neural seeds achieved OOD generalization comparable to programmatic policies. These results suggest that the previously reported gap is partly attributable to experimental confounds rather than representational differences alone."

### W4 (Major) — Memory-capacity argument conflates theoretical universality with practical limitations
Section 5 claims neural networks "cannot encode algorithms whose working memory grows with the input size" due to fixed-capacity hidden states. This conflates two distinct claims: (a) *capacity*—a fixed-width RNN cannot store Ω(log N) bits for arbitrarily large N (this is correct), and (b) *expressivity*—RNNs are computationally universal given unbounded computation time (Siegelmann & Sontag, 1995), meaning they can simulate Turing machines including BFS, but may require super-polynomial time. The paper's information-theoretic Ω(log |V|) argument is more precise than the blanket "cannot encode" claim, but the paper does not consistently maintain this precision. Furthermore, the LSTM counting example (Weiss et al., 2018) illustrates a discoverability failure (gradient descent cannot learn to count precisely for large n) rather than an expressivity failure—a distinction the paper itself introduces but then blurs.

**Required revision:** Reframe the argument using the precise information-theoretic lower bound: constant-capacity architectures have bounded state, so for inputs exceeding a certain size they cannot maintain the Ω(log N) bits needed for instance-sized index structures. Distinguish clearly between the formal expressivity limitation (for unboundedly large inputs) and the practical discoverability limitation (gradient descent may fail for smaller inputs too).

### W5 (Major) — PARKING comparison is ambiguous with conflicting metrics
Section 4.3 uses two metrics: "Successful-on-100" (fraction of models solving all 100 episodes) and "Success Rate" (average fraction solved). PSM wins on the first (0.06 vs 0.00 on test) while DQN wins on the second (0.18 vs 0.16). The paper acknowledges this but does not resolve which metric is appropriate. Without a principled argument for metric choice, the PARKING experiments do not support a clear conclusion about which representation generalizes better. This is especially problematic because PARKING is presented as the benchmark that "points in the direction of benchmarks that could distinguish the generalization power" of the two representations.

**Required revision:** Choose one primary metric with justification based on the deployment scenario; or report both with a clear interpretation framework; or add a continuous metric (e.g., distance to goal) that may provide unambiguous comparison.

### W6 (Moderate) — LSTM failure claim is overstated relative to Table 2
The main text states "The LSTM agent fails not only to generalize to larger problems, but it often also fails to learn how to solve even the smaller problems." However, Table 2 shows PPO with LSTM achieves 1.00 (0.00 std) on MAZE (small) and 0.63 on TOPOFF (small). The claim "often also fails" is misleading. Only on STAIRCLIMBER (0.13) does the LSTM clearly struggle.

**Required revision:** Replace with a more precise characterization: "LSTM performance was inconsistent across tasks—strong on MAZE (1.00) but weak on STAIRCLIMBER (0.13) and TOPOFF (0.63 on small)—and it failed to generalize to 100×100 grids on most tasks."

### W7 (Moderate) — Untestable conjectures presented without appropriate caveats
Section 4.4 includes speculative statements: "We conjecture that NDPS and PROPEL would not generalize to OOD problems if they could find better optimized policies" and "a decision tree could encode the repetitive behavior needed to solve [PARKING]." These are presented as plausible extensions but are not tested. While speculation can be valuable, it should be clearly demarcated as open questions requiring future work, not interleaved with empirical findings.

**Required revision:** Move these conjectures to a "Limitations and Open Questions" section or explicitly label them as untested hypotheses.

### W8 (Moderate) — Expressivity/discoverability definitions lack operational precision
Definition 2 (expressivity) requires existence of *some* policy π in Π that generalizes OOD. Definition 3 (discoverability) requires *some* algorithm that returns such a π "within a bounded time limit." The time bound is left unspecified, making discoverability unfalsifiable—for any Π and (X,F), one could claim a discoverability failure is just a matter of waiting longer. Moreover, the paper uses these definitions primarily retrospectively (explaining prior results) rather than prospectively (predicting which representations will work for new problems), limiting their practical utility.

**Required revision:** Either (a) specify a concrete resource bound for discoverability (e.g., polynomial in problem size), or (b) explicitly acknowledge that the framework is primarily analytic/diagnostic rather than predictive, and discuss what would be needed to make it predictive.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper tackles an important and timely research question with a valuable re-evaluation of prior work. The expressivity/discoverability framework provides useful conceptual clarity. However, the empirical evidence has critical limitations: (1) the central negative result about neural policies matching programmatic performance is undermined by selection bias in the TORCS evaluation (unconditionally, NDPS generalizes on 3/3 seeds vs ~30% of DRL seeds); (2) the proof-of-concept experiment is critically underspecified with only 3 synthesis runs reported; (3) the abstract and conclusion overstate the findings relative to what the data actually show; (4) the PARKING comparison is ambiguous due to conflicting metrics; and (5) the memory-capacity argument conflates theoretical universality with practical limitations. The paper's positive contribution is more convincingly demonstrated in the KAREL experiments (where PPO with a_{t-1} achieves strong OOD generalization on 4/5 tasks) and in the conceptual framework. A major revision addressing the selection bias, proof-of-concept details, and claim scope boundaries could substantially strengthen the paper.

**Score on research value and novelty:** The paper's central question is important and the re-evaluation is novel in its systematic approach. However, the methodological issues reduce confidence in the findings. Score reflects the gap between the claimed contributions and the evidence provided.
