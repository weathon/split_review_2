# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper proposes the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework for evaluating whether concept-class annotations generated by large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) are *sufficient* — i.e., whether the generated textual concepts alone enable accurate class discrimination. The framework introduces a Class Representation Index (CRI) metric that measures class prediction accuracy using only accumulated textual concepts, comparing a *fast mode* (direct visual inference) against a *slow mode* (multi-step concept-gathering then classification). Through experiments on five datasets (CUB-200, Cars-196, Flowers-102, CIFAR-100, Caltech-101) with six LLMs, the authors find that slow-mode CRI is 25–27% lower than fast-mode CRI on fine-grained datasets, suggesting that current automated annotations capture insufficient class-discriminative semantics. They further challenge the *utility-as-proxy* assumption by showing that fused visual+concept inference achieves ≈90% CRI while concept-only inference achieves only ≈50%, indicating high downstream accuracy can coexist with insufficient annotations.

The paper addresses a timely and important question — whether automated concept annotations are truly sufficient for interpretable XAI — and proposes a novel autonomous evaluation paradigm. However, the manuscript contains a critical formula error in the CRI definition (Eq. 2), the fused-mode experiment design does not faithfully represent concept-bottleneck pipelines, and several conceptual confounds (CRI conflating concept quality with LLM reasoning ability, distractor selection bias, dual-process theory analogy without justification) weaken the central claims. The core idea is promising but needs substantial methodological tightening and more cautious interpretation before it can support the paper's stronger conclusions.

## Strengths
**1. Timely and well-motivated problem.** The paper identifies a genuine gap in the XAI literature: automated concept annotations are widely used but rarely validated for sufficiency. The motivating example (Figure 1) effectively demonstrates that a model can make correct visual predictions while failing to produce concepts that support class discrimination, highlighting a real and practically important problem.

**2. Novel autonomous evaluation paradigm.** The FSE framework is the first fully automated approach (requiring no human supervision) for assessing annotation sufficiency. The fast/slow mode comparison is a clean experimental design that isolates the contribution of textual concepts vs. raw visual input, providing an interpretable diagnostic for annotation quality.

**3. Comprehensive model coverage.** The evaluation spans six models from three families (GPT-4o, Qwen2-VL, Llama-3.2-vision) across two sizes each, giving reasonable coverage of the current LLM/VLM landscape. The consistency of the negative CRI gap across models strengthens the empirical finding.

**4. Challenge to utility-as-proxy.** The fused-mode experiment (Table 4) provides compelling preliminary evidence that downstream accuracy can be high even when concept-level reasoning is poor. This is a valuable caution for the XAI community that should encourage more direct validation of annotations rather than relying solely on end-to-end metrics.

**5. Clear exposition of limitations.** Despite the brevity of the conclusion, the paper includes an Ethics and Limitations section that acknowledges dataset scope limitations, representing good scientific practice.

## Weaknesses
The weaknesses are ordered by severity, from critical to minor.

### W1 (Critical): CRI formula error — Eq. (2) uses wrong indexing

**Evidence**: Page 4-5 - Section 4.2 (Metrics), Eq. (2). The formula states:
$$CRI(\mathcal{F}, t; \mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}, \mathcal{D}_{\text{cls}}) := 100\% \times \frac{1}{t} \sum_{i=1}^t \mathbb{1}[y_i^t = y_i]$$

The denominator is `t` (annotation step index) and the summation runs `i=1..t`. However, `D_test` is defined as `{(c_i^t, y_i^t) | t = 1, ..., T; i = 1, ..., l}` where `l` is the total number of test cases. The CRI at step `t` should be computed over all `l` test cases, not over `t` steps.

**Impact**: Every reported CRI value in Figure 3 and Tables 2-4 is potentially miscalculated. For `t=1`, CRI(1) = (1/1)*sum over i=1..1 = accuracy on a single test case. For `t=5`, CRI(5) = (1/5)*sum over i=1..5, which is accuracy over at most 5 samples — contradicting the stated use of 100 samples per dataset. The quantitative basis of all empirical results is compromised.

**Fix**: Replace Eq. (2) with `CRI(t) = 100% * (1/l) * Σ_{i=1}^{l} 1[y_i^t = y_i]`. All experiments must be re-run with the correct formula and results updated. This is a must-fix.

### W2 (Major): Fused-mode experiment does not test utility-as-proxy properly

**Evidence**: Page 8 - Section 6 (Results), Table 4 and surrounding text. The fused mode feeds both the visual image AND the textual annotation to the LLM simultaneously, calling this "rigorously simulating the end-to-end inference pipeline commonly employed by standard concept-based multimodal models."

**Problem**: This design does not enforce a concept bottleneck. In standard CBMs, the visual encoder produces concept scores, and the class predictor receives ONLY these scores — not the raw image. The fused mode here allows the model to ignore textual concepts and rely solely on visual input, effectively operating in fast mode. The high fused-mode CRI (≈90%) can be explained by the model bypassing concepts rather than by the pipeline benefiting from concept-level reasoning.

**Fix**: (a) Implement a proper concept bottleneck: extract concept scores first, then predict class from scores only. (b) Alternatively, reframe the fused-mode experiment as testing multimodal fusion rather than the utility-as-proxy assumption, and revise the conclusion accordingly.

### W3 (Major): CRI conflates concept sufficiency with LLM reasoning capability

**Evidence**: Page 3 - Section 3 (Motivation) and Page 4-5 - Section 4.2. The paper defines annotation sufficiency (Definition 3.1) as whether concepts "alone are expressive, clear, and precise enough to enable accurate inference." CRI measures whether concepts + LLM *together* achieve correct classification.

**Problem**: A low CRI could reflect (a) genuinely insufficient concepts, (b) the LLM's inability to reason from textual concepts in a forced-choice setting, or (c) poor prompt design for the slow-mode stages. The paper attributes the negative CRI gap entirely to (a) without controlling for (b) or (c).

**Fix**: (a) Acknowledge this confound explicitly. (b) Use a different LLM for CRI evaluation than for concept generation to reduce self-evaluation bias. (c) Add a control experiment where human-authored gold-standard concepts are used as input to the same CRI evaluation; if CRI remains low, the bottleneck is LLM reasoning, not concept quality.

### W4 (Major): Distractor selection via ResNet-18 introduces model-specific bias

**Evidence**: Page 5-6 - Section 5.3 (Preliminary Experiment). The Semantic Similarity Dictionary is constructed using ResNet-18 predictions, so distractor classes reflect this specific CNN's confusion patterns.

**Problem**: Different backbone architectures have different confusion patterns. CRI values may be sensitive to the choice of SSD backbone. The large cross-model variance (e.g., QwenVL2-7b: -47.43% on Car vs. Llama-3.2-11b: -9.78%) could partly reflect interactions between the SSD and each LLM's visual/textual strengths.

**Fix**: (a) Report CRI values with distractors from at least two different backbones (e.g., ResNet-50, ViT-B/16, CLIP). (b) Include a sensitivity analysis showing CRI variation across distractor sources.

### W5 (Major): Conclusion lacks quantified findings and specific future directions

**Evidence**: Page 8 - Section 7 (Conclusion). The conclusion has only two substantive sentences and does not state the key numbers (25-27% CRI gap), the contrasting common-dataset results (>90% CRI), or the main caveats.

**Fix**: Expand to three paragraphs covering: (1) framework summary, (2) key empirical findings with numbers and boundaries, (3) limitations and 1-2 specific future directions (e.g., CRI-guided iterative concept refinement, cross-modal FSE extension).

### W6 (Major): Abstract over-generalizes the empirical finding

**Evidence**: Page 1 - Abstract. "CRI dropping by over 25% on average in slow mode" is stated without noting that on common datasets (CIFAR-100, Caltech-101) the opposite trend holds (slow mode outperforms fast mode, CRI > 90%).

**Fix**: Add a boundary condition: "On three fine-grained datasets, CRI drops by over 25% on average in slow mode compared to fast mode, while on two common object-recognition datasets, slow mode achieves CRI exceeding 90%."

### W7 (Major): Slow Mode Superiority hypothesis is not critically examined

**Evidence**: Page 4 - Section 4.2 and Page 1 line 49. The hypothesis is presented as a natural expectation from dual-process theory, and the negative finding is treated as a failure of LLMs.

**Problem**: The paper never considers alternatives: (a) dual-process theory applies to humans, not LLMs; (b) the negative gap may reflect reduced information in textual concepts vs. raw pixels; (c) the five-stage prompting structure may be suboptimal for certain classes.

**Fix**: (a) Explicitly state that dual-process theory is used as an analogy, not a causal model. (b) Add an information-theoretic interpretation: textual concepts may convey less discriminative information than visual input. (c) Test alternative stage structures.

### W8 (Minor): Background formalization conflates distinct model paradigms

**Evidence**: Page 2 - Section 2 (Background). The unified pipeline (f_v -> f_c -> f_p) lumps CBMs, CLIP, and VLMs into one formalism.

**Fix**: Separate into two subsections: (1) Concept Bottleneck Models, (2) LLM/VLM-based Automated Annotation.

### W9 (Minor): Contribution list artificially inflates contributions

**Evidence**: Page 1, lines 74-76. FSE and CRI are listed as separate contributions, but CRI is a component of FSE.

**Fix**: Consolidate into two contributions: (1) FSE framework (including CRI), (2) empirical findings.

### W10 (Minor): Full numerical results not reported in accessible format

**Evidence**: Figure 3 is image-only; the full CRI matrix (6 models × 5 datasets × 6 time steps) is not available numerically.

**Fix**: Include a supplementary table with all mean ± std CRI values.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Rationale**: The paper addresses a timely and important problem — validating automated concept annotations for XAI — and proposes a novel autonomous evaluation framework (FSE/CRI) with a clean experimental protocol (fast vs. slow mode comparison). These strengths merit recognition.

However, the score is constrained by the following evidence-grounded concerns:

1. **Critical formula error in Eq. (2)** (W1): The CRI formula uses `1/t` instead of `1/l` as the denominator. This error undermines confidence in all reported quantitative results (Figure 3, Tables 2-4). Until the correct formula is applied and experiments are re-run, the paper's central empirical claims cannot be accepted at face value.

2. **Fused-mode experimental design does not support the utility-as-proxy conclusion** (W2): The fused-mode comparison does not enforce concept-bottleneck constraints, so the high fused-mode CRI can be explained by the model bypassing concepts. The paper's strongest secondary claim — that utility-as-proxy is invalid — is not properly tested.

3. **CRI confounds concept quality with LLM reasoning capability** (W3): The paper attributes low CRI entirely to annotation insufficiency, but the metric jointly measures concept quality and the LLM's ability to reason from textual concepts. This confound weakens the interpretability of the core empirical finding.

4. **Distractor selection bias** (W4): Using a single backbone (ResNet-18) for distractor selection introduces model-specific confounds whose effect on CRI values is unknown.

5. **Overclaimed conclusion and underdeveloped narrative** (W5, W6): The abstract and conclusion over-generalize the fine-grained finding without adequately caveating the common-dataset results. The conclusion lacks quantified findings and specific future directions.

The paper's core idea has merit and the empirical pattern (negative CRI gap on fine-grained data, positive gap on common data) is interesting and worth pursuing. But the methodological issues — particularly the formula error and the fused-mode confound — are significant enough that the paper cannot be accepted in its current form. A major revision addressing the formula correction, experimental redesign for the fused-mode comparison, and more cautious claim-bounding is required before the paper can be considered for publication.

**Score Interpretation**:
- 7-10: Papers with minor issues, core claims well-supported
- 5-6: Papers with interesting ideas but significant methodological concerns
- 3-4: Papers with major flaws that may be fixable
- 1-2: Papers with fatal flaws

This paper falls at 5/10: interesting and timely topic, novel framework, but the formula error and experimental design issues prevent acceptance without substantial revision.
