# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper tackles the problem of detecting illicit Bitcoin transactions in Shared Send Mixers (CoinJoin) using semi-supervised learning. The authors make three core contributions: (C1) a large-scale dataset of 163M CoinJoin transactions assembled from multiple public sources, (C2) novel forensic features — KeyLinker address clustering and Shared Send Untangling (SSU) complexity metrics — designed to capture mixing structures, and (C3) a quality-driven SSL framework showing that pseudo-labeling guided by high-fidelity features can achieve competitive classification performance (F1 ≈ 0.84) while demonstrating that noisy features like OTC can degrade results.

**Strengths:** The paper addresses a practically important problem (illicit blockchain forensics) and the dataset scale is impressive among public-domain resources. The core intuition — that feature quality matters more than pseudo-label quantity in SSL — is conceptually sound and practically relevant. The systematic ablation of feature sets across both supervised and SSL settings provides a useful empirical comparison.

**Critical weaknesses:** (1) The claimed SSL improvement over supervised baselines is marginal (+0.003 F1) and not supported by statistical significance testing or variance reporting, undermining the central thesis. (2) The data quality principle (Section 5.2) is asserted without direct validation — no experiment measures pseudo-label accuracy by SSU class or clustering heuristic. (3) The SSL pipeline is underspecified (thresholds, round count, pseudo-label volume not reported), limiting reproducibility. (4) The supervised baseline may have been disadvantaged by deliberately omitting SMOTE/ADASYN, potentially inflating the relative SSL gain. (5) Multiple claims in the abstract and conclusion ("prove," "first complete dataset," "establishing") exceed what the evidence supports.

## Strengths
1. **Practically important problem.** Detecting illicit cryptocurrency flows in CoinJoin transactions is a genuine challenge for law enforcement and financial intelligence. The paper targets a well-motivated, high-stakes application domain.

2. **Large-scale dataset.** The assembled dataset of 1.15B transactions (163M CoinJoin) is, to the best of the reviewer's knowledge, among the largest publicly described collections for this task. The integration of multiple label sources (WalletExplorer, Elliptic++, MBAL, Kaggle) provides coverage that individual sources lack.

3. **Systematic feature ablation.** Tables 2 and 3 provide an informative comparison across feature sets (DEFAULT, REUSE, CS, OTC, SSU) for three model types. The finding that adding OTC features degrades performance is a useful practical insight, and the consistent benefit of REUSE+CS+SSU across supervised and SSL settings strengthens confidence in that feature combination.

4. **Clear central intuition.** The paper's thesis — that in SSL for blockchain forensics, feature quality matters more than pseudo-label quantity — is clearly stated and conceptually persuasive. The framing reframes the discourse from "more data" to "better features," which is a valuable perspective for the community.

5. **Transparent about limitations of SSL gains.** The authors acknowledge (line 191) that "the semi-supervised phase did not produce dramatic metric gains," which is more honest than many SSL papers that overstate improvements. This candor is appreciated, though it conflicts with the stronger claims made in the abstract and conclusion.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Statistical significance not established for central claim.** [Severity: Major] The paper's main thesis — that SSL outperforms supervised learning — relies on an F1 gain of +0.003 (0.842 → 0.845 for XGBoost, Default+REUSE+CS+SSU). No variance estimates, confidence intervals, or significance tests are reported for any metric in Tables 2 or 3. For tree-based models, single-run F1 differences below 0.01 are typically within noise range. Without multi-seed replication or a paired test (McNemar's), the claimed SSL advantage cannot be assessed statistically. This affects the credibility of Contribution C3. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Abstract, Page 1 - Experiments results paragraph)*

**W2. Data quality principle is asserted without direct measurement.** [Severity: Major] Section 5.2 argues that pseudo-labels from "Simple" SSU classes and KeyLinker clustering are more reliable, but this hierarchy is never empirically validated. The paper does not measure pseudo-label accuracy against held-out ground truth, stratified by SSU class or by clustering heuristic. The OTC inclusion/exclusion test (Tables 2, 3) tests whether OTC-as-a-feature hurts model performance, not whether pseudo-labels from OTC-clustered addresses are noisier. A direct validation experiment is needed to support the "quality over quantity" thesis. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Section 5.2)*

**W3. SSL pipeline is underspecified.** [Severity: Major] The pseudo-labeling procedure (Section 5.3, Pseudo-Labeling) omits critical details: (a) number of pseudo-labeled transactions added, (b) confidence threshold or percentile used for selection, (c) number of SSL rounds (single-shot vs iterative), (d) whether pseudo-labels are regenerated after retraining. Without these details, the reported SSL results cannot be independently reproduced — a critical gap for a paper whose third contribution is an SSL framework. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Pseudo-Labeling section)*

**W4. SMOTE omission may bias the supervised baseline.** [Severity: Major] The paper deliberately skips SMOTE/ADASYN because "pseudo-labeling later introduces new positive examples." However, SMOTE addresses within-distribution class imbalance, while pseudo-labeling expands the training set. They are not substitutes. By omitting rebalancing beyond class weights, the supervised baseline may be weaker than necessary, potentially inflating the apparent SSL improvement. A controlled experiment (supervised with SMOTE) is needed to rule out experimental-design bias. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Section 5.3)*

**W5. Label noise from clustering error propagation unaddressed.** [Severity: Major] The tag propagation rule (Section 4: A ~ A' => Tag(A) = Tag(A')) assumes perfect clustering accuracy. In practice, CS and OTC heuristics have non-zero false-positive rates, and errors can propagate to millions of addresses via large clusters. The paper qualitatively acknowledges off-chain label inaccuracies but provides no quantitative analysis of clustering error rates or their impact on downstream classification. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Problem Statement)*

**W6. Overclaiming in abstract and conclusion.** [Severity: Major] The abstract states "we prove that common heuristics like OTC...introduce noise" — no proof is provided, only correlational evidence from a single dataset. The conclusion states "these findings advance blockchain forensic methodology by establishing that gradient-boosted models...provide the most robust performance" — only three model families are compared on one dataset. The phrase "first complete historical dataset" is unverifiable without a literature survey. These statements should be bounded to match the evidence. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Conclusion, Page 1 - Contribution statements)*

**W7. Related Work is a chronological list.** [Severity: Moderate] Section 3 reads as a sequential survey rather than a structured comparison organized by axes (supervision regime, feature engineering depth, evaluation scope). The paper's positioning against the most closely related methods — particularly Nerurkar (2022) SSL-GNN and Rathore et al. (2022) decision tree — is not explicitly stated as a differentiated contribution table. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Related Work)*

**W8. False positive rate not reported.** [Severity: Minor] The paper claims "low false positive rates" (line 156) but reports only precision, recall, F1, and ROC AUC. No confusion matrix or FPR is provided. Given the paper's own emphasis on analyst workload, reporting FPR at the chosen operating point is essential for practical evaluation. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Section 6.2)*

**W9. Label conflict resolution protocol undocumented.** [Severity: Minor] The paper mentions manual resolution of label conflicts but does not specify the conflict rate, resolution rules, or inter-annotator agreement. This reduces the scientific value of the dataset as a reproducible resource. *(See annotation on Page 1 - Section 5.1)*

**W10. SSL gains are practically marginal.** [Severity: Minor, acknowledged by authors] The SSL phase improves recall by at most +0.03 while reducing precision by -0.04 to -0.05. The authors acknowledge this trade-off as "acceptable for forensic analysis," but do not provide a cost-benefit analysis (e.g., how many additional analyst hours per month the false positives would require, or how many additional illicit flows would be caught). *(See annotation on Page 1 - SSL results paragraph)*

## Score
**Final Score: 5.5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper tackles a practically important problem and contributes a large-scale dataset that may be valuable to the blockchain forensics community. The systematic feature ablation across multiple models is a methodological strength, and the central intuition about feature quality is conceptually sound. However, multiple major weaknesses prevent a higher score. The core claim — that SSL outperforms supervised learning in this setting — is supported by only a +0.003 F1 gain with no statistical significance testing, making it impossible to distinguish signal from noise. The data quality principle that serves as the paper's conceptual foundation is asserted rather than validated. Critical experimental details (SSL thresholds, number of rounds, pseudo-label volume) are omitted, preventing reproducibility. Several overclaims ("prove," "establishing") in the abstract and conclusion exceed what the evidence supports. The paper would benefit from: (a) multi-seed replication with significance tests, (b) a direct pseudo-label accuracy validation, (c) a controlled SMOTE comparison, (d) full specification of the SSL pipeline, (e) toned-down claims that match the empirical evidence. With these additions, the paper's score could potentially rise to the 6-7 range, reflecting its genuine practical value and solid empirical effort once the evidentiary basis is strengthened.
