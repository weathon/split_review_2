## Summary

This paper investigates the gap between theoretical and empirical watermarking capacity in images. The authors derive geometric upper bounds on capacity under PSNR and linear robustness constraints, showing that theoretical limits exceed current practice by orders of magnitude. Through controlled experiments that strip away real-world complexities (training Video Seal on a single gray image with only a PSNR constraint), they demonstrate that state-of-the-art architectures fail to approach even simplified theoretical bounds, while simple linear or handcrafted models succeed. The paper introduces Chunky Seal, a scaled-up Video Seal that achieves 1024-bit capacity (4× improvement) at the cost of a ~90× larger embedder. The core message is that significant architectural headroom remains unexploited, and the field is far from saturating fundamental capacity limits.

The paper makes a valuable contribution by providing a geometric framework for watermarking capacity that is more accessible than traditional information-theoretic approaches, and by designing diagnostic experiments that isolate architectural from fundamental limitations. However, the central claim about the size of the "unused capacity gap" depends on heuristic robustness bounds that the paper itself acknowledges are not formally guaranteed, and Chunky Seal's extreme parameter scaling raises questions about the practical significance of the demonstrated improvement. These issues temper the strength of the paper's main conclusions.

## Strengths
**1. Novel theoretical framework bridging watermarking capacity analysis and practice.** The paper's geometric approach to capacity bounds is more accessible than classical information-theoretic derivations while still providing nontrivial upper bounds. The box-ball geometric model (cube of image space intersecting with PSNR sphere) provides intuitive visual understanding of how capacity is constrained. The bounds cover the full PSNR range (low/medium/high) and are validated against exact lattice point counting where feasible, lending credibility to the theoretical results.

**2. Elegant diagnostic experimental design.** The strategy of "bringing models to the theory" rather than "bringing theory to practice" is clever and effective. By stripping away dataset complexity, augmentations, and perceptual losses to train Video Seal on a single gray image under only a PSNR constraint, the authors cleanly isolate the cause of the capacity gap. Showing that linear models and handcrafted schemes succeed where Video Seal fails provides compelling evidence that the bottleneck is architectural, not fundamental.

**3. Honest discussion of bound limitations.** The paper is transparent about the fact that the robustness heuristic bounds (Bounds 10-12) are not guaranteed lower or upper bounds, and that the conservative Bound 13 is "extremely conservative and unrealistic." This intellectual honesty strengthens the paper's credibility and helps readers calibrate their confidence in the conclusions.

**4. Practical demonstration of scaling feasibility.** Chunky Seal, despite its efficiency limitations, demonstrates that existing architectures can be pushed to higher capacities through scaling. This provides a concrete existence proof that the current 256-bit standard is not a fundamental limit.

**5. Actionable sanity checks for future research.** The proposed sanity checks (linear scaling with image size, outperforming linear baselines, predictable drops under augmentations) provide practical guidance for the community to develop higher-capacity watermarking methods.

## Weaknesses
**1. Heuristic robustness bounds undermine the central "unused capacity" claim (Major).** The paper's headline result — that theoretical capacity exceeds current practice by orders of magnitude — depends heavily on the heuristic robustness bounds (Bounds 10-12). The paper explicitly acknowledges these bounds are not guaranteed to be valid lower or upper bounds, and can either under-approximate or over-approximate the true capacity. The only provable lower bound (Bound 13) is acknowledged as "extremely conservative and unrealistic." For the most aggressive robustness setting (Crop&Rescale 75%), Bound 13 gives only 904 bits at 42 dB for a 256×256 image — comparable to Chunky Seal's 1024-bit capacity. This means that under provable bounds, the "gap" for robust watermarking may be far narrower than the paper implies. The paper's narrative relies on heuristic bounds that could be overestimating capacity by a large margin, yet this uncertainty is underemphasized in the abstract and introduction. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 2.5 (Adding Robustness Constraints).

**2. Chunky Seal's extreme parameter inefficiency weakens the practical demonstration (Major).** Chunky Seal achieves 4× the capacity of Video Seal (1024 vs 256 bits) but requires a 90× larger embedder (1022.7M vs 11.0M parameters) and a 23× larger extractor (773.7M vs 33.0M), totaling ~1.8B parameters. The parameter efficiency is roughly 5.7×10⁻⁷ bits/parameter versus 5.8×10⁻⁶ for Video Seal — a 10× degradation. This raises the question of whether scaling is a meaningful path forward. The paper acknowledges this in the conclusion but the main section (Section 4) frames the result as strong evidence that "substantially higher capacities are within reach." In reality, it shows that extreme scaling yields modest capacity gains with prohibitive efficiency costs. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 4 (Chunky Seal).

**3. The "ball in cube" regime does not apply at practical PSNR values (Major).** The validity condition for the high-PSNR volume approximation (Bound 3) requires $\tau \geq 20\log_{10}(2\sqrt{cwh})$, which for a 256×256×3 image is approximately 59 dB. All practical watermarking operates at 40-45 dB, placing it firmly in the non-trivial intersection regime where Bound 6 (an upper bound, not an exact count) applies. The paper's frequent references to "high PSNR bounds" may mislead readers who do not check the validity ranges. The claimed capacity of ~2000 bits at 45 dB relies on an upper bound, not a tight approximation. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 2.3.2 (Ball in Cube).

**4. Controlled experiments have optimization confounds that complicate interpretation (Moderate).** The Video Seal gray-image experiment trains on a single image repeated 256,000 times per epoch for 600 epochs (153.6 million identical-image presentations). This extreme overfitting setup creates an unusual optimization landscape where the model may collapse to trivial solutions or face optimization difficulties unrelated to architectural capacity limits. The paper attributes Video Seal's failure at 1024 bits to "structural limitations," but the experiment does not isolate whether the bottleneck is representational capacity, optimization difficulty, or the interaction between the two. A linear model may succeed simply because its optimization landscape is better behaved under these extreme conditions, not because Video Seal's architecture lacks the representational capacity. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 3.1.

**5. Overclaimed generalizability of the gap analysis (Moderate).** The conclusion states that controlled experiments "show that this gap cannot be attributed to factors such as data distribution, resolution, or augmentations." This is an overstatement. The experiments demonstrate that the gap persists when these factors are removed, which rules them out as the sole explanation. However, it does not show they have no effect — each factor could independently contribute to the gap in the full setting. The relative contribution of architecture vs. data vs. augmentations remains unquantified. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 5 (Conclusion).

**6. Missing zero-error modeling assumption (Minor).** Bound 1 assumes each message maps to a unique image under zero-error decoding. In practice, error-correcting codes and bit-error tolerance could increase effective capacity beyond cwhk bits, while robustness requirements reduce it. This assumption should be stated more explicitly to avoid confusion. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 2.2 (Absolute Capacity).

**7. Data distribution analysis uses an unrealistic worst-case assumption (Minor).** The collision argument assumes all 2^10240 VQ-VAE-distinguishable images could fall within one PSNR ball, which drastically overestimates collision risk because natural images are spread far wider in pixel space than a single PSNR ball. The conclusion (negligible effect) is correct, but the reasoning could be strengthened with empirical evidence of actual pixel-space distances between natural images. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 2.6.

**8. Introduction lacks evidence for "stagnation" claim (Minor).** The claim that "many believe we are nearing the limits" is unsupported by citations or quantitative evidence of plateauing capacity over time. See annotation on Page 1 - Section 1 (Introduction).

## Score
**Final Score: 6.5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper makes a solid contribution through its geometric capacity bounds and clever diagnostic experimental design. The theoretical framework is accessible and provides useful upper bounds that advance understanding of watermarking capacity. The controlled experiments cleanly isolate architectural limitations from fundamental constraints, which is a valuable methodological contribution.

However, the score is tempered by several concerns. The central "orders of magnitude" claim relies on heuristic robustness bounds that the paper itself acknowledges are not formally guaranteed, and the only provable bound gives much smaller gaps that are comparable to what Chunky Seal already achieves. The practical demonstration (Chunky Seal) achieves only 4× capacity at the cost of ~90× more parameters, with 10× worse parameter efficiency — raising questions about the practicality of scaling as a path forward. Additionally, the theoretical high-PSNR bounds that receive the most emphasis in the narrative apply at PSNR values (>59 dB) far above where watermarking operates in practice.

The paper's value lies primarily in (a) providing a geometric capacity analysis framework that can be built upon, (b) demonstrating through controlled experiments that architectural limitations exist, and (c) motivating the need for architectural innovation. Its main weakness is that the headline claim about the magnitude of unused capacity is softer than the narrative suggests when examined carefully.
