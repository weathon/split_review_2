## Summary
This paper proposes ELMUR (External Layer Memory with Update/Rewrite), a transformer architecture augmented with layer-local external memory for long-horizon reinforcement learning under partial observability. Each transformer layer is equipped with a memory track consisting of $M$ learnable memory slots, bidirectional cross-attention for read (mem2tok) and write (tok2mem) operations, and a Least Recently Used (LRU) update mechanism that blends new content via convex interpolation with hyperparameter $\lambda$. The architecture is evaluated on three benchmarks: the synthetic T-Maze (cue retention), the robot manipulation suite MIKASA-Robo (23 visual tasks), and the diverse POPGym benchmark (48 puzzle/control tasks). ELMUR achieves 100% success on T-Maze corridors up to $10^6$ steps, ranks first on 21 of 23 MIKASA-Robo tasks, and obtains the top score on 24 of 48 POPGym tasks.

The paper has three core claimed contributions: (C1) the ELMUR architecture itself, (C2) empirical demonstration across multiple domains, and (C3) a theoretical analysis of the LRU memory dynamics. The architecture is well-motivated and the empirical results are broadly positive. However, the theoretical contribution is overstated (standard convex combination properties), several claims in the Abstract and Introduction over-extend the evidence (e.g., "nearly doubles" is supported for only one of four reported tasks), and there is an unexplained discrepancy between the main results (0.89) and the ablation baseline (1.00) on the same task. Reproducibility is limited by undefined notation ($\text{sg}$) and underspecified action head details. Statistical significance is not reported for the POPGym aggregate comparison.

## Strengths
**S1 — Well-motivated architectural design.** The core idea of augmenting every transformer layer with a dedicated, persistent external memory track is logically sound and addresses a genuine limitation of fixed-window transformers. The three-ingredient design (layer-local memory, bidirectional cross-attention, LRU-based replacement) is clean and conceptually unified. The paper's motivation — that in long-horizon POMDPs, task-relevant cues may appear thousands of steps before they are needed — is clearly articulated and practically relevant for robotics.

**S2 — Strong empirical results on multiple benchmarks.** ELMUR demonstrates compelling results across benchmarks with different modalities (synthetic states, visual observations, puzzle/control). The T-Maze result (100% success up to $10^6$ steps) is particularly striking and clearly demonstrates the effectiveness of the memory mechanism for extreme-range retention. On MIKASA-Robo, ELMUR ranks first on 21 of 23 tasks, and the TakeItBack task improvement ($0.78 \pm 0.03$ vs $0.42 \pm 0.24$) shows a meaningful practical gain for a challenging delayed-reversal manipulation task.

**S3 — Comprehensive ablation study.** The ablation study (RQ5, Figure 6, Table 3) systematically examines the effect of memory size $M$, blending factor $\lambda$, initialization scale $\sigma$, and segment configuration $(L,S)$. The finding that $M \geq N$ (memory capacity sufficient for the number of segments) is a practical design guideline. The component ablations (shared memory, no rel. bias, no LRU) convincingly demonstrate that each architectural choice contributes to performance. The test on CartPole-v1 confirming that memory does not harm MDP performance is a useful sanity check.

**S4 — Reproducibility-oriented appendices.** The paper provides pseudocode for both the layer update (Algorithm 1) and the LRU module (Algorithm 2), which is valuable for implementation. The segment-level recurrence formulation with detached memory is clearly described.

## Weaknesses
**W1 — Numerical inconsistency between Abstract and main text (Abstract/POPGym).** The Abstract claims ELMUR "outperforms baselines on more than half of the tasks" on POPGym, but the main text reports "top score on 24 of 48 tasks" — exactly half, not more than half. This is a factual error that undermines reader confidence in the reported results. (Annotation ID: d0249596)

**W2 — "Nearly doubles" performance claim overstates the evidence (Abstract/MIKASA-Robo).** The Abstract states ELMUR "nearly doubles the performance of strong baselines" on MIKASA-Robo. Examining Table 1, only TakeItBack shows a ~1.86x improvement (0.78 vs 0.42). The RememberColor tasks show more modest gains of 1.27-1.37x. While these are meaningful improvements, "nearly doubles" selectively highlights the best case and misrepresents the overall results. (Annotation ID: 43986b34)

**W3 — Ablation vs. main result discrepancy on RememberColor3-v0 (Experiments).** Table 1 reports ELMUR on RememberColor3-v0 as $0.89 \pm 0.07$ (3 runs, 100 episodes each), while Table 3 reports "Baseline ELMUR" on the same task as $1.00 \pm 0.00$. The ablation uses only 20 episodes per run vs. 100 in Table 1, but this difference is not sufficient to explain why a perfect score appears in the ablation while the main evaluation shows clear variance. Without an explanation, this discrepancy calls into question the internal consistency of the experimental pipeline. (Annotation ID: 0e3cb31a)

**W4 — Overclaimed theoretical contribution (Section 4).** The paper lists a "theoretical analysis of LRU-based memory dynamics" as a core contribution. However, Proposition 1 (exponential forgetting) is a direct algebraic expansion of the convex update equation $m_j^{t+1} = \lambda m_{\text{new}} + (1-\lambda) m_j^t$, and Proposition 2 (memory boundedness) follows immediately from the convex combination of norm-bounded vectors. These are standard properties that do not constitute novel theoretical insights. Listing them as a core contribution inflates the paper's perceived theoretical depth. (Annotation ID: 97a23d49)

**W5 — Missing statistical significance testing (POPGym results).** The aggregate POPGym improvement (10.4 vs 9.5 for RATE, a ~9.5% relative gain) is reported without any significance test. Given that many baselines score close to ELMUR (e.g., RATE at 9.5, BC-LSTM at 9.0), and that the gain on reactive tasks is negligible (9.2 vs 9.3 for DT), a paired permutation test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test is essential to establish whether the overall advantage is statistically reliable. (Annotation ID: 789aeb9d)

**W6 — Undefined notation harming reproducibility (Section 3 — Method).** The notation $\text{sg}(\cdot)$ is used in the segment-level recurrence equation but is never defined. Whether this denotes a stop-gradient operation (as commonly used in segment-level recurrence) is critical for understanding the training dynamics — without it, a reader cannot reproduce the gradient flow policy. This is a reproducibility-critical omission. (Annotation ID: 5c0647d8)

**W7 — Underspecified action head (Section 3 — Token Track).** The action head is described generically ("passed to the action head") without specifying architecture (linear/MLP), pooling strategy (last token / mean / [CLS]), or how continuous vs. discrete action spaces are handled. This level of detail is insufficient for independent reimplementation. (Annotation ID: 988852c3)

**W8 — Missing limitations and failure mode discussion (Section 7 — Conclusion).** The Conclusion recaps contributions but does not discuss when ELMUR might fail, what tasks it is unsuitable for, or what computational costs are associated with scaling the memory size $M$ or number of layers. A limitations paragraph would significantly strengthen the paper's scientific candor. (Annotation ID: 10c11b7c)

**W9 — "Contributions are twofold" but lists three items (Page 1 — Introduction).** The text reads "Our contributions are twofold:" followed by three bullet points. This basic inconsistency signals lack of final polish and may cause readers to question the paper's attention to detail. (Annotation ID: b6cb1143)

**W10 — Novelty comparison deferred (system limitation).** Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode (external paper search unavailable), novelty verification against the closest prior work (e.g., Memformer, Block-Recurrent Transformer, RATE) cannot be performed with full literature grounding in this review. The authors should independently verify that ELMUR's per-layer memory design is not anticipated by existing slot-based or recurrent-memory transformer architectures beyond those cited. The current Related Work section provides useful categorizations but would benefit from explicit side-by-side mechanism comparison tables.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper presents a well-motivated architecture (ELMUR) with strong empirical results on multiple benchmarks, particularly the extreme-range T-Maze retention task and the MIKASA-Robo manipulation suite. The architectural design is clean and the ablation study is comprehensive. However, the score is constrained by:

1. **Overclaimed impact in Abstract and Introduction** (W1, W2): The "more than half" vs "24 of 48" inconsistency and the selective "nearly doubles" framing reduce confidence in the presentation of results.
2. **Ablation discrepancy** (W3): The 0.89 vs 1.00 gap between the main evaluation and the ablation baseline on the same task raises concerns about internal consistency that must be resolved.
3. **Overstated theoretical contribution** (W4): Claiming a theoretical contribution for standard algebraic derivations inflates the paper's novelty.
4. **Missing statistical rigor** (W5): Lack of significance testing on the key POPGym aggregate comparison weakens the empirical claims.
5. **Reproducibility gaps** (W6, W7): Undefined notation and underspecified action head hinder independent implementation.

The core empirical contribution — demonstrating that per-layer external memory with LRU updates improves long-horizon POMDP performance — is valuable and the results are promising. The identified weaknesses are fixable with relatively modest revisions: (a) correcting numerical claims and overstatements, (b) resolving the ablation discrepancy with a clear explanation or re-run, (c) adding significance tests, (d) adding a limitations paragraph, and (e) filling reproducibility gaps. With these revisions, the paper could become a solid contribution to the memory-augmented policy learning literature.
