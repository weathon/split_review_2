## Summary
This paper introduces LPFQA (Long-tail Professional Forum-based QA), a benchmark for evaluating LLMs on long-tail professional knowledge across 20 academic and industrial fields. The benchmark is constructed from real technical forum discussions using a partially automated pipeline: raw forum posts are crawled, filtered, processed by an MLLM to extract QA pairs, cleaned by an LLM, and verified by human experts. The final dataset contains 505 questions (with a discrepancy: Abstract states 502) in multiple-choice and short-answer formats. The authors evaluate 12 mainstream LLMs and report scores ranging from 32.40 to 47.28 on the full set. Filtered subsets are created by removing questions that no model can answer or that all models answer correctly. Ablation studies investigate the effect of code interpreter and search tool integration.

The paper addresses a genuine gap—existing benchmarks often lack coverage of specialized, long-tail knowledge in authentic professional contexts. The pipeline-based construction approach is potentially scalable, and the evaluation across 12 models provides a useful initial snapshot. However, the manuscript has several significant weaknesses that affect its contribution: (1) the core contribution claims (fine-grained evaluation dimensions, hierarchical difficulty, authentic scenarios) are stated as design goals without operational definitions or validation evidence; (2) the benchmark filtering methodology removes informative signals and introduces selection bias; (3) the experimental analysis lacks statistical rigor (no variance, no significance tests, no controlled comparisons); (4) the ablation studies draw causal conclusions unsupported by the evidence; and (5) the Related Work section contains factual inaccuracies about cited benchmarks. The novelty of the benchmark construction pipeline and the benchmark itself cannot be fully assessed without external literature comparison (deferred due to retrieval unavailability). Overall, LPFQA represents a promising direction but requires substantial methodological strengthening before it can serve as a reliable evaluation tool.

## Strengths
1. **Addresses a genuine gap.** The focus on long-tail professional knowledge from real forum discussions is a well-motivated and timely direction. Existing benchmarks (MMLU, GPQA, HLE) tend to use decontextualized or idealized questions, while LPFQA's source material—authentic practitioner queries from technical forums—reflects a realistic demand for professional knowledge evaluation.

2. **Scalable construction pipeline.** The three-phase pipeline (data collection, automated QA generation with quality control, expert verification) demonstrates an end-to-end approach that could be extended to additional domains or languages. The use of MLLMs to process screenshots of forum discussions is a creative methodological choice that preserves contextual information.

3. **Broad domain coverage.** Covering 20 academic and industrial fields with over 500 questions provides a useful breadth for cross-disciplinary capability assessment. The field-by-field breakdown (Figure 2) and radar charts (Figure 3) allow for per-domain strength/weakness analysis.

4. **Informative ablation experiments.** The code interpreter and search-tool ablations (Tables 3 and 4) are a valuable addition that most benchmark papers do not include. The finding that tool integration does not consistently help—and often hurts—on long-tail knowledge is a non-trivial observation that could inform future research on retrieval-augmented LLM evaluation.

5. **Transparency commitment.** The Reproducibility Statement promises public release of the benchmark, prompts, and forum list. If delivered, this would enable community verification and extension, which is crucial for benchmark adoption.

## Weaknesses
### W1. Post-hoc filtering introduces selection bias and discards meaningful signal (Major)
The authors remove questions that no model can answer (yielding LPFQA⁻) and questions all models answer correctly (yielding LPFQA⁼), arguing these "provide little discriminatory value." This is methodologically problematic. Unsolvable questions define the current upper bound of LLM capability and removing them artificially inflates reported scores (average jumps from 39.08 to 44.99). Universally solvable questions test baseline competence; removing them inflates apparent discrimination. Furthermore, filtering criteria are derived from the same models being evaluated, creating circular dependency. Standard benchmark design (MMLU, GPQA) does not remove items based on evaluatee performance. *Fix:* Report full-set scores as primary and treat filtered versions as secondary analysis; use IRT weighting instead of item removal.

### W2. Core contribution claims lack operationalization and validation evidence (Major)
The four claimed innovations (fine-grained evaluation dimensions, hierarchical difficulty, authentic scenario modeling, interdisciplinary integration) are stated as design desiderata without evidence of implementation. The four evaluation dimensions (knowledge depth, reasoning, terminology, contextual analysis) have no rubric definitions, annotation guidelines, inter-annotator agreement scores, or distribution statistics. "Hierarchical difficulty" is mentioned but the actual difficulty levels, their criteria, and the resulting distribution are not reported (only "empirical difficulty test" is mentioned vaguely). Without these, the contributions are non-falsifiable. *Fix:* Provide explicit rubrics, agreement metrics, difficulty tier definitions and counts, and per-dimension question distribution in an appendix table.

### W3. Factual inaccuracy about cited benchmarks (Major)
The Introduction (line 9) describes HLE as a benchmark that "leverages human annotations to approximate human preferences." This is incorrect. HLE (Humanity's Last Exam) is a collection of expert-crafted extremely difficult questions, not a human-preference evaluation like Chatbot Arena. This error suggests a misunderstanding of the cited work and undermines credibility. The paper also omits relevant benchmarks such as GPQA (graduate-level QA) and ExpertQA, which directly address professional knowledge evaluation. *Fix:* Correct the HLE description and add discussion of GPQA/ExpertQA with explicit gap differentiation.

### W4. No statistical rigor in experimental comparison (Major)
Table 1 reports scores averaged over 3 trials without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests. With a narrow score range (32.40–47.28), differences as small as ~0.3 points (e.g., Qwen-3 38.78 vs. Claude-4 38.05) are treated as meaningful rankings. The claim of "significant performance disparities" is unsupported without statistical testing. Models also vary widely in parameter count, training data, and release date, yet no capacity-controlled comparison is attempted. *Fix:* Report mean±std, perform paired significance tests (McNemar or bootstrap), include effect sizes, and add a correlation analysis with existing benchmarks to demonstrate LPFQA measures distinct capability dimensions.

### W5. Unsupported causal claim in the code-interpreter ablation (Major)
The ablation concludes that "LPFQA primarily reflects a model's mastery of domain knowledge rather than its reasoning ability" because adding a Code Interpreter (CI) tool decreases performance. This conclusion does not follow from the evidence: CI could degrade performance due to tool-integration overhead, prompt artifacts, or the model's over-reliance on code execution for questions better answered from memory. The drop in performance does not logically imply "primarily knowledge." This also contradicts the paper's own framing (Abstract claims LPFQA evaluates "complex reasoning"). *Fix:* Replace the causal claim with a descriptive observation or add a proper decomposition experiment that categorizes questions by recall vs. reasoning requirements and tests each category separately.

### W6. Numerical inconsistency (Minor)
Abstract states "502 tasks," but the Introduction, Section 3.1, and statistics consistently report 505 questions. This 3-item discrepancy undermines trust in reported numbers. *Fix:* Unify to 505 or explain the discrepancy.

### W7. Missing reproducibility-critical pipeline details (Minor)
The construction pipeline (Section 3.2) does not specify: which MLLM and LLM were used (model, version, prompt template, sampling parameters), how many items were removed at each filtering stage, expert qualifications and number of experts, or the empirical difficulty distribution targets. *Fix:* Add a comprehensive appendix table with per-stage counts, model specifications, prompt templates, and expert details as promised in the Reproducibility Statement.

### W8. Over-interpretation of search ablation (Minor)
The conclusion that "for tasks involving long-tail knowledge, simply augmenting models with online search does not provide a positive effect and may even be detrimental" is too broad. Only one search configuration (GoogleSearch + TextBrowserView) was tested, 2 of 10 tested models showed marginal improvement, and the average drop is driven primarily by one model (Qwen-3, -15.47%). *Fix:* Qualify the conclusion to reflect the specific configuration and call for testing with alternative retrieval strategies.

### W9. Contribution bullet points are generic design goals (Minor)
The four bullet-pointed contributions are phrased as desirable properties ("innovated," "hierarchical," "authentic," "interdisciplinary") rather than as specific, falsifiable claims. The automated pipeline using MLLMs to generate QA pairs from screenshots is a genuinely interesting methodological contribution but is not highlighted as one. *Fix:* Rephrase contributions as concrete technical innovations; add the automated pipeline as a distinct contribution.

### W10. Conclusion overclaims (Minor)
The final sentence claims LPFQA "guides future research toward more generalizable and reliable LLMs," which is unsupported by the presented evidence. *Fix:* Replace with a bounded claim about the benchmark's demonstrated utility as a complementary evaluation tool.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a genuine and timely problem—evaluating LLMs on long-tail professional knowledge from authentic forum sources. The construction pipeline is creative and the breadth of domain coverage is commendable. However, the score is limited by several critical weaknesses that affect the paper's core validity and contribution clarity:

- **Research value (moderate):** The benchmark concept fills a real gap, but the lack of operationalized evaluation dimensions and questionable filtering methodology reduce its reliability as a measurement tool. The potential impact is tangible but not yet realized.
- **Novelty (deferred, see note below):** The approach of constructing QA pairs from forum discussions using MLLMs has methodological novelty, but without external literature comparison (retrieval unavailable), a full novelty assessment cannot be performed. The four claimed innovations as currently stated are generic design goals rather than specific technical novelties.
- **Validity (below threshold):** The post-hoc filtering, absence of statistical testing, and unsupported causal claims in the ablation study undermine confidence in the reported findings. The factual error about HLE raises additional credibility concerns.
- **Reproducibility (insufficient):** Critical details about the pipeline (MLLM identity, prompts, filtering thresholds, expert qualifications, difficulty criteria) are missing.

The paper has a solid foundation and the direction is worthwhile. With substantial revision—particularly operationalizing the evaluation dimensions, correcting the filtering methodology, adding statistical rigor, and providing reproducibility details—the score could improve. In its current form, the manuscript requires major revisions before it meets the standards for a reliable evaluation benchmark publication.

*Note on novelty:* External literature verification was unavailable in this run (retrieval service not available). Novelty conclusions regarding the benchmark's position relative to existing datasets (e.g., GPQA, ExpertQA, LONGTAIL benchmarks) should be verified manually by the reader or authors.
