# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper presents O-Forge, an AI-powered tool that combines a frontier large language model (LLM) with Mathematica's `Resolve` computer algebra system (CAS) to verify asymptotic inequalities of the form $f \ll g$. The core idea is to use the LLM to propose a domain decomposition (partition the domain into subdomains where the inequality simplifies), then use Mathematica's quantifier elimination to symbolically verify the inequality on each subdomain. Two case studies are presented: an inequality $xy \ll x\log x + e^y$ from Terry Tao and a series decomposition problem. The paper also reports testing on 40-50 "easier" inequalities.

The paper addresses a genuine need — many mathematical proofs require verifying routine but time-consuming asymptotic estimates, and an automated assistant could save researchers significant effort. The LLM+CAS paradigm is a practical design choice that leverages the complementary strengths of LLMs (creative decomposition proposals) and CAS (rigorous symbolic verification).

However, the paper has several significant weaknesses. The empirical evaluation lacks baseline comparisons, failure analysis, and quantitative rigor. The central novelty claim — "no existing AI tools are able to complete and symbolically verify proofs of this kind" — is phrased as a negative universal that cannot be supported without thorough literature coverage (which is unavailable in this review due to retrieval limitations). Mathematical oversights exist in the presented "trivial" proofs (Case Study 1 requires a non-trivial inequality that is not fully justified). The grid-search approach for the constant $C$ (1 to $10^4$) is computationally crude. The trust issue with Mathematica's closed-source verification is understated. The paper also contains inaccurate characterizations of related work (e.g., AlphaProof). In its current form, the paper reads as a proof-of-concept demonstration rather than a fully validated contribution.

## Strengths
1. **Practical problem with clear impact.** Automating the verification of asymptotic estimates is a genuinely useful goal for working mathematicians in analysis, number theory, and theoretical computer science. The tool could reduce hours of manual verification for routine estimates.

2. **Sensible architectural choice.** The LLM+CAS paradigm cleanly separates the creative step (domain decomposition proposed by the LLM) from the verification step (symbolic computation via Mathematica). This is more robust than asking the LLM to produce a complete proof, since the CAS provides a correctness guarantee for each subdomain.

3. **Honest engagement with limitations.** The paper acknowledges that `Resolve` does not produce independently verifiable proof objects (Section 7), which is a genuine limitation for formal mathematics. The authors also note the dependence on expensive proprietary software (Mathematica and frontier LLM APIs) as an access concern.

4. **Demonstrates feasibility on non-trivial problems.** The two case studies (especially the series decomposition) show that the approach can handle problems that are genuinely difficult for direct CAS or SMT-based verification, providing a proof of concept for the LLM+CAS pipeline.

5. **Low barrier to adoption.** The web interface (o-forge.com) and CLI make the tool accessible to mathematicians with minimal programming experience, which is a practical strength for the target audience.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Empirical evaluation is insufficient for publication venue standards (Major).** The paper reports testing on "around 40-50 easier problems" but provides no dataset specification, no baseline comparisons, no failure analysis, and no quantitative success rate. The observations (k ≤ 4, linear growth with variables, etc.) are anecdotal. For a venue like ICLR, empirical validation must include: (a) a detailed problem suite with sources and characteristics, (b) comparison with at least one baseline (e.g., CAS without LLM decomposition, or LLM-only verification), (c) success/failure rates with confidence intervals, (d) computational cost analysis, and (e) failure case dissection. Without these, the paper reads as a proof-of-concept rather than a fully validated contribution.

**W2. Overclaimed novelty framing (Major).** The statement "No existing AI tools are able to complete and symbolically verify proofs of this kind" (Section 1.1) is a negative universal claim that is vulnerable to single-counterexample refutation. The paper itself mentions Maple's QuantifierElimination and Tao's Lean-based estimates tool as related work that covers a subset of the same space. The claim should be bounded: "To our knowledge, no existing tool combines LLM-proposed domain decomposition with CAS-based symbolic verification for asymptotic inequalities involving transcendental functions." Additionally, the paper claims to be "one of the first AI-powered tools that is useful for research-level mathematics today" — this requires literature evidence that was unavailable in this review (deferred due to retrieval limitations).

**W3. Mathematical gaps in the "trivial" proof (Minor).** The proof of Case Study 1 claims that $y > 2\log x$ implies $x\log x + e^y \geq e^{y/2} e^{y/2} \geq xy$. The final step $e^y \geq xy$ requires an additional justification ($e^{y/2} \geq y$ for $y\geq 0$) that is not stated. While the inequality is correct, this oversight weakens the paper's argument that post-decomposition proofs are "trivial" — even this simple example requires non-obvious reasoning. This undermines the central rhetorical distinction between "creative decomposition" and "trivial verification."

**W4. Grid search over C is computationally crude (Major).** Step 4 searches the constant $C$ over a finite grid 1 to $10^4$ with the justification that "all examples we tested were completed for $C \leq 2$." This is post-hoc and does not generalize. Quantifier elimination is doubly exponential in the worst case, so checking 10,000 values is not a scalable strategy. A more principled approach would treat $C$ as an existential variable in the quantifier elimination query, allowing Resolve to determine feasibility directly without grid search.

**W5. Trust issue in verification is understated (Major).** The paper's value proposition is that O-Forge eliminates the need to manually check LLM-produced proofs. But the verification step itself relies on trusting Mathematica's closed-source proprietary algorithms. This merely transfers trust from one black box (LLM) to another (Mathematica). For research mathematicians who value formal proof certificates (e.g., in Lean), this is a fundamental limitation that the paper acknowledges but does not adequately problematize.

**W6. Inaccurate characterization of related work (Major).** The paper states that AlphaProof "simply took an off the shelf LLM, and were able to perform reinforcement learning on it." This is a significant oversimplification of a complex system that combined a fine-tuned Gemini model with a specialized RL environment and Lean verification. Similarly, the claim about SMT solvers being unable to prove $\log x \leq \log y \implies \exp(x) \leq \exp(y)$ is presented without experimental details (solver version, encoding strategy, timeout settings), making it unverifiable.

**W7. Implementation section is incomplete (Major).** Section 4 shows a prompt template with empty XML tags and a code snippet that does not conform to standard Mathematica syntax. The "(\*\* describe the structure of the prompt\*\*)" placeholder (line 23 of the manuscript) indicates incomplete preparation. A paper describing a tool should include the actual prompting strategy, the decomposition parsing logic, and error handling for when the LLM produces invalid or insufficient decompositions.

**W8. Conclusion lacks empirical summary (Minor).** The conclusion restates motivation and approach but does not quantify achieved results (success rate, problem count, comparison), which is standard practice for systems papers.

**W9. Access and reproducibility concerns (Minor).** The tool depends on two expensive proprietary dependencies: Mathematica (license required) and a frontier LLM API (usage costs). This limits adoption for researchers outside well-funded institutions. The ethics statement acknowledges this but does not discuss mitigation strategies (e.g., fallback to free CAS options, caching LLM responses).

**W10. Novelty and comparison deferred (Verification needed).** Due to external paper search being unavailable in this review run, all novelty and literature comparison claims could not be independently verified against the research literature. This includes: whether the specific LLM+CAS loop has been previously proposed, whether Tao's estimates tool covers overlapping functionality, and whether the "40-50 easier problems" include standard benchmarks from the AI-for-math community. Authors should provide a thorough literature comparison table in their revision.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a practical and well-motivated problem with a sensible architectural design (LLM + CAS decomposition). The two case studies demonstrate feasibility on non-trivial problems. However, the empirical evaluation is far below the standard expected for a top-tier venue — lacking baselines, quantitative success metrics, failure analysis, and dataset specification. The novelty claims are overstated and partially inaccurate, and the mathematical reasoning contains a non-trivial gap in the presented proof. The implementation description is incomplete (with author place-holders left in the text), and the trustworthiness of the verification pipeline is not adequately addressed. The core idea has potential, but the paper in its current form reads as an early-stage proof of concept rather than a mature research contribution. Major revisions to the evaluation, framing, and presentation quality are required before the paper can be considered for acceptance.

**Summary of key strengths:** practical motivation, clean architectural separation of LLM and CAS, demonstrated feasibility on two challenging problems, low adoption barrier via web interface.

**Summary of decisive weaknesses:** insufficient empirical evaluation, overclaimed novelty, mathematical gap in proof, crude constant search strategy, understated trust issue, incomplete implementation description, inaccurate related-work characterization.
