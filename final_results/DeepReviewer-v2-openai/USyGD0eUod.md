## Summary

This paper investigates whether commonly used sparse autoencoder (SAE) evaluation metrics — particularly auto-interpretability scores (AUROC via 'fuzzing' and 'detection') and standard reconstruction metrics (explained variance, cosine similarity, L1 norm) — can distinguish features learned by trained transformers from those extracted from randomly initialized ones. The authors train TopK SAEs on residual stream activations of Pythia models (70M to 6.9B parameters) under five variants: trained, re-randomized with/without embeddings, Step-0 (initialization), and a Gaussian control. Their central finding is that, for larger models (≥410M parameters), these metrics yield surprisingly similar values for trained and randomized transformers, while a Gaussian control with noisy embeddings performs at chance level. The paper proposes token distribution entropy as a diagnostic for feature "abstractness" and presents toy models suggesting that random neural networks preserve or amplify superposition in their inputs.

The paper makes a timely and important methodological contribution to mechanistic interpretability. Its strength lies in the careful experimental design across model scales and the compelling demonstration that aggregate metrics can be misleading. However, the paper has notable weaknesses: (1) the headline claim overgeneralizes by not properly acknowledging that the CE loss recovery metric *does* distinguish trained from random models; (2) the re-randomization procedure preserves second-order statistics of trained weights, creating a confound that is not discussed in Limitations; (3) the toy model's mechanistic explanation is partially confounded by ReLU-induced sparsity; and (4) several framing and precision issues in the Introduction and entropy analysis weaken the paper's actionability. Overall, this is a valuable critical contribution that should inspire better evaluation practices, but it requires revision to tighten its claims and address confounds.

## Strengths
**1. Timely and important methodological question.** The paper addresses a foundational issue in mechanistic interpretability: do current evaluation metrics actually measure what we think they measure? The sanity check of comparing trained versus randomly initialized models is well-motivated and follows established practices in interpretability research (Adebayo et al., 2020). The negative result — that auto-interpretability scores can be similar for trained and random models at larger scales — is a genuinely important caution for the field.

**2. Thorough experimental design across model scales.** The paper covers five model sizes (70M to 6.9B parameters), five model variants (trained, re-randomized incl./excl. embeddings, Step-0, control), and multiple layers per model. This systematic sweep provides strong evidence that the failure of metrics is not an artifact of a specific model size or architecture but scales with model depth and capacity. The inclusion of the Step-0 variant (true initial weights) and the Gaussian control (noise embeddings) provides meaningful baselines.

**3. Constructive diagnostic proposal.** Rather than merely criticizing existing metrics, the paper proposes token distribution entropy as a concrete diagnostic for feature "abstractness" that reveals differences between trained and randomized models that aggregate AUROC scores miss. While preliminary, this is a useful proof-of-concept that points toward a more nuanced evaluation framework.

**4. Honest limitation disclosure and mechanistic speculation.** The paper clearly acknowledges the scope of its claims, admits when conclusions are deferred to future work (the two hypotheses about superposition), and provides a Limitations section that identifies the restricted model/data scope. The toy model analysis, while imperfect, represents a good-faith effort to explain the observed phenomenon mechanistically rather than leaving it as a purely empirical observation.

**5. Reproducibility-friendly reporting.** The paper specifies the SAE architecture (TopK, R=64, k=32), training data (100M tokens from RedPajama, activation buffer size of 10M), implementation frameworks, and the specific LLM used for explanation generation (Meta-Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct-AWQ-INT4). These details support reproducibility and community follow-up.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Overgeneralized headline claim that contradicts the paper's own data (Major).**

The paper's central narrative — that "automated interpretability metrics do not distinguish trained and random transformers" — is undermined by its own results. The CE loss recovery metric (Figure 2, row 5) is explicitly described as "only mak[ing] sense for the trained variant" (Page 4 - Results), meaning it successfully distinguishes trained from randomized models. The Conclusion fails to mention this exception, leaving readers with an overly broad negative message. This is not a minor omission: the fact that some metrics (model-level behavioral ones like CE loss recovery) work while others (auto-interpretability AUROC) fail is itself an important *positive* finding that should be foregrounded, not buried. The paper should explicitly reconcile this discrepancy and frame the result as a nuanced failure of *specific* metric families rather than a blanket indictment.

**W2. Re-randomization confound: preserved second-order statistics from trained weights (Major).**

The re-randomization procedure (Page 3 - Results) matches the mean and variance of each trained weight matrix, preserving second-order statistics that may themselves encode information about learned computation. This creates a confound: if the randomized variants' activations resemble the trained model's activations in their second-order structure, then the failure of metrics to distinguish them could reflect that preserved structure rather than a fundamental limitation of the metrics. The paper acknowledges this indirectly by noting that the Step-0 variant (true random initialization with different norm scales) behaves differently, but never explicitly discusses the confound. This is a critical omission that should be addressed in the Limitations section. A simple fix is to add a shuffled-weight baseline (random permutation of weight values within each matrix, preserving the exact empirical distribution) to test whether the matched-moment Gaussianization drives the metric similarity.

**W3. Toy model's sparsification effect is partially confounded by ReLU nonlinearity (Major).**

Section 4.2's central finding — that random MLPs "sparsify" their inputs and therefore amplify superposition — may be partly driven by the ReLU activation function, which sets negative values to zero and mechanically increases apparent sparsity regardless of input distribution. The paper does not control for this by testing alternative activation functions (e.g., tanh, which preserves negative values, or GeLU, which is more common in modern transformers). The claim that random NNs "amplify superposition" is thus on shaky causal ground: it could simply reflect a thresholding artifact of ReLU. The paper should either add a control experiment with a different activation function or explicitly bound this claim as conditional on the use of ReLU. This is particularly important because the Pythia models studied in the paper use GeLU activations, not ReLU.

**W4. Entropy diagnostic under-specified and lacks statistical validation (Minor).**

The token distribution entropy proposal (Page 5 - Results) is the paper's primary constructive recommendation, but it lacks the precision needed for adoption. The definition does not specify the number of maximally activating examples used, whether the distribution is over token types or token instances, or how latent activations are aggregated. No statistical test (confidence interval, effect size, or threshold recommendation) accompanies the claim that entropy separates trained from randomized models. The relationship between entropy and AUROC is relegated to Appendix H without summary. Without an implementable protocol, the diagnostic remains a proof-of-concept rather than an actionable tool.

**W5. Introduction underspecifies the model-size dependency of the main finding (Minor).**

The Introduction and Abstract state the finding as applying "in many settings" or "under certain conditions" without explicitly stating that the failure of metrics is scale-dependent (stronger at larger model sizes, weaker for small models like Pythia-70m). This imprecision risks misleading readers. The Related Work paragraph on Bricken et al. (2023) partially addresses this by noting that one-layer transformers remain discriminable, but the Introduction itself should preview this boundary condition. The paper would benefit from stating the scale-dependency of its findings explicitly in the Abstract, as this is itself a scientifically interesting result (suggesting depth-dependent superposition amplification).

**W6. Toy model lacks formal connection to transformer attention mechanism (Minor).**

Section 4.1's argument that matrix multiplication preserves superposition is formally sound for linear transformations, but transformers also involve attention mechanisms (softmax-weighted averaging), which introduce fundamentally different nonlinearities beyond elementwise activation functions. The paper's claim that "the same intuition might extend" (Page 6 - Section 4.1) is an unsupported leap. The toy model Figure 3 is visual and qualitative, without quantitative metrics for the claimed "amplification" effect. The paper should explicitly note this limitation and discuss how attention could either amplify or disrupt superposition in ways not captured by the MLP model.

**W7. Conclusion overstates aggregate metric similarity without stating its bounds (Minor).**

The Conclusion says "these metrics — particularly aggregate auto-interpretability scores — can be surprisingly similar in both settings." This is accurate for AUROC on large models, but the paper does not include a summary table showing *which* metrics fail and *which* succeed, under *which* conditions (model size, layer depth, SAE expansion factor). A concise comparison table would significantly improve clarity and actionability. The current reliance on the large Figure 2 grid makes it difficult for readers to extract the key failure pattern at a glance.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Score rationale:** The paper addresses an important and timely methodological question with a reasonably thorough experimental design across model scales. Its core finding — that auto-interpretability AUROC scores can be similar for trained and randomly initialized transformers at larger model scales — is a valuable caution for the mechanistic interpretability community. The proposed entropy diagnostic, while preliminary, points in a useful direction.

However, the score is reduced by the following factors: (a) the paper's headline claim is overgeneralized and contradicted by its own CE loss recovery results (a significant scientific rigor concern); (b) the re-randomization procedure introduces a confound (matched second-order statistics) that is not acknowledged in the Limitations section; (c) the toy model's mechanistic explanation is partially confounded by ReLU-induced sparsity without adequate control; and (d) the constructive recommendations (entropy diagnostic) are under-specified. These issues collectively limit the paper's immediate practical impact and scientific conclusiveness.

The paper would be strengthened by: (1) a nuanced reframing that explicitly acknowledges which metrics work (CE loss recovery) and which fail (auto-interpretability AUROC), (2) additional randomization controls to bound the matched-moment confound, and (3) an activation-function control for the toy model experiments. With these revisions, the paper could make a strong contribution at the 6-7/10 level.
