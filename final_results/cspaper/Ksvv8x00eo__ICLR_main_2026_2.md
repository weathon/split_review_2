# Expected Review Outcome:
## Summary
This paper introduces CaTS-Bench, a benchmark for context-aware time series captioning and reasoning built from 11 real-world datasets. Each sample includes a numeric time-series segment, metadata, a line-plot image, and a reference caption, with most captions generated by an oracle LLM and a smaller subset revised by humans. The paper also proposes multiple-choice Q&A tasks and tailored evaluation metrics for numeric fidelity, and reports results for a range of proprietary and open-source VLMs in zero-shot and finetuned settings.

## Strengths
The paper tackles a timely and underexplored problem. Time series captioning is a natural testbed for whether language and vision-language models can move beyond fluent wording and actually reason about temporal structure and numbers. The benchmark framing is relevant and likely useful to the community.

The benchmark is meaningfully broader than prior TSC resources described in **Table 1**. In particular, the inclusion of numeric series, metadata, and visual plots together is a sensible design choice for studying multimodal grounding rather than only text generation from templated or purely synthetic inputs.

The scale and diversity are respectable for a first benchmark in this area. **Table 2** shows coverage across 11 domains with heterogeneous series lengths and contexts, which is better than a narrow single-domain evaluation. I also appreciate the temporal split protocol in Section 3.1, which at least attempts to avoid obvious train-test leakage from future values.

The paper is strongest when it stops making grand claims and simply shows where current models break. The Q&A suite is a good addition because free-form captioning alone can be too forgiving. The results in **Figure 3** and **Table 17** are useful diagnostically: plot matching is consistently difficult, and finetuning on captioning does not reliably transfer to these reasoning tasks. That is exactly the kind of negative result the field needs.

I found the visual modality ablation interesting. **Figure 4** is one of the more informative figures in the paper because it directly supports the claim that most tested VLMs underuse the plot input. The mostly small, and often negative, deltas make the paper’s central empirical point quite tangible.

The paper is generally readable, and **Figure 2** gives a clear high-level overview of the benchmark construction pipeline. For a benchmark paper with many moving parts, this figure is helpful in orienting the reader.

## Weaknesses
1. **The benchmark’s core supervision is still heavily dependent on a single oracle LLM, and the validation presented in the main paper is not enough to establish reference quality at benchmark scale.**  
   This is the central issue. The paper repeatedly argues that semi-synthetic captions are a “reliable” proxy for human-written references, but the main-paper evidence is thinner than the claims suggest. In Section 3.2, the authors report manual validation of about 2.9k captions and high factual accuracy on selected categories, but the benchmark contains 4k test examples and 20k total samples. More importantly, the checked categories are fairly narrow, mostly extrema, mean/std mentions, and coarse trend descriptors. That does not validate broader semantic properties such as whether the caption focuses on salient events, whether comparisons to “normalcy” are well-phrased, whether omission patterns are systematic, or whether the oracle injects stylistic or inferential biases.  
   This matters because the entire benchmark hinges on the assumption that generated captions are appropriate targets. If the targets over-prefer certain forms of description or certain statistics, the benchmark risks measuring alignment to oracle habits rather than genuine time-series understanding. The human-revisited subset helps, but it is only 579 samples and concentrated in a small subset of domains, per **Table 2**.

2. **The paper does not convincingly separate “time series understanding” from “following prompt priors plus metadata priors.”**  
   The baseline prompt in Section 3.3 still gives the raw numeric series and metadata, and the metadata can be highly informative. In several sample captions, the metadata includes domain, location, units, time range, and historical statistics in the oracle generation stage. Even if the evaluation prompt excludes explicit statistics, it still includes rich contextual cues. This creates a benchmark where a model can generate plausible domain-appropriate descriptions without robust visual grounding, and perhaps without deep temporal reasoning beyond coarse trend reading.  
   The authors themselves indirectly demonstrate this in Section 4.3: **Figure 4** and **Table 15** show that removing the plot often barely hurts, and can even improve scores. That is a useful empirical finding, but it also undercuts the benchmark’s multimodal claim. If the image channel is mostly ignorable for current systems, the benchmark as instantiated is closer to “time series + metadata to caption” than to a strong test of multimodal grounding.

3. **The numeric evaluation metric is underspecified and has design choices that may reward brittle behavior.**  
   In Section 3.5, the “Numeric Score” matches each reference number to the closest generated number under a 5% relative tolerance, then combines “Accuracy” and “Recall” as  
   \[
   \lambda_A \cdot \text{Accuracy} + \lambda_R \cdot \text{Recall}, \quad \lambda_A=0.3,\ \lambda_R=0.7.
   \]
   Several aspects are problematic. First, the matching is only described as “search for the closest numeric value” in the generated caption, but it is not specified whether matching is one-to-one or many-to-one. Without a one-to-one constraint, a single generated number could potentially satisfy multiple reference numbers if they are close, which would inflate scores. Second, excluding time-related numbers is mentioned, but the extraction procedure is not rigorously defined. Third, the metric strongly emphasizes recall over precision, which the authors justify, but this can still encourage spraying many approximate numbers into a caption.  
   This matters because numeric fidelity is supposed to be one of the paper’s main contributions, yet the metric definition leaves room for pathological strategies and makes it hard to assess whether reported gains reflect real improvement or metric gaming. A more careful formulation, for example explicit bipartite matching between extracted reference and candidate numbers, would make the metric much more credible.

4. **The “Statistical Inference Accuracy” metric is conceptually awkward and can bias interpretation of model behavior.**  
   Section 3.5 says captions are not penalized for omitting statistics, only for reporting wrong values. The paper states that this measures hallucination and “favors omission over incorrect numeric claims.” That is precisely the concern. A model that avoids giving means and standard deviations can look artificially safer than one that attempts more complete quantitative descriptions. The metric therefore conflates calibration, risk-aversion, and competence.  
   This matters a lot for the paper’s narrative. In **Table 4**, very high max/min scores and variable mean scores are interpreted as evidence of statistical inference ability, but under this metric these numbers are not straightforward measures of capability. They can partly reflect a model choosing not to say much. The paper does not quantify mention rates alongside correctness, so the reader cannot tell whether a high score means “often correct when attempted” or simply “rarely attempts the statistic and therefore avoids mistakes.” That missing decomposition weakens several claims in Section 4.1.

5. **Some quantitative results are difficult to interpret because the tables mix models, settings, and inconsistent behavior without enough scrutiny.**  
   **Table 3** and **Table 4** contain interesting numbers, but the analysis is sometimes too glib for what the tables actually show. For example, finetuned QwenVL appears to improve sharply on HR metrics in **Table 3**, but on SS metrics it is identical or worse than pretrained QwenVL in multiple columns, including a dramatic drop relative to other finetuned models. In **Table 4**, some finetuned models show suspiciously uneven behavior across statistics and reference types. These oddities are potentially interesting, but the main paper mostly moves past them.  
   Similarly, **Table 17** shows that finetuning on captioning often hurts Q&A performance, sometimes substantially. That suggests task misalignment, overfitting to caption style, or loss of underlying reasoning ability. The paper mentions “catastrophic forgetting” as a likely explanation, but this is speculation rather than analysis. Since these tables are central evidence, the paper should do more than list them and move on.

6. **The claims about visual grounding are plausible but still under-supported in the main paper.**  
   The visual ablation result is useful, but the paper turns it into a stronger architectural conclusion than the evidence cleanly supports. The main support is **Figure 4**, **Table 15**, and a qualitative attention discussion in Section 4.3. However, the attention analysis is only briefly summarized in the main paper, and attention-based explanations are themselves fragile proxies for actual information use. Moreover, because the prompt already includes raw numeric values, it is unsurprising that many models rely little on the plot. The ablation therefore establishes low marginal utility of the image in this setup, not necessarily an intrinsic inability of VLMs to use charts.  
   This distinction matters. The paper’s framing sometimes sounds like a general indictment of current VLMs, but the evidence is specific to a task where the image is arguably redundant with the textualized series. A stronger setup would have included settings where the series is unavailable and only the plot is shown, or plots contain information not directly repeated in the text.

7. **The Q&A benchmark construction raises concerns about benchmark targeting and distribution shift induced by filtering on one strong model.**  
   Section 3.4 states that an initial pool of 4k questions per type was filtered by removing those correctly answered by Qwen 2.5 Omni, then 460 hard questions were sampled. The authors argue this makes questions harder and not merely Qwen-specific, citing Appendix results. Even taking that claim at face value, the main paper still relies on a selection procedure anchored to a single model’s failure modes. This is not a neutral benchmark construction strategy.  
   Why this matters: filtering on one model can reshape the benchmark toward adversarial pockets that are atypical of the underlying task distribution. It may be acceptable for a challenge set, but then it should be framed explicitly as such. Instead, the paper presents the final Q&A suite as a general diagnostic benchmark. That overstates what the sampled subset represents.

8. **There are several exposition and notation issues that, while not fatal alone, accumulate and reduce confidence.**  
   A few examples. In Section 3.5, “numeric Fidelity Metrics” is oddly capitalized and the description mixes definitions with informal justification. In **Equation (3)** for ROUGE-L, the formula is oversimplified in a way that obscures the actual precision/recall-based F-measure typically used for ROUGE-L. In **Table 8**, column names contain typos such as “SimCSF” and “ROUGE-I.” In Appendix equations, the notation for the visual attention maps around **Equations (6)–(8)** is not fully explicit, and the relation between token-level decoder attention, averaged ViT attention, and the final heatmap is hand-wavy.  
   These are not deal-breakers, but for a paper whose contribution is largely benchmark and evaluation methodology, sloppiness in metric and notation presentation matters more than usual.

9. **The paper’s strongest claim, that CaTS-Bench is a “reliable and extensible foundation” for future research, is stronger than what the main-paper evidence supports.**  
   A reliable foundation requires confidence not only in scale and interesting failure cases, but also in label validity, metric robustness, and task design neutrality. Here, all three still have unresolved issues. The benchmark is promising, but calling it reliable in the strong sense is premature.

10. **The literature positioning is decent but still incomplete in how it situates the benchmark relative to broader time-series language understanding, rather than only prior captioning benchmarks.**  
   The paper discusses TSC benchmarks well in **Table 1**, but the framing stays somewhat narrow. There is limited engagement with adjacent work on direct language-based reasoning over time series, anomaly understanding, and unified multimodal time-series-language modeling. That weakens the argument that this benchmark cleanly fills the most important open gap, rather than one of several neighboring gaps.

## Questions
1. For the **Numeric Score** in Section 3.5, can the authors specify the exact matching procedure between reference and generated numbers? Is it one-to-one matching, greedy nearest-neighbor, or unconstrained many-to-one matching? A formal definition here would materially increase my confidence.

2. For **Statistical Inference Accuracy**, please report both the **mention rate** and the **conditional correctness given mention** for each statistic. Right now **Table 4** is hard to interpret because omission is not penalized. If a model gets a high score by almost never attempting the statistic, that is very different from genuinely inferring it well.

3. Can the authors quantify how much information is recoverable from metadata alone? A stronger ablation would separate: (i) numeric series only, (ii) metadata only, (iii) plot only, (iv) numeric + metadata, (v) numeric + plot, (vi) full input. This would help determine whether the benchmark is truly testing multimodal integration or mostly text-conditioned description.

4. The main paper claims the benchmark is robust to oracle style. Could the authors provide, in the main paper rather than only appendix, a more direct comparison between model rankings under semi-synthetic references and the human-revisited subset, broken down by domain? That would help assess whether the benchmark conclusions are stable beyond one caption source.

5. For the Q&A suite, please clarify whether the final 460-question subset should be interpreted as a general benchmark or as a hard challenge set. If the latter, I would encourage labeling it explicitly as such.

6. Can the authors provide a few targeted error analyses tied to **Table 17** and **Figure 3**? In particular, why does captioning finetuning hurt several Q&A tasks? Is it verbosity, instruction-following drift, or loss of numeric reasoning?

7. Since **Figure 4** suggests low image utility, can the authors test a captioning setting where the raw numeric sequence is hidden and only metadata + plot are provided? If the plot still fails to help there, the claim about weak visual grounding would become much stronger.
