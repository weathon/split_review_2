# Expected Review Outcome:
## Summary
This paper proposes Content-Aware Mamba (CAM) for learned image compression, motivated by two perceived limitations of prior Mamba-based codecs: fixed content-agnostic scan order and strict causal dependence in 1D selective scan. The method introduces Content-Adaptive Token Permutation (CTP), which clusters latent tokens and reorders them before scan, and Global-Prior Prompting (GPP), which injects cluster-derived prompts into the state-space output projection. Built on these ideas, the full codec CMIC is reported to achieve strong rate-distortion performance on Kodak, Tecnick, and CLIC, outperforming prior Mamba-based LIC models and several strong learned codecs under the reported setup.

## Strengths
1. The paper tackles a real limitation of applying 1D Mamba-style scans to images. The core motivation is sensible: a raster scan is a poor proxy for redundancy structure in images, and image compression benefits from relating semantically or statistically similar regions even when they are spatially far apart. This is explained clearly in the introduction and illustrated effectively in **Figure 1(a,b)** on **Page 2**, where the contrast between content-agnostic and content-adaptive ordering makes the intended benefit of the method easy to understand.

2. The method has a coherent two-part design. CTP addresses ordering, while GPP addresses the one-directional information bottleneck of causal scan. Even if one debates how fully GPP removes causality, the decomposition of the problem into “who should be adjacent in sequence” and “how to inject global context” is conceptually clean.

3. The empirical results are strong overall. In **Table 1** on **Page 7**, CMIC reports the best BD-rate among the listed methods on all three datasets, including clear gains over the two most relevant Mamba-based baselines, MambaVC and MambaIC. The relative improvement over MambaIC on Tecnick is especially notable, and the complexity numbers are also favorable compared with that baseline. This is not just a tiny win squeezed out by a much larger model.

4. The ablation study is reasonably informative. **Table 2** on **Page 8** shows that both CTP and GPP contribute independently, and the combination is best. This supports the claim that the two components are complementary rather than a single dominant trick carrying the full model.

5. The architecture diagrams are helpful. **Figure 2(a,b)** on **Page 4** gives a readable view of where CAM sits in the codec and how clustering, permutation, prompt generation, and selective scan are composed. For a paper with several moving parts, this figure does useful explanatory work rather than serving as decoration.

6. The paper includes several attempts to probe mechanism rather than only reporting end metrics. In particular, **Figures 7, 8, and 9** on **Pages 8-9** try to connect the proposed changes to receptive-field behavior and non-causality. Whether one fully buys ERF as evidence is debatable, but I appreciate that the authors made an effort to visualize what their model is doing.

7. The complexity discussion is a practical plus. **Table 1** and **Table 3** suggest that the content-adaptive machinery does not destroy throughput, which is important because “adaptive scan” methods often hide painful implementation costs.

## Weaknesses
1. The mathematical specification of the prompting mechanism is not fully consistent, and this matters because GPP is one of the two central claims. In **Section 3.4** on **Pages 5-6**, the paper first defines the prompt matrix as $\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d_s}$, with one prompt vector per token, generated by $\mathbf{P}=\Gamma\mathbf{U}$. Then the paper writes
\[
\mathbf{O}_i = (\mathbf{C} + \mathbf{P}) \mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{x}_i.
\]
This is dimensionally unclear as written. If $\mathbf{C}$ is an output projection matrix and $\mathbf{P}$ is token-indexed, then the notation should be something like
\[
\mathbf{O}_i = (\mathbf{C}_i + \mathbf{P}_i)\mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{x}_i
\]
or
\[
\mathbf{O}_i = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{h}_i + \mathrm{diag}(\mathbf{p}_i)\mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{x}_i,
\]
depending on whether $\mathbf{p}_i$ is an additive row-vector bias in output space or a per-token modulation of the readout. As currently written, the core equation conflates a global matrix with a sequence-level tensor. This is not a cosmetic notation issue, it affects whether GPP is actually a valid state-space layer modification and how much expressivity it introduces.

2. The paper somewhat overstates what GPP achieves with respect to causality. The text repeatedly says that GPP “relaxes” or “mitigates” strict causality, and **Figure 9** on **Page 9** is used to argue that the model can “see beyond” the causal scan. But mathematically, the recurrence remains
\[
\mathbf{h}_i = \hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{h}_{i-1} + \hat{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{x}_i,
\]
so the hidden state update is still strictly sequential. Injecting a prompt into the readout $\mathbf{O}_i$ changes the emitted output, not the causal dependency structure of $\mathbf{h}_i$ itself, unless additional prompt-dependent terms enter the transition. That means the method introduces global conditioning, not true non-causal state propagation. This distinction matters because the paper frames GPP as solving a structural mismatch between causal Mamba and non-causal image data. The mechanism appears weaker than the rhetoric.

3. The clustering and permutation stage is non-differentiable and potentially brittle, but the main paper does not analyze this deeply enough. **Section 3.3** on **Pages 4-5** says assignments are produced by K-means-like updates with EMA-smoothed codebooks, while sorting/permutation is applied to the sequence. This makes the forward path depend on hard assignments and stable sorting, yet the paper does not state clearly what gradient estimator is used through token assignment and permutation, or whether gradients simply bypass these operations. The appendix mentions biased gradients, but in the main paper the training objective remains underspecified. Since CTP is a core contribution, the absence of a precise treatment of optimization through
\[
g_i = \arg\min_j \mathrm{Distance}_{i,j}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}_{\pi(\cdot)}
\]
is a substantive methodological gap, not just an implementation detail.

4. The claimed linear-complexity story is a bit too polished, while the practical overhead is only partially accounted for. The paper emphasizes preserving Mamba’s efficiency, but content-adaptive permutation requires token-to-centroid similarity computation, assignment, stable sorting, inverse permutation caching, and prompt lookup. These operations are not free, and some of them are bandwidth-dominated rather than compute-dominated. **Table 3** on **Page 8** reports only training throughput for a few models and a small drop from 23.19 to 22.05 samples/s for the authors’ own ablation, but this does not fully characterize the runtime cost of adaptive scan construction. Similarly, **Table 1** on **Page 7** reports favorable latency, yet there is no component-wise latency breakdown showing how much time is spent in clustering/permutation versus actual selective scan. If the paper’s pitch is “content-aware without sacrificing efficiency,” this missing breakdown matters a lot.

5. The experimental comparisons, while broad, leave some fairness questions unresolved. In **Table 1**, CMIC is compared against many strong codecs, but the setup details for baseline reproduction are thin in the main paper. It is not always obvious whether all models are evaluated at matched operating points, with the same distortion metric regime, and under identical latency measurement pipelines. For example, some listed models appear optimized for MSE only, some may have different entropy models or different parameter scales, and FTIC is reported with “>10” latency, which makes it look artificially dominated on efficiency without enough explanation in the main paper. A benchmark table this influential needs tighter experimental protocol disclosure.

6. The novelty relative to adjacent content-adaptive or clustering-based compression ideas is somewhat narrower than the paper suggests. The authors do cite clustering-based LIC and Mamba-based LIC, which is good, but the actual technical delta feels more like a targeted adaptation of known ingredients, clustering/reordering, codebook-style assignments, and prompt-conditioned state-space output, to the Mamba compression setting. I do not think the work is trivial, but the paper occasionally sells the method as if it resolves a fundamentally new principle, whereas much of the contribution is in a careful engineering synthesis. For ICLR main track, that is acceptable only if the evidence is airtight. Here the evidence is good, but not quite airtight.

7. Some key choices are justified empirically only in a limited way. **Table 6** on **Page 10** varies only the number of clusters $K \in \{32,64,128\}$ and only on Kodak. That is a narrow sensitivity analysis for a mechanism whose behavior likely depends on image resolution, feature dimension, and data heterogeneity. Likewise, the cluster activation statistics in **Table 5** are interesting, but they do not reveal whether performance degrades on unusual images that activate very few or very many clusters. Since the core claim is content adaptivity, stronger robustness analysis would have made the paper much more convincing.

8. The paper leans heavily on ERF visualizations as mechanistic evidence, but the interpretation is sometimes stronger than what the figures warrant. **Figure 7** on **Page 8** and **Figure 8** on **Page 8** are visually appealing, and **Figure 9** on **Page 9** is central to the “non-causality” story. But ERF is an indirect diagnostic, not a direct measurement of redundancy modeling or entropy reduction. The paper uses darker/larger regions to argue more global and more semantically aligned dependency capture, yet there is no quantitative correlation shown between ERF properties and actual coding gain. In other words, the figures are suggestive, not dispositive. The authors should be more careful not to oversell them.

9. There are several presentation-level inaccuracies and notation slips that reduce confidence. In **Section 3.1** on **Page 3**, the paper states that the analysis transform $g_a$ encodes an input image into latent features $\mathbf{y}=g_s(\mathbf{x};\theta_s)$, which is clearly inconsistent and appears to swap $g_a$ and $g_s$. The rate term is also written at scalar level as $p_{\hat y}(\hat y \mid \mu,\sigma,\varphi)$ rather than more consistently in vectorized latent notation. None of these alone is fatal, but together they create friction in a paper whose main selling point depends on custom architecture equations being precisely understood.

10. The contribution to entropy modeling appears limited, and the main paper admits as much. On **Page 8**, the authors state that adding CAM to the entropy model yields negligible gains while increasing latency. This is honest, but it also means the proposed architectural idea is narrower than the paper’s framing around “content-aware Mamba for learned image compression” might imply. In practice, most of the gain seems to come from improving transform networks, not from a broader rethinking of the full codec pipeline.

## Questions
1. Please clarify the exact dimensions and token-wise application of the prompting equation in **Section 3.4**. What is the precise form of $\mathbf{O}_i = (\mathbf{C}+\mathbf{P})\mathbf{h}_i + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{x}_i$? Is $\mathbf{P}$ intended to mean a per-token matrix $\mathbf{P}_i$, a vector bias, or a diagonal modulation of the readout? A clean tensor-shape specification would substantially increase my confidence.

2. Can the authors explicitly explain how gradients are handled through hard cluster assignment and sequence permutation in **Section 3.3**? If assignments and sorting are non-differentiable, what parameters receive gradient from the RD loss, and which parts are updated only through EMA or non-gradient statistics? This is central to understanding optimization stability.

3. The paper claims that GPP mitigates causality. Can the authors provide a more precise statement of what is and is not made non-causal? For example, does GPP only condition the readout, or does it alter the recurrent state transition in any implementation detail omitted from the paper? If it only affects the readout, I think the claim should be toned down from “non-causal” to “globally conditioned causal scan.”

4. A more detailed runtime decomposition would help. How much of the inference time in **Table 1** is spent on clustering, sorting, inverse permutation, and prompt construction versus standard convolutions/attention/SSM operations? If these costs are small, a component-wise table would strengthen the efficiency claim.

5. Could the authors provide sensitivity results for $K$, clustering iterations $T$, and EMA decay on more than just Kodak? Even a compact table on Tecnick or CLIC would help establish whether the clustering mechanism is robust rather than narrowly tuned.

6. In **Table 4** on **Page 9**, CAM beats Conv Block, 2D Mamba, Attention-only, and CAM-only. Could the authors clarify whether all these variants were trained with matched parameter budgets and identical entropy models? This matters because the observed gain is not huge relative to some alternatives, especially 2D Mamba.

7. The paper would benefit from a more direct coding-oriented analysis of what CTP improves. Can the authors report, for example, entropy of latents before and after the transform, or some statistic showing that the reordered sequence genuinely produces more compressible intermediate representations, instead of relying mainly on ERF visualizations?
