# Expected Review Outcome:
## Summary
This paper revisits Unified Speech Recognition (USR), a semi-supervised framework for jointly training one model for ASR, VSR, and AVSR, and identifies two practical weaknesses in the original formulation: expensive autoregressive pseudo-labelling and fragility of the attention branch under distribution shift. The proposed USR 2.0 replaces autoregressive teacher decoding with CTC-driven teacher forcing, adds a mixed sampling scheme that alternates between CTC-driven and AR modes, and reports improved efficiency, stronger out-of-distribution robustness, and better or comparable in-distribution performance across LRS3, LRS2, WildVSR, LibriSpeech, AVSpeech, and noise/length-shifted settings.

## Strengths
1. The paper addresses a real bottleneck in iterative self-training. The core motivation is well articulated and supported by evidence. In **Figure 1**, the right panel shows a large decoding speed gap between CTC and autoregressive decoding, and the left panel shows the practical robustness gap between CTC and attention decoding under length/domain shift. This makes the problem statement concrete rather than hypothetical.

2. The proposed change is simple and well integrated with the existing USR framework. Replacing AR pseudo-labelling with CTC-driven teacher forcing is conceptually clean, and the paper does a good job explaining why the usual objection, namely lack of sequence-level coherence, is less damaging in this matched teacher-student pseudo-labelling setting than it would be at inference time.

3. The empirical section is broad and mostly convincing. The authors do not limit themselves to in-distribution LRS3 numbers, but test robustness to long utterances (**Figure 3**), additive noise (**Table 1**), and cross-dataset transfer (**Table 3**). This is important because the claimed contribution is specifically about robustness of pseudo-labelling under distribution shift, so the evaluation matches the claim.

4. The paper provides a fairly strong ablation story around the central design choices. **Table 4** is especially useful because it tests which pseudo-label types should supervise which branch in CTC-driven versus AR mode, and the trends are informative rather than decorative. In particular, the OOD gap in Table 4 supports the authors’ claim that injecting CTC supervision into the decoder matters for robustness.

5. The reported efficiency gain is meaningful, not marginal. The training-time comparison in **Figure 5** suggests that USR 2.0 is not just a small accuracy tweak, but also improves wall-clock practicality. For a self-training method that regenerates pseudo-labels online, this matters a lot.

6. The paper is generally well written. **Figure 2** is helpful in clarifying the distinction between USR, CTC-driven mode, and AR mode. The diagram makes it easier to understand what is generated by the teacher, what is predicted by the student, and where the coupling between CTC and attention branches is introduced.

7. The in-distribution comparison in **Table 2** is solid overall. The method consistently matches or improves over USR and outperforms several modality-specific self-supervised baselines while using shared parameters across tasks. The gains are particularly notable for VSR in the Base+/Large settings, which is where pseudo-labelling quality should matter more.

## Weaknesses
1. The central conceptual claim, that global incoherence of CTC-driven attention pseudo-labels “does not hinder learning,” is argued more by intuition than by a sufficiently sharp analysis. This is discussed around **Section 4.1, “Global coherence”**, especially the paragraph following **Equation (4)**. The paper says matched conditioning between teacher and student makes the incoherence acceptable, but this remains partly hand-wavy. There is an important distinction between “token-wise supervision is well defined” and “the resulting supervision is equally useful for learning a decoder that will later run autoregressively.” The mixed sampling heuristic is an empirical patch for that mismatch, but the paper stops short of quantifying how severe the mismatch really is beyond aggregate performance curves. A more direct analysis of how often CTC-driven pseudo-labels are locally correct but globally inconsistent, and how that correlates with downstream gains, would have strengthened the central scientific claim.

2. The loss formulation in **Equations (5) and (6)** is underexplained, and this matters because it is the heart of the method. In CTC-driven mode, the decoder loss is
\[
\mathcal{L}^{\text{Att},m}=0.5\,\mathrm{CE}(\hat{y}^{\text{Att},m},\tilde{y}^{\text{Att}})+0.5\,\mathrm{CE}(\hat{y}^{\text{Att},m},\tilde{y}^{\text{CTC}}),
\]
while in AR mode the CTC branch gets dual supervision via
\[
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CTC},m} = 0.5\mathrm{CTC}(\hat{y}^{\mathrm{CTC},m},\hat{y}^{\mathrm{CTC}}) + 0.5\mathrm{CTC}(\hat{y}^{\mathrm{CTC},m},\hat{y}^{\mathrm{Att}}).
\]
The paper later ablates the 0.5/0.5 choice in **Table 10**, which is good, but the main text still does not justify why these branches should receive symmetric weighting, nor why the same weighting should be sensible across ASR, VSR, and AVSR. More importantly, the notation obscures whether confidence masking is applied independently to each target term when one pseudo-label stream is low confidence and the other is not. Appendix B.2 says the losses are “similarly masked,” but the exact masking operator for the mixed losses is not spelled out. Since low-quality pseudo-labels are a core failure mode in self-training, this omission is not cosmetic.

3. The comparison against the original USR is not always as clean as it could be when the paper makes stronger scaling claims. In the main paper, **Table 2** is reasonably controlled for Base/Base+/Large settings, but the strongest headline result on the Huge model is effectively presented only for USR 2.0, without a same-scale USR reference in the main paper. This weakens the claim that the improvement scales cleanly with model/data size, because at that point one cannot disentangle the effect of the new pseudo-labelling strategy from the effect of substantially larger data and capacity. The paper partly acknowledges this in the text, but the comparison remains incomplete for the strongest regime.

4. Some OOD evaluations rely on automatically transcribed targets from Whisper, which is understandable pragmatically, but the implications are not examined carefully enough. This affects the VoxCeleb2 long-utterance analysis in **Section 5.1** and the AVSpeech evaluation in **Table 3**. If the reference transcripts themselves have domain-dependent errors, especially on noisy or accented speech, the measured WER can confound model robustness with pseudo-ground-truth quality. The paper calls Whisper an “oracle” in Section 5.1, which is too strong. This does not invalidate the trends, but it does reduce confidence in the absolute robustness numbers and should have been discussed more carefully.

5. The novelty is meaningful but somewhat narrow. This is not “just engineering,” but it is still best understood as a targeted redesign of pseudo-labelling inside the existing USR student-teacher framework rather than a broader new learning principle. The paper is strongest as a systems-method paper for unified speech recognition, less so as a generally reusable representation-learning advance. The conclusion gestures at extensions to handwriting, music transcription, and biological sequences, but the paper itself gives no evidence beyond speech, and even within speech it is specialized to settings where input-output order is monotonic and a CTC branch already exists. That limitation should have been stated more sharply in the main contribution framing.

6. The exposition around the pseudo-label notation is occasionally confusing and could be cleaned up. For instance, in **Section 3** the text introduces \(\hat{y}^{\text{AR}}\) in prose but **Equation (1)** uses \(\hat{y}^{\text{Att}}\), and later Sections 4.1 and 4.2 mix \(\tilde{y}\) and \(\hat{y}\) conventions across teacher outputs and student predictions. The intent is recoverable, especially with **Figure 2**, but this is exactly the kind of notation drift that makes a new loss design harder to verify. Similarly, the “Aligned targets” discussion after **Equation (4)** argues that equal sequence lengths enable simultaneous decoder prediction, but the paper should explicitly state whether an EOS token is included in both streams and how padding/masking is handled in batched training.

7. Some of the strongest causal claims are still slightly ahead of the evidence. For example, the paper argues in **Section 5.1** that USR’s degradation on long utterances is “likely due to compounded autoregressive errors,” and that USR 2.0’s stability is due to “integrated CTC and attention supervision.” These are plausible explanations, but the evidence is mainly comparative rather than causal. **Figure 3a** and **Figure 6** do show a striking robustness gap, especially for attention-only decoding, but other factors are also changing, including the nature of teacher targets and the degree of branch coupling. The paper would benefit from a more surgical experiment separating “CTC-conditioned teacher forcing” from “dual-target supervision” from “mixed sampling,” instead of mostly bundling them.

8. There are also some presentation quality issues in the references and formatting that are minor individually but noticeable for a conference paper. Several references on **Pages 13–15** are malformed, with author names rendered incorrectly as initials plus “Y. K. Lin,” and there is an obvious line break issue in the “demon strating” sentence on **Page 9**. These do not affect the technical content, but they do give the paper a somewhat less polished feel than the main narrative deserves.

## Questions
1. For the mixed losses in **Equations (5) and (6)**, how exactly is confidence filtering applied when only one of the two pseudo-label streams is reliable? Is the mask computed per target stream and then applied termwise, or is there a single combined mask? A precise formula here would increase my confidence in the training objective.

2. The central argument in **Section 4.1** is that sequence-level incoherence of \(\tilde{y}^{\text{Att}}\) is acceptable because teacher and student share conditioning. Can the authors quantify this phenomenon more directly, for example by reporting the fraction of CTC-driven attention pseudo-labels that are not valid autoregressive continuations, or by correlating inconsistency severity with downstream performance? This would make the main intuition more convincing.

3. In **Table 2**, the improvements over USR are sometimes modest in the smallest setting, especially for AVSR/ASR, while much larger for VSR and for larger-data regimes. Can the authors clarify whether the method primarily helps when pseudo-label quality is the main bottleneck, and whether they observed diminishing returns once audio cues are already strong?

4. For the strongest scaled results, can the authors comment more explicitly on what portion of the gain seems attributable to the method versus the increased amount of labelled/unlabelled data and model size? Even if training a Huge USR baseline is expensive, a careful discussion of this confound would help calibrate the contribution.

5. In **Section 5.1**, Whisper transcriptions are used as reference labels for VoxCeleb2 and AVSpeech-style OOD evaluation. Do the authors have any estimate of the label noise in these references, or any sanity check on a manually verified subset? This matters for interpreting absolute WER differences.

6. The method alternates CTC-driven and AR mode with probability 0.5 by default. **Figure 4** suggests a tradeoff between ID performance, OOD robustness, and training time. Did the authors try adaptive schedules based on teacher confidence or sequence length, rather than a fixed Bernoulli mix? That seems more aligned with the proposed robustness motivation.

## Details Of Ethics Concerns
The concerns are not due to misconduct in the paper, but to the application domain and deployment implications. In **Broader Impact (Pages 28–29)**, the authors themselves note possible misuse for surveillance or covert monitoring through video feeds. That is a real privacy and safety concern for audiovisual speech recognition systems, especially in unconstrained in-the-wild settings such as AVSpeech and VoxCeleb2.

There are also fairness concerns. The datasets described in **Appendix A.1** are large, web-collected, and likely demographically imbalanced across accent, ethnicity, age, and recording conditions. The paper mentions this risk in the broader impact section, but it does not provide any subgroup analysis. Since the contribution is specifically about robustness under distribution shift, it would be valuable to know whether the gains are uniform across demographic groups or concentrated on the most represented populations.
