# Expected Review Outcome:
## Summary
This paper introduces the Open Proof Corpus (OPC), a dataset of 5,062 human-evaluated natural-language mathematical proofs generated by several frontier LLMs across 1,010 competition problems. Using OPC, the authors study three questions: the gap between natural-language and formal proof generation, the mismatch between final-answer accuracy and proof correctness, and the effect of best-of-\(n\) selection strategies; they also fine-tune an 8B judge model for proof correctness evaluation.

## Strengths
- The main contribution is substantial and useful: a relatively large, open, human-annotated corpus of LLM-generated math proofs with correctness labels and judge feedback. For this area, data creation is expensive and slow, so a resource of this scale is genuinely valuable.

- The paper asks focused, important questions rather than merely releasing a dataset and hoping the reader infers why it matters. In particular, the three empirical questions in Section 5 are well chosen and relevant to the current math-reasoning literature.

- The annotation pipeline is described in meaningful detail for a main-track paper. Section 3 gives a plausible account of judge qualification, abstention, double grading, pilot refinement, and the interface. Even though some design choices remain debatable, this is much more careful than many benchmark papers.

- The paper includes several results that are informative beyond this specific dataset. Figure 5 is especially useful: it makes the “final answer is not the same as proof competence” point concrete, and it shows that the gap differs materially by model rather than being a uniform offset. That is exactly the kind of nuance the field needs.

- Figure 6 is another strong point. It does not just claim that best-of-\(n\) helps, it compares multiple selection mechanisms and shows that ranking-based approaches continue scaling where simpler discrete/continuous methods flatten out. This makes the best-of-\(n\) section more than a trivial “more samples helps” story.

- Table 2 is a solid centerpiece result. The judge-model comparison is broad, includes both closed and open models, reports pass@1 and maj@5, and gives cost information. The result that an 8B model fine-tuned on OPC becomes competitive with much stronger proprietary models is interesting and helps justify the dataset’s downstream value.

- The comparison to formal proof generation, while not perfect, is still useful. Figure 4 makes the scale of the natural-language vs formal gap easy to grasp, and the paper is careful enough to note that formal proofs retain the major advantage of automatic verification.

- The paper is generally readable and the figures do real work. Figure 1 is effective as an overview: panel (a) makes the dataset schema concrete, while panel (b) previews the main findings without overcomplicating them.

## Weaknesses
1. **The dataset construction is adaptive and partially target-driven, which raises concerns about selection bias and makes some aggregate statistics harder to interpret cleanly.**  
   In Section 3.1, the authors explicitly state that problem selection and prioritization were adjusted during annotation based on ongoing model performance, judge availability, and “progress towards the specific conclusions we aimed to draw from the dataset” (Page 4). That sentence is a bit alarming. It suggests the corpus is not a fixed sample from a pre-declared distribution, but a moving target influenced by intermediate results and desired analyses. This matters because several headline claims are phrased broadly, for example average correctness in Fig. 3 or the overall \(43\%\) correct-proof rate. If the problem mixture was actively steered to maintain certain difficulty characteristics, then these aggregate rates are less like measurements of model ability on a stable benchmark and more like measurements on a curator-shaped evaluation set. The paper should be much clearer about which conclusions are robust to this adaptive curation and which are not.

2. **The annotation reliability analysis is too optimistic, and the mathematical argument used to infer per-judge error rate is overly simplistic.**  
   On Page 6, the paper states that judges agreed on \(90.4\%\) of double-graded cases and then estimates an individual judge error rate \(p\) by solving
   \[
   0.904 = (1-p)^2 + p^2.
   \]
   This calculation assumes independent judges with identical symmetric error rates and no ambiguity effects. Those assumptions are strong and almost certainly violated here, because difficult proofs induce correlated mistakes and some proofs are intrinsically borderline. So the inferred “\(p=5\%\)” should not be presented as indicating “low noise level” without caveats. At best, it is a toy back-of-the-envelope estimate. This matters because the paper later compares model judge accuracy to a “human baseline” in Table 2, and the credibility of that comparison depends heavily on how reliable the human labels actually are.

3. **The “human-level judge” claim is slightly oversold relative to the evidence in the main paper.**  
   Section 5.2 and Table 2 are interesting, but the framing is stronger than what the setup really supports. The human baseline is not measured on the same test subset as the model judges, as the authors acknowledge on Page 8. Instead, it is drawn from all double-graded OPC proofs. That may be reasonable as a rough reference, but it is not a matched evaluation. In addition, the test set has only 293 proofs, and the confidence intervals in the appendix-sized table version are wide enough that several models are statistically close. So “LLMs are human level judges” is plausible but not nailed down as cleanly as the section title suggests. The paper would be stronger if it framed this as “approaching human agreement levels on this binary labeling task” rather than near-equating the two.

4. **The comparison between natural-language and formal proof generation is informative but not fully apples-to-apples.**  
   In Section 5.3 and Figure 4, the paper compares general-purpose LLMs generating informal proofs to a specialized formal prover on PutnamBench, and then emphasizes a roughly \(4\times\) gap. The authors do note some caveats, but the framing still invites an overly broad conclusion. Formal proof generation and natural-language proof writing are very different tasks with very different verification standards; moreover, the strongest cited formal result from Seed-Prover is excluded because it is agentic/private, while the informal systems are frontier general-purpose models. This section still has value, but the headline “formal proof generation lags behind” is stronger than the evidence really justifies unless one constrains the claim very carefully to the exact compared systems and setup.

5. **Some experimental claims rely on significance language without an adequate statistical treatment in the main paper.**  
   On Page 10, the authors state that in Figure 6(b) “all selection methods rely on the same underlying answers from O4-MINI, making the relative performance differences significant.” That is not, by itself, a statistical argument. Shared underlying samples imply paired dependence, which can indeed be exploited, but significance still requires an actual paired test or at least a principled uncertainty estimate for the paired comparison. Similarly, confidence intervals throughout Section 5 use the large-sample normal approximation, but some subsets are small and accuracies are near boundaries, making Wilson or exact intervals safer. This is not fatal, but for a benchmark paper making several comparative claims, the statistical treatment is thinner than it should be.

6. **The best-of-\(n\) section is promising but somewhat limited in scope because it studies only one generator model and one main judge family.**  
   Figure 6 is one of the more interesting parts of the paper, but all main best-of-\(n\) experiments are centered on O4-MINI generations, with O4-MINI also serving as the selector in the described methods. That makes it difficult to know how general the observed ranking advantage is. Is Rank (Swiss) robust across weaker open models, stronger models, or settings where the selector differs from the generator? The result may hold broadly, but the current evidence is narrow. Since best-of-\(n\) is presented as one of the paper’s three main takeaways, I would have liked at least one cross-model check.

7. **The contamination discussion is thoughtful but still not fully convincing for the strongest comparative claims.**  
   Section 5.6 is more careful than many papers, and I appreciate that the authors do not hand-wave the issue away. Still, the argument remains indirect. Figure 8 in the supplement-style pages shows standard competitions being harder than “non-standard” ones, which supports the claim that difficulty matters more than contamination, but it does not actually estimate contamination. This especially matters for small performance differences such as those in Figure 3 between Gemini and O4-MINI. The paper concedes this somewhat, but some of the surrounding narrative still reads as more definitive than the evidence permits.

8. **The corpus is heavily concentrated on competition mathematics, which narrows the external validity of several claims.**  
   The paper is transparent about this in Section 6, but it remains a real limitation. Table 5 shows a strong focus on olympiad-style high school problems, and Figure 2 visually reinforces that concentration. That is fine for a competition-math benchmark, but several claims in the introduction and conclusion are phrased in broad terms about “mathematical proof generation.” Competition proofs are a very specific genre, often optimized for clever constructions and concise arguments. The extent to which the findings transfer to textbook theorem proving, research-style proofs, or formalized undergraduate mathematics is unresolved.

9. **Some presentation details undermine confidence, especially in prompts and data descriptions.**  
   The prompt sections on Pages 24 to 31 contain multiple formatting artifacts and malformed LaTeX delimiters, for example strings such as `"\l"` or mismatched escape sequences in the judging prompts. I understand these may be transcription issues, but as written, the prompts are difficult to verify precisely. There are also a few obvious content slips in the appendix tables, for example Table 5 labels IMO as an “International competition for university students,” which is plainly wrong, and “Punaam” appears where Putnam is presumably intended. These are not core scientific flaws, but in a paper centered on careful data construction, such errors are distracting.

10. **The related-work positioning is good overall, but the paper could do more to situate itself against finer-grained proof evaluation and grading setups.**  
    The current positioning is mostly at the level of proof corpora, binary proof evaluation, and LLM-as-a-judge. Given that OPC’s own labels are binary and that Section 5.2 makes a strong claim about judging ability, the paper would benefit from a clearer discussion of how binary proof correctness compares to partial-credit or step-level proof grading setups, and what is gained or lost by this simplification. The current treatment in Section 2 is a bit too quick on that axis.

## Questions
1. **Can the authors provide a cleaner accounting of dataset construction before and after adaptive curation decisions?**  
   Specifically, how many problems were added, removed, or deprioritized due to observed model performance or due to “progress towards the specific conclusions” mentioned on Page 4? A table with chronological dataset composition, or a frozen pre-adaptation subset analysis, would increase my confidence that the main findings are not artifacts of curator steering.

2. **Can the authors report matched human-vs-model judging on the exact same evaluation subset used in Table 2?**  
   This is the single most important clarification for the “human-level judge” claim. Even a smaller matched subset would help. I am less worried about whether GPT-5 is \(89.3\%\) or \(90.8\%\), and more about whether the human comparison is apples-to-apples.

3. **Can the authors provide a more careful analysis of annotation reliability beyond raw agreement?**  
   For example, please report disagreement types, uncertain-rate breakdowns, agreement stratified by problem source/difficulty, and whether the coordinator ever overruled initial labels. If possible, a simple latent-annotator or adjudication analysis would be more informative than the independence-based \(p\) estimate on Page 6.

4. **For the best-of-\(n\) experiments in Figure 6, can the authors add at least one cross-model check?**  
   Even a reduced experiment on a smaller subset using a second generator or a different selector would help establish whether Rank (Swiss) is broadly useful or mainly tuned to this one setup.

5. **Can the authors support the significance language in Section 5 with paired statistical tests?**  
   For Figure 6(b) in particular, a paired bootstrap, McNemar-style paired comparison where applicable, or another appropriate test would materially strengthen the claim that ranking methods outperform the alternatives.

6. **Can the authors clarify exactly how proof generations were retried on the MathArena subset?**  
   On Page 4, the paper says that for MathArena, the authors “only retained solutions with a correct final answer, retrying generation if necessary.” How many retries were allowed, and under what stopping rule? This matters because Section 5.4 compares final-answer accuracy to proof correctness, and the generation protocol should be completely explicit.

7. **How often were ground-truth solutions available to judges, and did agreement differ when they were present versus absent?**  
   Since the interface shown in Figures 9 and 10 includes the ground-truth solution, it would be useful to know whether its availability materially changed grading consistency.
