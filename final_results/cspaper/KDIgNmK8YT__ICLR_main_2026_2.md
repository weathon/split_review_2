# Expected Review Outcome:
## Summary
This paper introduces WorldAlignment, a synthetic multi-domain benchmark for pairwise preference evaluation of LLM outputs across three aspects: instruction following, mathematical reasoning, and code-related tasks. The benchmark is built using persona-guided prompt generation with GPT-4o, and the paper evaluates several proprietary and open models using GPT-4o and GPT-4.1-Mini as judges, together with a logistic-regression-based length-controlled win-rate metric inspired by AlpacaEval 2.0. The authors also provide analyses of prompt/response length distributions, task difficulty/feasibility/quality, and a case study comparing DPO and SimPO post-training variants.

## Strengths
The paper addresses an important and timely problem. Current automatic alignment benchmarks are indeed heavily concentrated on generic instruction-following, so a benchmark that tries to stress math and code preference alignment is relevant to the ICLR community.

The paper is generally easy to follow at a high level. Figure 1 gives a clear overview of the intended positioning of WorldAlignment relative to benchmarks like AlpacaEval 2.0, namely broader domain coverage and more complex preference pairs. Even though I have concerns about whether the paper ultimately substantiates all of these claims, the figure does communicate the benchmark’s scope and motivation effectively.

I also appreciate that the authors do not stop at reporting a single aggregate score. Table 1 breaks performance down by three aspects, and this already reveals some useful patterns, for example that models with strong raw WR can look notably weaker after length correction, especially in instruction-following. That is a practically relevant observation for alignment evaluation, and it is good that the paper emphasizes both WR and LC rather than only raw preference wins.

The benchmark construction is at least somewhat systematic rather than ad hoc. The persona-guided generation idea gives a reproducible mechanism for obtaining diverse prompts across aspects, and the paper does provide the actual generation templates in the appendix. For a benchmark paper, having a concrete generation recipe is useful.

The post-training comparison in Figure 5 is one of the more interesting empirical components. The figure suggests that preference optimization behavior differs across architecture families and domains, rather than yielding a uniform improvement everywhere. In particular, the observed difference between Gemma and Llama variants, and between instruction tasks versus math/code tasks, hints that the benchmark may capture nontrivial variation that generic instruction-following benchmarks would flatten out.

## Weaknesses
1. **The central “expert-level human preference” claim is not convincingly supported by the benchmark construction.**  
   The benchmark is described throughout as “expert-level” and “human preference alignment across domains and aspects,” but the actual dataset construction in Section 3.2, Pages 3 to 4, is entirely synthetic and driven by GPT-4o: prompts and responses are generated by \(G(p_i,d)\) in Equation 1, and the filtering/assessment pipeline is also done with GPT-4o. There is no evidence in the main paper that domain experts wrote the prompts, validated the preference pairs, or even audited a representative subset. This matters because the paper’s framing is much stronger than “synthetic multi-domain benchmark.” If the benchmark is really a GPT-4o-generated, GPT-4o-filtered, GPT-4o-judged pipeline, then the scientific claim is not expert alignment so much as alignment to one particular model’s synthetic distribution and preferences. That is a much narrower contribution.

2. **The paper repeatedly invokes “human preferences,” but no human annotation study is shown in the main paper.**  
   Section 3.1 defines the problem in terms of a human annotator providing \(y \in \{0,1\}\), and the abstract makes strong claims about benchmarking “expert-level human preference alignment.” However, the actual evaluation in Section 4 uses GPT-4o and GPT-4.1-Mini as judges, not humans. There is no human agreement study, no calibration against expert annotations, no inter-annotator analysis, and no evidence that the LLM judges are reliable specifically on the proposed math/code/domain-specific instances. This is not a cosmetic gap. It directly affects the validity of the benchmark’s target construct. Without showing that the automatic judgments track human or expert preferences on this dataset, the benchmark may simply measure agreement with the chosen judge models.

3. **The methodological advance over AlpacaEval 2.0 is limited and not convincingly differentiated.**  
   Section 3.3 states that the paper proposes a “novel multi-domain regression framework,” but Equation 2 looks much more like a lightly modified extension of the AlpacaEval-style logistic model than a substantially new methodology. The domain term is also not clearly formalized. In  
   \[
   q_{\theta,\phi,\psi}(y=1\mid z_m,z_b,m,b,x,d)
   := \text{logistic}\Big((\theta_m-\theta_b) + \phi_{m,b}\tanh(\cdots) + d((\psi_m-\psi_b)\gamma_x)\Big),
   \]
   the notation \(d((\psi_m-\psi_b)\gamma_x)\) is mathematically unclear. Is \(d\) an indicator, an embedding, a selector, or a scalar multiplier depending on domain? The text says the model “incorporates domain heterogeneity,” but does not specify how the domain variable is encoded or estimated. As written, the equation is underspecified and difficult to reproduce. The claimed “preserves identity and symmetry properties” statement on Page 5 may hold for the model/length components under a symmetric parameterization, but it is not obvious once the domain-dependent prompt term is introduced in this ambiguous way.

4. **Equation 2 has multiple notation and modeling issues that weaken reproducibility and soundness.**  
   This is not just a style complaint. A benchmark paper that hinges on length-corrected win rates should make the statistical model precise. Several details are missing or confusing:
   - The “Prompt” term uses \(\gamma_x\), but the text refers to “intrinsic difficulty \((\gamma)\) of each prompt,” without explaining how \(\gamma_x\) is obtained or fit.
   - The term \(\psi_m-\psi_b\) suggests model-specific coefficients interacting with prompt difficulty, but no estimation procedure, regularization, or identifiability discussion is provided.
   - The length term uses \(\phi_{m,b}\), which is pair-specific. This scales quadratically in the number of models if learned naively, but the paper does not discuss data sufficiency, parameter sharing, or how many pairwise comparisons are needed.
   - The text says “setting the length term to zero” yields Equation 3, but Equation 3 also drops the domain notation from the expectation and implicitly retains only a prompt term \((\psi_m-\psi_b)\gamma_x\) without making domain dependence explicit.  
   These are substantive omissions because the reported LC scores in Table 1 are central to the paper’s conclusions.

5. **The benchmark quality analyses in Figures 2 and 3 are largely self-referential and do not establish benchmark usefulness.**  
   Figure 2 shows that WorldAlignment prompts and responses are longer than AlpacaEval 2.0, and Figure 3 shows higher GPT-4o-assigned difficulty, feasibility, and quality. But longer prompts are not automatically better prompts, and higher synthetic “difficulty” scores from the same model family used to generate/filter data are not strong evidence of benchmark quality. In fact, Figure 2 may be interpreted in the opposite direction: the benchmark may simply induce verbosity and thereby create a setup where length correction becomes especially important. Likewise, in Figure 3(c), the reported average quality of \(9.95\) for WorldAlignment is almost saturated. That raises a red flag that the quality scoring rubric is not discriminative, especially since it is again produced by GPT-4o. A benchmark paper needs external validation, not just internal scoring loops that conclude the synthetic data is excellent.

6. **The experimental setup risks judge contamination and self-preference effects, especially given the choice of GPT-4o as both baseline and primary judge.**  
   In Section 4.1, GPT-4o is used as the baseline reference model, and GPT-4o also serves as the primary evaluator. This is a problematic design choice. Even if one does not assume explicit self-enhancement bias, the setup still creates an uncomfortable circularity: the benchmark examples are GPT-4o-generated, benchmark quality is GPT-4o-scored, one baseline is GPT-4o, and the main judge is GPT-4o. That stack of dependencies makes it hard to interpret any win-rate gaps as model-independent preference signals. The issue becomes especially important when the paper uses these results to claim that “many academic post-training and alignment-tuned models still lag behind GPT-4-level performance.” The evaluation pipeline is simply too entangled with GPT-4o to make that a clean statement.

7. **The empirical evidence is narrower than the paper’s claims, especially for domain coverage.**  
   The paper repeatedly emphasizes broad multi-domain evaluation, but the main quantitative results in Table 1 are only at the coarse aspect level: instruction following, math, code. Table 2 then reports only the top five domains from the instruction subset and only for three mini models. This is thin support for the broader narrative of comprehensive domain-oriented alignment benchmarking. The situation is made more confusing by Figure 6 in the appendix, whose caption says “we focus exclusively on the General domain,” while Table 2 clearly includes medicine, biology, history, and engineering. That inconsistency suggests the domain taxonomy and its role in evaluation are not cleanly presented.

8. **Several empirical conclusions are overstated relative to the evidence shown in Table 1 and Table 2.**  
   Table 1 is informative, but the paper often interprets differences too aggressively without uncertainty estimates, significance tests, or variance analyses. For example, Section 4.2 argues that GPT5 shows “overwhelming superiority” in math reasoning. The table does show strong scores, but there are no confidence intervals, no repeated-judge variability estimates, and no indication of how robust these rankings are to prompt order, judge swap, or prompt paraphrases. Similarly, in code generation, the gap between GPT-4o-judge LC and GPT-4.1-Mini-judge LC is often very large, which the paper attributes to evaluator-specific bias. That is plausible, but the paper does not do the follow-up analysis needed to support such a claim.

9. **Figure 5 is interesting, but the associated experiment is underdescribed and hard to trust fully.**  
   The figure compares DPO and SimPO variants across Gemma and Llama model series, and the text claims architecture-specific optimization differences. However, the paper does not provide the training data source, preference data details, hyperparameters, checkpoint selection criteria, or whether these models were reproduced by the authors or taken from existing releases. Because the observed differences are being used as evidence that WorldAlignment can expose post-training behavior, the missing experimental details matter. Otherwise, Figure 5 risks reading like an anecdotal case study rather than a robust evaluation.

10. **Some wording and terminology are imprecise or incorrect, which hurts credibility.**  
   There are multiple places where the exposition becomes sloppy enough to obscure the intended meaning. The paper refers to “morbidity bias” on Pages 5, 7, and 9, where it almost certainly means verbosity or length bias. Section headings fluctuate in style, some notation is inconsistently spaced or typeset, and the mathematical exposition around Equation 2 is not polished enough for a benchmark paper whose main methodological hook is a regression-based evaluation metric. These issues do not by themselves sink the paper, but they contribute to the sense that the core methodology is not fully nailed down.

11. **The paper does not engage enough with prior concerns about LLM-as-a-judge reliability in the context it relies on most.**  
   The related work covers classic benchmark references, but the main paper does not seriously discuss modern evidence that judge behavior can vary with prompt design, ordering, self-preference, and domain. Given that the entire evaluation pipeline depends on LLM judges, this missing positioning matters scientifically, not just bibliographically. A stronger paper would explicitly analyze judge agreement, swap order sensitivity, and agreement with at least a small set of human or expert labels.

12. **The benchmark may confound task difficulty, verbosity, and judge preference rather than isolating alignment.**  
   Figure 2 and Table 1 together actually hint at this. WorldAlignment responses are much longer on average than AlpacaEval-style responses, and Table 1 shows sizable gaps between WR and LC for many models. This suggests the benchmark may be particularly susceptible to verbosity effects, perhaps because the prompts themselves are designed to elicit long, comprehensive responses. If so, then some of the benchmark’s apparent difficulty may come from interaction with the judge and scoring protocol rather than from better measurement of alignment. The paper frames LC as the remedy, but it does not demonstrate that the correction is sufficient in this new, much longer-response regime.

## Questions
1. The biggest issue for me is construct validity. Can the authors provide evidence, ideally from the main paper or a concise additional analysis, that WorldAlignment judgments correlate with **human** or **expert** preferences on a nontrivial subset of the benchmark, especially for math and code? Even 100 to 200 manually judged examples with agreement analysis would materially increase my confidence.

2. Please clarify Equation 2 precisely. What is the exact mathematical form of the domain term \(d((\psi_m-\psi_b)\gamma_x)\)? Is \(d\) a one-hot selector, domain-specific coefficient vector, or some other mapping? How are \(\gamma_x\), \(\psi_m\), and \(\phi_{m,b}\) estimated in practice, and with what regularization?

3. Since GPT-4o is used in so many roles, generator, quality scorer, baseline, and primary judge, can the authors quantify how much the rankings change if GPT-4o is removed from one or more of these roles? For example, what happens if the primary reported leaderboard is based only on GPT-4.1-Mini, or on another independent judge?

4. For Table 1, can the authors provide uncertainty estimates, for example bootstrap confidence intervals for WR and LC? Several claims of ranking differences and architecture effects are difficult to assess without variance.

5. For Figure 5, please provide the missing training/evaluation details for the DPO and SimPO models. Were these trained under matched settings? What preference dataset was used? How were checkpoints selected? Without this, it is hard to interpret the figure as evidence about the benchmark rather than about uncontrolled training variation.

6. The paper argues that Figure 3 demonstrates higher difficulty and quality. Can the authors explain why near-saturated quality scores, especially WorldAlignment at \(\mu=9.95\), should be considered meaningful rather than evidence that the evaluator rubric has poor discrimination on synthetic data?

7. Table 2 and Figure 6 leave me confused about the actual domain structure. Is evaluation performed across many domains, or is the instruction subset primarily “General” with a few metadata tags? A more precise description of the taxonomy and per-domain sample counts in the main paper would help.
