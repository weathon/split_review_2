# Expected Review Outcome:
## Summary
This paper studies modern recurrent sequence models, especially SSM-style architectures such as Mamba and Hyena, against Transformers on two synthetic retrieval benchmarks, multi-query associative recall (MQAR) and copying. The main empirical claim is that recurrent models are much more sensitive to learning rate choice than Transformers, and that this optimization brittleness substantially affects conclusions about recall/copying ability and preferred scaling strategy. The paper also reports that single-layer Mamba can solve MQAR when properly tuned, unlike a single-layer Transformer, and presents ablations suggesting that local convolution is a key architectural ingredient behind this behavior.

## Strengths
1. The paper asks a relevant and timely question. There is real confusion in the current literature about whether observed gaps between Transformers and SSMs on recall-style tasks are due to expressivity limits or optimization. Framing the comparison through learnability rather than just asymptotic capability is useful for the ICLR community.

2. The empirical message about hyperparameter sensitivity is interesting and, at least qualitatively, clearly visible in the figures. **Figure 1** is probably the strongest piece of evidence in the paper: it shows a broad plateau of good learning rates for Attention, versus a narrow success window for Mamba and Hyena. The same qualitative pattern reappears on copying in **Figure 5**, which is a good cross-task sanity check and makes the central observation harder to dismiss as a single-benchmark artifact.

3. The scaling comparison is provocative and potentially useful. **Figure 3** and **Figure 4** make the paper’s “width for recurrent models, depth for Transformers” story easy to grasp. In particular, **Figure 3** shows a clear qualitative separation between 1-layer attention and 1-layer recurrent models on MQAR, while **Figure 4** usefully emphasizes that parameter count alone is not the right matching criterion for these architectures on this task.

4. The paper includes targeted ablations rather than only reporting black-box performance. **Table 2** is a strong part of the submission: the contrast between “Attention” at 2%, “Attention + Conv on QKV” at 99%, “Mamba” at 99%, and “Mamba w/o conv1d” at 2% is a clean and informative result. Even if the broader mechanistic interpretation is not fully nailed down, this table gives a concrete architectural takeaway.

5. The paper is generally readable and well organized. The narrative from optimization instability, to scaling, to 1-layer dynamics, to architectural ablations is coherent. The main claims are easy to follow, and the figures are placed in sensible locations relative to the discussion.

6. The work is fairly extensive for a synthetic-benchmark paper. The authors report many runs, multiple seeds, and a nontrivial sweep over widths, sequence lengths, layers, and architectures. Even though I have concerns about evaluation protocol, the amount of experimentation is clearly substantial.

## Weaknesses
1. **There is a serious experimental methodology issue around hyperparameter selection that currently undermines the strength of the empirical claims.** On **Page 15, Appendix A.2**, the paper states: “Each configuration ... undergoes a learning rate sweep to identify the optimal learning rate. The reported accuracy for each configuration corresponds to the best performance achieved across the tested learning rates.” In the same section, the authors describe only training and test sets, with no validation split. As written, this implies model selection by test accuracy, or at least leaves that interpretation as the most natural one. That is not a minor bookkeeping issue. It directly affects the central claim of the paper, because the whole argument is about the existence of narrow success windows in learning rate. If the best learning rate is chosen using test performance, then the reported gains from “careful tuning” in **Figure 2**, **Table 1**, and related discussions are optimistic and not a valid estimate of generalization performance under a proper selection protocol. This matters especially because the paper’s main thesis is comparative: “Transformers are robust, recurrent models are brittle.” Without a held-out validation set and a fixed tuning budget per architecture, the practical significance of this brittleness is hard to assess rigorously. If the authors did use validation for model selection and only described it poorly, that should be clarified explicitly in the rebuttal, because right now this reads as test-set tuning.

2. **The central causal claim, namely that the main differentiator is optimization rather than expressivity, is substantially stronger than what the evidence supports.** On **Pages 2-3**, the paper states that “Transformers differ from SSMs not in terms of expressive power but mainly because of their optimization dynamics.” That is a strong statement. But the experiments only show that some previously reported failures of recurrent models can disappear under finer learning-rate tuning on MQAR/copying. This is not enough to establish that expressivity is not an important differentiator. In fact, several of the paper’s own results point to architectural capability differences depending on depth and convolutional augmentation. **Figure 3** shows 1-layer Attention failing where 1-layer Mamba succeeds; **Table 2** then shows that a convolution can almost entirely change this picture. This suggests that the representational story is still deeply entangled with the architectural inductive bias, not simply optimization. The paper is at its strongest when it argues that optimization is an important confounder; it overreaches when it tries to replace the expressivity narrative with an optimization-only narrative.

3. **The evidence for “induction-head-like” dynamics in 1-layer models is too indirect relative to the paper’s confidence in the interpretation.** Section 6 leans heavily on the idea that the loss bump in **Figure 6** resembles induction-head formation. But what is actually shown in **Figure 6** is a training/validation loss trajectory and accuracy curve, not attention patterns, circuit analysis, token-copy pathways, or any other mechanistic diagnostic. For the Transformer, the paper even notes that the model fails to solve the task despite the bump. For Mamba, the analogy is even more speculative, since the architecture is not attention-based in the standard sense. At present, the interpretation seems to be “there is a phase-transition-like kink in loss, therefore this may be analogous to induction heads.” That is too weak for the prominence it receives in the paper’s contribution list on **Page 3**. A more defensible presentation would be to describe this as a training phase transition with superficial similarity to prior induction-head observations, rather than implying a meaningful mechanistic parallel.

4. **The mathematical exposition is too thin for several of the mechanistic and optimization claims, and some notation is imprecise.** The paper introduces the SSM recurrence and the induced lower-triangular mixing matrix in **Equation (1), Page 4**, then later argues on **Page 9** that DeltaNet is more stable because off-diagonal terms such as \(C_N \prod_{k=1}^N A_k B_0\) “do not necessarily incur in vanishing gradients,” whereas Mamba and Mamba2 have decays that induce vanishing gradients. There are multiple issues here. First, **Equation (1)** is an input-output unrolling, not a gradient expression, so the jump from decay in forward off-diagonal coefficients to a claim about optimization stability is suggestive but not established. Second, the matrices \(A_i,B_i,C_i,D_i\) are input-dependent, so talking about gradient vanishing through \(\prod_k A_k\) requires assumptions on parametrization, norms, gating, and backpropagation paths that are not stated. Third, the indexing in **Equation (1)** is a bit sloppy, since the recurrence is written for \(i\) but the matrix display uses rows from \(0\) to \(N\), while the input was introduced as \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}\). This is not a fatal bug, but it contributes to the sense that the mathematical layer is illustrative rather than rigorous. Since the paper’s headline story is about optimization landscapes and gradient behavior, I expected either a much more careful derivation or a more modest wording.

5. **The benchmark scope is narrow, and the paper sometimes extrapolates beyond what MQAR and copying can support.** The authors do acknowledge this in the conclusion, which I appreciate. Still, the paper repeatedly connects the findings to language modeling retrieval and in-context learning in a broad way. MQAR and copying are useful probes, but they are synthetic tasks with very stylized structure. A result like **Table 1**, where a wider Mamba reaches 100% on copying and a deeper/narrower one fails, is informative about that benchmark, but it does not by itself justify broad guidance such as “architectures must be scaled along their preferred axes” without downstream evidence. Similarly, the claim that these findings “re-contextualize prior performance evaluations” would be more convincing if at least one small-scale language modeling or algorithmic generalization experiment were included in the main paper.

6. **The comparison protocol is not always obviously fair across architectures, and some important details are buried or absent from the main text.** For example, **Figure 2** compares official Zoology results, a replication with the original grid, and the authors’ finer grid. That is useful, but it is unclear whether the tuning budget is normalized in a way that reflects practical model selection cost. If one architecture needs a much denser sweep to find its narrow optimum, that is itself part of the comparison, not merely a nuisance to be factored away. Likewise, **Table 1** on the copy task compares a 12-layer Transformer at width 1024 and 150M parameters to several Mamba variants, but there is no sense of variance over seeds, sensitivity to LR, or whether each row is reported at its best-tuned point under identical search budgets. Since the paper’s argument is about brittleness, this missing protocol detail matters.

7. **Some claims are overstated relative to the actual baselines presented in the main paper.** Section 7 says that DeltaNet achieves “Transformer-level robustness” on MQAR, based mainly on **Figure 7**. But **Figure 7** only shows two model dimensions and a limited learning-rate sweep, and the text itself says DeltaNet was only tested up to width 256 because of implementation limits. That is not enough evidence for a broad robustness equivalence claim. Similarly, the statement in Section 4 that increasing layers beyond two “does not provide any further improvement in MQAR performance” is not really established in the main paper, and relies on appendix material and the known nature of the task rather than direct evidence in the core presentation.

8. **The literature positioning is somewhat incomplete for a paper making strong claims about stability and Mamba recall mechanisms.** The paper cites a substantial amount of related work, but it would benefit from a more direct discussion of prior work on stability analyses of Mamba-like systems and more mechanistic studies of associative recall in these models. As written, the paper’s positioning is strongest against Zoology-style benchmark results, but weaker against the broader body of work on recurrent/SSM training dynamics. This matters because part of the submission’s framing is that it is revisiting and partially overturning existing conclusions.

9. **Presentation is generally good, but there are places where the wording blurs three distinct notions: expressivity, learnability, and practical tuning sensitivity.** This shows up repeatedly in Sections 3, 4, and 8. For example, saying that “Mamba demonstrates greater expressivity” in Section 7 because 1-layer Mamba solves a task that 1-layer Attention does not is too quick, especially when **Table 2** simultaneously shows that adding a simple convolution allows the 1-layer Transformer to solve the same task. The cleaner interpretation is that shallow Mamba with its default local convolution has a more favorable inductive bias for this benchmark, not necessarily that it is categorically more expressive.

## Questions
1. The biggest point that could change my opinion is the tuning protocol. Did you select learning rates using a **validation set**, and only then report test accuracy for the selected hyperparameter? If yes, please state this explicitly and point to where it is described. If no, can you provide validation-based versions of **Figures 1, 2, 5, 7** and **Table 1 / Table 2**? This is crucial because the central contribution is about optimization sensitivity, and test-based hyperparameter selection would materially weaken the conclusions.

2. Can you soften or better justify the strong claim that the main difference between Transformers and SSMs is optimization rather than expressivity? What exact statement do you believe your evidence supports? For example, is the intended claim merely that prior empirical conclusions on these tasks are confounded by optimization, or that expressivity gaps are negligible once tuning is fair? Those are very different claims.

3. For the induction-head discussion in Section 6, do you have any direct mechanistic evidence beyond the loss bump in **Figure 6**? For the Transformer, attention maps or token-pair diagnostics might help; for Mamba, hidden-state or effective-kernel analyses could be informative. Without this, the current interpretation reads as intriguing but speculative.

4. On the mathematical side, can you clarify the connection between **Equation (1)** and the optimization-stability argument on **Page 9**? In particular, what is the precise reasoning linking decay in forward coefficients such as \(C_N \prod_{k=1}^N A_k B_0\) to gradient vanishing and narrow learning-rate windows? If this is only an intuition, I recommend labeling it as such.

5. For **Table 1**, please report variance across seeds and the learning-rate search space for each row. The current table is striking, but without uncertainty and tuning details it is hard to tell whether the comparison reflects a robust scaling law or a single best-case operating point.

6. Could you quantify brittleness more directly, rather than visually? For example, one useful statistic would be the measure of the “successful LR interval” under a fixed performance threshold, or the probability of success under a log-uniform LR prior. That would make the qualitative picture in **Figure 1** and **Figure 5** more rigorous.

7. Since **Table 2** is one of the cleanest results in the paper, it would help to expand it slightly in the main text. In particular, what exact convolution kernel size, placement, and parameter count changes are used? Does the result remain when matching parameter counts more tightly between “Attention” and “Attention + Conv on QKV”?
