## Summary

IncentRL augments standard RL with a KL-based reward-shaping term that penalizes misalignment between a predicted outcome distribution \(p(o|s,a)\) and a preferred outcome distribution \(q(o|s)\), weighted by a hyperparameter \(\beta\). The paper claims as its central novelty a Bayesian scheme that treats \(\beta\) as a random variable updated online, eliminating manual tuning. Experiments on a 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid show that fixed-\(\beta\) variants of IncentRL improve over the \(\beta=0\) baseline across these tasks.

---

## Strengths

1. **Clean, well-motivated formulation.** The KL-based preference-prediction shaping (Section 3.2) is clearly stated, and the connections to dopamine reward prediction error and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 2.3–2.4, 6.3) provide a coherent cognitive framing that distinguishes the method from policy-regularization approaches (TRPO, PPO, SAC).

2. **Consistent empirical improvement under fixed \(\beta\).** Across all three environments, at least one fixed \(\beta>0\) value outperforms the \(\beta=0\) baseline in terms of cumulative reward, success rate, or episode length (Tables 1–2, Figure 2). The MiniGrid result (98% success rate vs. 90.5%, 29 vs. 75 steps) is substantively meaningful.

3. **Proposition 1 provides a minimal correctness guarantee.** The continuity argument that small \(\beta\) preserves optimality of the original optimal policy (Section 4.1) is standard but not incorrect, and it serves as a sanity check that the shaping does not introduce pathological behavior for near-zero \(\beta\).

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation mechanism — the paper's central claimed novelty — is never specified.** The abstract and introduction state that "the central novelty is treating the incentive weight \(\beta\) as a Bayesian random variable, updated online," and the contribution list leads with "Bayesian adaptation (novelty)." However, the algorithm listing in Section 5.1 (steps 1–5) contains no step for updating \(\beta\). The paper never describes: what prior is placed on \(\beta\), what likelihood function relates \(\beta\) to observed data, what evidence triggers an update, how the posterior is incorporated into the policy (e.g., posterior-mean vs. sampling), or what constitutes a "round" in Figures 3–4 and Table 3. The only mention is "we tracked the posterior evolution of \(\beta\) using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" (line 230) and "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (line 228). The mechanism itself is absent, making the paper's primary technical contribution unverifiable and irreproducible. *This is verifiable from the paper as written: the algorithm section describes the full IncentRL procedure and omits any \(\beta\)-update rule.*

### Major

- **The Bayesian adaptation's task performance is never evaluated.** The paper's headline claim is that Bayesian adaptation eliminates manual tuning. Yet none of the three main experiments uses the Bayesian version as the learning algorithm. The 2-state MDP and MountainCar experiments sweep over fixed \(\beta\) values; MiniGrid compares fixed \(\beta=0.0\) vs. fixed \(\beta=0.01\). The Bayesian adaptation appears only in a tracking exercise (Figures 3–4, Table 3) showing posterior concentration. The actual RL performance of the adaptive scheme — the experiment that would directly validate the central claim — is referenced as "additional runs (not shown)" with no quantitative results plotted or tabulated. Showing posterior concentration is necessary but not sufficient; one must demonstrate that the adaptive version achieves comparable task performance to the best fixed \(\beta\) on at least one environment.

- **The preferred outcome distribution \(q(o|s)\) encodes privileged goal information, and baselines receive no equivalent knowledge.** In the 2-state MDP, \(q(o|s_0)=\{s_1:1.0, s_0:0.0\}\) directs the agent to the reward-yielding state. In MountainCar, \(q(o|s)\) "assign[s] all probability to the goal (\(x \ge 0.5\))" — the entire difficulty of MountainCar is that the agent does not know where the goal is. In MiniGrid, \(q\) encodes goal-reaching. The baseline (\(\beta=0\)) receives no such information. Claiming an "18% improvement over standard DQN" is therefore misleading: the comparison conflates the effect of the KL shaping mechanism with the effect of providing goal-location information that the baseline lacks. A fairer evaluation would compare against standard RL augmented with an equivalently informative shaping signal, such as potential-based reward shaping using the same goal-distance potential (Ng et al., 1999), or would test IncentRL in settings where \(q\) is learned from data rather than hand-specified.

- **No comparison against existing intrinsic motivation methods.** The paper motivates itself by arguing that existing intrinsic motivation methods "rely on fixed trade-offs between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards" and that the contribution is removing this need for manual tuning. Yet the only baseline is standard RL (\(\beta=0\)), which has no intrinsic motivation at all. No comparisons are made against curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., 2017; Burda et al., 2018), empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende, 2015), VIME (Houthooft et al., 2016), count-based exploration, or entropy bonuses. The claim that "Bayesian adaptation of preference gaps removes the need for manual trade-off tuning, a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods" is untested without comparisons to those methods.

- **The computation of \(p(o|s,a)\) is underspecified for non-toy environments.** For the 2-state MDP, \(p(o|s,a)\) is explicitly given by the transition probabilities. For MountainCar and MiniGrid, the paper states only that \(p\) "may be obtained from a forward model or from environment dynamics" (line 63). It never specifies: was \(p\) taken directly from the environment dynamics (i.e., treating the next state as the outcome)? Was a separate forward model learned? If learned, what architecture was used and how was it trained? Without this information, the MountainCar and MiniGrid experiments cannot be reproduced, and it is impossible to determine whether the observed improvement comes from the KL shaping itself or from having a learned model that the baseline lacks.

### Minor

- **Figures 3–4 and Table 3 contain an inconsistency between the reported posterior and the claimed "target."** The figure captions state "A dashed red line indicates the target effective \(\beta = 0.1\)" and the text repeatedly says the posterior concentrates "near the effective region (\(\beta \approx 0.1\))." However, Table 3 shows the posterior mean decreasing from 0.106 (Round 1) to 0.017 (Round 4) — close to the MiniGrid effective \(\beta=0.01\), not 0.1. If the "target" refers to the MountainCar effective \(\beta\), this needs clarification; as presented, the figures and text appear to reference an inconsistent target value.

- **Propositions 1 and 2 are basic limit statements.** Proposition 1 (small \(\beta\) preserves optimality) follows from continuity of a bounded reward perturbation in finite MDPs, and Proposition 2 (large \(\beta\) yields pure preference-following) is immediate from the objective's definition. They do not constitute a substantive theoretical analysis — there is no analysis of convergence rates, regret, the dynamics of Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation, or the interaction between KL shaping and policy optimization.

- **Low seed counts.** MountainCar and MiniGrid results are averaged over only 3 seeds. No confidence intervals or statistical significance tests accompany the headline numbers (Tables 1–2).

- **Only one \(\beta>0\) value succeeds in MountainCar.** \(\beta=0.3\) and \(\beta=1.0\) degrade performance by 52–75% relative to baseline. The paper shows this honestly in Table 2 but does not explain why higher \(\beta\) values fail so catastrophically, which weakens the claim of robustness.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation separating the effect of privileged goal information from the effect of the specific KL form (e.g., compare against a simple Euclidean-distance-to-goal bonus).
- A sweep over \(\beta\) in MiniGrid to assess sensitivity, complementing the single \(\beta=0.01\) value tested.
- Comparison against at least one intrinsic motivation baseline (curiosity, count-based exploration, or entropy bonus) on a single environment.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the complete Bayesian update mechanism.** Provide the prior, likelihood, update rule, and how the posterior is used in policy learning. Without this, the central contribution cannot be evaluated.

2. **Run the Bayesian adaptation end-to-end on at least one environment** and compare its task performance (cumulative reward, success rate) against both the fixed-\(\beta\) sweep and the optimal fixed \(\beta\), under identical random seeds. This single experiment would validate or invalidate the paper's core claim.

3. **Address the privileged-information confound** by either (a) comparing against standard RL augmented with an equivalently informative shaping signal (e.g., potential-based shaping with the same goal-distance potential), or (b) demonstrating IncentRL in a setting where \(q\) is learned from data rather than hand-specified.

4. **Specify how \(p(o|s,a)\) is obtained** in MountainCar and MiniGrid — is it the raw next-state distribution from the environment dynamics, or a learned forward model? If learned, describe the architecture and training procedure.

5. **Include at least one intrinsic motivation baseline** (e.g., curiosity-driven exploration, count-based bonus) on one environment to ground the claim that IncentRL's approach to balancing intrinsic/extrinsic rewards is advantageous.

---

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes an interesting cognitively-motivated shaping framework and demonstrates that fixed-\(\beta\) versions improve over a no-shaping baseline on three tasks. However, the central claimed novelty — Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation — is never specified as an algorithm and its task performance is never evaluated, making the primary contribution unverifiable. The experimental comparison is further confounded by providing the method with privileged goal information that baselines do not receive. These issues cannot be resolved through minor revisions; the paper would require substantial rewriting and additional experiments to support its claims. I cannot recommend acceptance in the current form.
