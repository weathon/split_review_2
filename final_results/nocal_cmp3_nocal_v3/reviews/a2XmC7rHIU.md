## Summary

This paper presents the Open Proof Corpus (OPC), a dataset of 5,062 LLM-generated mathematical proofs across 1,010 competition problems (USAMO, IMO, etc.), each human-graded by former IMO participants. The dataset is designed to support training and evaluation of proof-generation models. Using the OPC, the authors address three open questions: the gap between informal and formal proof generation, the relationship between final-answer accuracy and proof correctness, and the effectiveness of best-of-\(n\) selection strategies. They also fine-tune an 8B model (OPC-R1-8B) on the dataset, achieving 88.1% judging accuracy — matching Gemini-2.5-Pro and approaching GPT-5.

## Strengths

1. **Fills a genuine, recognized gap.** Multiple prior works (Petrov et al., 2025; Mahdavi et al., 2025; Guo et al., ) have noted that existing proof-evaluation datasets are small, use outdated models, contain few correct proofs, or are not open-sourced. The OPC addresses all of these at once: 5,062 proofs, six state-of-the-art models, a balanced mix of correct/incorrect proofs, and open release. This is a meaningful step forward.

2. **Rigorous annotation pipeline.** The use of 13 former IMO participants as judges, a pilot phase with 35% double-grading, iterative refinement of instructions based on judge feedback, an abstention mechanism (<3% uncertain), and the LLM issue-summary feature (with a bias check) all demonstrate care in the annotation process. The 90.4% inter-judge agreement rate is plausible and transparently reported.

3. **Well-structured dataset for multiple use cases.** The four-way split (MathArena, PutnamBench, Best-of-n, Generic) is well-motivated and directly supports the three open questions the paper addresses. This is better design than a monolithic dump of graded proofs.

4. **Fine-tuning demonstration validates utility.** OPC-R1-8B improves 17% over its base model (R1-QWEN3-8B) and matches Gemini-2.5-Pro's majority-voting performance on proof judging. This non-trivial result confirms the dataset's value for training, not just evaluation.

5. **Self-evaluation analysis (Table 3) is informative.** The finding that models are consistently worse at judging their own proofs than others' is non-obvious and has practical implications for agentic systems relying on self-verification.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **MathArena §5.4 comparison rests on an unclear sampling procedure (evidential concern).**  
   In §3.1 (line 103), the paper states: "For the MathArena subset... we only retained solutions with a correct final answer, retrying generation if necessary." This means the proofs in the MathArena subset were not sampled i.i.d. — they were generated by repeatedly sampling until a correct final answer appeared. Then in §5.4, Fig. 5 compares each model's "final-answer accuracy" against "proof correctness." The paper never explicitly states whether the final-answer accuracy column comes from the original MathArena benchmark (standard one-shot evaluation) or from the retried, correct-final-answer-conditioned sample.  

   - If the former: the two columns are computed on different distributions (standard evaluation vs. a subset where the model was retried until it produced a correct final answer). The headline claim — "o3 suffers a drop of almost 30%" — convolves the effect of retrying with the effect of requiring a correct proof.  
   - If the latter: final-answer accuracy is near 100% by construction for the MathArena subset, making the reported comparison meaningless.  

   This ambiguity undermines one of the paper's three stated open questions. The finding that a gap exists is qualitatively plausible and supported by prior work, but the precise numerical magnitudes in Fig. 5 and the associated claims cannot be interpreted without clarification of the sampling procedure used for each column.

### Minor

2. **Best-of-n uses O4-MINI as both generator and judge, with known self-judgment bias (§5.5 / Table 3).**  
   Table 3 shows that O4-MINI's accuracy judging O4-MINI's own proofs is 81.3%, notably lower than its accuracy judging other models' proofs (e.g., 86.9% on Gemini proofs, 84.8% on o3). Yet in §5.5, all four best-of-n selection methods use O4-MINI as the judge to select among O4-MINI's own generations. The paper does not discuss whether this self-judgment bias affects the reported magnitudes or the relative ordering of selection methods. The absolute performance numbers should not be taken as general estimates of what an unbiased judge could achieve.

3. **Human baseline (90.4%) is computed on a different sample than the test set.**  
   The paper acknowledges this in §5.2 (line 246): "the human baseline is not measured on the test subset, but rather on all double-graded proofs in the OPC." It then asserts that "since the test samples are uniformly drawn from the OPC, this does not significantly affect the comparison." However, the double-graded subset is not guaranteed to be a uniform random sample: the pilot phase involved 35% double-grading (vs. ~10% overall), and double-grading was partly triggered by flagged disagreements. The authors should report the human baseline on the actual test subset, or at minimum demonstrate that the difficulty distribution of double-graded proofs matches that of the test set.

4. **LLM issue-summary bias check is weaker than presented.**  
   The paper checks whether introducing O4-MINI-generated issue summaries changes human-O4-MINI agreement (line 115). This tests whether summaries shift human judgments toward O4-MINI's opinions, but does not test whether summaries shift human judgments away from ground truth. A stronger check would compare human-only grading against human+summary grading on a held-out gold-standard set.

### Trivial

5. **Independence assumption in judge error-rate computation.**  
   The individual per-judge error estimate of 5% (solved from \(0.904 = (1-p)^2 + p^2\)) assumes judges' errors are independent. In practice, judges may make correlated errors on the same subtle proof flaws, which would inflate agreement without indicating lower error. This is a rough estimate and should be caveated.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Report confidence intervals explicitly for Fig. 6(b) rather than describing them as "relatively large" without quantification.
- Add a paired statistical test (e.g., McNemar's) for the best-of-n comparisons.
- Move the OPC-R1-8B out-of-distribution evaluation (§C) into the main paper, since it directly addresses the most obvious skepticism about the fine-tuning result.
- For future work: include an unbiased judge (e.g., GPT-5 or Gemini-2.5-Pro) as a control in the best-of-n setup.

## Novel Insights

The review identifies a structural ambiguity in the §5.4 comparison that the paper itself does not flag as a limitation. The MathArena retrying procedure — described in §3.1 as a data-construction detail — has direct implications for one of the paper's headline empirical claims (the gap between final-answer accuracy and proof correctness). The paper presents the two columns in Fig. 5 as if they are directly comparable, but they may arise from different sampling distributions. This is not a fatal flaw (the dataset remains valuable and most other analyses are independent of this point), but it means one of the three "open questions" requires reframing or additional analysis. The review also highlights that the self-judgment bias documented in Table 3 is not carried through to the best-of-n analysis, creating a disconnect between two sections of the paper that should inform each other.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the §5.4 comparison.** Explicitly state whether the "final-answer accuracy" in Fig. 5 comes from standard MathArena evaluation or from the retried subset. If the former, report proof correctness on standard (non-retried) generations, or reframe the finding as "conditioned on the model eventually producing a correct final answer, what fraction of solutions contain valid proofs?" and explain why this differs from the standard final-answer accuracy baseline.

2. **Add a discussion of self-judgment bias to §5.5.** Acknowledge that O4-MINI's known self-judgment difficulty (Table 3) means the best-of-n absolute numbers might differ with an unbiased judge, and clarify that the relative ordering of methods is the primary result.

3. **Report the human baseline on the actual test subset** to eliminate any concern about distribution mismatch, or provide evidence that the double-graded subset's difficulty composition matches the test set.
