## Summary

The paper introduces Marginal Flow, a density estimation framework that defines the model density as a Monte Carlo average over parametric component densities whose parameters are sampled from a learnable distribution (generated by an unconstrained neural network). The approach claims to provide efficient exact likelihood evaluation, fast single-step sampling, flexibility in architecture and training objectives, and the ability to learn densities on lower-dimensional manifolds. Experiments on synthetic data, simulation-based inference, Wishart matrices, and image latent spaces demonstrate competitive performance and orders-of-magnitude speedups over existing methods.

## Strengths

- **Novel and principled idea.** Marginalizing over a learned distribution of latent parameters rather than optimizing a fixed set of mixture components is a conceptually clean way to build flexible density estimators without restrictive architectural constraints.
- **High practical efficiency.** The model achieves superior runtime for both sampling and density evaluation compared to Normalizing Flows, Flow Matching, and Free-form Flows, scaling well to high dimensions (up to 10⁵ in the paper).
- **Flexibility and universality.** The framework accommodates unconstrained neural networks, various parametric families for $q(x|w)$, lower-dimensional base distributions (manifold learning), conditional modeling, and multiple training objectives (forward and reverse KL) without any architectural modifications.
- **Convincing synthetic experiments.** On a range of 2D densities, Marginal Flow accurately recovers the target distribution under both log-likelihood and reverse KL training, often converging orders of magnitude faster than competitors.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

**Misleading claim of “exact density evaluation.”** The model density in Eq. 2 is a Monte Carlo average over $N_c$ samples from $q_\theta(w)$. For any finite $N_c$, the density estimate is stochastic—its value depends on the random draws of $\{w_i\}$. The paper repeatedly states that Marginal Flow provides “exact density evaluation by construction,” but this conflates the model *definition* (as an expectation) with its practical *implementation* (a finite-sample approximation that has non-zero variance). Unlike Normalizing Flows, where the density at a point is a deterministic function, Marginal Flow’s density evaluation is an unbiased estimator that becomes exact only in the limit $N_c\to\infty$. This fundamental issue undermines a core advertised advantage and invalidates direct runtime comparisons where “exact density” is plotted against deterministic methods. The paper neither reports the variance of this estimator nor discusses how $N_c$ should be chosen to achieve a given accuracy.

### Major

- **Limited high-dimensional validation.** The only “high-dimensional” experiments are conducted in VAE latent spaces of dimension 10–20, not on raw data (e.g., pixels). The model’s ability to scale to realistic high-dimensional densities is not demonstrated. The Wishart experiment reaches dimension 5050, but the manifold is extremely low-dimensional (1D), which is a very structured problem.  
- **No quantitative density estimation on image data.** The MNIST and JAFFE examples are purely qualitative (manifold interpolation). There are no likelihood numbers, sample quality metrics, or comparisons to other density models on these datasets.  
- **Theoretical thoroughness is lacking.** The paper asserts universality for kernel choices (Micchelli et al., 2006) but provides no proof or discussion of required conditions. The expressivity of the model for general high-dimensional distributions is not analyzed.

### Minor

- **The paper does not discuss the variance of the density estimator** or how to set $N_c$ in practice for reliable log-likelihood evaluation. This is especially relevant for test-set evaluation and comparison to deterministic models.

### Trivial

- The term “Marginal Flow” is somewhat misleading (no “flow” transformation of densities is involved), but this is a minor nomenclature issue.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A discussion of how $N_c$ affects the bias–variance trade-off in density estimation, along with practical guidelines.
- Results on standard high-dimensional density estimation benchmarks (e.g., image likelihoods on CIFAR-10, tabular UCI datasets) to validate scalability.
- A comparison to other efficient density models such as Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) with learned bandwidth, or mixture models with many components, to clarify when the marginalization is beneficial beyond a large fixed mixture.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper’s own contributions.

## Suggestions

1. **Revise the “exact density” claim.** Clearly distinguish between the model definition (expectation) and the practical estimator (Monte Carlo). Provide an analysis of the estimator’s variance and suggest how to choose $N_c$ to achieve a desired accuracy.  
2. **Add quantitative density estimation on real data.** Report log-likelihoods (or at least bounds) on a standard benchmark to demonstrate the model’s competitiveness in higher dimensions.  
3. **Include a discussion of the trade-offs.** For tasks requiring deterministic likelihood (e.g., variational inference where the density must be evaluated many times), the stochasticity of Marginal Flow may be a liability—acknowledge this.

## Score and Decision

Based on the critical flaw concerning the “exact density” claim and the lack of high-dimensional quantitative evaluation, the paper in its current form does not meet the bar for acceptance. The core idea is interesting and the experiments are promising, but the central selling point is overstated and not properly qualified.
