## Summary

This paper proposes EDPA, a model-agnostic adversarial patch attack for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that disrupts visual understanding by maximizing embedding discrepancies and disrupting semantic alignment between visual and language representations, requiring only access to the encoder parameters. The authors also introduce adversarial fine-tuning for the visual encoder as a defense, encouraging similar latent representations for clean and perturbed inputs. Evaluations on the LIBERO benchmark across multiple VLA models (OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, π₀) show EDPA substantially increases failure rates, while the defense effectively mitigates these attacks with minimal clean performance degradation.

## Strengths

- **Practical attack assumptions**: EDPA requires only encoder parameter access and no knowledge of the action space, robotic manipulator, or VLM backbone parameters, making it significantly more practical than prior attacks like UADA/UPA (Table 1). This is a meaningful contribution over Wang et al. (2024).

- **Comprehensive evaluation across multiple VLA models**: The paper evaluates EDPA on three different VLA architectures (OpenVLA, OpenVLA-OFT, π₀), including both single-camera and multi-camera settings (Tables 2, 3), demonstrating that the attack is genuinely model-agnostic rather than tailored to a single model.

- **Novel defense with practical benefits**: The adversarial fine-tuning scheme is lightweight (only the visual encoder is fine-tuned, not the full VLM backbone) and generalizes to defend against attacks from other methods (UADA, UPA) beyond those generated by EDPA itself (Table 2).

- **Interesting empirical discovery**: The observation that generated adversarial patches consistently resemble robotic arm structures (Section 5, Figure 2) and the hypothesis about visual encoder overfitting due to limited, viewpoint-constrained robotic datasets provide valuable insight into VLA vulnerabilities.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **No physical-world evaluation**: The paper evaluates attacks and defenses entirely in the LIBERO simulation benchmark. While simulation is a reasonable starting point, the primary motivation for patch attacks (over pixel-level perturbations) is their physical-world deployability (printable stickers placed in the camera's view). Without any physical robot experiments or even a sim-to-real transfer analysis, the real-world validity of both the attack and defense remains unsubstantiated.

2. **Defense evaluation only on OpenVLA**: The adversarial fine-tuning defense is evaluated solely on OpenVLA (Section 4.2). The paper explicitly states OpenVLA was chosen because it showed the weakest robustness, but this raises questions about whether the defense would transfer effectively to other models (OpenVLA-OFT, π₀) that use different visual encoders or pretraining procedures.

3. **Lack of detailed comparison with potential alternative defenses**: The defense is compared only against the pre-attack baseline. There is no comparison with standard adversarial training baselines, certified defenses, or input preprocessing defenses that have been explored for general vision-language models, making it difficult to contextualize the defense's relative effectiveness.

### Minor
1. **Patch placement strategy not specified**: While the patch is applied via a binary mask (Equation 1), there is no discussion of where in the image the patch is placed (fixed position, random placement, or optimized placement). Patch position can significantly impact attack effectiveness, and this omission limits reproducibility.

2. **Limited analysis of the trade-off between attack objectives**: The two losses (ℒ_patch and ℒ_align) are combined with a fixed α₁=0.8. The paper mentions EMA normalization but does not provide ablation studies showing how different α₁ values affect attack success, making it unclear which loss component is more critical.

3. **Only one baseline comparison for multi-camera models**: For OpenVLA-OFT and π₀, EDPA is only compared against random noise and clean conditions. While UADA/UPA are noted as difficult to transfer, some adaptation or approximation could have provided a stronger comparative baseline beyond random noise.

### Trivial
- The paper would benefit from error bars in Figure 2 patch visualizations (though these are inherently qualitative).
- Some cited references in the ethics statement are not fully listed (e.g., "UADA" and "UPA" citations omitted).

## Nice-to-Haves
- Physical-world experiments (e.g., printing patches and placing them in a real robot camera view) would substantially strengthen the practical claims.
- Ablation studies varying α₁ (patch vs. alignment loss weighting) and α₂ (clean vs. adversarial objective weighting in defense).
- Evaluation of the defense on OpenVLA-OFT and π₀ to test transferability.
- Adaptive attack evaluation where the attacker is aware of the defense mechanism.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most novel insight is the hypothesis connecting adversarial patch structure to visual encoder overfitting on limited-viewpoint robotic data. The observation that patches consistently mimic robotic arm appearances (Figure 2) and that models processing more diverse viewpoints (π₀ > OpenVLA-OFT > OpenVLA) show progressively better robustness suggests a fundamental vulnerability in how VLA models learn visual representations from structurally limited datasets. This insight could guide future work on data augmentation and viewpoint diversification as a complementary defense strategy, independent of the specific adversarial training approach proposed.

## Suggestions
- Conduct at least a preliminary physical-world evaluation or sim-to-real transfer experiment to validate real-world applicability.
- Evaluate the adversarial fine-tuning defense on at least one additional VLA model to demonstrate broader applicability.
- Add ablation studies for the key hyperparameters α₁ and α₂ to provide deeper understanding of the method's sensitivity.
