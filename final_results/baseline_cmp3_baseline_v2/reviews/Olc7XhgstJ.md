## Summary

This paper proposes Steady Thought (ST), a thought-level preference optimization framework to mitigate the "under-thinking" phenomenon in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), where models excessively switch between reasoning trajectories and fail to commit to promising lines of reasoning. ST operates in three stages: (1) entropy-based thought segmentation, (2) thought completion via logit suppression of switch-indicating tokens, and (3) fine-grained preference optimization (STPO) that trains the model to prefer completing a promising thought over switching to new trajectories. Experiments across multiple model sizes (1.5B-14B) and benchmarks show accuracy improvements of up to 5.3% with token reductions of 19-39%.

## Strengths

- **Novel problem formalization**: The paper formalizes under-thinking as a preference optimization problem at the thought level, providing a principled framework (Equation 2) that connects the observed phenomenon to a tractable training objective. This is a meaningful conceptual contribution beyond prior suppression-based approaches.
- **Strong empirical results**: The method demonstrates consistent improvements across three model families and four datasets, including an out-of-distribution (LiveCode) benchmark. The 5.3% accuracy gain on Qwen3-8B LiveCode with 19% token reduction is particularly compelling evidence of generalization.
- **Well-designed ablation studies**: Section 4.4 provides thorough analysis of the method's mechanisms, including the proportion of correct thoughts (Table 2), entropy threshold sensitivity (Table 3), and comparison with alternative training methods (Table 4). These analyses convincingly support the claim that ST teaches more judicious switching rather than simply suppressing all switching.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Limited baseline comparisons**: The paper compares against NoThink, NOWAIT, and SEAL, but these are primarily inference-time intervention methods. The most relevant comparison would be against other training-based approaches for mitigating under-thinking or improving reasoning efficiency, such as reinforcement learning with length penalties or other preference optimization methods applied at the response level. Without these comparisons, it's unclear whether ST's advantage comes from the thought-level formulation or simply from using preference optimization with a well-constructed dataset.
- **Potential data contamination concern**: The training data (omni-math) and evaluation datasets (MATH-500, AIME 2024, GSM8K) are all math benchmarks. While LiveCode serves as an OOD test, the paper doesn't discuss whether any problems in the training set overlap with evaluation sets. Given that omni-math contains competition-level problems, overlap with AIME 2024 is plausible and should be addressed.

### Minor
- **Entropy threshold sensitivity**: The optimal threshold (3.0) is determined empirically, but the paper only shows results for three threshold values (2.8, 3.0, 3.2) on one model. The sensitivity analysis would be stronger with more granular threshold exploration and results on additional models.
- **Thought completion stage overhead**: The paper mentions discussing "consumption generated by this stage" in Appendix E, but the appendix is stripped. The computational cost of generating completions for each thought (which requires running the model multiple times per training example) is a practical concern that should be addressed in the main text.

### Trivial
- The paper uses "↓" arrows next to accuracy and token columns in Table 1, which is confusing since higher accuracy is better (should be ↑) while lower tokens is better (should be ↓). The arrows appear to be formatting artifacts.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Analysis of how ST affects the model's behavior on problems where the base model already gets the correct answer—does it still reduce tokens without harming accuracy?
- Discussion of failure cases: are there problems where ST's reduced switching leads to worse performance?
- Comparison with a variant that uses DPO instead of SimPO at the thought level, to isolate the benefit of length normalization from the thought-level formulation.

## Novel Insights

The key insight is that under-thinking can be addressed not by globally suppressing switching behavior (which risks discarding useful exploration), but by teaching the model to recognize and commit to promising intermediate thoughts through fine-grained preference optimization. This reframes the problem from "how to stop switching" to "how to decide when to switch," which is a more nuanced and potentially more effective approach. The thought-level preference optimization formulation, where the preference pair is constructed from the divergence point onward rather than from the entire response, provides a principled way to deliver this learning signal.

## Suggestions
- Add comparisons with training-based baselines (e.g., DPO/SimPO applied to full responses, or RL with length penalties) to isolate the benefit of the thought-level formulation.
- Report the computational cost of the thought completion stage (e.g., average number of model calls per training example) and discuss how this scales.
- Include a discussion of potential data overlap between training and evaluation sets, and if possible, verify that no evaluation problems appear in the training data.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a well-motivated, technically sound approach to an important problem in LLM reasoning. The empirical results are strong and consistent across multiple models and datasets. The main limitation is the lack of comparison against training-based baselines, which makes it difficult to fully assess the contribution of the thought-level formulation. However, the paper's strengths—particularly the novel problem formalization, thorough ablation studies, and strong OOD generalization—warrant acceptance.
