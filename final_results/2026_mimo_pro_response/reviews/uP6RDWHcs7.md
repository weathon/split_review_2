## Summary
This paper introduces "Marginal Flow," a density estimation framework that defines a model q_θ(x) as a finite mixture of a parametric family q(x|w) whose parameters w are generated by feeding base-distribution noise through an unconstrained neural network f_θ and resampled at each training iteration. The framework claims to offer exact density evaluation, efficient single-step sampling, unconstrained architecture, and manifold learning simultaneously — a combination no prior method achieves.

## Strengths
- **Novel marginalization concept well-motivated by Figure 1**: The core idea of resampling mixture components from a learned distribution at each iteration (rather than optimizing fixed components) is a genuine conceptual contribution. Figure 1 directly demonstrates the difference: with N_c=10, the GMM produces discrete overlapping blobs while Marginal Flow produces a smooth density, supporting the claim that "modeling capacity is not directly linked to N_c anymore" (Section 2.1).
- **Orders-of-magnitude speedups well-evidenced**: Figure 3 shows concrete empirical evidence across dimensions 10²–10⁵ with Marginal Flow consistently orders of magnitude faster than NF, FM, and FFF for both sampling and density evaluation. NF and FM hit OOM at high dimensions while Marginal Flow scales gracefully. Figure 7 confirms this across all five synthetic datasets with faster convergence vs. wall-clock time.
- **Unique combination of properties (Table 1)**: Marginal Flow is the only framework that simultaneously provides efficient density evaluation, efficient single-step sampling, unconstrained architecture, and lower-dimensional manifold support — NF lacks free-form Jacobians and manifold support; GANs/FFF lack exact likelihood; FM has neither exact likelihood nor manifold support.
- **Strong quantitative Wishart experiment**: Section 4.3 demonstrates that swapping q(x|w) from Gaussian to Wishart enables learning distributions on positive-definite matrices. Marginal Flow achieves ~0.0088 test KL vs. ~0.82 for NF on 10×10 Wishart mixtures, and scales to 100×100 matrices (d=5050) where NF was computationally prohibitive. This is a strong, quantitative demonstration of the framework's flexibility.
- **Supports both forward and reverse KL training**: The reverse KL results (Section 4.1, Figure 8) show Marginal Flow matches or outperforms NF on synthetic datasets, demonstrating that the framework's dual efficiency in sampling and density evaluation enables flexible training objectives that were previously restricted to NF only.
- **Effective manifold learning demonstrated across scales**: By choosing m < d in the base distribution, Marginal Flow learns densities on lower-dimensional manifolds without architectural modifications (Figure 4, MNIST Figure 10, JAFFE Figure 11). The JAFFE experiment at only 214 images is a particularly challenging low-data regime.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **"Exact density" claim requires more nuanced treatment**: The model is defined in Eq. 2 as q_θ(x) := (1/N_c) Σ q(x|w_{θ,i}) where w_{θ,i} ~ q_θ(w). The paper explicitly states "the {w_{θ,i}} are not fixed but sampled again for each evaluation or sampling of q_θ(x)" (Section 2.2). This means the density function is stochastic — evaluating q_θ(x) at the same point x yields different values depending on the draw of z_i. This differs from NF's deterministic density computed via change-of-variables. While the density IS exact for the model as defined (a finite mixture), Table 1 places checkmarks for both NF and Marginal Flow under "Efficient exact likelihood" without acknowledging this meaningful distinction. The paper should: (a) discuss the variance of density estimates across different z_i draws, (b) analyze how this variance depends on N_c, and (c) clarify that "exact" means exact for the stochastic finite-mixture model, not deterministic as in NF.
- **No systematic sensitivity analysis on N_c**: The number of mixture components N_c determines both computational cost and model quality at test time. The paper claims "the modeling capacity is not directly linked to N_c" (Section 2.1) but provides no systematic analysis of how performance varies with N_c. Figure 1 uses N_c=10 as a motivating example, but no test log-likelihood curves or variance estimates as a function of N_c are reported. This is a critical gap: without this analysis, it is impossible to assess whether the method's results depend on a particular N_c setting.
- **Experimental evidence insufficient for broad claims**: The paper claims to "overcome these limitations altogether" (Abstract) and presents Table 1 with checkmarks in every category, but the experiments are limited to 2D synthetic data, MNIST/JAFFE latent spaces, and 214 JAFFE images. The MNIST and JAFFE experiments are purely qualitative — no quantitative metrics (FID, held-out log-likelihood) are reported. The SBI results (quantitative, C2ST with SOTA) are deferred to the appendix. No standard density estimation benchmark (e.g., UCI tabular datasets standard for NF evaluation) is used. For a paper making such sweeping claims, at least one experiment on a standard quantitative benchmark is needed.

### Minor
- **Final test performance not reported numerically**: The convergence plots (Figure 7) show training curves vs. wall-clock time but not final test log-likelihood values with error bars. Reporting these would clarify whether Marginal Flow converges faster to competitive performance or just to a worse solution.
- **Comparison asymmetry in multi-modal experiment (Figure 5)**: Using a uniform base distribution "for a fair comparison" disadvantages bijective models (NF, FM) since the base's modality must match the target's. While Marginal Flow's advantage here is real (its non-bijective structure inherently handles modality changes), the paper should acknowledge this asymmetry rather than framing it as "fair."

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Connection to kernel density estimation: When q(x|w) = N(w, σ²I), Marginal Flow with fixed w_i is closely related to KDE. The paper should clarify what the network f_θ adds beyond standard KDE with learnable kernels.
- Quantitative metrics for MNIST/JAFFE: Even a simple metric (e.g., held-out log-likelihood in VAE latent space) would strengthen these qualitative demonstrations.
- More details on runtime comparison: Figure 3 should specify architectures, parameter counts, and latent dimensions used for each method to make the speed comparison fully transparent.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:
- Harsh critic's assertion that the "exact density" claim is fatally misleading: The model IS defined as Eq. 2 (a finite mixture), and for that model the density IS exactly computable. The concern about stochastic density evaluation is valid and kept as a Major weakness, but it does not invalidate the approach — it requires clarification, not abandonment.
- Strength finder's claim that Table 1 demonstrates a "genuine advance": While the combination of properties is real, the paper's experiments don't fully verify all claimed advantages (e.g., efficient training is shown only on small problems). The combination claim is kept as a strength but with the caveat that not all entries are experimentally verified.

## Novel Insights
The paper's genuinely novel contribution is the observation that by resampling mixture component parameters from a learned distribution at each training iteration (rather than optimizing fixed components), one can decouple modeling capacity from the number of components N_c while maintaining efficient density evaluation. This insight — that stochastic generation of components via a neural network provides implicit regularization and prevents collapse to a GMM — is well-motivated by Figure 1 and offers a new perspective on the mixture-model-vs-flexibility tradeoff. The plug-and-play parametric family choice (demonstrated via Wishart at d=5050) and the ability to support both forward and reverse KL training are genuine practical advantages.

## Suggestions
1. Add a brief empirical analysis of the variance of q_θ(x) across different draws of z_i, as a function of N_c. Even one synthetic dataset would substantially strengthen the "exact density" claim.
2. Report final test log-likelihood values with standard deviations across multiple runs on the synthetic benchmarks.
3. Add one quantitative benchmark on standard density estimation data (e.g., UCI) to match the broad claims in Table 1.
4. Quantify the MNIST/JAFFE experiments with at least one metric.
5. Clarify in Table 1 or a footnote the distinction between Marginal Flow's exact density (exact for a stochastic model realization) and NF's exact density (deterministic).
