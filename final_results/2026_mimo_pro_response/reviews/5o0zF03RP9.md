## Summary

This paper introduces IncentRL, a framework that augments RL rewards with a KL-divergence penalty between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), combined with a claimed Bayesian scheme for adapting the weighting parameter β. The paper presents theoretical propositions about extreme-β behavior and reports experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8.

## Strengths

- **Genuine empirical gains on MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 (Figure 2, Section 5.4):** IncentRL with β=0.01 achieves ~98% success rate vs ~90.5% baseline, with mean episode length dropping from 75 to 29 steps over 2M timesteps. This is a concrete improvement in a non-trivial sparse-reward navigation task requiring key-finding, door-unlocking, and goal-reaching.
- **Transparent sensitivity analysis (Tables 1, 2):** The paper honestly reports that higher β values degrade MountainCar performance (−75% at β=1.0), providing useful practical guidance about failure modes rather than hiding them.
- **Well-articulated conceptual distinction from FEP (Sections 1, 2.3):** The paper carefully and consistently distinguishes IncentRL from active inference by noting that IncentRL assumes fixed preferences and shapes behavior, whereas FEP adapts preferences to reduce surprise.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian β adaptation mechanism — the paper's declared central novelty — is never specified.** The abstract states "the central novelty is treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online." The contributions list identifies "Bayesian adaptation (novelty)" as the first item. The conclusion claims this "removes the long-standing need for manual trade-off tuning." Yet the Methods section (Section 3) contains only the shaped reward r_shaped = r_ext − β · KL(p||q) with a fixed β. There is no prior distribution for β, no likelihood model, no posterior update rule, and no algorithmic description of how β is adapted online. Figures 3–4 show "posterior concentration" across "rounds," but what constitutes a round, how the posterior is updated between rounds, and what evidence it conditions on are never described. The paper presents its primary contribution without ever explaining how it works.

### Major

- **Outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and q(o|s) are radically underspecified for the non-toy experiments.** For MountainCar (continuous state space), the paper says "q(o|s) assigns all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)" and "p(o|s, a) is the predicted outcome distribution" (Section 5.3) — but what is o? Is it the next state? A terminal outcome? How is KL computed over continuous states? For MiniGrid (discrete grid navigation), there is no description whatsoever of how p and q are defined (Section 5.4). Only in the toy MDP (Section 5.2) are p and q concretely specified as distributions over {s₀, s₁}. This makes the two non-trivial experiments irreproducible and leaves the core mechanism undefined where it matters most.
- **No comparison to any intrinsic motivation or reward shaping baseline.** The paper's Related Work (Section 2.1) discusses ICM, empowerment, VIME, RND, and potential-based reward shaping, yet the experiments compare only against β=0 (vanilla Q-learning/DQN). The abstract's claim of improvements over "fixed-regularization baselines" refers only to IncentRL with different fixed β values, not competing methods. Without any external baseline, it is impossible to determine whether gains come from the specific KL mechanism or simply from having any additional dense reward signal.
- **MountainCar results directly contradict the paper's central message.** Table 2 shows β=0.1 yields +18% improvement, but β=0.3 yields −53% and β=1.0 yields −75%. This extreme sensitivity demonstrates that β is a critical hyperparameter requiring careful tuning — the opposite of the paper's claim that Bayesian adaptation "removes the long-standing need for manual trade-off tuning." The paper acknowledges this in Section 6.2 ("KL dominance") but frames it as future work rather than engaging with the contradiction.

### Minor

- **Bayesian adaptation results are internally inconsistent with the paper's claims.** The paper states β "concentrates near the effective region (β ≈ 0.1)" (Section 5.4), but Figure 3's own caption states "The posterior mean starts at approximately 0.11 in Round 1 and decreases to about 0.02 by Round 4." Table 3 confirms: mean β goes from 0.106 → 0.045 → 0.019 → 0.017 across rounds. The posterior concentrates at β ≈ 0.02, a value 5× lower than the claimed "effective" β=0.1, and one never directly validated in fixed-β experiments (MiniGrid tested only β=0.01 and β=0.0).
- **Theoretical results are vacuously general and not specific to IncentRL.** Proposition 1 (for sufficiently small β, optimal policy is preserved) is a standard perturbation/continuity argument holding for any additive bonus/penalty. Proposition 2 (as β→∞, KL dominates) is trivially true for any non-negative penalty. Neither proposition characterizes when IncentRL is beneficial or distinguishes it from any other reward shaping approach.
- **Very few seeds with no statistical tests.** MountainCar and MiniGrid use only 3 seeds with no confidence intervals or statistical significance tests.
