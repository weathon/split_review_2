\title{MINT: Evaluating LLMs in Multi-turn Interaction with Tools and Language Feedback}

\begin{abstract}
To solve complex tasks, large language models (LLMs) often require multiple rounds of interactions with the user, sometimes assisted by external tools.
However, current evaluation protocols often emphasize benchmark performance with single-turn exchanges, neglecting the nuanced interactions among the user, LLMs, and external tools, while also underestimating the importance of natural language feedback from users. These oversights contribute to discrepancies between research benchmark evaluations and real-world use cases.
We introduce \textbf{\approach}, a benchmark that evaluates LLMs' ability to solve challenging tasks with \textbf{m}ulti-turn \textbf{int}eractions by (1) using tools and (2) leveraging natural language feedback.
To ensure reproducibility, we provide an evaluation framework where LLMs can access tools by executing Python code and receive users' natural language feedback simulated by GPT-4.
We repurpose a diverse set of established evaluation datasets focusing on reasoning, coding, and decision-making and carefully curate them into a compact subset for efficient evaluation.
Our analysis of 20 open- and closed-source LLMs offers intriguing findings.
(a) LLMs generally benefit from tools and language feedback, with performance gains (absolute, same below) of 1--8\% for each turn of tool use and 2--17\% with natural language feedback.
(b) Better single-turn performance does not guarantee better multi-turn performance.
(c) Surprisingly, among the evaluated LLMs, supervised instruction-finetuning (SIFT) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) generally hurt multi-turn capabilities.
We expect \approach can help measure progress and incentivize research in improving LLMs' capabilities in multi-turn interactions, especially for open-source communities where multi-turn human evaluation can be less accessible compared to commercial LLMs with a larger user base.
\footnote{Code is available on our project website: \url{https://xingyaoww.io/mint-bench}}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


To address complex tasks, Large Language Models (LLMs) often need multiple rounds of interaction with the users, sometimes aided by external tools \citep{schick2023toolformer, chatgpt_plugins, mialon2023augmented}.
LLMs' performance during multiple turns of user-LLM exchanges is crucial in real-world applications: roughly 73\% of Human-ChatGPT conversations contain more than one turn based on 94k entries of \cite{sharegpt_data}. 
Meanwhile, the ability to adapt to user-provided natural language feedback is also pivotal for their practical utility.
However, current LLM evaluations predominantly focus on \textit{single-turn} input-output \citep{hendrycks2020measuring,chen2021evaluating} and often overlook user-provided natural language feedback \citep{liu2023agentbench,deng2023mind2web,yang2023intercode,shridhar2020alfworld}, creating a discrepancy between real-world use cases and evaluation.
Measuring how much LLMs can benefit from \textbf{both} tools and natural language feedback during \textbf{multi-turn interaction} is essential to incentivize future research to improve LLMs' capabilities in a broader range of real-world scenarios.

To bridge these gaps, we introduce \approach.
It is a benchmark for LLMs that measures their performance during \textbf{multi-turn interaction}, focusing on two particular capabilities (\sref{sec:interaction-framework}): (1)~\textbf{tool-augmented task-solving}; (2)~\textbf{leveraging natural language feedback}.
\approach mirrors the real-world user-LLM-tool collaborative problem-solving setting. To solve a problem, the LLM can use external tools by generating and executing Python programs \citep{wang2024executable} and/or collecting natural language feedback to refine its solutions; the feedback is provided by GPT-4 \citep{openai2023gpt4}, aiming to simulate human users in a reproducible and scalable way.\footnote{We use \texttt{gpt-4-0613} version in this work.}
For a comprehensive evaluation, we include eight established datasets spanning reasoning, code generation, and decision-making (\sref{sec:repurpose-datasets}).
To facilitate affordable multi-turn evaluation, after collecting 29,307 diverse instances from existing datasets (\tref{tab:dataset-size}), we construct a subset of $586$ challenging and representative instances that require multi-turn interaction to solve\footnote{Evaluating an LLM using \approach costs around 100 USD ($\approx 3$M prompt tokens and $\approx 100$K completion tokens) with feedback from \texttt{gpt-4-0613} (\$0.03/1K prompt tokens and \$0.06/1K completion tokens), roughly 7\% of the cost compared to hiring real-human annotators (\sref{sec:human-evaluation-of-feedback}).
}.
We evaluate 4 closed- and 16 open-source LLMs with \approach.
We measure LLMs' \textbf{tool-augmented task-solving capability} by analyzing their performance from multi-turn tool use (\sref{sec:effect-of-interaction}). 
To assess the \textbf{ability to leverage natural language feedback}, we measure their performance upon natural language feedback by GPT-4 (\sref{sec:effect-of-feedback}).
Our results show that: 
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,partopsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
\item All models benefit from tool interaction and natural language feedback, with absolute performance gains by 1--8\% for each additional turn of tool use, and 2--17\% with natural language feedback.
\item Better single-turn performance does \textit{not} necessarily entail better multi-turn performance. For example, while Claude-2 outperforms its predecessor Claude-1 in single-turn evaluation, the latter benefit more from interaction and performs better with $>2$ turns. 
\item There is a notable gap between open- and closed-source LLMs in multi-turn interaction performance. For example, with the help of language feedback, even the best open-source model, Lemur-70b-chat-v1, lags behind the best closed-source model by 8.7\% in absolute success rate.
\item On most LLMs we evaluated, models trained with supervised instruction fine-tuning (SIFT, \citealp{DBLP:conf/iclr/WeiBZGYLDDL22}) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF, \citealp{ouyang2022training}) perform worse in multi-turn settings regardless of the presence of language feedback.
For example, SIFT hurts Codellama-34B's multi-turn performance by $11.1\%$ and $15.4\%$ (w/ feedback), and RLHF negatively affects LLaMA-2-70B by $8.5\%$ and $8.7\%$, respectively.
Notable exceptions are Vicuna-7B and Lemur-70b-chat-v1, where SIFT improves multi-turn interaction.
\end{itemize}

By fixing the LLM to evaluate and changing the feedback-provider LLM, \approach can measure different LLMs' capabilities in \textit{providing useful feedback} (\sref{sec:efficacy-of-LLM-in-providing-feedback}); We find that feedback-providing ability could be orthogonal to task-solving ability: despite performing the worst in task-solving, CodeLLaMA-34B-Instruct can provide feedback to improve the stronger GPT-3.5.
Additionally, \approach's challenging evaluation reveals undesired artifacts in \citet{sharegpt_data}, a widely used dataset for instruction tuning (\sref{sec:debug-llm}).
Furthermore, we show that GPT4-simulated language feedback is as helpful as human-written feedback based on human evaluation and task performance (\sref{sec:human-evaluation-of-feedback}). 

We expect that \approach can help track progress and incentivize future research in improving LLM's multi-turn task-solving and/or feedback-providing capabilities, especially for open-source communities where human evaluation can be less accessible than commercial LLMs with a large user base.

\subsection{Interaction Framework}
\label{sec:interaction-framework}

\approach aims to simulate real-world applications of LLMs, emphasizing user-LLM and LLM-tool interactions. In user-LLM collaborative problem-solving, a human user provides initial instruction and aims to obtain a correct solution with little effort to help the LLM.
On the other hand, augmenting LLMs' with tools can effectively improve LLMs' task-solving capabilities \citep{mialon2023augmented}, suggesting the importance of LLM-tool interaction.
We instruct the LLM (\sref{sec:agent-template}) to perform the following steps \textit{in each turn}: \textbf{(1)}~optionally express its reasoning process (``Thought:" in \fref{fig:illustrative-example}, similar to \citealt{yao2022react}); \textbf{(2)}~then either interact with tools by generating Python code and executing it through a Python interpreter (``Execute:" in \fref{fig:illustrative-example}), or proposing a solution to the user (``Propose Solution:" in \fref{fig:illustrative-example}).
We adopt code as a unified tool interface due to its flexibility and performance, as demonstrated by \cite{wang2024executable}.
In our implementation, the model is instructed to wrap their ``Execute" and ``Propose Solution" actions with pairs of \texttt{<execute>} and \texttt{<solution>} tags for ease of parsing. We standardize the prompts and in-context examples for different LLM variants (base vs.
In what follows, we introduce three settings with increased interaction complexities to measure different aspects of multi-turn interaction.

\textbf{LLMs interacting with a lazy user.}
We consider the scenario where a user provides an initial instruction and makes \textit{minimal} efforts to guide the LLM toward the final solution.
This setting covers real-world problems that are ``hard to solve directly, but easy to verify'': the user may not know how to solve the problem or understand the reasoning process, but it is still possible for the user to provide minimal feedback on the final outcome (e.g., ``this is not what I want'').
This will serve as a baseline for evaluating LLM's ability to solve tool-augmented tasks and leverage natural language feedback.
The LLM is given \textit{two} attempts to propose solutions for each problem, with a limit on the number of interaction turns $k$ (\sref{sec:experiment-setup}).
Upon a proposed solution, \approach simulates users that check the solution's correctness with ground truths. When the first attempt is wrong, the user responds ``Your answer is wrong."
The interaction ends either after the LLM has made two attempts to propose a solution, or when a proposed solution is correct (5th turn of \fref{fig:illustrative-example}), or when the $k$-th turn of interaction is reached.
We consider this as the case of \textit{Lazy User-LLM Interaction} since the simulated user provides \textit{at most one} additional binary feedback during interaction.
We follow standard evaluation practice and use established evaluation metrics for each task in \sref{sec:repurpose-datasets}.

\textbf{LLMs interacting with a lazy user and tools.}
Under the lazy User-LLM interaction setting, we measure the LLM's ability to solve tasks using tools by comparing their task-solving success rate across different interaction limits $k$.
For each turn, the LLM can choose to interact with tools (generate code to call equation-solver in \fref{fig:illustrative-example}) or propose a solution (5th turn in \fref{fig:illustrative-example}).
To keep the LLM from getting stuck in an infinite loop of tool-calling without proposing a solution, \approach reminds the LLM: ``You have X steps left and Y chances to propose solution left," and provides an additional instruction at the last turn: ``You should take the last step to propose a solution."
Intuitively, with more interaction with tools, the LLM can get more useful observations through the Python interpreter (e.g., calculation results, error messages).
We vary $k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ and compare the models' success rate with each $k$. We consider LLM's performance gain w.r.t. $k$ and the absolute performance at $k=5$ as their \textbf{tool-augmented task-solving ability} (\sref{sec:effect-of-interaction}).

\textbf{Informative user-LLM interaction with language feedback.}
Beyond lazy User-LLM interaction, we investigate how the LLM performs when the user mirrors a patient teacher who provides useful suggestions (e.g., technical users who understand the problem-solving process).
However, collecting human language feedback for LLM evaluation presents reproducibility challenges due to inconsistent standards and can be costly, particularly for open-source communities with relatively fewer resources\footnotemark.
To address these issues, we prompt GPT-4 (\sref{sec:feedback-template}) to simulate user language feedback (dotted boxes in \fref{fig:illustrative-example}).  
We validate the effectiveness of GPT-4 feedback in a human evaluation (\sref{sec:human-evaluation-of-feedback}).
We compare the performance between (1) simulated language feedback and (2) lazy user-LLM interaction, both in the setting of tool-augmented interaction with an interaction limit $k=5$.
We consider performance (absolute) and improvements from language feedback as LLM's \textbf{ability to leverage natural language feedback}.

\footnotetext{
Based on our human evaluation (\sref{sec:human-evaluation-of-feedback}, \sref{sec:human-evaluation-details}), we estimate annotators, on average, take 96 seconds to provide language feedback for \textit{one turn}, which translates to 90 USD per 100 feedback with hourly wage of US workers.
}
\subsection{Repurposing Existing Datasets for \approach}
\label{sec:repurpose-datasets}
 
Evaluating LLMs in multi-turn interaction can be costly due to the need for iterative inference. For instance, HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa} has 7,405 test examples. Evaluation with five turns requires at least 7,405 $\times$ 5 = 37K LLM inference runs. Previous methods \citep{yao2022react, shinn2023reflexion} choose to evaluate on randomly drawn test examples, increasing the barriers to fair comparisons. 
We select \textbf{diverse} tasks from established datasets that \textbf{requires multi-turn interaction to solve} while also maintaining the selected subset \textbf{compact} for accessible evaluation. 
The following paragraph describes our three-step approach to repurposing datasets for \approach.
We provide dataset sources and statistics in \tref{tab:dataset-size}.

\input{tables/data_stats}

\noindent\textbf{Data sources of \approach.}
Our primary goal is to create a comprehensive evaluation covering tasks that benefit from interaction. We choose three types of tasks:
\setlist{nolistsep}
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,partopsep=0pt,leftmargin=*]
    \itemsep0em 
    \item Reasoning, including math reasoning (GSM8K, MATH, TheoremQA), multi-hop question answering (HotpotQA), and knowledge problem-solving (MMLU). We implicitly filter out knowledge-intensive questions that do not require multi-step reasoning in the next step.
    \item Code generation, including HumanEval and MBPP.
    \item Decision-making tasks in ALFWorld, an embodied household simulator with a text-only interface based on TextWorld \citep{DBLP:conf/ijcai/CoteKYKBFMHAATT18}.
\end{itemize}
From eight datasets, we create an initial test set of 29,307 instances.
All instances are initially designed for single-round evaluation without interaction, except for decision-making (ALFWorld).
Similarly to \cite{yao2022react,DBLP:conf/icml/GaoMZ00YCN23}, we adapt reasoning tasks into multi-turn interaction tasks by augmented LLM with tools for problem-solving (\sref{sec:icl-reasoning}).
Through prompting (\sref{sec:icl-code}), we encourage LLMs to use the Python interpreter to test their generated code on the provided public test suite for code generation problems before committing to a solution. 

\noindent\textbf{Keeping instances that require multi-turn interaction.}
To better answer our research question ``how LLM benefits from multi-turn interaction," we only keep instances that are challenging and require multi-turn interaction.
Since we allow LLM to propose solutions more than once, we filter out instances that a random guess baseline can do well, e.g., multiple-choice instances with $<4$ options.
We then run \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} \citep{openai_api} on the initial dataset and exclude instances finished within two turns (e.g., easy problems that can be solved without multi-turn).

\noindent\textbf{Stratified sub-sampling for efficient evaluation.}
We use stratified sampling \citep{neyman1992two} to create a representative set of 586 examples, ensuring that the ratio of correct to incorrect examples in the resulting set mirrors that of the original data to balance the difficulty of the resulting samples.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:experiment-setup}

\textbf{Evaluated LLMs.}
To comprehensively measure multi-turn interaction capability and identify the potential gap between open- and closed-source LLMs, we evaluate 4 closed- and 16 open-source LLMs. We cover different sizes and training techniques to better understand how they affect LLMs' multi-turn interaction capability.
Training techniques lead to three model variants: pre-trained (base) models, supervised instruction fine-tuned (SIFT, \citealp{DBLP:conf/iclr/WeiBZGYLDDL22}) models, and models trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF, \citealp{ouyang2022training}).
For closed-source models, we evaluate popular commercial LLMs, including \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} from \cite{openai_api}; \texttt{claude-instant-1, claude-2} from Anthropic \cite{claude_api}\footnote{According to \url{https://docs.anthropic.2 for \texttt{claude-instant-1} and v2.0 for \texttt{claude-2}.}; Bard \texttt{chat-bison-001} from \cite{bard_api}.
For open-source LLMs, we evaluate the \texttt{LLaMA-2} model family (7B, 13B, 70B) \citep{touvron2023llama}, including \texttt{base} and \texttt{chat} (RLHF); \texttt{Vicuna-v1.5} (7B, 13B) \citep{zheng2023judging}, a SIFT model fine-tuned on multi-turn conversations based on \texttt{LLaMA-2-base};
the \texttt{CodeLLaMA} model family (7B, 13B, 34B) \citep{roziere2023code} that pre-train \texttt{LLaMA-2-base} on code, including \texttt{base} and \texttt{instruct} (SIFT); \texttt{Lemur-v1-70B} \citep{xu2023lemur} pre-train \texttt{LLaMA-2} on code intensive data, including \texttt{base} and \texttt{chat} (SIFT).

\textbf{Metric.}
We consider \textbf{Success Rate $SR$} as our evaluation metric, which measures the percentage of successful task instances.
For interaction limit $k$, we start from scratch and allow each LLM to interact up to the $k$-th turn and measure their corresponding $SR_k$.
Unless otherwise noted, we limit $k\in \left[1, 5\right]$ where $k=1$ means no interaction and $k=5$ maximizes interaction turns within most modern LLMs' context window (4,096 tokens).

\subsection{Measuring LLM's Tool-augmented Task-solving in Multi-turn Interaction}
\label{sec:effect-of-interaction}

We ask LLMs to solve tasks (\sref{sec:repurpose-datasets}) with different interaction limits $k \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ \textit{without} natural language feedback (\fref{fig:illustrative-example} without red dotted box), and quantify LLMs' tool-augmented task-solving capability by (1) absolute performance $SR_5$ and (2) improvement per additional interaction turn $\Delta_\text{tools}$ estimated as the slope $b$ from least-square regression $\min_{b, a} \sum_{k} (b \cdot k+a - SR_k)^2$ (\tref{tab:sr-with-interaction}). Since the underlying $SR_k$ vs. $k$ relationship might not be linear, we only use the regression coefficient (with $R^2$) as a rough estimate of the improvement rate to complement the absolute success rate $SR_5$ for a more comprehensive understanding of the models' capabilities.

\input{tables/sr_with_k}
\textbf{Overall observations.} In \fref{fig:sr-vs-max-iteration}, we find all open-source models fall behind best commercial closed-source models in both $SR_5$ and $\Delta_\text{tools}$, with \texttt{claude-2} and \texttt{claude-instant-1} surpassing all open-source LLMs in $\Delta_\text{tools}$ with high $R^2$, suggesting near-linear improvement.
Notably, despite performing badly at $k=1$, \texttt{claude-instant-1} surpasses \texttt{claude-2} as $k$ increases to 3, eventually achieving a higher $SR_5$ ($45.9$\% vs. $39.9$\%), suggesting \texttt{claude-instant-1}'s superior ability to improve with multi-turn interaction.
\textbf{Absolute performance and improvement-per-turn scale with model size.} 
For open-source \texttt{CodeLLaMA} and \texttt{LLaMA-2}, we observe a trend on all variants (Base, SIFT, and RLHF) that $\Delta_\text{tools}$ and $SR_5$ increase when scaling up LLMs.
As we discuss in \sref{sec:debug-llm}, \texttt{Vicuna-v1.5} models are an exception, potentially due to their training artifacts that hurt task performance.

\textbf{SIFT on multi-turn data can be helpful.} Despite the issue above, \texttt{Vicuna-v1.5} (7B, SIFT) does show stronger performance compared to \texttt{LLaMA-2} (Base and RLHF, 7B) in $\Delta_\text{tools}$ ($+3.4$\% vs. $+2.2$\% / $+1.5$\%) and $SR_5$ ($12.6$\% vs. $9.7$\% / $7.3$\%). \texttt{Lemur-v1} (70B, SIFT) also shows stronger performance than its Base variant.
However, except \texttt{CodeLLaMA} (7B), we do not find similar improvements on \texttt{CodeLLaMA} (SIFT).
We hypothesize that the performance gain on \texttt{Vicuna-v1.5} and \texttt{Lemur-v1} could be attributed to fine-tuning on ShareGPT's multi-turn human-ChatGPT conversations.
\textbf{RLHF could hurt LLM-tool multi-turn interaction.} We find that on \texttt{LLaMA-2} series, RLHF alignment generally hurts models' performance in both $\Delta_\text{tools}$ ($-0.7\%$ to $-2.6\%$) and $SR_5$ ($-2.4\%$ to $-8.5\%$), similar to the prior observation that alignment can degrade task performance \citep{DBLP:conf/nips/Ouyang0JAWMZASR22}.
However, it's hard to conclude that RLHF in general hurts model performance. We leave it for future work to explore the role of RLHF in multi-turn interaction.

\subsection{Measuring LLM's Ability to Leverage Natural Language Feedback}
\label{sec:effect-of-feedback}

\input{tables/feedback}

On top of LLM-tool interaction, we use \texttt{gpt-4-0613} to simulate user feedback for evaluated LLMs (\fref{fig:illustrative-example} with red dotted box). With a $k=5$ interaction limit, we measure the LLM's ability to leverage natural language feedback using the absolute performance $SR^{\text{feedback}}_{5}$ and the performance difference after feedback is given: $\Delta_{\text{feedback}} = SR^{\text{feedback}}_{5} - SR_5$. We present results in \tref{tab:sr-with-feedback}.
\textbf{Overall observations.} We find no significant difference between open- and closed-source models in terms of $\Delta_{\text{feedback}}$. Open-source models obtain $+1.7$ -- $+17.2\%$ from feedback, while closed-source models obtain $+6.5$ -- $+15.2\%$. However, there is still a gap between them in absolute success rate $SR^{\text{feedback}}_{5}$, as the best open-source model \texttt{Lemur-v1} (70B, SIFT) still lags behind the best closed-source model \texttt{claude-instant-1} by $8.7\%$.
Surprisingly, we find that \texttt{CodeLLaMA-34B-base} can achieve comparable performance to GPT-4 on decision-making tasks with language feedback from it, showing its strong ability to leverage language feedback.

\textbf{The effect of SIFT and RLHF.} Similar to \sref{sec:effect-of-interaction}, we find that SIFT and RLHF hurt models' ability to leverage feedback. The results on \texttt{CodeLLaMA} (except 7B) and \texttt{LLaMA-2} show that SIFT/RLHF models all have lower $\Delta_{\text{feedback}}$ and $SR^{\text{feedback}}_{5}$ than their base variants. 
Another two exceptions are \texttt{Vicuna-v1.5} (7B) and \texttt{Lemur-v1} (70B). We speculate using multi-turn conversations (ShareGPT) for SIFT contributes to these two exceptions.
\subsection{Measuring the Efficacy of Different LLM's Ability to Provide Feedback}
\label{sec:efficacy-of-LLM-in-providing-feedback}

Fixing the evaluated model to be \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613}, we assess seven LLMs' feedback-providing capability through $\Delta_{\text{feedback}}$ (\tref{tab:measure-llm-feedback-ability}).
Our main finding is that task-solving ability could be orthogonal to feedback-providing ability: LLM's higher task-solving performance does not guarantee better feedback-providing capability and vice versa.
For example, although GPT-3.5 (16k) performs well in task-solving ($SR_5$ ranked 3rd in \tref{tab:measure-llm-feedback-ability}), it leads to a performance degradation of $-10.4$\% in GPT-3.5; Similarly, GPT-4 with self-feedback in \tref{tab:sr-with-feedback} also experiences degraded performance.
On the other hand, despite performing the worst in solving tasks in \tref{tab:measure-llm-feedback-ability}, \texttt{CodeLLaMA-34B-Instruct} can provide feedback that improves the stronger GPT-3.5.

\subsection{\approach Can Help Detect Failure Patterns of Evaluated LLMs}
\label{sec:debug-llm}

Surprisingly, beyond evaluating LLMs' multi-turn interaction ability, we find complex multi-turn tasks in \approach can also test an LLM for unexpected behavior.
We find two main types of anomalies: (1) inability to follow formatting instructions and (2) unexpected outputs likely due to artifacts.

\textbf{Inability to follow formatting instructions.} We find that some models (e.g., smaller \texttt{CodeLLaMA} and \texttt{LLaMA}, \texttt{chat-bison-001}) have trouble producing a parsable format as instructed, hindering task-solving (statistics can be found in \tref{tab:pct-fail-format}).

\textbf{Unexpected output likely due to data artifact.}
We find that Vicuna models (SIFT on ShareGPT data) generate escaped underscore (``$\backslash$\_") instead of underscore (``\_") across all tasks, causing syntax errors when executing code and reducing performance. We examine \cite{sharegpt_data} and find at least one escaped underscore (``$\backslash$\_") artifact on $15\%$ examples, suggesting artifacts in training data could cause this issue.
We observe a similar issue with \texttt{CodeLLaMA-Instruct}: We find that \texttt{CodeLLaMA-Instruct} (34B) \textit{always} ignores user-given instructions on the code generation tasks ``wrap your code with \texttt{<execute>} tag'' and uses \texttt{[PYTHON]} to wrap the code (happens on 100\% of code generation tasks, 0\% on other tasks). \cite{touvron2023llama} uses \texttt{[PYTHON]} as the tag to generate self-instruct data on code problems for SIFT. We suspect \texttt{CodeLLaMA-Instruct} models are trained and overfitted to \texttt{[PYTHON]} token, causing them to produce \texttt{[PYTHON]} regardless of user instruction. 
We refer to \sref{sec:vicuna-13b-issue} and \sref{sec:codellama-artifact-issue} for examples and quantitative results.
\subsection{Can GPT-4 Generate Human-Level Natural Language Feedback?}
\label{sec:human-evaluation-of-feedback}
We perform a human evaluation quantitatively comparing the feedback generated by GPT-4 and written by humans. In \tref{tab:human-evaluation}, human annotators consider 91.2\% of GPT-4 generated language feedback to be as helpful as, if not better than, human written feedback. It's also hard for humans to distinguish GPT-4 generated feedback from human feedback (human-like) in 92\% of the cases.
We also compare GPT-4 generated and human-written feedback by asking \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} to continue problem-solving with either a turn of (1) human language feedback or (2) GPT-4 feedback. Results show that human feedback and GPT-4 feedback lead to similar model performance $SR^{\texttt{feedback}}_5$ (32.7\% vs. 33.6\%).

\section{Related Work}


\subsection{LLM in Interaction}
\textbf{Interact with users.}
LLMs have demonstrated extensive potential in seamless interaction with human users and in assimilating real-time human feedback during inference processes~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2305-00955}. 
According to recent studies, this collaborative synergy between humans and LLMs has been explored across various domains and applications, including sentence editing~\citep{DBLP:conf/emnlp/ReidN22, DBLP:conf/iclr/SchickYJPLIYNG023, chen2023dress}, semantic parsing~\citep{yan-etal-2023-learning, yao-etal-2019-model}, code generation~\citep{nijkamp2022conversational, elgohary-etal-2020-speak}, iterative output refinement~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2206-05802}, and creative writing~\citep{DBLP:conf/chi/0002LY22, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2305-15685,selfcollaboration2023}, generative
information-seeking~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2307-16883}, and even theorem proving~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2306-15626}. 
The partnership between users and LLMs continues to redefine possibilities across diverse research areas.

\textbf{Interact with tools.}
Engaging with external tools allows LLMs can lead to more accurate and reliable outputs~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2302-12813, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2305-11738,toollearning2023}.
LLMs can be connected with real-world Application Programming Interfaces (APIs), enabling them to actively engage with diverse external tools~\citep{qin2023tool, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2205-12255, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2302-04761, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2306-05301, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2305-15334, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2306-06624, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2305-11554, yuan2023craft}. 
For example, LLMs can connect with (1) the Internet to obtain latest information~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2112-09332, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2208-03188, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2303-09014, DBLP:conf/kdd/LiuLYXZDZDT23}; (2) the program interpreter to run the generated code~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2211-12588, DBLP:conf/icml/GaoMZ00YCN23, drori2022neural, DBLP:conf/acl/PanWLLWKN23, wang2023leti, wang2024executable}; (3) multimodal perceiver to obtain the information beyond the language modality~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2304-12995, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2304-09842}; (4) physical simulator to better understand the physical law~\citep{DBLP:conf/iclr/LiuWGWVCZD23}.

\subsection{Evaluating Interaction}
Existing work on interaction evaluation mostly focuses on a specific task or dimension, like task completion~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2308-03688}, code generation~\citep{yang2023intercode}, human-LLM collaborative task solving~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2212-09746, huang2023lateval,fu2023improving}, tool manipulation~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2306-05301}, and web nevigation~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2307-13854, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2306-06070}. 
That is, they solely focus on interacting with either the environment or humans, often on a specific task, overlooking the fundamental importance of both elements in LLM interaction. Different from prior work, \approach covers a range of diverse tasks and is designed to measure the multi-turn interaction capabilities of LLMs with both tools and user feedback that are more aligned with real-world applications.



\section{Conclusion}


In this work, we present \approach, an evaluation benchmark designed to evaluate LLM's task-solving ability in multi-turn interaction by using tools and leveraging natural language feedback, which we simulate using GPT-4.
We hope \approach can serve as a helpful resource to help track progress and incentivize future research in improving LLM's multi-turn task-solving capabilities.
We refer to \sref{sec:limitation-future-work} for a discussion of limitations and future work.
