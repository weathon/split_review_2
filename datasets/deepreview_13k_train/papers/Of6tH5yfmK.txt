\title{Self-Control of LLM Behaviors by Compressing Suffix Gradient into Prefix Controller}

\begin{abstract}

We propose \method, an inference-time model control method utilizing gradients to control the behavior of large language models (LLMs) without explicit human annotations. Given a desired behavior expressed in a natural language suffix string concatenated to the input prompt, \method computes gradients of the LLM's self-evaluation of the suffix with respect to its latent representations. The gradients are used to directly control the auto-regressive generation process towards desired behaviors, which eliminates human supervision, achieves precise and transparent control, and offers on-the-fly adaptability. To further enhance efficiency, we introduce \prefix, a compact module that encapsulates the learned representations from gradients into a \pc, facilitating efficient inference-time control with no latency compared to the original model and allowing control for multiple behaviors simultaneously. Our experiments demonstrate \method's efficacy across multiple domains, where it improves over SOTA for \textbf{8.3\%} in detoxification, \textbf{3.1\%} in truthfulness enhancement, \textbf{4\%$\sim$10\%} in controlling on emotion tones, and \textbf{48.2\%} in privacy protection, i.e., completely remove privacy leakage issue. Additionally, we demonstrate that \method can be used for data synthesis and to improve reasoning abilities. Our interactive demo and code are available at \href{https://colab.research.google.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT \citep{achiam2023gpt}, Claude \citep{anthropic2023claude}, Gemini \citep{deepmind2023gemini}, and LLaMA \citep{touvron2023llama} have showcased capabilities in understanding and generating human-like text. Users can interact with LLMs by writing their instructions in natural language, served as prompt input to LLMs. However, LLMs may often behave in ways that deviate from human intentions \citep{shen2023large}. Such deviations include the generation of toxic content, leakage of private information \citep{wang2023decodingtrust}, or inappropriate emotional tones \citep{dathathri2020plugplaylanguagemodels}.
To address these issues, controlling LLMs to ensure alignment with desired behaviors—such as non-toxicity, truthfulness \citep{ouyang2022training, dathathri2020plugplaylanguagemodels}, privacy preservation \citep{wang2023decodingtrust}, and emotional tone adjustments \citep{li2023large}—is crucial (see Figure \ref{fig:teaser}).

One approach to enhance LLM control involves fine-tuning model parameters via online \citep{ouyang2022training} and offline \citep{rafailov2024direct} Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods.
However, these methods face several challenges:
1) They demand substantial effort to collect large-scale human-annotated preference data \citep{bai2022training};
2) The control process is indirect and lacks transparency, which may fail when encountering out-of-domain behaviors not represented in the preference data \citep{huang2023catastrophic};
3) Incorporating new desired behaviors necessitates additional fine-tuning, which can potentially compromise the control quality of previously aligned behaviors \citep{qi2023fine}.

Besides aligning LLM behavior during training time, recently much research has been focused on controlling LLM at inference time, e.g., through latent representation engineering (RepE)\citep{zou2023representation}. These works still require curating a set of contrastive pairs as a demonstration. Building upon this, in this paper, we're studying whether we can control LLMs' behavior via their self-evaluation, i.e. use LLMs as a judge to assess the quality of their own outputs \citep{kadavath2022language, bai2022constitutional, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2306-05685, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2401-10020}.
We thus introduce \method, a novel gradient-based framework for precise LLM behavior control. 

The core idea of \method is to leverage the LLM's self-evaluation to control its behavior. 
For each input prompt, we formulate a desired behavior as a natural language question, asking the model to assess whether its output aligns with the specified behavior.
We term the question as \textit{suffix string} and concatenate the input prompt, the model's output, and the suffix string, feeding this combined input back into the model.
We then compute the likelihood of the behavior-aligned response to the suffix string as a learning signal for behavior control, which we named as \textit{suffix score}. Then, we compute the suffix score's gradient with respect to the latent representations of the original input, which term as \textit{suffix gradients}.
These suffix gradients are then utilized to update the latent representations, controlling the LLM's behavior towards the desired outcome.
We run this procedure multiple times to iteratively update the input's latent representations, each time using the modified representations to generate new model outputs that increasingly align with the desired behavior.
\method offers several advantages over traditional fine-tuning approaches:
1) \textbf{Elimination of human-annotation}: \method leverages the model's self-evaluation as learning signals, substantially reducing the effort and resources required for preference data collection and scaling.
2) \textbf{Precise and transparent control}: \method operates at inference time and directly modifies the latent representations, which allows for explicit behavior specification and fine-grained control, consequently enhancing control interpretability.
3) \textbf{On-the-fly adaptability}: \method does not alter model parameters, enabling easy implementation of behaviors and control of combinations of multiple behaviors, thus providing unparalleled flexibility.
\method demonstrates superior performance compared to contrastive learning-based control methods, particularly in areas such as detoxification, truthfulness enhancement, privacy protection, and emotion control. 
As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:teaser}, \method exhibits remarkable flexibility in controlling LLM for multiple attributes simultaneously, e.g., happiness and calmness. 
To enhance its adaptability, efficiency and compositionality, we further propose \prefix on top of \method as a general controller across inputs. The core module of \prefix is the \pc, a prompt-based adapter \citep{hu2021lora,zhang2023llama} optimized to match the latent representations conditioned on this \pc to the latent representations under regular \method. \pc brings ideas from prefix-prompt tuning research~\citep{DBLP:conf/emnlp/ShinRLWS20, DBLP:conf/acl/LiL20, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2309-03409} to achieve efficient control, which has almost no latency compared to the original model greatly outperforms other control baselines. Furthermore, we show that \pc is a learnable and composable module that can be easily integrated into the LLM to control multiple model behaviors simultaneously (e.g., being happier, while staying calm), shown in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}, thereby enhancing the practicality of \method for real-world applications.

In summary, our primary contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize} [ leftmargin=*, after=\strut, label=\textbullet]
    \item We introduce \method, a gradient-based LLM control framework that leverages the model's self-evaluation to eliminate the need for human-annotated data, offering more efficient, precise, transparent, and adaptable control.
    \item We further develop \prefix using \pc, a PEFT (parameter-efficient fine-tuning) module that enhances \method's adaptability and compositionality, enabling the dynamic application of controlling multiple behaviors simultaneously.
    \item We show \method is effective on a diverse range of control tasks to align LLM behaviors with user intentions and ethical standards, including improvements over SOTA by 
    \textbf{8.3\%} in detoxification,\textbf{ 3.1\%} for truthfulness enhancement, \textbf{4\%$\sim$10\%} for control on emotion tones and \textbf{48.2\% for privacy protection}, i.e., completely remove privacy leakage issue.
\end{itemize}








\section{Related work}




\paragraph{LLM Control and Representation Engineering.}
Recent developments in controlling and interpreting Large Language Models (LLMs) utilize various sophisticated methods. For behavior control, techniques such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)~\citep{ouyang2022training}, Direct Policy Optimization (DPO)~\citep{rafailov2024direct}, and knowledge editing methods like ROME~\citep{meng2022locating}, MEND~\citep{mitchell2021fast}, and MEMIT~\citep{meng2022mass} modify model outputs or parameters to align with human preferences or factual accuracy. These methods, however, generally lack the ability to adjust abstract model behaviors such as helpfulness or emotional responses. Other strategies, such as Constrained Decoding (CD) \citep{dathathri2020plugplaylanguagemodels}, involve constrained optimization and sampling with Langevin dynamics for token-level output control~\citep{kumar2021controlled, kumar2022gradient}, which may lack flexibility in that they often require human supervision.

Representation Engineering (RepE)~\citep{zou2023representation,turner2023activation,rimsky2023steering} instead, is a flexible method which mainly focus on finding steering vectors to add on LLMs' hidden representations. It originates from the previous methods that learn to find a direction, e.g. linear probes, and then add/subtract the direction from model hidden representations. Unlike the supervised methods, recent technique such as Activation Addition \citep{turner2023activation} or Contrast Vector \citep{zou2023representation}, directly engineer the steering vector in a zero-shot manner. We start from the point of view that gradients are naturally engineering model representations. Thus, we also treat the gradients constructed in our methods a different kind of steering vectors.


\paragraph{LLM Self Evaluation}
LLM self-evaluation has been shown to be effective in answering multi-choice questions \citep{ren2023self}, judging LLMs' output, and serving as safeguards \citep{phute2023llm}. However, some argue that there are some pitfalls \citep{panickssery2024llm,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2306-05685} in LLM self-evaluation. These pitfalls include position bias, distribution bias \citep{panickssery2024llm}, and sycophancy during evaluation. These issues may affect LLMs' evaluation and lead to undesired consequences. However, they generally do not apply to our method. Similar to \cite{phute2023llm}, we simply probe LLMs' next token probability on \texttt{Yes} and \texttt{No}, guiding LLMs toward their own preferences, which has been demonstrated to be feasible by recent study~\citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2401-10020}.}

In this section, we present our \method framework, which leverages the LLM's self-evaluation to control its behavior.
We begin by detailing the standard instance-level \method approach.
This encompasses the process of transforming desired behaviors into suffix strings, computing suffix scores and suffix gradients, and controlling model behaviors through iterative updates to latent representations.
Subsequently, we introduce the across-instance version, \prefix.
\prefix compresses instance-level suffix gradients into a \pc, enabling adaptable model control on new inputs and facilitating the simultaneous control of multiple behaviors.

\subsection{Instance-Level \method}

\method controls the LLM's by transforming a desired behavior into a natural language question, referred to as a suffix string.
The model then performs self-evaluation of its response to this question, generating a suffix score corresponding to the likelihood of the response aligning with the desired behavior.
Then, gradients of the suffix score with respect to the latent representations of the original input are computed.
Model behaviors are then controlled through iteratively updating the latent representations with the suffix gradients.
Figure~\ref{fig:framework} illustrates this process.

Formally, we consider an $L$-layer autoregressive Transformer-based Language Model with parameters $\theta$, denoted as $\texttt{LM}_\theta$.
Given a prompt \texttt{input}, such as ``\textit{The corner store, with your favorite candy, has new ownership.}'', we first process it through the model to obtain the latent Key and Value representations for each layer.
These representations are denoted as $H_{\text{input}} \coloneqq \{(K^l_{\text{input}}, V^l_{\text{input}})\}_{l=1}^L$
 representing the Key and Value matrices for layer $l$, respectively. We use $h$ to denote the function for obtaining these latent representations: $H_{\texttt{input}} = h(\texttt{LM}_\theta, \texttt{input})$.
Subsequently, we sample an $\texttt{output}$ sequence one token at a time from the model, conditioned on the input representations:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\theta}(\texttt{output} \bigm| \texttt{input}) = \prod_{t=1}^{|\texttt{output}|} P_{\theta}(\texttt{output}_{t+1} \bigm| \texttt{output}_{[1: t]}, H_{\texttt{input}}).
\end{equation}

Without any control, the model may generate an undesired output, such as ``\textit{Oh no! The corner store, where I always get my favorite candy, has new ownership? That's so sad.}''.
To perform LLM self-evaluation of the output, we form a \texttt{suffix} string representing the desired behavior.
In this case, to improve the output's happiness, we might use: ``\textit{Your response was happy. Yes or No?}''.
Conditioned on this \texttt{suffix}, we probe the probability of the predicted \texttt{<next-token>} being either ``\texttt{Yes}'' or ``\texttt{No}'':
\begin{align*}
    P_{+}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) &=  P_{\theta}(\texttt{<next-token>} = \texttt{Yes} \bigm| \texttt{suffix}, \texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) \\
    P_{-}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) &= P_{\theta}(\texttt{<next-token>} = \texttt{No} \bigm| \texttt{suffix}, \texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}})
\end{align*}

Here, ``\texttt{Yes}'' and ``\texttt{No}'' are used solely to assess the $\texttt{LM}_\theta$'s evaluation of the response for a certain behavior, and are not necessarily the tokens with the highest predicted probability. We quantify the model behavior by defining the suffix score $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$ as the probability ratio between ``\texttt{Yes}'' and ``\texttt{No}'':
\begin{align*}
    S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) = \text{sigmoid}\Big(\log P_{+}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) - \log P_{-}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}})\Big) \nonumber
\end{align*}

The suffix score $S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}})$ is directly influenced by the \texttt{output}, and \texttt{output} is exactly the object we want to control.
A higher score indicates a stronger alignment between the \texttt{output} and the behavior specified in the \texttt{suffix}.
Consequently, the objective of LLM control becomes the generation of an \texttt{output} that maximizes this suffix score:
\begin{align}
\texttt{output}^* = \underset{\{\texttt{output}, \ \theta, \ H_{\texttt{input}}\}}{\arg \max}S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}), \ \   \text{where: } \texttt{output} \sim \texttt{LM}_\theta(H_{\texttt{input}}) \label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
Equation (\ref{eq:objective}) presents three interdependent variables that can potentially be optimized to maximize $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$: $\texttt{output}$, $\theta$, and $H_{\texttt{input}}$.
The most apparent choice is $\texttt{output}$, which could be directly searched.
However, recent research has shown that guided searches of LLM-generated token sequences can be complex and inefficient \citep{zou2023universal, huang2023catastrophic, qi2023fine, liu2023autodan, wei2024jailbroken, zeng2024johnny}.
An alternative approach is to optimize the model parameters $\theta$, which corresponds to fine-tuning the model, e.g., RLHF.
As discussed in the introduction, this approach encounters several challenges, including the need to collect large amounts of human-annotated preference data, a lack of precise control and transparency, and difficulty in incorporating new behaviors during inference time.

Therefore, \method takes the third choice to maximize $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$, which is to optimize the latent input representations $H_{\texttt{input}}$ (abbreviated as $H$ when input is clear from the context). \method computes the suffix gradients $\Delta H = \nabla_{H} S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}, H)$ and adds $\Delta H$ to $H$ to employ inference-time model control. 
This optimization process is performed iteratively, with the $i$-th iteration consisting of two steps:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item In \textbf{Step 1}: Use the $i$-th iteration $H_i$ to sample multiple outputs $\{ \texttt{output}_i^1$, \dots, $\texttt{output}_i^K \}$, in which each $\texttt{output}_i^k \sim \texttt{LM}_\theta(H_i)$, then select the best $\texttt{output}_i^*$ that gives the highest $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$.
    \item In \textbf{Step 2}: Calculate $ \Delta H_i = \nabla_{H} S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}_i^*, H_i)$, then update $H_{i+1} = H_{i} + \gamma\cdot\Delta H_i$, with $\gamma$ denoting the step size.
\end{itemize}
This iterative update process progressively refines the latent representations $H$, enabling the sampling of $\texttt{output}$ in each iteration to increasingly align with the desired behavior.
Through multiple iterations, we obtain a final optimized representation $H^*$, which can be used to sample the controlled outputs at inference time
Algorithm~\ref{algo:suffix_control} provides a detailed pseudocode of \method.
In practice, we employ a line search technique to dynamically adjust the step size $\gamma$, ensuring a consistent increase in the suffix score across iterations.
)

\subsection{\prefix: Compress Suffix Gradients into \pc}

\method can efficiently search for proper input representations to enable LLM behavior control at the instance level. To further improve it for across-instance control, we propose to compress the suffix gradients from a set of instances runs into a \pc, which can be easily integrated into the LLM and used to control the model behavior.
We implement \pc as a learnable adapter $\texttt{adapter}_{ \theta_a}$, which is prepended to each layer of the LLM as a ``prefix'', including the input embeddings layer\footnote{The soft tokens at the input layer are initialized using a neutral prompt, e.g., \texttt{``You are an assistant.''}}. Similarly to \method, \pc does not directly change the model parameters but control the model through modifying the latent representations at inference time. We denote the latent representations after applying \pc as $H_{\texttt{prefix}}: = h(\texttt{adapter}_{\theta_a}, \texttt{LM}_{\theta}, \texttt{input})$.

To learn the \pc, we first run \method to collect a set of $\{\texttt{input}_i, H^*_{\texttt{input}_i}\}_{i=1}^N$ pairs. For the best performance, we also filter the dataset by only keeping the instances that have high suffix scores.). Then we adapt the following objective to minimize the mean squared error between $H^*_{\texttt{input}_i}$ and $H_{\texttt{prefix}}$ to optimize the its parameters $\theta_a$:
\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{L}_{\texttt{prefix}}(\theta_a) & \coloneqq \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \big(H^*_{\texttt{input}_i} - H_{\texttt{prefix}, i}\big)^2, \\
    & \text{where } H_{\texttt{prefix}, i} = h(\texttt{adapter}_{\theta_a}, \texttt{LM}_{\theta}, \texttt{input}_i)
\end{align*}

Each learned \pc works as an adaptable module that elicits a specific LLM behavior independently.
These modules can be used as plug-and-play components to control model behaviors.
Furthermore, by combining multiple \pc's, we can guide the LLM output to exhibit a composite of desired behaviors.
For instance, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:prefix}, we demonstrate that the model can be directed to display increased happiness while maintaining a calmer demeanor.

\cm{Maybe we need a one-liner to recap our method, since some Neurips reviewers thought that it's a bit tedious and vague.}
In this section, we introduce \method, the framework for controlling model behaviors using suffix gradients. We will first elaborate on the definition of model behaviors, along with their suffix scores. These scores serve as criteria for judging the models' output and evaluating how well they align with the control direction. Then, we will demonstrate how we get the suffix gradients and use them to control model behaviors without changing model parameters, which enable inference-time control for arbitrary desired LLM behavior (defined in the suffix, the middle part of Figure~\ref{fig:framework}). Finally, we introduce how we compress suffix gradients into a Prefix Controller that can be directly generalized to control model behaviors on new inputs in a flexible plug-and-play manner (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:prefix}).

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}, given an autoregressive Transformer-based Language Model $\texttt{LM}_\theta$ and a query \texttt{input}, e.g., ``\texttt{How much water shall I put in the gas tank of my car?}'', $\texttt{LM}_\theta$ first calculate the intermediate Key and Value representations of \texttt{input} in each layer, storing as KV-cache $H_{\texttt{input}} = \{(K^l_{\texttt{input}}, V^l_{\texttt{input}})\}_{l=1}^L$, and then sample $\texttt{output} \sim \texttt{LM}_\theta(H_{\texttt{input}})$ token by token via:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\theta}(\texttt{output} \bigm| \texttt{input}) = \prod_{t=1}^{|\texttt{output}|} P_{\theta}(\texttt{output}_{t+1} \bigm| \texttt{output}_{[1: t]}, H_{\texttt{input}}).
\end{equation}
Without proper guidance, the language model can generate an unreasonable output, such as ``\texttt{The amount of water to put depends on your car's specification or your location.}'', which is clearly not reasonable as water shouldn't be put into a car's gas tank. One way to identify such unreasonable output is through LM self-judgment, which we call the \textit{suffix score}. To get the score, we append a \texttt{suffix} to the model input, such as ``\texttt{Is the above output helpful? Give the answer Yes or No:}''. Given such suffix string, we probe the probability of the predicted \texttt{<next-token>} to be either ``\texttt{Yes}'' or ``\texttt{No}'':
\begin{align*}
    P_{+}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) &=  P_{\theta}(\texttt{<next-token>} = \texttt{Yes} \bigm| \texttt{suffix}, \texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) \\
    P_{-}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) &= P_{\theta}(\texttt{<next-token>} = \texttt{No} \bigm| \texttt{suffix}, \texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}})
\end{align*}
Here ``\texttt{Yes}'' and ``\texttt{No}'' are two suffix labels we use to assess the $\texttt{LM}_\theta$'s judgment on the response for following a certain behavior. 
Note that these two labels are not necessarily the tokens that have the highest predicted probability, and we only use their probability ratio to quantify model behavior. Formally, we define the suffix score as:
\begin{align*}
    S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) = \text{sigmoid}\Big(\log P_{+}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}) - \log P_{-}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}})\Big) \nonumber
\end{align*}
A higher suffix score $S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}})$ indicates that the model output better follows the behavior specified in \texttt{suffix}. Therefore, the objective of suffix control becomes finding an \texttt{output} that can maximize the suffix score:
\begin{align}
\texttt{output}^* = \underset{\{\texttt{output},\ \theta,\ H_{\texttt{input}}\}}{\arg \max}S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}, H_{\texttt{input}}), \ \   \text{where: } \texttt{output} \sim \texttt{LM}_\theta(H_{\texttt{input}}) \label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
In principle, there are three variables in Eq. (\ref{eq:objective}) that can be optimized to maximize $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$. An obvious choice is the $\texttt{output}$, which can be directly searched by first sampling $K$ different outputs from the LM with a non-zero temperature, and then selecting the one that gives the highest $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$. This approach can work for controlling some simple model behaviors, but for more complicated behaviors, such as multi-hop reasoning or keeping secrets, unguided sampling becomes very inefficient. On the other hand, guided sampling is non-trivial for LLMs. Unlike the \textit{classifier guidance} for diffusion models in image generation, where gradients for the output can be directly computed as the image outputs are continuous, the LLM output is a sequence of non-differentiable discrete tokens. Even though some LLM-attack works~\cite{zou2023universal, huang2023catastrophic, qi2023fine, liu2023autodan, wei2024jailbroken, zeng2024johnny} employ evolutionary search or projected gradient descent to help search the optimal output, there is no guarantee for controlled outputs with these approaches.


Another choice to maximize $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$ is to optimize the model parameters $\theta$. This approach is widely used via RLHF, where $\theta$ are optimized to maximize the likelihood of generating outputs corresponding to higher rewards (in our case, the reward is analogous to the suffix score $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$). For RLHF, a separate reward model is usually trained to provide the reward, and then the $\texttt{LM}_\theta$ is fine-tuned to maximize the reward. Though effective, such RLHF methods are even more costly as they require collecting a large amount of annotated data to train an accurate reward model that reflects a desired model behavior. Moreover, once the reward model has been trained, it becomes impossible to add a new behavior control during inference time as $\theta$ cannot be changed on the fly.

In this paper, we take the third choice to maximize $S_{\texttt{suffix}}$, which is to optimize the latent input representations $H_{\texttt{input}}$ (abbreviated as $H$ in the following). Such method is also called ``Representation Engineering'' proposed in \cite{zou2023representation}, in which authors learn a $\Delta H$ from contrastive pairs of good and bad samples, which they call ``Contrast Vectors", and then add to each $H$ to employ inference-time control. 
However, such a learning method does not guarantee the learned ``Contrast Vectors'' can activate the target behavior. 
We thus propose to learn such ``Contrast Vector" ($\Delta H$) by directly optimizing Eq.~\ref{eq:objective} using the EM algorithm. Specifically, we treat \texttt{output} as a discrete latent variable that is intractable, which we estimate during the E-step that maximizes suffix score; then during the M-step, we use this variable to optimize $H$. Specifically:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item In \textbf{E-Step}: Use the $i$-th iteration $H_i$ to sample multiple outputs $\{ \texttt{output}_i^1$, \dots, $\texttt{output}_i^K \}$, in which each $\texttt{output}_i^k \sim \texttt{LM}_\theta(H_i)$, then select the best $\texttt{output}_i^*$ that has highest suffix score.
    \item In \textbf{M-step}: calculate $ \Delta H_i = \nabla_{H} S_{\texttt{suffix}}(\texttt{output}_i^*, H_i)$, then $H_{i+1} = H_{i} + \gamma\cdot\Delta H_i$, where $\gamma$ denotes the step size.
\end{itemize}
As is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:framework}, using such EM update, at each M-step update we can get a better input query $H=H_{\texttt{input}}$ that is able to sample better output that satisfies the behavior constraint (Step 3, $H_{i+1} = H_{i} + \gamma\cdot\Delta H_i$ highlighted in orange block). We then use such updated query representation to sample new $\texttt{output}$ in E-step (Step 4) that better satisfy the behaviour constraint. Through multi-iteration updates, we can get a final $H_{\texttt{input}}^*$, which is represented as the Key-Value Cache at each Transformer layer, that can be applied to sample all controlled outputs.
We call this algorithm \method, with detailed implementation illustrated in Algorithm~\ref{alg:suffix_control}. In practice, we also adjust the step size $\gamma$ through line search (\texttt{search\_step\_size} in Algorithm ~\ref{alg:search_step_size}) to ensure the suffix score keeps increasing through multi-iteration.

\paragraph{Compress Suffix Gradients into \prefix.}
\cm{Just write here concretely -- what's the prompt and what's the lora adapter}
\method can efficiently search for proper input representations to enable LLM behavior control at the instance level. However, it is still expensive and requires a gradient-based search for each input during inference time. We thus propose to just use \method to collect a bunch of \{(\texttt{input}, $H^*_{\texttt{input}}$)\} pairs, and train a Prefix Controller to immediately enable the LM to generate the updated representation. We call this method \prefix. This idea is motivated by earlier prefix-prompt tuning research~\cite{DBLP:conf/emnlp/ShinRLWS20, DBLP:conf/acl/LiL20, DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2309-03409} that searches for continuous/discrete tokens to maximize certain outputs. Ours is different in that the reward signal as well as the learning objective are collected through the model itself.

The Prefix Controller does not directly change the model parameters and ensures flexible model control. Each Prefix Controller can be treated as a plug-and-play ``prompt'', which elicits a certain LM behavior standalone. In addition, by applying multiple such controllers together, we can steer LM output to follow a composite of desired attributes. For example, in Figure~\ref{fig:prefix}, we show the model can be guided to behave with more reasoning capability, calmer and more helpful.
As is shown in the Figure~\ref{fig:prefix}, the Prefix Controller contains two components: a LoRA adapter $\texttt{adapter}_{ \theta_a}$ and a learnable prefix prompt $\texttt{prompt}_{\theta_p}$, where $\theta_a$ and $\theta_p$ denote the learnable parameters of the adapter and prefix prompt respectively. The learnable prompt is prepended to each input query, while the LoRA adapter serves as an additional module that directly influences the latent representations. Altogether they will affect the latent representations.

Then given the LoRA adapter, prefix prompt, $\texttt{LM}_{\theta}$, and input query, we first define $H_{\texttt{prefix}}$ as the KV-cache representations with these inputs:
\begin{align*}
H_{\texttt{prefix}}: = h(\texttt{adapter}_{\theta_a}, \texttt{prompt}_{\theta_p}, \texttt{LM}_{\theta}, \texttt{input}).
\end{align*}
Then, the objective of \prefix is to train the adapter and prompt to match the generated representation $H_{\texttt{prefix}}$ to the representation obtained from regular \method (the $H^*_{\texttt{input}}$, which is highlighted in orange on the right-hand side of Figure~\ref{fig:prefix}). In particular, let $\{(\texttt{input}_i, H^*_{\texttt{input}_i})\}_{i=1}^N$ be a collection of the input-representation pairs, the objective of \prefix is formulated as the following:
\begin{align*}
\min_{\theta_a,\theta_p} \mathcal{L}_{\texttt{prefix}}(\theta_a,\theta_p): = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^n \big(H^*_{\texttt{input}_i} - h(\texttt{adapter}_{\theta_a}, \texttt{prompt}_{\theta_p}, \texttt{LM}_{\theta}, \texttt{input}_i)\big)^2.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}

\noindent{\bf Language Detoxification.} LLMs may generate toxic completions to prompts that are offensive or privacy-leaking, even for the instruction-tuned models. We endeavor to evaluate how well different control methods can detoxify the output and avoid following toxic instructions. We use RealToxicityPrompts \citep{gehman2020realtoxicityprompts} for toxicity following \cite{han2023lmswitch}, and Perspective API~\citep{PerspectiveAPI} to measure toxicity scores.

\noindent{\bf Privacy Protection.}
 Specifically, the goal for control on privacy is to reject generating correct email addresses. Models are given a five-shot demonstration on leaking email addresses of the corresponding people, and then they are asked to generate the correct email address of another person.

\noindent{\bf Emotion Control.} We also study if model emotion can be well controlled using \method. We use datasets of five emotional attributes from RepE~\cite{zou2023representation}, i.e. \texttt{anger}, \texttt{fear}, \texttt{happiness}, \texttt{surprise}, and \texttt{disgust}. Specifically, we use the last one hundred data from each emotional dataset for evaluation and the first one hundred to train \prefix and Reading Vector. We use \texttt{GPT-3.

\noindent{\bf HH-dialogue.} For HH-dialogue, we benchmark how well the responses align with the principle given in \cite{fränken2024selfsupervised}. Besides, simply control with \method on the embedding level. We also benchmark \method as a data generation method to generate preference pairs. The preference pairs will be used to train the base model using DPO. We test on the first 250 data from Anthropic-HH~\cite{bai2022training} harmless-base and helpful-base. We follow \cite{fränken2024selfsupervised} and use \texttt{GPT-4} to select the winner of each response when competing with the original response.

\noindent{\bf Reasoning.} We also demonstrate that \method can be used to improve the mathematical reasoning ability of LLMs, measured by performance on GSM-8K~\cite{Cobbe2021TrainingVT}, a dataset of 8.5K high quality linguistically diverse grade school math word problems.

\noindent{\bf Truthfulness ICL.}
We further benchmark \method on truthfulness under a simple in-context learning setup, using synthetic data from \cite{marks2023geometry}. Specifically, we use the \texttt{cities} and \texttt{neg\_cities} datasets. The data is generated with the template \texttt{``[city] is in [country]''} or \texttt{``[city] is not in [country]''}. A fixed 2-shot is prepended to each sentence during evaluation. This is aimed at evaluating \method's capability of enhancing performances on simple question answering tasks. Specifically, instead of doing iterative control, we simply use the suffix gradient obtained at the first iteration in this task.
In all the above scenarios, for \prefix, we generate the gradients using the default sampling strategy, with two iterations of control and search for the best step size at each iteration. To train \prefix, we generate up to 800 (\texttt{input}, \texttt{representation}) pairs for the training set using 100 inputs as seed data. For the validation set, we use another 100 inputs as seed data and generate up to 100 pairs.

\noindent{\bf Baselines.}
We compare our method with four baselines, including two \textbf{Representation Engineering} (RepE) methods: Reading Vector and Contrast Vector~\cite{zou2023representation}, and a \textbf{Prompting} method: System Prompting, and a \textbf{Constrained Decoding} (CD) method, Model Arithmetic \cite{dekoninck2023controlled}. Specifically, we consider both with and without classifier for Model Arithmetic, and use the setup that has relatively low perplexity following \cite{dekoninck2023controlled}. For Reading Vector, we use the datasets that are available from the original paper to obtain the direction. Additionally, we use SAMI~\citep{fränken2024selfsupervised} as the baseline for data synthesis on HH-dialogue, and CoT-decoding~\citep{wang2024chainofthought} as the baseline on reasoning.

\noindent{\bf Models.}
For a fair comparison with existing literature, we use \texttt{LLaMA-2-7b-chat}, \texttt{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2} \cite{jiang2023mistral}, and \texttt{LLaMA-3.1-8b-instruct} on toxicity; \texttt{LLaMA-2-7b-chat} \cite{touvron2023llama} on emotion and HH-dialogue; \texttt{LLaMA-2-13b-chat} on True/False ICL; and \texttt{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2} on reasoning. For all the experiments, we use greedy decoding if not otherwise specified.

We visualize suffix gradients of various attributes and their composition to gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between different attributes and the composite attribute. \paragraph{Where does each behavior pattern store at Transformer? }
The suffix gradient can be regarded as a stimulus to activate or suppress a certain behavior inside Transformer weights. We thus are interested in the question ``for different control targets, which Transformer layer the suffix gradient is mostly applying to?'' Specifically, we calculate the $\log{\|H^*_{input}\|_2} - \log{\|H\|_2}$ measuring after gradient how the latent representation per layer increases the norm or decreases. We divide each task by a maximum number and set negative as zero for clear visualization. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:norm_sad}, different tasks focus on different layers / regions of Transformer layers. Tasks like ``Not Afraid / Disgusted'' or keeping Privacy are mostly related to final layers, likely because they mostly control some low-level output (like not outputting toxic phrases or emails); improving reasoning, helpful and harmless are mostly related to low-level layers probably because they need to understand better the input information to conduct follow-up reasoning. This is just a small experiment using \method, but we see the potential to use suffix gradient to better reveal mechanistic interpretability of neural language model.

\paragraph{Suffix Attention}
We study how the suffix score and suffix target attend to other tokens in some attention heads. For example, Figure \ref{fig:attention_short} depicts the attention of the target token to other tokens on the 9th attention head of layer 29. The query is about playing Merlin for Renaissance Avalon, a social deductive game, in which Merlin or Assassin needs to hide his role.

The target token attends to previous words like \texttt{``Merlin''} and \texttt{``Assassin''} in the generated texts. Although the target token still attends to the words \texttt{``Merlin''} or \texttt{``Assassin''} in the queries and suffixes, these words no longer appear in the generated text, and the model successfully reaches the target response. As is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:attention_short} and Figure~\ref{fig:attention}, the head has high attention scores on both identities in each case, which may indicate the mechanistic feature of this head. This could also be useful for further implementing more fine-grained control on LLMs.

\subsection{Visualizing Controlled Representations}

To better understand why \method is able to control LLM behavior, we analyze the difference of \method against Contrast Vector with respect to representation engineer (i.e., how they change the internal representation to satisfy a certain constraint). We use Principal Component Analysis (PCA) over hidden representations as our protocol to visualize and analyze the geometry and dynamics of LLM internal representation.

\paragraph{Data source for PCA.} Firstly, we employ a controlled setting with the prompt: \texttt{``[2-shot ICL] [city] is not in [country] Answer: ''}. The city names and country names from the \texttt{neg\_cities} dataset will be filled into the slot of the template. Then we extract the representations at the final token (which is the colon) position from layer 17, forming a \textbf{set of representations}. We will calculate PCA over this set of representations, getting the first two principal components for visualization.
\paragraph{Label of representations.} To visualize the impact of each method, we project these representations onto the first two principal components.  Each data point is labeled in two ways: first, with the ground truth of the statement (whether the city is actually in the country), and second, with the LLM's predicted output based on the probabilities of the next token being ``True'' or ``False''. 
As is shwon in the Figure, the dots with label \textcolor{blue}{True} are in blue and dots with label \textcolor{orange}{False} are in orange. The leftmost and the rightmost sub-figures are shown with the ``GT label'', i.e., the \textcolor{blue}{True} dots come from the sentences that are \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{factually correct}}; and the middele ones are with ``model output labels'', i.e., the \textcolor{blue}{True} dots come from sentences that the \textbf{\textcolor{blue}{model thinks they are correct}}. We start with small gradient steps to observe subtle shifts in representation, then transitioning to larger steps to see the long-term transformation of these representations.

Figure \ref{fig:vis_comparison} shows the PCA plots. Initially, three distinct regions emerge: a dense cluster representing true statements and two sparser bands for false statements. As we apply \method and Contrast Vector with increasing strength, we observe the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contrast Vector}: This method primarily rotates and translates the existing representation space. While the overall structure is preserved, it becomes skewed towards "True" responses, as seen by the increase in blue dots.
    \item \textbf{\method}: This method fundamentally restructures the representations. Initially, the changes appear chaotic. However, as the control coefficient increases, a clear linear pattern emerges, particularly noticeable at \texttt{coeff=-10}. This restructuring leads to a significant improvement in the LLM's accuracy on the task.
\end{itemize}




\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we introduced \method, a framework leveraging suffix gradients to control the behaviors of large language models effectively. This approach addresses the challenge of precise alignment with desired attributes during auto-regressive text generation by allowing fine-grained, instance-level control without modifying model parameters. Additionally, we proposed \prefix, a prefix-based module that generalizes suffix gradients for efficient, inference-time control over multiple attributes simultaneously. Our extensive experiments validate the effectiveness of both \method and \prefix in various tasks, including emotional tone regulation, language detoxification, privacy protection and in-context learning. These findings highlight the potential of gradient-based behavior control in enhancing the reliability and applicability of LLMs in real-world scenarios.
