\title{DP-SGD for non-decomposable objective functions}

\begin{abstract}
    Unsupervised pre-training is a common step in developing computer vision \narxiv{\linebreak} models and large language models. 
    In this setting, the absence of labels requires the use of similarity-based loss functions, such as contrastive loss, that favor minimizing the distance between similar inputs and maximizing the distance between distinct inputs. As privacy concerns mount, training these models using differential privacy has become more important. However, due to how inputs are generated for these losses, one of their undesirable properties is that their $L_2$ sensitivity can grow with increasing batch size. This property is particularly disadvantageous for differentially private training methods, such as DP-SGD. To overcome this issue, we develop a new DP-SGD variant for similarity based loss functions --- in particular the commonly used contrastive loss --- that manipulates gradients of the objective function in a novel way to obtain a senstivity of the summed gradient that is $O(1)$ for batch size $n$.  We test our DP-SGD variant on some preliminary CIFAR-10 pre-training and CIFAR-100 finetuning tasks and show that, in both tasks, our method's performance comes close to that of a non-private model and generally outperforms DP-SGD applied directly to the contrastive loss.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\label{sec:intro}
Foundational models --- large models trained in an unsupervised  manner to be fine-tuned on specific tasks---  have become one of the cornerstones of modern machine learning. These models have generally outperformed other approaches in multiple tasks, ranging from language generation, to image classification and speech recognition. In fact, models such as LaMDA \cite{TDHS22}, BERT \cite{DCLT19}, GPT \cite{RNSS18} and diffusion models \cite{SCSL+22, midjourney} are now at the frontier of the generative AI revolution and are interacting with millions of users per day. Due to the complexity of these models, there are multiple concerns in the privacy community that these models may \emph{memorize} some of the training data. For models trained on user-generated content, this may result in a catastrophic privacy breach, where the model may unintentionally reveal private information about a user. Recent work from \cite{mia, balle2022reconstructing} has shown that these risks are not just a theoretical concern and that it is possible to (i) know whether a particular example was in a dataset for training the model and (ii) reconstruct training data using only black box access to the model. 
On the other hand, the same line of research has shown that these attacks become significantly harder when models are trained using differential privacy. This is generally expected as differential privacy provides an information theoretic guarantee that the model does not depend drastically on any example. 

It is for the above reason that private training methods have received considerable attention from the privacy community in the past decade. Some of the foundational work on this area was set by \cite{chaudhuri2011} who provided algorithms for private learning with convex loss functions and \cite{ACGM+16} who proposed the now celebrated differentially private stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) algorithm for training neural networks in a private way. Multiple lines of work have stemmed from this research area, ranging from tighter privacy analysis \cite{GMKRD22} to more efficient implementations of DP-SGD \cite{LTLH21}. However, most of the literature on private machine learning makes one crucial assumption about the objective function they are trying to minimize: the objective decomposes as a sum (or average) of example level losses. This assumption drastically simplifies the \emph{sensitivity analysis} (how the objective changes as one changes one point in the dataset) of  DP-SGD algorithm.

In this work, we focus on models that are trained using non-decomposable objectives. That is, an objective function that cannot be described as a sum (or average) of individual losses. Our study is motivated by the use of contrastive losses \cite{CKNH20, CKSNH20} for pre-training foundational models. Contrastive losses generally compare each example against all other examples in the batch. That is, adding or removing an example to a batch of examples can affect the objective function in unpredictable ways. This type of behavior generally makes it hard, if not impossible, to train models privately. In this work however, we show that common non-decomposable losses have a critical property that makes them amenable to private training. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We provide a general framework for measuring the sensitivity of DP-SGD with non-decomposable objectives.
    \item We show how to apply this framework to two non-decomposable losses: contrastive loss and the spreadout (regularization) loss \cite{ZYKC17}.
    \item We conduct experiments on privately pre-training image classification models and show that we can achieve performance comparable to non-private pre-training. Our experiments analyze the performance of simple pre-training as well as fine tuning on a downstream task.  
\end{itemize}


\section{Preliminaries and Notation}

\label{sec:setup}
Given a non-Euclidian feature space $\calX$, such as a space of images or sentences, we focus on unsupervised learning of embedding models $\Phi_w:\calX \to \Rset^d$ parametrized by $w \in \calW$ where $\calW$ is a parameter space $\calW \subset \mathbb{R}^p$. 

Let $\calD = \{(x_i, x'_i)\}^n_{i=1}\subseteq \calX \times \calX$ be a batch with $n$ records, such that $x_i$ and $x'_i$ are similar (positive pairs) in the feature space. These positive pairs can correspond, for instance, to two version of the same image,  a sentence and its translation on a different language or an image and its caption. Let $\Sim: \Rset^d \times \Rset^d \to \Rset$ be a function measuring similarity of two points in $\Rset^d$, the objective is to find a parameter $w \in \calW$ that preserves the similarities defined by pairs in $\calD$.

 Given vectors $x_1,...,x_n \in \Rset^d$, define $\mathbf{x}=(x_1, ..., x_n)$, and denote $\Phi(\boldx) = (\Phi(x_1), ..., \Phi(x_n))$. Given embeddings  $u, v_1, v_2, ..., v_n \in \Rset^d$, define the similarity profile of $u$ respect to $v_1,...,v_m$ for $m\leq n$ as the vector $\bSim^m(u, \boldv)$ of similarities between $u$ and the first $m$ vectors in $\boldv$. Formally, $\bSim^m(u, \boldv) \in \Rset^m$ where entry $j$ for $j=1,...,m$ is defined as $[\bSim^m(u, \boldv)]_j = \Sim(u, v_j)$. 

Given a batch $X = \{(x_1,x_1'), \ldots, (x_n, x_n')\}$ and a family of loss functions $\elln{i,n} \colon \Rset^{n} \to \Rset$  that calculate the loss on the similarity profile of point $x_i$ based on the $n$ points on batch $X$, define 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:similarity-loss}
    \calL(w, X) =  \sum_{i=1}^n \elln{i,n}\left(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_n')\right)
\end{equation}

Given $\eta > 0$, contrastive loss models, which aim to minimize $\calL(\cdot, X)$, are typically trained iteratively using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) as follows:
\begin{equation*}
    w_+ = w - \eta (\nabla_w\calL(w, X))
\end{equation*}
\begin{example}[Contrastive loss]
\label{ex:contrastive} 
Let us provide a more concrete example of the type of losses we are considering. Let $\phi_w$ denote an embedding function and let 
\[
u_{ij} := \Sim(\phi_w(x_i), \phi_w(x_j')) = \left \langle\frac{\phi_w(x_i)}{\|\phi_w(x_i)\|}, \frac{\phi_w(x_j')}{\|\phi_w(x_j')\|}\right\rangle
\]
denote the cosine similarity between the embeddings. The contrastive loss $\ell^{(i,n)}$ is given by:

\begin{equation*}
    \ell^{(i,n)}(u_{i1}, \ldots, u_{in})= -\log \left( \frac{e^{u_{ii}}}{\sum_{j=1}^n e^{u_{ij}}}\right) =\log \left(\sum_{j=1}^n e^{u_{ij}}\right) - u_{ii}
\end{equation*}
This loss essentially treats the unsupervised learning problem as a classification problem with $n$ classes, where the pair $(x_i, x_i')$ has a positive label and $(x_i, x_j')$ has a negative label for all $j\neq i$. For this reason we refer to the similarity terms $u_{ij}$ the contrastive logits.
\end{example}
The contrastive loss is widely used by the vision community \cite{CKNH20, CKSNH20, RKHR+21} and has been shown to be extremely sucessful at obtaining pre-trained models for image classification. 
\begin{example}[Spreadout regularizer loss]
\label{ex:spreadout}
Let $u_{ij}$ be defined as in Example~\ref{ex:contrastive}. The spreadout (regularizer) loss is defined as 
\begin{equation*}
    \ell^{(i,n)}(u_{i1}, \ldots, {u_{in}}) = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i} u_{ij}^2
\end{equation*}
\end{example}
The spreadout regularizer is commonly used when training embeddings for computer vision \cite{ZYKC17, YRMK20} used as a method to promote orthogonality in the embedding space among disimilar objects and use of the whole feature space. 

\paragraph{Differential Privacy.} 
Let us now define the notion of differential privacy \cite{DMNS06}. Let $\cZ$ denote an arbitrary space and let $D = \{z_1, \ldots, z_n\}\subset \cZ$ denote a dataset. We say that datasets $D$ and $D'$ are neighbors if $D' = D \cup \{z_{n+1}\}$ for some $z_{n+1} \in \cZ$. A mechanism $M \colon \cZ^* \to \mathcal{O}$ is a randomized function mapping a dataset to some arbitrary output space. Let $\epsilon, \delta > 0$. We say that mechanism $M$ is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differentially private if for all neighboring datasets $D, D'$ and all $S \subset \mathcal{O}$ the following inequality holds:
\begin{equation*}
    P(M(D) \in S) \leq e^\epsilon P(M(D') \in S) + \delta
\end{equation*}
A simple way of ensuring that a mechanism is differentially private is by using the Gaussian Mechanism.
\begin{example}[The Gaussian Mechanism]
Let $\epsilon, \delta > 0$ and let $f \colon \cZ^* \to \Rset^d$. Let $\Delta = \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$ denote the $L_2$-sensitivity of the function $f$. The mechanism defined by 
$$M(D) = f(D) + \Delta \xi,$$
where $\xi \sim {\cal N}(0, \sigma)$, is $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differentially private for an appropriate choice of $\sigma$ (for example, see \cite{borjagauss} for some conditions on $\sigma$).
\end{example}
For the rest of the paper, our goal will be to implement the Gaussian mechanism for the function $$X \mapsto \nabla_w \cL(w, X).$$
\begin{example}[Naive DP-SGD similarity-based loss functions.] Let
\arxiv{\linebreak}
$X=\{(x_1, y_1), ..., (x_n,y_n) \}$ be a dataset of features and label pairs, and consider a loss function $\calL_{\text{sum}}(w,X) = \sum_{i=1}^n f^{i}(w)$ where $f^i$ is the example-level loss on example $(x_i, y_i)$. DP-SGD implements the gaussian mechanism to privately estimate $\nabla_w    \calL_{\text{sum}}(w, X)$, the gradient of $\calL_{\text{sum}}(w,X)$ respect to $w$ given by

\begin{equation*}
\nabla_w    \calL_{\text{sum}}(w, X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla_wf^i(w).
\end{equation*}

 The norms of $ \nabla_w    \calL_{\text{sum}}(w, X)$ and individual gradients $\nabla_wf^i(w)$ are unknown in most settings therefore practitioners select a clip value $B$ to bound the norm of individual gradients $\nabla_wf^i(w)$ to the gradient. Estimating an optimal clip norm $B$ is hard in deep learning settings \cite{LRC20, SWZK+22} because small clip values can introduce bias and slow-down (or ruin) convergence \cite{MJ21, KHS23}, while large clip values will result on large amounts of noise being added to the gradient. Adding or removing one example from $X$ will not change the norm of $\nabla_w    \calL_{\text{sum}}$ by more than $B$, but notice that the batch-gradient $\nabla_w   \calL_{\text{sum}}(w, X)$ can have norm $nB$, mitigating the bias introduced by clipping while the noise magnitude remains constant in $n$.

Now consider a dataset $X=\{(x_1, x_1'), ..., (x_n,x_n') \}$ and the loss function $\calL$ defined in \cref{eq:similarity-loss}, which will be denoted $\calL_{\text{sim}}(w, X)$ in this example. The gradient $\nabla_w \calL_{\text{sum}}$ is given by 

\begin{equation*}
    \nabla_w \calL_{\text{sim}}(w, X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla\ell^{(i,n)}\left(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_n')\right)
\end{equation*}
Let $X'=\{(x_1, x_1'), ..., (x_{n-1},x_{n-1}') \}$ be a neighboring dataset of $X$, and \arxiv{\linebreak} $\nabla_w \calL_{\text{sim}}(w, X')$ the gradient computed on $X'$. 

The sensitivity of $ \nabla_w \calL_{\text{sim}}$ on $X$ and $X'$ is given by  

\begin{align*}
    \| \nabla_w \calL_{\text{sim}}(w, X) & -  \nabla_w \calL_{\text{sim}}(w, X') \| = \\
    & \left\|\nabla\ell^{(n,n)}\left(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_n), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_n')\right)\quad + \right. \\
    &  \hspace{1cm} \sum_{i=1}^n \nabla\ell^{(i,n)}\left(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_n')\right) \\
    &\hspace{2cm}-  \left.\nabla\ell^{(i,n-1)}\left(\bSim^{n-1}(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_{n-1}')\right)\right\|
\end{align*}

None of the terms above cancels because $x_n'$ appears in all the sumands. Using triangle inequality it is possible to show that  individual terms 
\narxiv{$\nabla\ell^{(i,n-1)}\left(\bSim^{n-1}(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_{n-1}')\right) - \nabla\ell^{(i,n-1)}\left(\bSim^{n-1}(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_{n-1}')\right)$}
\arxiv{\begin{align*}
    \nabla\ell^{(i,n-1)}&\left(\bSim^{n-1}(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_{n-1}')\right) \\
    &\quad - \nabla\ell^{(i,n-1)}\left(\bSim^{n-1}(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_1'), ..., \Phi_w(x_{n-1}')\right)
\end{align*}}
are bounded by $2B$ and the overall sensitivity is bounded by $2Bn$. In this case, even with per-example clipping the noise magnitude grows with $n$. 

Alternatively, clipping directly $\nabla_w \calL_{\text{sim}}(w, X)$ to have norm at most $B$ could reduce the amount of noise added; this is the strategy suggested by \cite{HWMXZ20, KLLW21} which we call Naive-DP in our experiments' section. 

\end{example}



\section{Related work}

\label{sec:related-work}
Contrastive learning has had large impact on unsupervised pretraining of computer vision models \cite{CKNH20, CKSNH20} and representation learning for language models \cite{LL18, CYCY+18}, or both \cite{RKHR+21}. \cite{FWZDX20, GNWB20, WWSGKSM20} use a contrastive loss function for pre-training and fine-tuning BERT with data augmentation.  More recently it has been used for reinforcement learning with BERT-type models \cite{BBPWSMB21}. 

In the private setting, the majority of the work has been focused on improving the original implementation of DP-SGD \cite{ACGM+16} for decomposable losses. Research has particularly focused on tighter privacy guarantees on DP-SGD via advanced privacy accounting methods \cite{M17, GMKRD22} or solving computational issues associated with gradient clipping \cite{goodfellow2015efficient}. For non decomposable losses, some work has studied private learning from pairwise losses in the convex and strongly convex case (\cite{HWMXZ20, XYHD21}) and test only in the diabetes dataset.  Later work \cite{YLLY21, KLLW21} obtains similar results for the non-convex case; all these approaches circumvent clipping by assuming access to the Lipschitz constant of the loss function, which depends on the encoder function (typically a deep neural network). However, this Lipschitz constant is generally not easy to estimate and  this is an active area of research \cite{LRC20, SWZK+22}. 


In \cite{XCWP22} the authors learn private image embeddings with user-level differential privacy, but avoid unsupervised training (and consequently non-decomposable loss functions used for unsupervised pre-training like the contrastive loss, or the triplet loss). Instead, this work relays a supervised multi-class classification problem, and avoids dependencies among different records, at the cost of labeling the data. 

In \cite{LYWZ22} the authors propose noising the similarity matrix between pairs of inputs and compute a noisy loss function. They combine this with a noisy gradient but assume a per-gradient bounded sensitivity. 

Orthogonal work study attacks to embedding models. For instance \cite{SR20} showed that when trained without differential privacy, embedding models can be inverted. More specifically, model attacks designed to recover information from embeddings trained with a contrastive loss are able to recover members from the training set \cite{LJQZ21}. To prevent specific attacks \cite{RVAK22} developed an architecture that learns an obfuscator that prevents reconstruction or attribute inference attacks. \cite{HZ21} quantifies the exposure risk under contrastive learning losses and develops an adversarial training procedure to mitigate the risk. However, none of these approaches provide differential privacy guarantees. 

Finally, \cite{WWQHX22} explores contrastive learning in federated settings but here users feed a user-embedding; the negative samples are created at the server with the differentially private embeddings sent by the users. 




\section{Bounding pairwise-contributions}


 This section first introduces a condition on the family of loss functions $\{\ell^{(i,n)}\}$ that, when combined with a clipping operation on the gradient of the similarity between each pair of records, permits the derivation of a DP-SGD variant that benefits from increasing the batch size when using similarity based loss functions.

We start by deriving an expression for the gradient of $\calL$ in \cref{lemma:sim-loss-grad} that highlights the dependence on the gradient of pairwise similarity values (or logits) $\Sim(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x'_j))$. By leveraging this decomposition, we find a bound on the overall loss $\calL$ gradient's sensitivity in Theorem~\ref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss}. Finally, we combine these two facts to produce a differentially private optimization algorithm for similarity based loss functions. We defer proofs to the supplementary material. 

\subsection{Computing gradient sensitivity}
Lemma~\ref{lemma:sim-loss-grad} below shows that the gradient of a similarity based loss function can by expressed in terms of the pairwise similarity gradients $\nabla_w\Sim(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_j'))$. 

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma:sim-loss-grad}
Let $\calL(w, X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ell^{(i,n)}(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(\boldx')))$ be a similiarity based loss function as defined in Section~\ref{sec:setup}, then the gradient of $\calL$ with respect to parameter $w$ is given by

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:similarity=loss-grad}
    \nabla_w \calL(w, X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n      \frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}}{\partial Z_{ij}}(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(\boldx'))) \nabla\Sim(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_j'))
\end{equation}

Where $Z_{ij} =\Sim(\Phi_w(x_i),\Phi_w(x_j'))$
\end{lemma}

We now describe conditions on the family $\{ \ell^{(i,n)}\}$ function that allow us to derive a bound on the $L_2$ sensitivity of the $\calL$'s gradient.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss}
Let $\calL(w, X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ell^{(i,n)}(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(\boldx')))$ be a similiarity based loss function as defined in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. Let $\calC\subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be a compact set and let $\boldz' \in \calC^{n-1}, z_n\in \calC$, and $\boldz = (\boldz',z_n) \in \calC^n$. Assume that for all $i \in [n]$ the family of functions ${\{\ell^{(i,n)}:\Rset^n \to \Rset\}_{n\in \mathbb{N}}}$ satisfies 
\begin{align}
\label{eq:L_cond}
     {\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left|\frac{\partial\ell^{(i,n)}}{\partial z_j}(\boldz) - \frac{\partial\ell^{(i,n-1)}}{\partial z_j}(\boldz')\right| \leq L} \\
    \sum_{j=1}^n \left|\frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}(\boldz)}{\partial z_j}\right| \leq G_1\\
    {\sum_{i=1}^n \left|\frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}(\boldz)}{\partial z_n}\right| \leq G_2}
\end{align}
where $L, G_1$ and $G_2$ can depend on $n$. Assume that ${\|\nabla_w\Sim(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_j'))\|_2 \leq B}$. 
Then the $L_2$-sensitivity of $\nabla_w \calL$ can be bounded as

$$\Delta_2(\nabla_w \calL) \leq (G_1+G_2+ (n-1)L)B$$

\end{theorem}
We are now in a position to present our main algorithm. The following two corollaries show that one can obtain a private estimate of the gradient of the training loss by doing clipping on pairwise similarity gradients and applying a Gaussian mechanism. 

\begin{corollary}
Let $B > 0$, $z \in \Rset^d$ and $\text{Clip}_B(x) = \min({B}/{\|x\|}, 1) x$ denote the vector $x$ clipped to have norm at most $B$. Suppose the family of functions $\ell^{(i,n)}$ satisfy the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss}. Then, the function
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:clipped_gradient}
    \mathbf{X} \mapsto  \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n      \frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}}{\partial Z_{ij}}(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(\boldx'))) \text{Clip}_B\left(\nabla\Sim(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_j'))\right) 
\end{equation}
has $L_2$ sensitivity bounded by $(G_1 + G_2 + (n-1)L)B$. 
\end{corollary}
\begin{corollary}
If the family of loss functions $\ell^{(i,n)}$ satisfies the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss}, then each iteration of Algorithm~\ref{alg:logit-dp} satisfies $(\epsilon, \delta)$-differential privacy for $\epsilon = \sqrt{\log(1.25/\delta)}/{\sigma}$ \footnote{A slightly tighter relation between $\sigma $ and $\epsilon$ can be given using the results on the analytic Gaussian mechanism of \cite{borjagauss}.} 
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof} 
The proof is immediate since each step of the algorithm corresponds to the Gaussian mechanism with noise calibrated to the sensitivity of the mechanism. 
\end{proof}
An observant reader may point out that our sensitivity bound has a dependence on $n$ that is undesirable, as our algorithm would seem to add as much noise as a na\"ive implementation of DP-SGD. In the following lemmas, we show that for commonly used losses like the contrastive loss and spreadout regularizer loss, condition \eqref{eq:L_cond} happens to hold with $L = O(1/n)$. Consequently, this ensures that the total sensitivity remains constant as the number of examples in batch increases.
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:contrastive-sensitivity}
Let $\ell^{(i,n)}$ denote the contrastive loss of \cref{ex:contrastive}. Then $\ell^{(i,n)}$ satisfies the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss} for  
\begin{equation}
    G_1 + G_2 + (n-1)L \leq 2 \ \left(1+\frac{(n-2)e^2}{e^2+(n-1)} \right)
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:spreadout}
Let $\ell^{(i,n)}$ denote the spreadout regularizer of \cref{ex:spreadout}. Then $\ell^{(i,n)}$ satisfies the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss} for  
$$G_1+ G_2 + (n-1)L \leq 6$$
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By definition the spreadout loss is given by:
\begin{equation*}
    \ell^{(i, n)}(\boldz) = \frac{1}{n -1} \sum_{j\neq i}^n  z_j^2
 \end{equation*}
 We now calculate the values of $L, G_1, G_2$ that make this function satisfy the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss}.
 Let $\boldz = (\boldz', z_n)$ then we have that
 \begin{equation*}
     \frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}(\boldz)}{\partial z_j} - \frac{\partial \ell^{(i, n-1)(\boldz')}}{\partial z_j} = \frac{2}{n-1} z_j - \frac{2}{n-2} z_j
     = - \frac{2}{(n-1)(n-2)} z_j 
 \end{equation*}
 for $j \neq i$ and $0$ otherwise. Let $C \leq \max_{j = 1\ldots n} z_j$ we then have:
 \begin{align*}
     \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \left | \frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}(\boldz)}{\partial z_j} -  \frac{\partial \ell^{(i, n-1)}(\boldz')}{\partial z_j}\right|
     \leq \sum_{j\neq i}^{n-1}  \frac{2}{(n-1)(n-2)} |z_j |
     \leq \frac{2C}{n-1} =: L
 \end{align*}
 
 Similarly we have 
 \begin{equation*}
     \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\partial \ell^{(n,n)}(\boldz)}{\partial z_j} \leq  2C =: G_1
\qquad
 \text{and }
 \qquad
     \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}(\boldz)}{\partial z_j} \leq 2C =: G_2
 \end{equation*}
 Therefore we have that 
 \begin{equation*}
     G_1 + G_2 + (n-1)L \leq 6C
 \end{equation*}
 Finally, note that the input to the spreaduout regularizer  is given by $z_{ij} = \Sim(\Phi(x_i), \Phi(x_j'))$ where the $\Sim$ corresponds to the cosine similarity. Therefore we must have that $C = 1$ and the result follows. 
\end{proof}
\subsection{Main algorithm}

We introduce our proposed private optimization routine as \cref{alg:logit-dp}.
The algorithm receives as input standard constants batch size $n$, learning rate (or schedule) $\eta$, and a number of iterations $T$. In addition, it receives constants $G_1$, $G_2$, and $L$ defined in \cref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss}, and the similarity gradient clip norm $B$, and computes the sensitivity of the overall gradient $C$ (line~\ref{algline:sensitivity}).

The algorithm proceeds to the training loop where at each iteration $t$ samples a batch of size $n$. Then, instead of per-example gradients, computes similarity gradients $g_{ij}$ (\cref{algline:similarity-grads}), clips all $g_{ij}$'s to obtain bounded sensitivity $\bar{g}_{ij}$, and computes an approximate gradient for $\calL$ using \cref{eq:clipped_gradient} (line~\ref{algline:approx-grad}). Finally, it applies noise (line~\ref{algline:noised-grad}) and updates the model (line~\ref{algline:update}).   

We would like to remark that all previous work on privacy accounting for DP-SGD also applies to our algorithm as each iteration simply generates a private version of the gradient of the batch loss.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
    \KwIn{Similarity gradient clip norm $B$, constants $G_1$, $G_2$, and $L$ in \cref{thm:sensitivity-similarity-loss}, dataset $D = \{(x_i,x_i') \}^N_{i=1}$, batch size $n$, number of iterations $T$, learning rate $\eta$, noise multiplier $\sigma$, model architecture $\Phi$}
    \KwOut{ Embedding model $\Phi_{w_T}$}
        initialize $w_0 \in \Rset^p$\; \label{algline:init}
         Compute gradient sensitivity $C = (G_1+G_2+nL)B$\; \label{algline:sensitivity}
        \For{$t=0,2,...,T-1$}{
          Sample batch $X = \{(x_1,x_1'), ..., (x_n, x_n')\}$\;   \label{algline:sample-batch}
       Compute similarity gradients $g_{ij} = \nabla_{w_t}\Sim(\Phi_{w_t}(x_i),\Phi_{w_t}(x_j'))$\;     \label{algline:similarity-grads} 
             Clip gradients $\bar{g}_{ij} = \min\left\{\frac{B}{\|g_{ij}\|}, 1 \right\} g_{ij}$\; \label{algline:clip}
            Compute approximate gradient $\bar{g}$ using \eqref{eq:clipped_gradient}.\;  \label{algline:approx-grad}
             Compute noisy gradient $\tilde{g} = \bar{g}+Y$ for $Y\sim \calN(0, \sigma CI_{p})$.\; \label{algline:noised-grad}
           Update the model $w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta\tilde{g}$.\;   \label{algline:update}
        }
  \caption{Logit-DP, a DP-SGD algorithm for similarity based non-decomposable loss functions.}
 \label{alg:logit-dp}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{A memory bottleneck and a potential fix}

In our implementation of Logit-DP (Algorithm~\ref{alg:logit-dp}), one of our noticeable computational bottlenecks was the materialization of the $n^2$ logit gradients ($g_{ij}$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:logit-dp}) for a batch $n$ examples, which were needed to compute the final aggregated gradient ($\bar{g}$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:logit-dp}). 
Specifically, due to the large $\Theta(n^2|w|)$ memory cost of computing these gradients, we were unable test our proposed approach on larger embedding models $\Phi_w$ during pre-training.

A potential solution is to compute gradients $g_{ij}$ sequentially. While addressing the memory bottleneck, this solution is computationally slow. 

Below, we describe an alternative approach for computing $\bar{g}$ and argue that it is more efficient for certain choices $\Phi_w$. Consider the function
\[
F_X(w) := \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \lambda_{ij} \textbf{S}^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_j'))
\]
where $\lambda_{ij}$ are fixed, real-valued weights given by 
\begin{align*}
\tau_{ij} := \frac{\partial \ell^{(i,n)}}{\partial Z_{ij}}(\bSim^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(x_j'))), \quad
    \lambda_{ij} := 
\tau_{ij} \min\left\{\frac{B}{\|g_{ij}\|}, 1 \right\}\quad \forall i,j,
\end{align*}
and note that $\bar{g} = \nabla F_X(w)$ (cf. \eqref{eq:clipped_gradient}). In view of the previous identity, an alternative approach to computing $\bar{g}$ is to first compute each $\lambda_{ij}$ and \textit{then} compute $\nabla F_X(w)$. 

This new approach has the following advantages: (i) given $\lambda_{ij}$, the memory and runtime cost of computing the gradient of $F_X(w)$ is on the same order of magnitude as computing the gradient of ${\cal L}(w,X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \ell^{(i,n)}(\textbf{S}^n(\Phi_w(x_i), \Phi_w(\boldx')))$ when both methods employ backpropagation, 
(ii) the memory cost of storing the weights $\lambda_{ij}$ is only $\Theta(n^2)$, and 
(iii) the costs of computing the weights $\lambda_{ij}$ requirs only computing the $n^2$ scalar pairs $(\tau_{ij}, \|g_{ij}\|)$ rather than computing the $n^2$ gradients $g_{ij}$ of size $|w|$ as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:logit-dp}. 

The last advantage is of particular interest, as there are well-known methods \cite{goodfellow2015efficient, rochette2019efficient, lee2020scaling} in the literature to efficiently computing the norms $\|g_{ij}\|$ without materializing each $g_{ij}$. Specifically, most of these methods involve decomposing $g_{ij}$ into some low-rank representation $g_{ij} = U_{ij} V_{ij}^\intercal$ for low-rank matrices $U_{ij}$ and $V_{ij}$, and then exploiting the identity
\[
\|g_{ij}\|^2 = \|U_{ij} V_{ij}^\intercal\|^2 = \langle U_{ij}^\intercal U_{ij}, V_{ij}^\intercal V_{ij} \rangle.
\]
For example, when $U_{ij}$ and $V_{ij}$ are column vectors, then the last expression above reduces to $\|U_{ij}\|^2\|V_{ij}\|^2$, which can be substantially more efficient than first materializing $g_{ij} = U_{ij} V_{ij}^\intercal$ and then computing $\|g_{ij}\|$. A correct implementation of this technique is far from trivial and we leave this as future research in this exciting area of privacy.


\section{Discussion}

\label{sec:discussion}
 As observed in section 3, naive implementations of DP-SGD for similarity based losses is ineffective because the standard deviation of the noise in the gaussian mechanism grows with $n$. Experiments in the previous section show that even with careful hyperparameter tuning, the loss remains almost constant during pre-training. Fine-tuned models also perform poorly compared to both the non-private baseline and the Logit-DP algorithm.   

Careful analysis of these losses and their decomposition shows that by cliping logit gradients, Logit-DP obtains a sensitivity that is constant on the batch size, considerably reducing the magnitude of the noise added to privatize the gradient. These insights expand the suite of tasks that can be trained in a privacy preserving way with little accuracy drop. Work on efficient implementation of these algorithms is an interesting avenue of future work and we introduce concrete ideas at the end of the previous section.
