\title{Understanding the Interplay between Parametric and Contextual Knowledge for Large Language Models}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) encode vast amounts of knowledge during pre-training (parametric knowledge, or \pk) and can further be enhanced by incorporating contextual knowledge (\ck).
Can LLMs effectively integrate their internal \pk with external \ck to solve complex problems?
In this paper, we investigate the dynamic interaction between \pk and \ck, categorizing their relationships into four types: \textit{Supportive, Complementary, Conflicting}, and \textit{Irrelevant}. To support this investigation, we introduce \textsc{EchoQA}, a benchmark spanning scientific, factual, and commonsense knowledge. Our results show that LLMs tend to suppress their \pk when contextual information is available, even when it is complementary or irrelevant. While tailored instructions can encourage LLMs to rely more on their \pk, they still struggle to fully leverage it. These findings reveal a key vulnerability in LLMs, raising concerns about their reliability in knowledge-intensive tasks.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\begin{displayquote}
``\textit{ If a man keeps cherishing his old knowledge, so as continually to be\\ acquiring new.} '' \hfill ---~\textit{Confucius}
\end{displayquote}

Large language models (LLMs)
\citep{NEURIPS2020_1457c0d6,ouyang2022training,touvron2023llama,zeng2023glmb,openai2023gpt4} capture a substantial amount of knowledge through pre-training on large-scale corpus, referred to as \textit{parametric knowledge} (\pk). When applied to problem-solving, LLMs often rely on additional information provided as context, known as \textit{contextual knowledge} (\ck)\footnote{For simplicity, \pk and \ck denote parametric and contextual knowledge throughout this paper.}. 
A critical area that remains underexplored is how effectively LLMs can integrate \pk and \ck to solve complex problems. This ability is crucial for human-like intelligence, where we effortlessly draw upon stored knowledge to adapt to new environments, reflecting our capacity to \textit{perceive, recall, and integrate} information when encountering new stimuli \citep{gibson1988exploratory}. Effective collaboration between \pk and \ck is also vital for real-world applications, such as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)~\citep{schick2023toolformer,trivedi-etal-2023-interleaving,an2024threadlogicbaseddataorganization,gutiérrez2024hipporag}. 

However, \textit{we find that for LLMs, an effective interaction between \pk and \ck can be challenging.} For example, we observe a counter-intuitive phenomenon that when supplementing an LLM with more task-relevant knowledge in the context (\ck), sometimes the performance degrades because the LLM fails to effectively utilize the parametric knowledge (\pk). Examples are shown in \cref{fig:main_example}. This catastrophic test time forgetting greatly limits LLMs capability to effectively solve user queries when relevant context is provided. In this paper, our objective is to thoroughly investigate \textit{the intricate dynamics between \pk~and \ck}, leading to a better understanding of how well LLMs integrate and prioritize these sources of knowledge. Previous works separately study when \ck is conflicting or irrelevant to \pk, raising concerns about the vulnerability \citep{wang2023resolving,xie2024knowledgeconflict,wu2024easily}. However, they fail to answer more fine-grained questions such as: To what extent are LLMs aware of \pk, in the presence of \ck? How well can LLMs leverage \pk~given \ck? What factors can affect such ability?

To perform a systematic study, we formulate \textit{reasoning types} based on various relationships between \ck~and \pk\textit{—Supportive, Complementary, Conflicting} and \textit{Irrelevant}, illustrated by examples in Figure \ref{fig:main_example} and Table \ref{tab:type_and_metrics}.
Due to the lack of {appropriate} datasets to examine this ability, we introduce a new benchmark \textbf{\textsc{EchoQA}} (Section \ref{sec:dataset}) spanning diverse knowledge-intensive reasoning tasks, \textit{i.e.,} scientific, factual and commonsense, across various LLMs. We extract \pk~by ensuring LLMs can reach 100\% performance for the knowledge.
Next, we construct \ck~from \pk~to fit our reasoning types, as in the middle of Figure \ref{fig:main_example}.
We hope that \textsc{EchoQA} will serve as a valuable resource to steer future explorations on LLMs leverage and integration of \pk~with \ck. 

To investigate to what the extent LLMs can leverage \pk, we design a series of reasoning instructions,
which progressively enforce the use of \pk, guiding LLMs preference to varying degrees, as detailed in Section \ref{sec:reasoning_instructions}.
Furthermore, we investigate other factors
affecting LLMs abilities to leverage their internal knowledge, \textit{e.g.,} popularity of entities and methods of new knowledge fabrication, shedding lights on future improvements (Section \ref{sec:discussion}).
Our investigation draws the following findings:

\noindent  $\bullet$ \textit{The suppression of \pk~in the presence of \ck~is universal}, regardless of the model, knowledge type or reasoning type (Complementary, Conflicting or Irrelevant). LLMs
\textit{disregard} their own knowledge, \textit{e.g.,} more than 60\% of cases in scientific knowledge for all tested models, relying solely on the context for reasoning. This highlights the vulnerability of LLMs in leveraging \pk.

\noindent  $\bullet$ LLMs are more likely to recall their knowledge for some knowledge and reasoning types, \textit{e.g.,} the commonsense knowledge. We find evidence indicating that the reason behind is likely the imbalance of knowledge in training corpus. 

\noindent  $\bullet$ \textit{Explicit instruction can help LLMs remember more \pk,
but still way off from fully leveraging \pk.} This implies more sophisticated prompt or framework design has the potential to solve this problem to a larger extent.


\section{Related Work}



\textbf{Parametric Knowledge in LLMs} is encoded in models parameters through vast amounts of text data during pre-training. Previous research extensively explores how LLMs leverage \pk~for reasoning \citep{li2024understandingpatchingcompositionalreasoning,yang2024largelanguagemodelslatently,wang2024grokkedtransformersimplicitreasoners}. Some reveal the challenges in leveraging \pk \citep{wang2023survey,allen2023physics}. These studies typically focus on models ability to utilize \pk~to perform various tasks, \textit{e.g.,} multi-hop reasoning, without considering the influence of contextual knowledge (\ck). While valuable, these analyses do not address how \pk~is dynamically applied in real-world contexts where \ck also plays an important role. Moreover, \pk~embeded in language models can be outdated or incorrect, prone to hallucination \citep{elazar-etal-2021-measuring,lazaridou2021mind,zhong-etal-2023-mquake}. Though some work propose to edit \pk~\citep{dai-etal-2022-knowledge,meng2022locating,meng2023massediting}, additional effort would be needed for the loss of other abilities.
\noindent\textbf{Contextual Knowledge for LLMs}
refers to the information (or tools) augmented in LLMs context window to enhance generation (RAG), which hopefully supplements LLMs internal \pk~to improve faithfulness \citep{REALM,Qin2023ToolLW,mallen-etal-2023-trust}. Previous work primarily focuses on optimizing the retrieval of relevant documents for reasoning \citep{press-etal-2023-measuring,asai2023selfrag,zhuang2024efficientragefficientretrievermultihop,gutiérrez2024hipporag}. However, as documents contain diverse information, LLMs may be confused when reasoning, even with optimal retrieval, \textit{e.g.,} when the given knowledge contradicts their knowledge \citep{xu2024knowledgeconflictsllmssurvey}. We find that even complementary \ck~can hinder LLMs ability to fully leverage their knowledge. In this paper, we formulate the relationship between \pk~and \ck~to investigate the influence of different kinds of \ck~on LLMs utilization of \pk.

\noindent\textbf{Interplay between Parametric and Contextual Knowledge} mainly includes behavioral analyses or fusion of both knowledge.
Some assess models handling of new knowledge \citep{yin-etal-2023-alcuna}, focusing on knowledge construction. Other works elicit \pk~from LLMs and design frameworks to introduce conflict \citep{wang2023resolving,xie2024knowledgeconflict} or irrelevant \ck \citep{wu2024easily}.
However, the effectiveness of LLMs leveraging of \pk under various \ck is understudied. Besides, the focus on single type of knowledge may overlook the complex relation between the two knowledge sources
reflected real-world scenarios, \textit{e.g.,} LLM-based search engines or chatbots like ChatGPT Plugins and New Bing \citep{nakano2021webgpt,deng2023mind2web} or human reasoning process, \textit{e.g.,} when exploring new environments, human intelligence can naturally integrate knowledge in brain with outside nature. While some studies explore the fusion of \ck~and \pk~for improved sufficiency \citep{jiang-etal-2023-active,zhang2024evaluating}, the intricate relationship between them is understudied. We aim to comprehensively examine LLMs ability to manage and integrate these two knowledge sources.

As shown in Table \ref{tab:type_and_metrics}, we design four reasoning types based on various relationships between \pk~and \ck. Without the loss of generality, we adopt Question Answering (QA) as the reasoning task, following prior work \citep{mallen-etal-2023-trust,cheng-etal-2024-call}. 
Formally, given an instruction, a question ${Q}$ and \ck, an LLM leverages \pk~and \ck~to reason the answer ${A}$. As shown in Equation \ref{reasoning_equation}, if $Q$ is posed, then \ck~operated with \pk~under the reasoning types (\textit{rt}) leads to the answer $A$. 
Note that for all settings, \pk~always holds true for Equation \ref{reasoning_equation}, meaning that we ensure the LLM has \pk~required for $Q$. In experiments, we vary \ck~based on our designed \textit{rt} as shown in Figure \ref{fig:main_example}. Different from \citealp{xie2024knowledgeconflict}, we only present  \ck~in the context, to assess the ability to utilize \pk~more naturally.

\begin{align}
&Q \rightarrow (\ck ~\textit{rt} ~\pk \rightarrow A) \textit{\quad where {rt}} \in \{\odot, \land, \oplus, \lor\} \textit{~and \pk~holds true.}   \label{reasoning_equation} 
\end{align}
\noindent \textbf{\textit{$\odot$ Supportive}} We have \ck~and \pk~convey the same information.
Our prior experiments show that models behave consistently with or without \ck~(as expected), or LLMs can effectively adopt \pk~in this case. Therefore, we do not report this type in main results.)

\noindent\textbf{\textit{$\land$ Complementary}}\label{subsec:complementary_reasoning} This type typically requires both \pk~and \ck~(multi-hop)~for reasoning.
We maintain knowledge sufficiency by assuring \pk is known by LLM and \ck is new LLM. A key challenge is to establish meaningful connections between them. Previous study construct multi-hop questions by replacing entities with descriptions \citep{talmor-berant-2018-web,huang-etal-2023-markqa}, which does not ensure that \ck~is new to LLMs. To address this, we create \textit{entity-profiles} that relate to \pk. For the example in Table \ref{tab:type_and_metrics}, given \pk~``\textit{the earth is spherical}'', \ck~can be ``\textit{\texttt{Z-man} was born on earth}'', which is complementary to \pk~to answer ``\textit{The shape of the planet \texttt{Z-man} was born on}''.

\noindent\textbf{\textit{$\oplus$ Conflicting}} In this type, a fact in \ck~is fabricated by \textit{deletion, modification} or \textit{extension} from \pk. We can also create longer narratives around the fabricated fact. For the example in Figure \ref{fig:main_example}, by modification, we can change ``\textit{Michael Jordan is a Basketball Player}'' into ``\textit{Michael Jordan works as an ESPN basketball commentator for 20 years}''.
We ensure the question $Q$ require that exact fact to answer. 
We denote the answer that align with \ck~and \pk~as $A_{ck}$ and $A_{pk}$, respectively.

\noindent \textbf{\textit{$\lor$ Irrelevant}} For this type, we randomly sample irrelevant information as \ck, under certain constraints, \textit{e.g.,} \ck~for questions about \texttt{occupation} should still pertain to occupations. As  the goal is to test LLMs ability to leverage \pk,
we only consider the case when \pk~is necessary for $Q$ in main results.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We design various metrics to assess LLMs ability to leverage \pk, as shown in Table \ref{tab:type_and_metrics}. Specifically:

\noindent $\bullet$ \textbf{Accuracy ($Acc$)} evaluates whether the output exactly matches the ground truth.
 For Supportive, Complementary, and Irrelevant reasoning types, each question $Q$ has a corresponding ground truth answer $A_{gt}$. We compute \textbf{$Acc$} to assess model performance for these reasoning types.
 
\noindent $\bullet$ \textbf{Memorization Ratio ($M_R$)} measures the extent to which the model adheres to \pk~for Conflicting Reasoning. It is calculated as $M_R = \frac{f_{pk}}{f_{pk}+f_{ck}}$, where $f_{pk}$ and $f_{ck}$ denotes the frequency of answer faithful to $A_{ck}$ and $A_{pk}$, respectively, following prior work \citep{longpre-etal-2021-entity}.

\noindent $\bullet$ \textbf{Unknown Ratio (${U_R}$)} measures the ratio of models generating unknown. 
On one hand, ${U_R}$  reveals models uncertainty to some extent. On the other hand, since we make sure \pk is required by $Q$, higher ${U_R}$ shows lower perception of \pk. We calculate $U_R$ for all reasoning types.
\subsection{Reasoning Instructions}
\label{sec:reasoning_instructions}
Recent post-training techniques, \textit{e.g.,} instruction-tuning \citep{chung2024scaling}, enable LLMs to fluently follow human instructions. To explore to what extent LLMs can leverage \pk, we design a series of progressively-enforced instructions to guide models behavior (detailed in Table \ref{tab:detailed_prompt_reasoning_instructions}).

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=black!15!white, colframe=black, title=Examples of Progressively-Enforced Reasoning Instructions]

    \textbf{\textit{Neutral}:} Combine the given information and your OWN knowledge to answer questions.

    \textbf{\textit{Trust Yourself}}: 
     The given information is NOT SUFFICIENT, you should USE your OWN knowledge combined with the information to answer questions. \hfill(Complementary) \\
     If you think the given information is CONFLICTED with your knowledge, you should USE your OWN knowledge to answer questions.\hfill(Conflicting)
    \textbf{\textit{Speak Out Loud}}: You must first OUTPUT your OWN knowledge about the question. If you think the given information is CONFLICTED with your knowledge, should USE your OWN knowledge to answer questions. \hfill(Conflicting)
    \label{tab:prompts}
\end{tcolorbox}
\noindent $\bullet$ \textbf{\textit{Neutral Instruction}} is natural and straightforward. We treat LLMs as a helpful assistant with knowledge to answer questions. Note that we indeed ask LLMs to use their own knowledge.

\noindent $\bullet$ \textbf{\textit{Trust Yourself Instruction}} proactively shows the insufficiency or asks LLMs to trust their own knowledge. Concerningly, such enforcing instruction is to test LLMs leverage of \pk, but not practical in real-world scenarios where the sufficiency and trustworthiness of knowledge are not ensured.

\noindent $\bullet$ \textbf{\textit{Speak Out Loud Instruction}} further pushes LLMs to explicitly utter its \pk~then trust themselves, mirroring the Chain-of-Thought reasoning \citep{wei2022chain}. This differs from \citealp{xie2024knowledgeconflict}, who separately constructs \pk~and \ck, and asks models to make a choice when \pk~and \ck~are both in context. Also, this enforcing instruction is not for practical use.

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}
We introduce our dataset \textbf{\textsc{EchoQA}} to test how well LLMs echo their \pk~when encountering \ck.
Questions are in multi-choice format and provided an unknown option, following \citealp{xie2024knowledgeconflict}. The key step is to firstly obtain models \pk by questioning about a knowledge triple, where a correct answer indicates that the LLM possesses the relevant knowledge, as in the left of Figure \ref{fig:main_example}. Then, we construct \ck from \pk based on our designed reasoning types, as in the middle of Figure \ref{fig:main_example}. We conduct human evaluations on the quality of the constructed \ck and $Q$, showing that 97.6\% and 95.3\% of $Q$ and \ck~perfectly fit our reasoning types (Table \ref{tab:type_and_metrics}). Notably, our construction can adapt to any knowledge-intensive task

\noindent \textbf{ALCUNA}~\citep{yin-etal-2023-alcuna} is a scientific (biological) dataset featuring artifact entities. The knowledge is structured in json format, obtained by adding, variation and dropping from existing knowledge, from which we construct conflict \ck. We adopt knowledge differentiation subset for Conflicting and Irrelevant Reasoning, and knowledge association subset for Complementary Reasoning, respectively. 
For fair comparison, we construct conflicting \ck under ``variation'' in Section \ref{sec:conflict_reasoning} and further discuss how the way of \ck construction affects LLMs awareness of \pk in Section \ref{sec:way_of_fabrication}.

 \noindent\textbf{ConflictQA}~\citep{xie2024knowledgeconflict} provides factual parametric evidence elicited from LLMs, conflicting evidence and corresponding answers by an LLM-based framework.
 We adopt the PopQA subset \citep{mallen-etal-2023-trust}, questioning about a single fact. Complementary \ck and questions are constructed by \textit{entity-profiles} with templates (Table \ref{tab:template_popQA}) for Complementary Reasoning. We adopt the original conflict knowledge and randomly sample several irrelevant evidences with the same relation type for Conflicting and Irrelevant Reasoning, respectively.

 \noindent \textbf{MuSiQue} \citep{trivedi2022musique} is a factual dataset featuring longer document and multi-step reasoning, with sub-questions and evidence. We firstly question LLMs with sub-questions to label supporting paragraphs as \pk and \ck. Then, we filter out examples with both \ck and \pk for Complementary Reasoning. We adopt {Llama 3.1-70B} (\textit{zero-shot}) to construct conflict \ck by modifying \pk~and generate related $Q$. Prompts are in Table \ref{tab:prompt_examples}. Other operations are similar to ConflictQA. 

 \noindent \textbf{OpenBookQA} \citep{mihaylov-etal-2018-openbook} is a commonsense multi-choice QA dataset. We elicit the commonsense \pk~by questioning LLMs with Chain-of-thought \citep{wei2022chain} and then filtering out ones with correct answers. We instruct {Llama 3.1-70B} with \textit{one-shot} demonstration to fabricate \ck and multi-hop $Q$ requiring both \pk~and \ck~for Complementary Reasoning. Please refer to Table \ref{tab:openbook_knowledge} for prompt examples. Other operations are similar to MuSiQue.
\subsection{Language Models}
\label{sec:language_models}

We include multiple LLMs spanning diverse parameter scales, \textit{i.e.,} two closed-source (OpenAI o1-preview \citep{openai2024o1preview}, GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini \citep{openai2023gpt4o}) and three open-source (Llama 3.1-70B, Llama 3.1-8B \citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels} and Qwen 2-7B \citep{yang2024qwen2}). These models have demonstrated strong performance in knowledge-intensive tasks and instruction-following capabilities, making them well-suited for our tasks. 
In \textsc{EchoQA}, we filter \pk for each LLM (as in the left of Figure \ref{fig:main_example}) to assess their ability to integrate \pk with \ck, statistics of examples are detailed in Table \ref{tab:number_of_filtered_questions}. For all LLMs, we experiment with Chain-of-Thought \citep{wei2022chain} for behavioral analysis and adopt \textit{zero-shot} setting to avoid the bias introduced by demonstrations. Other setups remain as default.

First, \textit{LLMs cannot fully leverage \pk, even though the given \ck~with \pk~is sufficient for the question}. All tested LLMs across all knowledge types demonstrate an upward trend in Accuracy, with stronger models exhibiting higher absolute performance. 
With all required knowledge in context, LLMs perform effectively (orange bar). However, the performance drops dramatically with only \ck in context (red/green bar). Figure \ref{fig:complementary_reasoning} (a) (scientific knowledge) shows that the introduction of complementary \ck even degrades the performance for most tested LLMs, compared to no knowledge given. We scrutinize 200 samples and find that the presence of \ck hinders LLMs from engaging in deeper reasoning, \textit{e.g.,} inferring entity properties by names, leading to an over-reliance on context. We further discuss the phenomenon in Section \ref{sec:factors_affecting}. 
Second, \textit{with complementary \ck, LLMs show even increased uncertainty.} Table \ref{tab:ur_for_complementary} indicates that the complementary scientific \ck~``confuses'' most tested LLMs significantly, doubling the $U_R$ for Llama and Qwen models, comparing ``NI'' with ``\textit{w/o} K'' column. This implies that LLMs depend excessively on the relevant context to seek answers, suppressing their own \pk even though \pk is required for the question.

\textbf{Instruction-following aids in recalling some memories, but still way off.}
By asking LLMs to adopt \pk~in a progressively enforced tone, our reasoning instructions (Section \ref{sec:reasoning_instructions}) significantly bring up the performance, demonstrating the strong instruction-following capabilities of LLMs and showing that instructions can modulate perception of knowledge to some extent. However, even when explicitly guided, LLMs still cannot fully recall the necessary \pk~they already have, leaving a large margin compared to when all knowledge is provided in the context. This highlights the need for dedicate prompt or framework design for better integration of different sources of knowledge.

\noindent\textbf{Commonsense knowledge is easier to remember, while the scientific and factual is harder.}
Comparing different types of knowledge in Figure \ref{fig:complementary_reasoning}, we have the following observations:

\noindent First, LLMs exhibit nearly doubled accuracy in commonsense relative to scientific knowledge (red and green bar Figure \ref{fig:complementary_reasoning} (a)(d)). We speculate the higher recall of specific \pk is likely because such information appears more frequently the in training corpus (more discussion in Section \ref{sec:factors_affecting}). 

\noindent Second, LLMs can hardly answer without knowledge for ConflictQA (blue bar in Figure \ref{fig:complementary_reasoning}(b)). Different from other datasets, to fit the original question, we fabricate entity profiles and questions by templates in Table \ref{tab:template_popQA}, \textit{e.g.,} ``\textit{What's the occupation of the best friend of PersonA in high school?}'', leaving little room for inference. This inversely suggests LLMs reasonable reasoning ability.

Moreover, we observe that the upper bound for MuSiQue is considerably lower, especially for weaker models. However, the upward trend in performance persists, suggesting that while multi-step questions challenge LLMs reasoning ability, they may not significantly impair the recall of \pk.



\subsection{Conflicting Reasoning}
\label{sec:conflict_reasoning}

  \caption {Memorization Ratio for Conflicting Reasoning. LLMs rarely trust themselves (\pk) when faced with conflicting \ck, though instructions modulate their preference to some extent.}
  \label{fig:conflicting_reasoning}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{LLMs rarely trust themselves when faced with conflicting context.} Figure \ref{fig:conflicting_reasoning} depicts the ratio of LLMs relying on their parametric knowledge (\pk) when presented with conflicting contextual knowledge (\ck). Under Neutral Instruction, other than the strong OpenAI o1, few LLMs lean on themselves for more than 10\% cases across all datasets. It is demonstrated that \textit{LLMs are faithful to the conflict context across all types of knowledge}, including commonsense, echoing findings in prior work \citep{xie2024knowledgeconflict}. This observation underscores limitations in LLMs confidence and usage on their own knowledge when confronted with disinformation, warranting dedicated content filtering and careful safety design for LLM-based systems.

\textbf{Instructions significantly influence LLMs preferences, but still struggle to fully recall their memory.}
We further explore the extent to which LLMs can recall \pk~when faced with conflicting \ck. As we instruct LLMs to trust themselves in a progressively  more enforced tone, the Memorization Ratio ($M_R$) consistently goes up in all datasets, with stronger model exhibiting relatively higher $M_R$ (Figure \ref{fig:conflicting_reasoning}). Compared to Neutral Instruction, the Trust Yourself Instruction almost doubles the $M_R$, with the Speak Out Loud Instruction further boosting the $M_R$. However, despite being instructed to trust themselves, few of $M_R$ exceed 60\% for scientific and factual knowledge, indicating that LLMs still cannot fully recall their internal knowledge when the context is in conflict. Again, o1 exhibits more confidence than others, especially when told to ``Trust Yourself'', but there is still space for improvement. This also highlights the vulnerability of LLMs prone to disinformation.

\textbf{LLMs exhibit greater resistance to conflicts in commonsense knowledge.}
Figure \ref{fig:conflicting_reasoning}(d) shows that LLMs can recall commonsense knowledge relatively better, with our instructions leads to stronger enhancement than others. Specifically, 
most tested LLMs are confident against conflicting \ck in over 60\% cases with the ``Speak Out Loud Instruction'' (green bar). Since commonsense facts are less likely to become outdated or change over time, it is crucial that LLMs stand on themselves when encountering conflicting information. Although not perfect, this offers promising insights into preventing attacks. 
In contrast, while the upward trend still holds, most LLMs are less likely to trust their own factual knowledge, often succumbing to conflicting \ck~(Figure \ref{fig:conflicting_reasoning}(b)(c)), highlighting the importance of knowledge authenticity in LLM-based systems.

\section{Discussion}

\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Models Leverage of Parametric Knowledge Given Contextual Knowledge}

In previous sections, we comprehensively investigate LLMs behavior in the dynamic integration of parametric knowledge (\pk) and contextual knowledge (\ck). Specifically:

\noindent $\bullet$ We identify four reasoning types based on various relationships between \pk~and \ck. To test models ability to leverage \pk~when encountering \ck, we introduce a new dataset \textsc{EchoQA}, spanning scientific, factual and commonsense knowledge (Section \ref{sec:experimental_setup}). 

\noindent $\bullet$ Our observations show that while LLMs possess the required knowledge, the presence of contextual information can significantly suppress their ability to leverage \pk, regardless of the model, reasoning type, or knowledge type (Section \ref{sec:Experimental_Results}).

\noindent $\bullet$ We also observe that certain types of knowledge are easier to recall. LLMs can better utilize their commonsense knowledge or when the context is irrelevant, compared to other types. This sheds lights on the improvement of integration of \pk~and \ck~to enhance reasoning performance.

\noindent $\bullet$ To improve the utilization of \pk, we designed a range of reasoning instructions. Results demonstrate excellent instruction-following capabilities of LLMs. When instructed to ``Speak Out Loud'' or ``Trust Yourself'', LLMs substantially improve their memory recall. However, a significant gap still remains in fully leveraging \pk~when \ck~is present (Figures \ref{fig:complementary_reasoning}, \ref{fig:conflicting_reasoning}, \ref{fig:irrelevant_reasoning}).

\subsection{Factors Affecting Models Leverage of Parametric Knowledge}\label{sec:factors_affecting}
In previous findings, we show that the awareness and utilization of \pk~can be influenced by instructions, knowledge type, and the intricate relations with \ck. Here, we discuss additional factors:

\noindent \textbf{LLMs can recall their popular knowledge more effectively.} Inspired by studies showing that LLMs lean on more popular entities, \textit{i.e.,} monthly associated Wikipedia page views \citep{mallen-etal-2023-trust,xie2024knowledgeconflict}, we evaluate representative LLMs on ConflictQA with Conflicting Reasoning \textit{w.r.t,} popularity of the topic entity in the question, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:ppl_graph}. Intuitively, LLMs can recall memories better for more popular questions. This upward trend holds across all LLMs and our reasoning instructions. Specifically, GPT-4o-mini and Llama 3.1-70B achieve nearly 80\% Memorization Ratio for the most popular questions, almost doubling that of the least popular ones. This observation might also explain why LLMs remember better for commonsense knowledge than others, indicating the reason behind is likely the knowledge frequency in training corpus.
\textbf{Introduction of knowledge relevant to the question may draw overly excessive attention to the context.} As mentioned in Section \ref{sec:complementary_reasoning} (Figure \ref{fig:complementary_reasoning}(a)), counter-intuitively, even though the given \ck~with \pk~is sufficient to answer the question, performance can still degrade compared to direct questioning. We manually examine 200 random samples generated by Llama 3.1-70B with Neutral Instruction on ALCUNA where LLMs were able to answer on their own but failed when complementary information was introduced. Note that we do ask LLMs to use their own knowledge in the instruction (Section \ref{sec:reasoning_instructions}). We discover that in 93. This suggests that  \textit{LLMs tend to ignore their own knowledge, inhibit deeper thinking and depend excessively on the context}. Although our ``Trust Yourself Instruction'' improves performance, it is still far from expectation, indicating that \textit{current LLMs struggle with integrating \pk~and \ck.}

We speculate that the phenomenon may stem from the post-training process \citep{chung2024scaling}. LLMs are fine-tuned with instruction, question and optional context in a fashion shown in Equation \ref{eqa:training}. This training approach encourages LLMs to answer based on either solely the context (\ck) or their internal knowledge (\pk) separately, rather than integrating both. We hypothesize that this training objective is the reason behind LLMs struggles in effectively combining \pk~and \ck.

\begin{equation}
    \textit{Instruction;~Context} \text{(Optional)};~\textit{Question} \rightarrow ~\textit{Answer} \\ \label{eqa:training}
\end{equation}
\noindent \textbf{The method of new knowledge fabrication impacts LLMs recall of memory.} \label{sec:way_of_fabrication}
 As mentioned in Section \ref{sec:experimental_setup}, conflicting facts (\ck) can be introduced through various methods: \textit{adding}, \textit{variation}, and \textit{dropping}.
For fair comparison, we report results under ``variation'' in Figure \ref{fig:conflicting_reasoning}, since all datasets introduce conflicts by default through this method.
Here, we further investigate how different fabrication methods affects LLMs awareness and leverage of \pk, by experimenting representative LLMs on ALCUNA in Conflicting Reasoning. Figure \ref{fig:alcuna_conflict} provides the following insights:

First, \textit{a significant gap exists between different fabrication methods}, although the overall trend observed in previous experiments remains consistent. This highlights that LLMs awareness of \pk~is strongly related to the how \ck is fabricated from (or related to) their original \pk.
 
Second, \textit{when new properties are added to an existing entity, LLMs are highly receptive to the new information}, exhibiting mostly lower than 10\% Memorization Ratio. This raises safety concerns, as LLMs may be convinced of the addition of disinformation to known entities, \textit{e.g.,} fake news. 

Third, \textit{dropping of known facts does less harms in LLMs memory recall}. Compared with the other methods, \ck constructed by dropping provides some information about the entity irrelevant to the question. In Figure \ref{fig:alcuna_conflict}(c), with only Neutral Instruction, LLMs can leverage \pk in up to 60\% of cases, echoing results in Irrelevant Reasoning (Section \ref{sec:irre_reasoning}) where LLMs recall their memories better than other reasoning types. However, they are still far from fully echoing their \pk.

\subsection{How to Better Leverage Parametric Knowledge Given the Context}

As demonstrated above, LLMs struggle to integrate their parametric knowledge (\pk) with contextual knowledge (\ck). However, this ability is crucial for both artificial and human intelligence. We have designed instructions to elicit \pk~to some extent (Section \ref{sec:reasoning_instructions}). Although effective, these instructions are progressively-enforced and may not be natural enough for practical applications. The key challenge is to enable LLMs to naturally learn how to balance the two sources of knowledge.
A promising direction is to develop an agent framework where LLMs assume a controlled amount of responsibility. By separating the process into two steps: first recalling the required knowledge, then reasoning, LLMs may avoid distraction from the context. Another potential solution is to fundamentally integrate \ck~and \pk~in post-training (Equation \ref{eqa:training}), which we leave for future work.


\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we comprehensively investigate how well LLMs can leverage their parametric knowledge (\pk) in the presence of contextual knowledge (\ck). We formulate four \textit{reasoning types}: \textit{Supportive, Complementary, Conflicting and Irrelevant}, based on diverse relationships between \ck~and \pk. To facilitate the study, we introduce a new dataset \textsc{EchoQA} spanning scientific, factual and commonsense knowledge for multiple LLMs. We design a series of \textit{reasoning instructions} with progressively more enforced tones to showcase LLMs leverage of \pk. We find that LLMs consistently struggle to fully leverage their \pk~when \ck~is present under different reasoning types. While the tailored reasoning instructions substantially improve their awareness of \pk, they still fall short of expectations. Furthermore, we show that LLMs awareness of their \pk~can be affected by the relation between \ck and \pk, instruction, entity popularity and methods of fabricating \ck, showing the vulnerability when faced with external knowledge. We hope that our work provides valuable resources and insights to guide future explorations in understanding LLM-based systems.
