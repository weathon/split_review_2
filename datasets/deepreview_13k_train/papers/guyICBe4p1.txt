\title{Truth-value judgment in language models: belief directions are context sensitive}

\begin{abstract}
Recent work has demonstrated that the latent spaces of large language models (LLMs) contain directions predictive of the truth of sentences.
Multiple methods recover such directions and build probes that are described as getting at a model's ``knowledge'' or ``beliefs''.
We investigate this phenomenon, looking closely at the impact of \textit{context} on the probes.
Our experiments establish where in the LLM the probe's predictions can be described as being \textit{conditional} on the preceding (related) sentences.
Specifically, we quantify the responsiveness of the probes to the presence of (negated) supporting and contradicting sentences, and score the probes on their consistency.
We also perform a causal intervention experiment, investigating whether moving the representation of a premise along these \textit{belief directions} influences the position of the hypothesis along that same direction.
We find that the probes we test are generally context sensitive, but that contexts which should not affect the truth often still impact the probe outputs.
Our experiments show that the type of errors depend on the layer, the (type of) model, and the kind of data.
Finally, our results suggest that belief directions are (one of the) causal mediators in the inference process that incorporates in-context information.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




As Large Language Models (LLMs) enjoy increasingly mainstream adoption, it becomes more important to understand why they fail in some cases, while excelling in others. 
Hallucination is a type of failure where the LLM produces grammatical but inaccurate text.
Recent work shows that LLM latent spaces contain directions that are predictive of the truth of sentences \citep{burns_discovering_2023,marks_geometry_2023}, and that this enables mitigating hallucination without additional training \citep{li_inference-time_2023}.
The presence of such directions suggests that the model represents sentences as more or less (likely to be) true, resembling a kind of occurrent belief.
And, by projecting representations on such \textit{belief directions} we obtain a probe whose output can be interpreted as an LLM's degree of belief in the truth of a sentence.
We call such probes \textit{belief probes}. 

Research into belief directions has already shown them to be useful for the mitigation of factual errors \cite{li_inference-time_2023}, a type of hallucination that can occur independently of context. 
Another type of hallucination is characterized by inconsistency \citep{huang_survey_2023}. 
Working towards the mitigation of this type of hallucination requires understanding the impact of context on belief probes.
Our experiments investigate the behaviour of \textit{belief probes} on sentences that appear in contexts with other related sentences. 
This enables us to determine how inferential contexts influence an LLM's assessment of truth-values. 
We call the task of correctly and coherently incorporating context in such assessments: \textit{truth-value judgment}. 
This name is borrowed from a task in language acquisition where subjects determine truth values with respect to a given situation \citep{mcdaniel_truth-value_1996}. 

LLMs perform well on tasks which are commonly held to require reasoning \citep{suzgun_challenging_2022}. 
Thus, we expect LLMs' degrees of belief to be sensitive to relevant information provided in context.
Given a premise $Q$ followed by a hypothesis $H$, we might expect a model to represent (potentially at different points) both $P(H)$ and $P(H | Q)$.
Our experiments determine to what extent, and in which layers, an LLM's degree of belief depends on context.

However, there are at least two ways in which $Q$ may be incorporated.
On the one hand, for the purposes of determining a degree of belief for $H$, the LLM could simply assume that if $Q$ is asserted it must be true.
On the other hand, the model could disregard whether $Q$ or $\neg Q$ is asserted, and instead use its own degree of belief in $Q$ when determining if it believes $H$.
Thus, our experiments determine when model behaviour: 1) assumes previous assertions are correct, or 2) is based on previous degrees of belief.

Finally, we investigate if the belief directions causally mediate truth-value judgment, or if they only reflect the outcome of that process.
In other words, we establish whether a representation's positioning along a belief direction determines (in part) where subsequent entailed or contradicted statements are positioned along the same direction. 

We find that \textit{belief probes} are generally context sensitive, but are also sensitive to irrelevant contexts, and can update probabilities incorrectly.
Finally, our results suggest that the belief directions are (one of the) causal mediators in
the inference process that incorporates in-context information.

In summary, our contributions are: 
(1) experiments demonstrating the context sensitivity of \textit{belief probes} to and the consistency with which they incorporate it; we quantify both across layers, model sizes (7 and 13 billion), type of training (pretrained-only vs. instruction-tuned); and
(2) an experiment demonstrating that \textit{belief directions} are causal mediators in natural language inferences.
We also propose a new variant of CCS \citep{burns_discovering_2023} for which convergence is more stable, and otherwise behaves and performs similarly.

\section{Related Work}

Using probes to predict the truth of sentences from LLM representations has recently received much interest.
\cite{burns_discovering_2023} propose two unsupervised probing methods based on the representations of contrasting sentence pairs. Their probes often outperform a (zero-shot) prompting approach. One of these methods (Contrast Consistent Search, CCS), when used with a linear probe, finds a direction in latent space that is optimized such that probabilities assigned to the contrasting sentences sum up to one. 
\cite{li_inference-time_2023} identify truth-correlative directions and shift model activations in the `truth direction' at inference time, thereby mitigating hallucination. They obtain directions using: 1) probes trained with logistic regression (LR) and CCS; and 2) a new method (Mass Mean Shift), which identifies the direction as the difference between the means of the true and false sentence representations. 
\cite{azaria_internal_2023} train truthfulness probes on a new dataset of out-of-distribution text constructed from tabular data. They find that probes trained for truthfulness also correlate with sentence length and the frequencies of words. But, their probe nonetheless outperforms the language model's predictions.
\cite{marks_geometry_2023} repurpose the Mass Mean Shift method by \citet{li_inference-time_2023} to create Mass-Mean Probing (MMP), taking the same method of finding belief directions and turning them into probes.
They introduce new datasets, some of which curated to include only statements that are ``uncontroversial, unambiguous, and simple enough that our LLMs are likely to understand them''.
They show that all probes (based on LR, CCS, and MMP) generalize well between their datasets, with MMP performing the best. 
Finally, they do a causal intervention experiment, where the representations are moved in the identified directions. 
They show that MMP is the best mediator, causing the highest increase in probability of the model calling a false statement true.
Most of the above-mentioned methods have performed their analyses on data consisting of single facts.
While \cite{burns_discovering_2023} did include datasets with facts appearing in context, including various NLI datasets, they did not study the impact of context on their probes in detail. 
We specifically study the in-context behaviour of these probes, analysing their consistency, and what this means for the way LLMs incorporate contextual information.

Like \citet{marks_geometry_2023}, we also investigate the causal implication of directions in LLM latent space.
However, rather than investigate what causes the greatest change in token predictions, we investigate which direction to move a premise in, such that it causes the correct change in the probability of a related hypothesis, as evaluated by the same direction.
Recent work has also criticised this type of probing. 
\citet{levinstein_still_2023} reproduce the work of \cite{burns_discovering_2023} and \citet{azaria_internal_2023}, and show their probes do not generalize to negated variants of the data, even when that negated data is also present during training.
\citeauthor{levinstein_still_2023} further point out conceptual problems, regarding the difficulty of isolating truth as a feature, especially using only coherence properties (such as probabilities summing up to one).
Probes might identify various sentence properties if they correlate with truth in the model's training data, such as what beliefs are commonly held.
Further criticism was provided by \citet{farquhar_challenges_2023}, specifically regarding unsupervised approaches such as CCS.
Their analysis includes a theoretical result showing that for any CCS probe there is an arbitrary probe with identical loss.
They also provide empirical results demonstrating that unsupervised probes fail to reveal ``knowledge'' when the truth is not the most salient feature. 
A common theme in these criticisms is the possibility of the truth of sentences being confounded with other features.
Specifically, both point out that these probing methods do not automatically find an objective evaluation of truth by the LLM, but could also find evaluations of the truth from another perspective. For example, how commonly held the belief in the sentence is, or what a person mentioned in the text would believe about the sentence.

In our experiments we do not use data mentioning specific people, minimizing the risk of finding their beliefs.
We acknowledge the difficulty of separating hypothetical LLM `beliefs' from commonly-held beliefs, but consider our results valuable regardless of who the apparent beliefs are best attributed to. 

\citet{zhu_language_2024} turn the risk of finding subjective beliefs to their advantage and use it to investigate LLM theory of mind.
Their method makes use of short stories told in third-person, paired with a belief that the may be correct or not depending on if it's judged from the main character's perspective or from a `God's-eye view'.
They find that the beliefs from both perspectives can be decoded accurately using linear probes.
Using the identified directions, they are also able to apply inference-time intervention \citep{li_inference-time_2023} to improve the model's overall performance on theory-of-mind tasks.











\section{Methodology}



We aim to establish the degree and kind of context sensitivity exhibited by the outputs of \textit{belief probes} when applied to each layer of LLMs. 
Specifically, we study the probabilities assigned by these probes to hypotheses before and after changing the premise that precedes it, or the model's representation thereof. 

We assume we are given propositions consisting of a premise $Q$ and a hypothesis $H$. 
The truth values of these propositions are denoted as $q,\neg q$ and $h,\neg h$. 
The meaning relation between the hypothesis and the premise is: entailment, contradiction, or neutral, which we denote with $R \in \{ e, c, n\}$.

\subsection{Belief probing methods}

We use three belief probing methods in our experiments.
These methods use datasets of sentences, consisting of both true and false statements. 
We distinguish between: positive (or affirmative) statements, which say something \textit{is} the case; and negative statements, which say something \textit{is not} the case.
We denote these two cases with: 
$X^+$%
and %
$X^-$%
, and their LLM vector representations are given as $\vec{x}^+$, $\vec{x}^-$. 

The belief probes $p(\vec{x})$ are assumed to (approximately) recover degrees of belief latently assigned to statements by a language model: $\PLM(X)$.
In our experiments we investigate to what extent and under which conditions belief probes recover conditional probabilities: $p(\vec{h}; q) \approx P_{\text{LM}}(h | Q)$, where $p(\vec{h}; q)$ represents the application of the probe to a representation obtained from a language model such that its input consisted of $H$ preceded by $Q$.


\paragraph{Contrast Consistent Search (CCS)}
is an unsupervised method which works by training a parameterized probe $p(\vec{x}) = \sigma(\phi(\vec{x}))$ such that:
\begin{align}
    p(\vec{x}^+) + p(\vec{x}^-) = 1. \label{eq:ccs_main}
\end{align}

This equation has a degenerate solution when 
$ p(\vec{x}^+) = p(\vec{x}^-) = 0.5 $. \label{eq:ccs_degen}
Thus, the probe is trained by gradient descent using two loss terms: 
\begin{align}
    L \; = \quad
    \frac{1}{N} \sum_i^N \left[ 1 - p(\vec{x}^+_i) - p(\vec{x}^-_i) \right]^2  
    \quad + \quad
    \frac{1}{N} \sum_i^N min\{ p(\vec{x}^+_i), p(\vec{x}^-_i) \}^2
    \label{eq:ccs_consistency}
\end{align}
which are called the consistency-loss and the confidence-loss respectively.
The first term encourages solutions for which equation \ref{eq:ccs_main} holds, the second encourages solutions other than the degenerate one.
We use CCS exclusively with linear probes, in which case the objective is to find a hyperplane $\vec{w}$ that, for each pair: (1) separates $\vec{x}^+$ from $\vec{x}^-$, and (2) is equidistant to $\vec{x}^+$ and $\vec{x}^-$.
The belief direction found by this method is the normal to this hyperplane
(also see \autoref{apx:ccs_interpretation}).

\paragraph{Contrast Consistent Reflection (CCR)} 
is proposed here as a new variant of CCS. 
Rather than only ensuring equal distances from the hyperplane, this method's objective is for $\vec{x}^+$ and $\vec{x}^-$ to be each other's reflection in the hyperplane $\vec{w}$.
It has the following loss: 
\begin{align}
    L \; = \quad
    \frac{1}{N} \sum_i^N ||\vec{x}_i^+ -  \vec{P}\vec{x}_i^- ||_2,
\end{align}
where $\vec{P} = \vec{I} - 2\vec{w}\vec{w}^\intercal$ which is the Householder transformation that performs the reflection.
\paragraph{Mass Mean Probing (MMP)}
is a supervised method, which defines the belief direction as the difference between the average of the correct ($x^\top$) and incorrect ($x^\bot$) statements (according to the gold labels, and regardless of polarity):
\begin{align}
    \bm{\theta_{\text{mm}}} &= \overline{\vec{x}}^\top - \overline{\vec{x}}^\bot 
\end{align}
Probes are then constructed as 
$
    p(\vec{x}) = \sigma(\bm{\theta_{\text{mm}}}^\intercal \vec{x})
$.
In our experiments, we evaluate the probes on data which was constructed differently from its training data. Therefore, we do not include the version of MMP that requires an i.i.d. assumption.
\paragraph{Logistic Regression (LR)} is also used to train a supervised probe. 
The inputs on which we train the LR probes are $\vec{x}^- - \vec{x}^+$, i.e. the difference between the negative and positive statements.
We use LR without a bias/intercept term. For all other hyperparameters we use the default values of SKLearn \citep{pedregosa_scikit-learn_2011}. 

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{ssec:evaluation}
 When evaluating the probes of any method we obtain the probability of a sentence as:
$$p(\vec{x}) = \frac{1}{2}(1 - p(\vec{x}^-) + p(\vec{x}^+)).$$
To evaluate the methods on their in-context behaviour we include a number of error scores, each indicating the extent to which methods violate some desirable behaviour. 
\autoref{tab:error_scores} gives an overview of the error scores and the expectations on which they are based.

The methods we include in our experiments can differ in magnitude of response to the alteration of the premise. 
We define the \textit{premise effect} ($PE$) as the difference in probability assigned to the hypothesis when preceded with an affirmed premise and probability assigned to the hypothesis on its own: $PE = p(\vec{h}; q^+) - p(\vec{h})$. 
We call the mean absolute $PE$ that a method obtains when evaluated its \textit{premise sensitivity}.

The first error score (SV) sums up all the \textit{premise effects} that occur in the opposite direction from what can be expected given the label.
Premise-hypothesis pairs which are labeled as entailment are expected to have a positive \textit{premise effect}, since the premise supports the hypothesis.
Similarly, those labeled as contradictions are expected to have a negative effect.

The other error scores measure the extent to which methods are self-consistent.
Unlike SV, the following scores do not depend on whether the probe's outputs agree with the label.
In order to make the error scores of different methods comparable to each other, we express the magnitude of the errors in multiples of the premise effect $PE$. 
This makes the consistency scores independent of the overall premise sensitivity of the method. 

E1 and E2 (see \autoref{tab:error_scores}) are based on the fact that we expect the probabilities to only depend on factors actually capable of influencing the truth value of the hypothesis. 
Thus, these error scores are proportional to the change in probability that occurs after having the hypothesis preceded by either: 1) a corrupted premise $\tilde{q}$, or 2) an unrelated premise $q'$.

E3 and E4 capture opposing expectations of how the language model should assign probability to a negated premise.
In one case (captured by E3), we assume the model bases itself on what is stated, and thus should treat the premise false when negated (and true when affirmed).
If the premise is false, the original meaning relation either switches (between entailment and contradiction), or becomes neutral. 
When the relationship switches the probability should change in the opposite direction from the case with the affirmed premise.
But, if negation creates a neutral relationship, then the probability should be the same as when there is no premise. 
In the other case (captured by E4), if the language model bases itself on its own evaluation of the premise, then it should ignore whether the premise is affirmed or negated. 
In that case, the probability assigned to the hypothesis should be equal regardless of the polarity of the premise assertion.
Because these are opposing scores, it is impossible to have zero errors on both simultaneously.

See \autoref{apx:error_scores} for additional details on the error scores.

\section{Experiments}

To answer our research questions, we make use of datasets with samples of related sentences, whose truth values depend on each other.
We use samples from these datasets by creating prompts where the sentences are either affirmed or negated.

\subsection*{Data}
We use two existing datasets in our experiments. The first dataset (SNLI, \citet{bowman_large_2015}) contains statements that describe a picture to which the LLM has no access. The second dataset (EntailmentBank, \citet{dalvi_explaining_2021}) contains hypotheses that are sentences with general world knowledge. These are facts the LLM may have encountered during training and for which it could already have a strong prior belief.
In the examples below, the bracketed characters determine if the premise and hypothesis are positive or negative. 

\paragraph{SNLI}
This dataset is a Natural Language Inference dataset, it consists of premise-hypothesis pairs, which are labeled as: entailment, contradiction, or neutral.
These labels describe the meaning relation $R$ between the sentences.
We embed the sentences as follows:
\begin{align*}
        \quad & \text{\small You are looking at a picture.}\\
    Q   \quad & \text{\small Describing it as: ``Four children are playing in some water.'' is [in]correct.}\\
    H   \quad & \text{\small Saying (about the picture) that: ``The children are wet.'' is [in]correct.}
\end{align*}
To avoid ambiguity, we establish a context based on the image-based origin of the dataset.

\paragraph{EntailmentBank} 
This dataset is similar in structure to SNLI, consisting of hypotheses and premises.
However, it does not contain contradictory meaning relations, only entailments.
The subject of the statements are also different since EntailmentBank was derived from ARC \citep{clark_think_2018}, which consists of grade-school level science questions.
We combine the premises of EntailmentBank with the questions and answers from ARC on which they were based.
In order to create contradictions we combine the premises of EntailmentBank with an incorrectly answered question. For example:
\begin{align*}
           \quad & \text{\small You are given the following question:}\\
           \quad & \text{\small $>$ In New York, the shortest period of daylight occurs during? (A) December (B) June}\\
    Q_{a}  \;\;  & \text{\small The statement ``New York is located in the northern hemisphere.'' is [in]correct.}\\
    Q_{b}  \;\;  & \text{\small The statement ``December is during the winter for New York.'' is [in]correct.}\\
    H      \quad & \text{\small Answering the question with ``(B) June'' is [in]correct.}
\end{align*}%
The answer ``June'' is incorrect, and thus $H$ contradicts the information in $Q_a, Q_b$ (when it is not negated), while in the sample with the correct answer $H$ would be entailed by $Q_a, Q_b$.

We use multiple variants of our datasets, starting with 
the data where the premises are affirmed to obtain $p(\vec{h}; {q}^+)$. 
Next, we include a variant with the premises' polarity changed from `correct' to `incorrect', resulting in $p(\vec{h}; {q}^-)$. 
We also alter the content of the premises by either: replacing them with unrelated premises; or, swapping their contents with random characters; yielding $p(\vec{h}; {q'})$ and $p(\vec{h}; \tilde{q})$, respectively.
Finally, we remove the premises outright, giving us $p(\vec{h})$.
See \autoref{apx:data_samples} for examples for each of these variants.

The unrelated premises are either: premises from other samples (SNLI), or distractor premises as provided in EntailmentBank.

\subsection*{Training probes}
We train probes on two variants of our data: 
on the data without any of the premises present (\texttt{no-prem}), and 
on the data with each premise present and affirmed (\texttt{pos-prem}).
In the first case, there is not context so any difference between $\vec{x}^+$ and $\vec{x}^-$ is the result of the model's training.
In the second case, $\vec{x}^+$ and $\vec{x}^-$ may have deviated in another way, because of their differing relation to the context.

Our probes are trained on the mean-normalized representations of the answer tokens of the sample.
To make the results from different probing methods comparable, we calibrate the probes such that their predictions on the data with no premise $p(\vec{h})$ have the same variance. 

We train probes on the following LLMs: Llama2-7b, Llama2-13b \citep{touvron_llama_2023b}, and 
OLMo-7b 
with and without instruction tuning \citep{groeneveld_olmo_2024}. 


\subsection{Effect of altering premises}
We evaluate the probes on held-out data, including data from all the other variants. 
We also include an additional baseline based on the LLM's language modeling head, where 
the probabilities assigned to the `correct'/`incorrect' tokens are rescaled to sum up to one.
\paragraph{Results.}
\input{tables/main_llama2-7b}
\autoref{tab:results} gives an overview of the average probabilities assigned to different parts of the data by probes trained on Llama2-7b. 
We observe that the probabilities assigned to hypotheses depend strongly on the presence of relevant premises.
When the hypothesis is entailed, the probabilities are higher, when the hypothesis is contradicted they are lower.
This is even true for probes trained without the premises present (\texttt{no-prem}), although the sensitivity to premises is lower.
Our results indicate that the belief direction derived from non-contextual data is still shows premise sensitivity when evaluated on data with premises.

Looking at the error scores in \autoref{tab:results}, we see that especially E1 and E2 are quite high.
The probes trained on \texttt{no-prem} often have E1 and E2 close to one.
Because these error scores are normalized by the premise effect, a value of one means that on average a corrupted or unrelated premise has an effect with the same magnitude as the original affirmed premise.
The error scores improve when probes are trained on \texttt{pos-prem}.
Interestingly, the error scores for Llama2-13b are not consistently lower than for Llama2-7b (see \autoref{tab:llama2-13b_results}).

The SNLI dataset is known to be a flawed benchmark, because it is possible to predict the label solely from the hypothesis \citep{poliak_hypothesis_2018}.
This is reflected by CCR probes trained on \texttt{no-prem}, despite it showing no premise sensitivity it still achieves an accuracy of $.63$.
LR and MMP do show some premise sensitivity, suggesting that they find directions indicative of more than just the spurious correlations present in the hypotheses of SNLI.
However, for LR the probe's behaviour does seem affected by the spurious correlations.
Its average probabilities for samples with negated premises is not between the probabilities obtained for samples with positive premises and no premises, resulting in a high E3+E4 score.

\subsection{Intervening on premise beliefs}%

In this experiment, we alter the LLM's internal representations directly, rather than only altering the input data. 
We take the \textit{belief directions} found by probing methods in the first experiment, and move the representations of the premises along this direction.

We perform this experiment on the \texttt{pos-prem} and \texttt{neg-prem} EntailmentBank data, moving the premises backwards or forwards (respectively) in the directions identified by each method.
We evaluate the probability of the hypothesis using the same direction as the premise was moved in. 
We perform the intervention using the same method and parameters as \citet{marks_geometry_2023}.
The intervention is done on Llama2-13b in layers 8-14, and applied to the representations of the answer tokens (correct, incorrect), and the period after.
All interventions have the same magnitude: $|\;\overline{\vec{x}}^\top - \overline{\vec{x}}^\bot|$.
In \autoref{fig:causal_result}, we can see the result of the causal intervention on the \texttt{pos-prem} variant.
When we move the affirmed premises backwards in the belief directions, the probabilities of entailed hypotheses decrease and the probabilities of contradicted hypotheses increase, exactly as expected.
We see that intervening with the direction found by LR has a smaller effect than MMP and CCR.
The largest change is a reduction of around ten percentage points for entailed hypotheses. 
See \autoref{fig:causal_neg} for the results on \texttt{neg-prem}.


\section{Conclusion}

We have investigated LLMs on the task of truth-value judgment, which requires correctly incorporating context when determining the truth value of a sentence.
Based on our expectations of how the probability of a sentence should change in supporting or contradicting contexts we created four error scores.
In our experiments, we used three probing methods on four language models and quantified how they assign probabilities to hypotheses in different contexts.
From our results it is clear that LLMs do incorporate context when representing sentences as more or less (likely to be) true. 
However, we also observe that contexts which should have no bearing on truth values still have a sizeable impact on a sentence' position along the \textit{belief directions} revealed by the probes.
Finally, our intervention experiment shows that the positioning of premises along belief directions (partially) determines the positioning of related hypotheses along the same direction.

We believe that our work is a first step to better understand and address inconsistencies in LLM generations. 
Fully understanding the in-context behaviour of belief-probing methods will help to ascertain exactly why inconsistent generations arise, for example whether: the model has represented part of the context as false; the model fails to accurately represent the meaning relation between the context and possible generations; or both.





\subsubsection*{Limitations and future work}

In future work, we would like to delve deeper into the representations of meaning-relations in LLMs, and the exact mechanisms responsible for incorporating that information into the belief directions.
For example, by investigating the construction of probes that reveal if a model represents two sentences as having a particular meaning relation. 
This would give us probabilities for each of three relevant variables in our setup: $H$, $Q$, $R$, and would allow for a more precise evaluation.
