\title{Deterministic Error Bounds for Euclidean Clustering}

\begin{abstract}
This paper gives a closed-form solution to Euclidean clustering, also known as k-means clustering. The key observation behind our solution is that the features of clustered data lie near a subspace whose projection operator encodes the clustering.  In contrast to classical alternating approaches like Lloyd's algorithm or K-means++, which suffer local minima, our solution can be trivially computed with a singular value decomposition. Moreover, we show that if the distinct clusters are sufficiently well-defined (meaning different clusters are sufficiently separated, and data in each cluster not too scattered), our solution is deterministically guaranteed to be correct. We corroborate our theoretical findings with a comprehensive array of experiments, showing that simple relaxations of our solution yield algorithms that not only rival but also surpass the current state-of-the-art in a wide variety of settings, both in terms of accuracy and speed.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


Euclidean clustering is a fundamental learning task, with applications spanning robotics, computer vision, environmental modeling, and many more (Ahmed et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2023). In words, this type of clustering involves identifying groups of data points that are close to each other in Euclidean space with respect to some metric, typically the $\ell_{2}$ -norm. Predominantly, this task is performed through variants of Lloyd’s algorithm (Lloyd, 1982), such as K-means $^{++}$ (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007), or spectral clustering $\mathrm{Ng}$ et al., 2001). In essence, these algorithms operate iteratively, starting with the selection of K initial cluster centroids. Then, in each iteration, these algorithms alternate between two main steps: (i) assigning each data point to its nearest centroid, forming clusters, and then (ii) recalculating the centroids as the mean of all the points assigned to each cluster. This process is repeated until convergence, typically stopping when the centroids no longer change significantly or after a specified number of iterations. The main idea behind this approach is to try to minimize the sum of squared distances between data points and their assigned centroids. Unfortunately, the alternating nature of these methods induces local minima, meaning that they heavily depend on initialization. Due to this and other shortcomings in existing methodology, coupled with the cornerstone applicability of this learning task, Euclidean clustering remains an active and vibrant area of research (see (Liu et al., 2017; He & Zhang, 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Lee, 2019; Yang et al., 2018; Wu & Peng, 2017; Ma et al., 2023; Ahmed et al., 2020; Chen & Witten, 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023; Laber et al., 2023) and references therein for yet a very incomplete list of related work).  



\section{2 PREAMBLE AND RELATED WORK  }


Let $\{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}\}$ be a collection of n samples in $\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m}}$ . Let $\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ be a partition of $\{1,\ldots,\mathrm{n}\}$ indicating the true clustering of the samples among $\mathrm{K}\leq\operatorname*{min}(\mathrm{m},\mathrm{n})$ groups. Suppose  

$$
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}\;=\;\sum_{\mathrm{i}=1}^{\mathrm{n}}\mu_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\mathbb{1}_{\{\mathrm{i}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}}+\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{i}},
$$  

where $\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m}}$ is the true centroid of the $\mathrm{k^{th}}$ cluster, $\mathbb{1}$ denotes the indicator function, and $\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{i}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m}}$ determines the separation of $\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}$ from its corresponding centroid, which can be considered as noise. Given $\{{\bf x}_{\mathrm{i}}\}$ , our goal is to estimate $\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ and $\{\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ . This is typically pursued through the following optimization  

$$
\operatorname*{min}_{\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}},\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\}}\,\sum_{\mathrm{k}=1}^{\mathrm{K}}\,\sum_{\mathrm{i}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}}\;\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}-\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}\|,
$$  

where $\|\cdot\|$ is any suitable metric, usually the $\ell_{2}$ norm, in which case (2) minimizes the intra-cluster variance. Traditionally, (2) is sought through variants of Lloyd’s Algorithm (Lloyd, 1982), which initializes centroid estimates $\{\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}\}$ , and alternates between solving the following problems until convergence  

$$
\hat{\mathrm{k}}_{\mathrm{i}}=\underset{\mathbf{k}}{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}\,\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}-\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}\|,\qquad\qquad\hat{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}=\underset{\mu_{\mathrm{k}}}{\mathrm{arg}\,\mathrm{min}}\,\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}:\mathbf{\beta}}\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}-\mu_{\mathrm{k}}\|.
$$  

In words, these two problems iteratively assign the $\mathrm{i^{th}}$ sample to its closest centroid estimate, indexed by $\hat{\mathrm{k}}_{\mathrm{i}}$ , and recomputes the centroid $\hat{\pmb{\mu}}_{\mathrm{k}}$ as the distance minimizer among the samples assigned to the $\mathrm{k^{ih}}$ group. There are numerous variants of this algorithm, including a weighted version (Liu et al., 2017), a kernel Nystrom approximation (He & Zhang, 2018), a fast adaptive version (Wang et al., 2019), a non-alternating stochastic version (Lee, 2019), deep learning methods (Yang et al., 2018; Wu & Peng, 2017), and many more (Ma et al., 2023; Ahmed et al., 2020; Chen & Witten, 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2023; Laber et al., 2023).  



\section{3 MAIN RESULTS  }


Here we propose a new method to learn $\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ and $\{\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ directly, without the need for an alternating algorithm. The key insight behind our solution is that the features of the data lie near a subspace whose projection operator $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ encodes the clustering. We simply estimate such operator using a singular value decomposition, and threshold it to reveal the clustering. To see this, let $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m}\times\mathrm{n}}$ be the matrix containing the columns in $\{{\bf x}_{\mathrm{i}}\}$ . Let $\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{n\timesK}}$ be the matrix containing the K leading right singular vectors of $\mathbf{X}$ . Let $\hat{\mathbf{P}}:=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\,\in\,\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{n}\times\mathrm{n}}$ be the projection operator onto span $(\mathbf{V})$ . Define $\hat{{\mathbf P}}_{\lambda}$ as the entry-wise thresholded version of $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ with entries  

$$
[\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{:}}]_{\mathrm{ij}}\;:=\;\left\{\begin{array}{l l}{\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{ij}}}&{\mathrm{if~}|\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{ij}}|>\lambda}\\ {0}&{\mathrm{otherwise},}\end{array}\right.
$$  

where $\lambda\in[0,1]$ is a parameter that depends on the positions of $\{\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ and the intra-cluster dispersion level determined by $\{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{i}}\}$ . Our estimates are the distinct supports (i.e., non-zero patterns) of the columns in $\hat{{\mathbf P}}_{\lambda}$ , denoted as $\{\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{k}}\}$ , and  

$$
\hat{\pmb{\mu}}_{\mathrm{k}}\ :=\ \underset{\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}}{\arg\operatorname*{min}}\ \sum_{\mathrm{i}\in\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{k}}}\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}-\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}\|.
$$  

For example, in the special case of the $\ell_{2}$ norm, $\hat{\pmb{\mu}}_{\mathrm{k}}$ simplifies to $\frac{1}{\mathrm{n}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}\in\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{k}}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}$ .  

Our main theoretical result shows that these estimates are deterministically correct as long as the samples in each cluster are not too scattered relative to the location of the centroids. In order to present this result, let $\mathcal{M}^{\star}\,\in\,\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m\timesK}}$ be the matrix containing the columns in $\{\mu_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ . Let $\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\:\in$ $\{0,1\}^{\mathrm{n}}$ be the vector whose $\mathrm{i}^{\mathrm{th}}$ entry is equal to 1 if $\mathrm{i}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}$ , and equal to zero otherwise. In words, $\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}$ is the binary vector indicating the samples that belong to the $\mathrm{k^{th}}$ cluster. Let $\Omega^{\star}\,\in\,\{0,1\}^{\mathrm{n}\times\mathrm{K}}$ and $\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m}\times\mathrm{n}}$ be the matrices containing the columns in $\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{i}}\}$ . With this we are ready to present our main result, which we summarize in the following theorem.  

Theorem 1. Let $\delta>0$ be the gap between the $\mathrm{K}^{\mathrm{th}}$ singular value of $\mathbf{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}\Omega^{\star\sf T}$ and the $(\mathrm{K}\!+\!1)^{\mathrm{th}}$ singular value of X. Suppose  

$$
\delta\ >\ \sqrt{2^{3}\mathrm{K}}\|\mathbf{Z}\|\operatorname*{max}_{\mathrm{k}}|\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}|.
$$  

Then the clusters $\{\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{k}}\}$ obtained with 2 max1k |Ω⋆| are identical to the true clusters {Ω⋆k} (up to a label permutation).  

Intuitively, the $\mathrm{K}^{\mathrm{th}}$ and $(\mathrm{K}+1)^{\mathrm{th}}$ singular values of $\mathbf{\mathcal{M}}^{\star}\Omega^{\star\sf T}$ and $\mathbf{X}$ can be respectively interpreted as the smallest separation between centroids, and the largest separation of a sample from its cluster. The condition on $\delta$ essentially requires that the former (distance between clusters) is large enough relative to the later (cluster width), so that the clusters are discernible from our estimator, and no sample is misclustered. In this sense, $\delta$ can be interpreted as the signal to noise ratio. The proof is in Section 4, and it essentially uses the Davis-Kahan $\mathrm{sin}(\Theta)$ Theorem (Davis & Kahan, 1970; Stewart & Sun, 1990) to show that the support of $\hat{{\bf P}}_{\lambda}$ will coincide with that of the true $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ (projection operator onto the s√pan of $\Omega^{\star}$ ). For incoherent matrices Pˆ, the condition on $\delta$ can be relaxed by a factor of $\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{K}^{4}/\dot{\sqrt{n}})$ using the $\ell_{\infty}$ perturbation bound in (Fan et al., 2018) instead. In practice, however, we see that both conditions appear to be overly sufficient, as the method succeeds under much weaker conditions (see Figure 1).  

Notice that in general, $\mathrm{max_{k}}\left|\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\right|$ is unknown, and therefore, so is $\lambda$ . However, since Theorem 1 guarantees the existence of a parameter $\lambda$ that results in a perfect recovery of (the support of) $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ , $\lambda$ can be trivially found by searching for the threshold that produces a matrix $\mathbf{P}_{\lambda}$ with exactly K distinct disjoint supports that cover all the columns and all the rows.  

We point out that perfect recovery of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ is a sufficient condition for clustering, convenient for analysis, but by no means necessary, and overly strict in practice. This is confirmed by our experiments in Section 6, showing that perfect clustering is possible even if $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ is not perfectly recovered. This is quite fortunate and unsurprising, because even if the support of $\hat{{\mathbf P}}_{\lambda}$ is different than that of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ for every $\lambda\in[0,1]$ , if the difference between $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ is not overwhelmingly large, $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ may still contain enough information to reveal the clustering through a relaxed method. An example of such relaxation would be to agglomerate samples $(\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j})$ if $[\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\lambda}]_{\mathrm{ij}}>0$ as we decrease $\lambda$ from 1 to 0. Our future work will investigate methods like this, their properties, and their guarantees.  

Finally, notice that under the conditions of Theorem 1, our estimators $\{\hat{\pmb{\mu}}_{\mathrm{k}}\}$ are the optimal estimators of $\{\mu_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ . We formalize this in the following corollary  

Corollary 1. Under the same assumptions of Theorem $^{\,I}$ , and up to a label permutation, our closedform estimators $\{\hat{\pmb{\mu}}_{\mathrm{k}}\}$ satisfy  

$$
\hat{\pmb{\mu}}_{\mathrm{k}}~=~\underset{\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}}{\arg\operatorname*{min}}\sum_{\mathrm{i}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}}\|\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{i}}-\pmb{\mu}_{\mathrm{k}}\|.
$$  

The proof of Corollary 1 follows immediately because under the assumptions of Theorem 1, $\{\hat{\Omega}_{\mathrm{k}}\}$ are equal to $\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ (recall the definition of $\hat{\pmb{\mu}}_{\mathrm{k}}$ in (3) and compare to (5)). We point out that our solution truly minimizes the distance to the centroid (e.g., variance) of the true clustering. This should be contrated with the classical solution to (2) pursued by Lloyd’s Algorithm and its variants, which minimizes distance with respect to some estimated clustering $\{\Omega\}$ , which may be correct or incorrect.  



\section{4 REASONING AND PROOF  }


The key idea behind our approach is that the rows of $\mathbf{X}$ lie near the subspace spanned by $\Omega^{\star}$ , whose projection operator $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ encodes the clustering through its nonzero entries. Since projection operators are unique, we can estimate $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ . Using perturbation theory we show that under the assumptions of Theorem 1, our estimator Pˆ cannot be too different from $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ , and so a simple thresholding of Pˆ with an appropriate parameter $\lambda$ will reveal the nonzero entries of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ , and in turn, the desired clustering.  

To start, recall that $\mathcal{M}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m\timesK}}$ and $\mathbf{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathrm{m\timesn}}$ contain the columns in $\{\mu_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ and $\{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{i}}\}$ , and that $\Omega^{\star}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathrm{n}\times\mathrm{K}}$ indicates the clustering. With this, we can rewrite (1) simultaneously for every i as  

$$
\mathbf{X}\,=\,\mathcal{M}^{\star}\Omega^{\star\top}\,+\,\mathbf{Z}.
$$  

For example, if there were $\mathrm{K}=3$ clusters, and the if the $\mathrm{k^{th}}$ block of columns in $\mathbf{X}$ lie in the $\mathrm{k^{th}}$ cluster, our construction would look like:  

![](images/15b1742c3bb268ecfcb092cf14f6a4262ea01e607161f2b25ef915b22a3f981c.jpg)  

Here, the white and shaded areas represent zero and non-zero entries, respectively. It follows that the rows of $\mathbf{X}$ approximately lie in the subspace spanned by $\Omega^{\star}$ . We can estimate this subspace as the span of $\mathbf{V}$ . Now, in general, even if $\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{0}$ , $\mathbf{V}$ may not be equal to $\pmb{\Omega}$ , because $\mathbf{V}$ and $\pmb{\Omega}$ may be  

different bases of the same subspace. However, projector operators are unique, so we can estimate $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ as $\hat{\mathbf{P}}=\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\top}$ (recall that the columns of $\mathbf{V}$ contain the K leading right singular vectors of $\mathbf{X}$ ). Next observe that the supports of $\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\}$ are disjoint, so $\Omega^{\star}$ is orthogonal. Hence,  

$$
\mathbf{P}^{\star}\,=\,\bar{\Omega}\bar{\Omega}^{\mathsf{T}},
$$  

where $\bar{\pmb{\Omega}}$ is the matrix formed with $\left\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}/\|\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\|\right\}$ as columns. That is, $\bar{\pmb{\Omega}}$ is equal to $\Omega^{\star}$ except its columns are normalized. From (6) we can see that the columns and rows of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ have the exact same supports as the columns in $\Omega^{\star}$ . For example, if the first third of the samples were in one cluster, and similar for the second and third thirds, as in our construction above, the supports of a pair $(\Omega^{\star},\mathbf{P}^{\star})$ would look like:  

$$
\Omega^{\star}=\boxed{\overline{{\ L}}},\qquad\mathbf{P^{\star}}=\boxed{\overline{{\ L}}}.
$$  

We will now show that the entries of our estimator $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ cannot be too different from the entries in $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ . Specifically, we will show that corresponding entries in these matrices cannot differ by more than 2 max1k |Ω⋆|. To see this, write  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{\|\mathbf{P}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}\|_{F}^{2}\,=\,\|\mathbf{P}^{\star}\|_{F}^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\sf T}\mathbf{P}^{\star})+\|\hat{\mathbf{P}}\|_{F}^{2}\,=\,2\mathrm{K}-2\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{P}}^{\sf T}\mathbf{P}^{\star})}\\ &{=\,2\mathrm{K}-2\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\Omega}\bar{\Omega}^{\sf T})\,=\,2(\mathrm{K}-\|\mathbf{V}^{\sf T}\bar{\Omega}\|_{F}^{2})}\\ &{=:\,2(\mathrm{K}-\cos^{2}(\Theta))\,=\,2\sin^{2}(\Theta)\,\le\,2\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{F}^{2}/\delta^{2},}\end{array}
$$  

where the last inequality follows directly by the Davis-Kahan $\mathrm{sin}(\Theta)$ Theorem (Davis & Kahan, 1970; Stewart & Sun, 1990). Then  

$$
\|\mathbf{P}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,\|\mathbf{P}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}\|_{F}\,\leq\,\frac{\sqrt{2}\|\mathbf{Z}\|_{F}}{\delta}\,\leq\,\frac{\sqrt{2\mathrm{K}}\|\mathbf{Z}\|}{\delta}.
$$  

Substituting $\delta$ from (4), and defining $\mathrm{N:=max_{k}\,\lvert\Omega_{k}^{\star}\rvert}$ as the size of the largest cluster, we see that  

$$
\|\mathbf{P}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}\|_{\infty}\,\leq\,\frac{\sqrt{2\mathrm{K}}\|\mathbf{Z}\|}{\delta}\,<\,\frac{\sqrt{2\mathrm{K}}\|\mathbf{Z}\|}{\sqrt{2^{3}\mathrm{K}}\|\mathbf{Z}\|\operatorname*{max}_{\mathrm{k}}|\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}|}\,=\,\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}.
$$  

This implies that the difference of any two entries in $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ is bounded as  

$$
-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}\;<\;\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{ij}}^{\star}-\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{ij}}\;<\;\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\forall\,\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}.
$$  

On the other hand, from (6) and the definitions of $\Omega^{\star}$ and $\bar{\pmb{\Omega}}$ , we can see that the entries of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ are  

$$
\mathbf{P}_{\mathrm{ij}}^{\star}\;:=\;\left\{\begin{array}{l l}{\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}|}}&{\mathrm{if~i,j\in\Omega^{\star}_{k}~}}\\ {0}&{\mathrm{otherwise.}}\end{array}\right.
$$  

Plugging (8) in the second inequality of (7), we see that if $\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}\in\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}$ ,  

$$
\hat{\bf P}_{\mathrm{ij}}^{\star}\;>\;\frac{1}{|\Omega_{\bf k}^{\star}|}-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}\;\geq\;\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}}-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}\;=\;\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}.
$$  

Similarly, plugging (8) in the first inequality of (7), we see that if i and j are not in the same $\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}$ ,  

$$
\hat{\bf P}_{\mathrm{ij}}^{\star}\;<\;0+\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}\;=\;\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}.
$$  

Taking $\begin{array}{r}{\lambda=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}}\end{array}$ , we see that after thresholding,  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{[\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\lambda}]_{\mathrm{ij}}\;=\;\left\{\begin{array}{l l}{\hat{\mathbf{P}}_{\mathrm{ij}}>\frac{1}{2\mathrm{N}}}&{\mathrm{if~i,j\in\Omega_{k}~}}\\ {0}&{\mathrm{otherwise.}}\end{array}\right.}\end{array}
$$  

We thus see that the supports of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ and $\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}_{\lambda}$ are identical, which concludes the proof.  



\section{5 PRACTICAL RELAXATIONS  }


Note that achieving perfect recovery of the support of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ serves as a sufficient condition for clustering that proves to be convenient for analytical purposes. Nonetheless, it is important to recognize that this condition is not necessary and can be overly stringent in practical scenarios. Our experiments in Section 6 corroborate this fact, as they demonstrate that perfect clustering can still be achieved even when the noise level is above the requirements of Theorem 1, and even when the support of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ cannot be exactly recovered through $\mathbf{\bar{P}}_{\lambda}$ . This outcome is both fortunate and unsurprising because, even if the support of $\hat{{\mathbf P}}_{\lambda}$ differs from that of $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ across all values of $\lambda$ within the range [0, 1], as long as the dissimilarity between $\mathbf{P}^{\star}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ is not excessively large, $\hat{\mathbf{P}}$ can still contain sufficient information to unveil clustering patterns through a more lenient approach. This section outlines several candidate approaches, each with advantages and disadvantages, which we also discuss.  



\section{6 EXPERIMENTS  }


To verify our results and test their flexibility, we ran a series of experiments on synthetic and real data. In these experiments we generated data according to (1), where the entries of each $\mu_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}$ are drawn i.i.d according to a standard normal distribution, the n samples are evenly distributed among the $\mathrm{K}$ clusters $\left\{\Omega_{\mathrm{k}}^{\star}\right\}$ (up to rounding error), and the entries of each $\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{i}}$ are i.i.d normal with zero mean and variance $\mathrm{s}^{2}$ .
