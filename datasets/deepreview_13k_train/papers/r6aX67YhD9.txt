\title{Learning to Watermark LLM-generated Text via Reinforcement Learning}

\begin{abstract}

We study how to watermark LLM outputs, i.e. embedding algorithmically detectable signals into LLM-generated text to track misuse. Unlike the current mainstream methods that work with a \textit{fixed} LLM, we expand the watermark design space by including the LLM \textit{tuning} stage in the watermark pipeline.
While prior works focus on \textit{token}-level watermark that embeds signals into the \textit{output}, we design a \textit{model}-level watermark that embeds signals into the LLM \textit{weights}, and
such signals can be detected by a \textit{paired} detector.
We propose a co-training framework based on reinforcement learning that iteratively (1) trains a detector to detect the generated watermarked text and (2) tunes the LLM to generate text easily detectable by the detector while keeping its normal utility. We empirically show that our watermarks are more accurate, robust, and adaptable (to new attacks). It also allows watermarked model open-sourcing. In addition, if used together with alignment, the extra overhead introduced is low -- only training an extra reward model (i.e. our detector). We hope our work can bring more effort into studying a broader watermark design that is not limited to working with a fixed LLM.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Watermarking LLM (Large Language Model) outputs, i.e., embedding algorithmically detectable signals into LLM-generated text, has recently become a potential solution to track LLM misuse~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark,kuditipudi2023robust}.
So far, LLM watermarking methods focus on \textit{token-level} distortion in the LLM output. This framework has several limitations. (1) Since we still need the watermarked text to be humanly readable, the output distortion induced needs to be minimized. As a result, watermark accuracy might be suboptimal because the watermark signal injected in the output space is constrained by the readability tradeoff. (2) For the same reason, the limited output distortion leads to vulnerability to paraphrasing attacks~\cite{kirchenbauer2023reliability}. (3) The design space of watermark is inflexible -- all the practitioners can do is post-processing the generated text from a fixed LLM, which leads to certain problems, e.g. lack of adaptability to newly discovered adversarial attacks. (4) It forbids practitioners from open-sourcing the watermarked LLMs. If they want to do so, they would also have to release the unwatermarked LLM because the watermarks are added post hoc, defeating the original purpose of protecting intellectual property.
In this work, we ask: Can we watermark LLM texts by directly finetuning the LLM, so that we can enlarge the watermark design space? The watermark in our case is injected by model-level changes, and the resulting LLM outputs carry the signals that can be identified by detection.



In other words, we include the LLM \textit{tuning} stage into the watermark pipeline as opposed to the prior methods that only work with a fixed LLM, and thus expand the design space of watermark. Unlike prior works which detectors are simple statistical tests, our detector is a language model that predicts the likelihood of whether a text is watermarked or not. Specifically, we tune the LLM to inject the watermark signal while training a \textit{paired} detector model that detects the signal. The key insight is: by tuning the LLM to adapt to the detector, we make the detection easier and more accurate.

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (right) shows the overview of our reinforcement learning-based watermark framework. We iteratively co-train both the LLM and the detector. In each step, we instruction-tune the LLM to distort its weights and therefore its output distribution. Then, we train the detector to detect the signal from the distorted outputs.

We choose reinforcement learning~\cite{ouyang2022training,arulkumaran2017deep} %\yang{if we are citing RL, we shouldn't be citing this RLHF paper, or at least not only it} \xiaojun{added} 
as the co-training framework for several reasons. (1) We can adapt the reward model as a detector. (2) We can leverage the strong generalizability of the RL algorithm~\cite{ouyang2022training} to make sure the finetuned LLM can generate text that is easily detectable by the detector. (3) We still need to preserve the text readability in general, which can be done by RLHF's utility-preserving objective.
Our approach has several advantages compared to the prior works. (1) \textbf{Detection Accuracy}: Since we tune the LLM to fit the detector, we create more space for the detector because we explicitly ask the LLM to generate text easily detectable to the detector. (2) \textbf{Robustness}: Because we do not aim to rely on low-level (e.g. token-level) output distortion for watermark detection, our watermark can be more robust to adversarial attacks like paraphrasing. (3) \textbf{Adaptability}: Since our framework is data-driven, we can easily iterate the LLM to adapt to new attacks by incorporating adversarially generated text into the training data, in the style of adversarial training. This is not a feature supported by the traditional fixed-model approach. (4) \textbf{Zero Watermark-Generation Cost}: Once the LLM is deployed, we do not need any special operations during text generation to embed watermarks. This zero-cost watermark generation makes our approach appealing when the LLM is deployed to serve at a very large scale. (5) \textbf{Open-source Feasibility}: Since our watermarks are internally embedded into the LLM weights and no special operation is needed in a post-hoc text generation, practitioners can release the watermarked LLM without being forced to release an unwatermarked version.

Our approach has several limitations compared to the fixed-model approaches. (1) The one-time cost during the instruction-tuning stage might make the overall watermark cost higher. (2) We require practitioners to prepare relevant training data for instruction-tuning since ours is a data-driven approach. (3) Our method has a higher detection overhead\footnote{i.e. running inference on a language model vs. performing simple statistical tests in the fixed-model approach.}.
The first two limitations can be mitigated if our framework is used together with the standard LLM alignment process. If combined with the alignment, the extra overhead introduced is low -- only training an extra reward model (i.e. our detector).

Through the experiments, we show that our framework achieves near-perfect detection rate and outperforms existing token-level approaches. We observe that our watermark is also robust to small perturbations on the watermarked text. If we encounter large perturbations, we can include the perturbed samples in the training stage, following the style of adversarial training, and achieve high detection rate (AUC over 0.99), showing a strong adaptability of our approach unsupported by the token-level watermarks.






\section{Preliminary}


\paragraph{Notations.} Let $\mathcal{V}$ denote the LLM token space. We use ${x} = [x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots] \in \mathcal{V}^*$ to denote a sequence of tokens (i.e. a sentence). An LLM is a function that, given a sequence of tokens, predicts the probability of the next token using the model with parameters $\theta$.
Given a prompt $x$, we use $\pi_{\theta}(a_t|s_t)$ to denote the probability distribution of the next token, where $s_t={x}$ is the current ``state'' following notations in the RLHF literature.
We use $f({x};\theta)$ to represent the text $y \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot | x)$ generated by $\theta$ given prompt $x$ in the autogressive way.\footnote{We slightly misuse the notation to use a function $f(.)$ to represent the sampling process of text generation.}

\paragraph{Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback.} Reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)~\cite{ouyang2022training} is the standard pipeline at this moment to align an LLM with human preferences.
In RLHF, we first train a reward model (RM) $r: \mathcal{V}^* \times \mathcal{V}^* \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, where $r(x,y)$ is the reward that measures whether the completion $y$ given the prompt $x$ is desired by human or not.\footnote{
Since we do not want the optimized LLM to deviate from the reference model to avoid out-of-distribution problems, we also add a KL divergence term to the reward~\cite{zheng2023secrets,holtzman2019curious}, i.e.,
$r_{total}(x,y;\theta) = r(x,y) - \eta \text{KL}(\pi_\theta (a_t|s_t), \pi_{ref}(a_t|s_t))$} The RM training requires an RM dataset $\mathcal{D}_{RM} = \{(x_i, y_i^r, y_i^c)\}_{i=1}^n$, where $x$ is the prompt, $y_r$ is a rejected completion and $y_c$ is a chosen completion based on human preference, and the RM is optimized to minimize $r(x_i,y_i^r) - r(x_i,y_i^c)$.\footnote{More precisely, the full RM objective is $\log\sigma(r(x_i,y_i^r) - r(x_i,y_i^c))$ where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the sigmoid function. We omit it for simplicity. Whenever we say $r(x_i,y_i^r) - r(x_i,y_i^c)$ in the paper, e.g. in Eqn.(\ref{eq:overall}), we mean the full objective.}
Second, we use Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{ouyang2022training} to maximize the following objective for the LLM $\theta$'s policy given the trained reward model $\theta^{RM} $ and the original model $\theta^o$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ppo}
\begin{aligned}
&\text{objective}(\theta, \theta^{RM} )= \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\pi_\theta}} \biggl [  r_{\theta^{RM} }(x,y) - \\
&\beta \cdot \log \left ( \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\theta^o}(y|x)} \right ) \biggr ] + \gamma \cdot \text{KL}\left (\pi_{\theta^o}(y | x),  \pi_{\theta}(y | x) \right ) 
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{\theta}$ is the learned RL policy for model $\theta$, $\beta$ is the KL reward coefficient, and $\gamma$ is the strength of KL penalty.



\section{Scenario and Goal}

\paragraph{Scenario.} We assume we are LLM service providers who aim to track the generated text from the LLMs we develop through watermarks.
In addition, we have the computational resources to finetune the LLM and the ability to collect relevant finetuning data. The goal is to distinguish the text generated by our LLM from any other sources (e.g. written by humans or generated by different LLMs) as accurately as possible within a reasonable cost while not hurting the utility of the LLMs on normal tasks.

\paragraph{Goal.} Given the original LLM with parameter $\theta^{o}$, we want to finetune it into another LLM $\theta^{w}$ paired with a detector $D: \mathcal{V}^* \times \mathcal{V}^ * \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ that has the same architecture as an RM, except that it outputs a detection score that quantifies how likely the output $y$ given a prompt $x$ is generated by our watermarked model $\theta^{w}$.
Let $\theta^{d}$ denote the parameter of the detector, $D(x, y; \theta^{d})$ denote the predicted score from $\theta^{d}$ that output $y$ is generated by model $\theta^{w}$ given prompt $x$.\footnote{We omit $\theta^w$ in the inputs for simplicity. The detector $\theta^d$ is paired with the watermarked LLM $\theta^w$.} We want $\theta^w$ and $\theta^d$ to satisfy the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
    \item Given an output $y^w := f(x; \theta^w)$ generated by the watermarked model $\theta^w$ from prompt $x$, the detection score $D(x, y^w; \theta^{d})$ is high.
    \item Given an output $y^{nw}$ not generated by the watermarked model $\theta^w$, e.g. written by humans or generated by other LLMs, the detection score $D(x, y^{nw}; \theta^{d})$ is low.
    \item Our procedure should distort the output distribution as little as possible, preserving the utility from the original LLM, i.e. $f(x; \theta^{w}) \approx f(x; \theta^{o})$.
\end{enumerate}


\section{Reinforcement Learning-based Watermark}

\subsection{Overview}

Our key insight is: \textit{we design the watermark detector to be the reward model in RLHF so that the LLM can be finetuned to generate text with high scores predicted by the detector}.
Given a non-watermarked dataset $\mathcal{D}^{nw} := \{(x_i, y_i^{nw})\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{D}^{nw}|}$ where $y^{nw}$ is the non-watermarked (e.g. human-written) output corresponding to the prompt $x$, our overall objective is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overall}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\theta^d, \theta^w}&{\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^{nw}) \sim \mathcal{D}^{nw}}[D(x, y^{nw}; \theta^d) - D(x, f(x;\theta^w); \theta^d) }] \\
& + \lambda \cdot \text{Reg}(\theta^w,\theta^o)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $f(x;\theta^w)$ is the generated watermarked text from the watermarked LLM $\theta^w$ that detector $\theta^d$ needs to distinguish from the non-watermarked text $y^{nw}$, $\text{Reg}(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the regularization term that ensures the reliability of generated text not deviated much from the original LLM $\theta^o$, and $\lambda$ is the penalty strength. We directly use the KL penalty as the regularization in Eqn.(\ref{eq:ppo}). 

However, the objective in Eqn.(\ref{eq:overall}) cannot be directly optimized because obtaining the generated text $f(x;\theta^w)$ involves sampling $y^w \sim \pi_{\theta^w}(\cdot | x)$. We therefore propose a RL-based algorithm that iteratively switches between updating $\theta^w$ and $\theta^d$.

\subsection{Algorithm}
In the practical algorithm, we alternate between updating $\theta^w$ and updating $\theta^d$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*]
    \item Given a fixed detector $\theta^d$, we tune the LLM $\theta^w$ to fit into  $\theta^d$'s labeled reward (i.e. detection score) with PPO in the objective (\ref{eq:ppo}) where $r_{\theta^{RM}}(x,y)   = D(x,y;\theta^d)$.
    \item Given a fixed LLM $\theta^w$, we train the detector $\theta^d$ to distinguish between the watermarked text $y^{w}$ generated by $\theta^w$ and the text from any other sources (e.g. written by humans) $y^{nw}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:detect}
    \min_{\theta^d}{[D(x, y^{nw}; \theta^d) - D(x, y^{w}; \theta^d)]}.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}


Note that, unlike the conventional RLHF, we also update the reward model, i.e. our detector $\theta^d$, along with the LLM $\theta^w$ in the PPO. 

Algorithm~\ref{algo:main} shows our overall pipeline. We first pretrain the detector to distinguish between non-watermarked text $y^{nw}$ and text generated by the original LLM $\theta^o$ (line 1-8). Then we fine-tune the LLM to obtain the watermarked LLM weights $\theta^w$ while simultaneously training the detector $\theta^d$ (line 9-18). In particular, in each training step, we first freeze $\theta^d$ and update $\theta^w$ using the PPO objective to increase the labeled detection score from $\theta^d$ on the text generated by $\theta^w$ (line 12-14). Then we generate the latest version of generated watermarked text $y_w$, and train the detector to classify between the watermarked and non-watermarked text (line 15-17).




\paragraph{Detection.} The detection of watermark is a simple forward pass through the detector. Given prompt $x$ and output $y$, we calculate the detection score $D(x,y;\theta^d)$. A high score indicates that the output $y$ is likely to be generated by our LLM. We pick the threshold based on the criteria that the True Positive Rate (TPR) reaches a certain value. %, similar to the standard practice in content moderation~\cite{}.


\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Reinforcement Learning-based Watermark pipeline.}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]	
\label{algo:main}
\REQUIRE \quad\\
$\theta^{o}$: The original LLM.\\
$\mathcal{D}^{nw}$: A dataset containing prompt $x$ and its corresponding output $y^{nw}$ generated by any source that is not $\theta^{o}$ (e.g. written by humans).
\ENSURE \quad\\
$\theta^w$: The watermarked LLM. \\
$\theta^d$: The detector paired with $\theta^w$.
\vskip1pt
\STATE {\color{blue}\texttt{/* Pretrain the detector weights*/}}
\STATE Initialize $\theta^d$
\FOR{iteration \( = 1, 2 \ldots\)}
        \STATE $(x, y^{nw}) \sim \mathcal{D}^{nw}$
        \STATE $y^{w} \leftarrow  f(x; \theta^o)$
        \STATE {\color{blue}\texttt{/* Train the detector like a reward model*/}}
        \label{line:update_det} \STATE Update $\theta^d$ with Eqn.(\ref{eq:detect})
\ENDFOR
\STATE {\color{blue}\texttt{/* Use RL to iteratively update the LLM $\theta^w$ and the detector $\theta^d$}*/}
\STATE $\theta^w \leftarrow \theta^o$
\FOR{iteration \( = 1, 2 \ldots\)}
        \STATE {\color{blue}\texttt{/* Tune the LLM $\theta^w$ to fit the detector $\theta^d$*/}}
        \STATE $(x, y^{nw}) \sim \mathcal{D}^{nw}$
        \STATE Freeze $\theta^d$ and update $\theta^w$ with the PPO objective (\ref{eq:ppo}) where $r_{\theta^{RM}}(x,y) = D(x,y;\theta^d)$
        \STATE {\color{blue}\texttt{/* Tune the detector $\theta^d$ to fit the LLM $\theta^w$*/}}
        \STATE $y^{w} \leftarrow  f(x; \theta^w)$
        \STATE Freeze $\theta^w$ and update $\theta^d$ with Eqn.(\ref{eq:detect})
\ENDFOR
\OUTPUT $\theta^w$ and $\theta^d$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Combining with Alignment}
Since we need to use RL to co-train the LLM and the detector, we have a computationally expensive stage for offline preparation. Therefore, it is best used together with the standard alignment so that the additional overhead induced by our watermarking can be reduced significantly. 

Given a normal alignment task where the reward model is $\theta^{RM}  $, we can use the combined reward from both $\theta^{RM}  $ and our detector $\theta^d$ in the PPO objective (\ref{eq:ppo}), i.e. replacing the labelled reward in objective (\ref{eq:ppo}) with the following:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:mix}
\alpha \cdot r_{\theta^{RM}  }(x,y) + (1-\alpha) \cdot D(x,y;\theta^d)
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the weight balancing the alignment task's reward and the watermarking task. All other steps, e.g. LLM finetuning, are the same.

Compared with the standard RLHF pipeline, the extra cost we introduce is only training an extra reward model (i.e. our detector) and running inference on it (i.e. labeling detection score). Today's RLHF already tends to use multiple reward models, and our watermarking reward model can be incorporated into the current RLHF pipeline easily.

\subsection{Setting}
\paragraph{Task and Data.} We choose two LLMs: OPT-1.3B~\cite{zhang2022opt} and Llama2-7B~\cite{touvron2023llama} in the experiment, and two tasks: (1) prompt completion and (2) safety alignment in Q\&A. For (1) we use C4 RealNewsLike Dataset~\cite{2019t5} for the completion task and we follow the same data preprocessing procedure as prior works~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark,kuditipudi2023robust} with completion length 128. For (2) we use PKU safe RLHF~\cite{ji2023beavertails} dataset for the alignment task. Following the standard RLHF pipeline, we first perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and then perform the RL alignment.% on the SFT model.
\paragraph{Metric.} We evaluate (1) watermark detection performance and (2) original task performance (i.e. completion and safety alignment). For detecting watermarks, we evaluate $1K$ prompts and distinguish between their human-written and LLM-generated responses. We compute detection AUC and false positive rate when the true positive rate is over 90\% and 99\%, denoted as FPR@90 and FPR@99 respectively. For the original utility on the completion task, we evaluate log-perplexity, denoted as logPPL, of the generated text on the C4 dataset following previous works~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}. For the original utility on the alignment task, we evaluate the safety score on the PKU dataset using the safety evaluation model released with the dataset~\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/PKU-Alignment/beaver-7b-v1.0-cost}}.
\paragraph{Baseline
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item KGW~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}: Randomly split the vocabulary into two partitions for each token and increase the probability of sampling for one partition during training.
    \item ITS~\cite{kuditipudi2023robust}: Define a pre-set random key and sample for each token location based on the key.
    \item EXP~\cite{kuditipudi2023robust}: Similar to ITS, but the key is used to adjust the sampling probability.
    \item Ours (No-FT): Our watermark pipeline but only training the detector $\theta^d$ without finetuning the LLM $\theta^w$ .
\end{itemize}
Note that the first three baseline methods are inference-time watermarks that do not finetune the LLM. When generating watermarks using those methods, we generate them on the pretrained model for the C4 dataset and on the aligned model after performing RLHF on the PKU dataset.

\paragraph{Hyper-parameters.} For both datasets, we finetune the LLM for $10K$ steps with batch size 4 on OPT-1.3B, and $5K$ steps with batch size 1 on Llama2-7B\footnote{The small batch size is due to the memory and runtime constraint.} For the PPO hyperparameters in Eqn.(\ref{eq:ppo}), we use $\beta=0.1$ for the KL reward coefficient, $\gamma=0.01$ on Llama2-7B and $\gamma=0.0$ on OPT-1.3B as the KL penalty. On the alignment task, we use $\alpha=0.5$ in Eqn.(\ref{eq:mix}) to balance with the normal safety alignment task. 

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:exp-result}

We show detection performance in Table~\ref{tab:result}. Our watermarks show better detection performance when compared at a similar level of utility. Note that ITS and EXP methods are supposed to be ``distortion-free'', but we do observe a significant drop in their original utility on C4 data with OPT-1.3B. Only in this case, our detection is slightly underperformed because our utility (logPPL) is much better. We suspect the utility loss is because those methods would need to change the LLM sampling while other types of LLM sampling often achieve a better utility. In our method, we choose multinomial sampling in top-50 tokens (which is the standard setting) while ITS and EXP cannot be adapted to the same sampling. In addition, our detection performance on the PKU dataset is relatively low for Llama2-7B, we suspect it is because we only use $5K$ samples to run RLHF, which is not enough for the larger Llama2-7B. 


Furthermore, if we only train the detector without finetuning the LLM, the performance would be much worse. This showcases the importance of finetuning the LLM model besides training a detector. In addition, we can observe that utility in our method is not negatively impacted when we finetune it for the watermarking purpose, which matches our intuition that we induce semantic-level signals that might have a less negative impact on the utility compared to lower token-level signals.

\subsection{Word Substitution Attacks}
\label{subsec:sub}

We conduct a study to understand the robustness of our method under substitution attacks. One of the unique advantages of our method, compared to the fixed-model approaches, is our watermark can be adapted to different newly discovered attacks, in the style of adversarial training~\cite{madry2017towards}.

To perform the substitution attack, we randomly replace a fraction of tokens in the response with random tokens from the vocabulary, and then see if watermarks can still be detected or not. In addition, we include our method when combined with adversarial training. Specifically, we generate substituted responses on the training set, used as the adversarial examples, as the training samples used in our RL pipeline. In other words, when we train the detector $\theta^d$, we label the substituted response, $f(x; \theta^w) + \Delta$ where $\Delta$ is the substitution perturbations, as still watermarked. We then test if the detector's ability to identify substituted responses as watermarked in the training set can generalize to the unseen test set.
 Unsurprisingly,  ITS and EXP outperform us because they are designed to be robust against word substitutions~\cite{kuditipudi2023robust}. However, when we incorporate adversarial examples into our training, we can achieve much stronger robustness, especially when the substitution ratio is high -- we can achieve almost no AUC loss even when substituting $50\%$ tokens.

We evaluate the robustness of our method under paraphrasing attacks. We paraphrase responses by two paraphrasing models: Pegasus~\cite{zhang2019pegasus} and DIPPER~\cite{krishna2023paraphrasing}. Similarly in Section~\ref{subsec:sub}, we incorporate the paraphrased responses as the watermarked text into our training in the style of adversarial attack. Paraphrasing strength in Pegasus is quantified by temperature $T$, and we evaluate at $T=1.0,1.5,2.0$. Paraphrasing strength in DIPPER is quantified by diversity $q$ for both lexical diversity and order diversity, and we evaluate at $q=20,40,60$.

Figure~\ref{fig:result-pegasus} shows the results w.r.t. Pegasus. Unlike substitution attacks, our method can already achieve decent robustness against paraphrasing and outperform the baselines even when the paraphrasing strength is low. It is because token-level methods are known to be vulnerable to paraphrasing while our model-level approach watermarks the response not based on replacing specific tokens, but modifying the response as a whole, therefore the change we induce is at the semantic level, which is less vulnerable to paraphrasing. In addition, similar to substitution attacks, our method can achieve stronger robustness by adversarial training.
Figure~\ref{fig:result-dipper} shows the robustness of the LLM adversarially trained on Pegasus-paraphrased responses and tested on DIPPER-paraphrased responses. We can see that finetuning the LLM with Pegasus attacks can also improve the robustness against DIPPER attacks, showing the flexibility to incorporate new attacks into the watermarks, which is a feature that fixed-model approaches have no feasible way to provide.


\subsection{Detecting Text Generated by Another LLM}
So far, all the non-watermarked text used in our framework is generated by humans (i.e. existing responses in C4 and PKU datasets). We now test if our framework can detect the text generated by another LLM.

We test our previously trained LLM, which is fine-tuned on human-written text and named as \name(H), using text generated by another LLM. We use OPT-1.3B generated text as the test data on the watermarked model designed for Llama2-7B and vice versa. We show the results in Table~\ref{tab:result-other}. We also include the model finetuned on the non-watermarked text that includes text from both humans and the other LLM, named as \name(H+L).
When finetined on human-written text only, but tested with the other LLM's generated text, our method suffers from minor out-of-distribution problems, which is reasonable considering the training process does not include the test text. However, when we include the test LLM's generated text into our training process (\name(H+L)), our detection accuracy can be recovered. Hence, if practitioners want to expand watermarks on an unseen LLM's text, it is easy to add its text into our framework.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{LLM Watermark.}
 KGW~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark} first shows how to watermark an LLM output by randomly splitting the vocabulary into two parts and setting a higher probability to samples from one. In a followup work~\cite{kirchenbauer2023reliability}, researchers show that the approach works when the watermarked text is long. Several followup works study a similar approach. \cite{lee2023wrote} adapt KGW to code generation by only focusing on high-entropy tokens. \cite{zhao2023provable} uses a fixed vocabulary splitting and shows it can lead to a provable watermark. \cite{fernandez2023three,hu2023unbiased} proposes better techniques to improve the generation and detection performance. \cite{hou2023semstamp,liu2023semantic} proposes to sample vocabulary based on the semantic meaning so that the watermark can be robust against paraphrasing attacks. 

KGW-based approach has limitations, e.g. distributional change and inability to be publicly verifiable~\cite{ajith2023performance}. Partially motivated to overcome those limitations, \cite{kuditipudi2023robust} proposes a distortion-free watermark schema by pre-sampling a random key for the LLM generation. \cite{christ2023undetectable} uses a private key and proposes the undetectable watermark from the view of cryptography. \cite{fairoze2023publicly} proposes that the message can be publicly verifiable using rejection sampling. Note that those approaches are inference-time techniques and do not fine-tune the model. More recently, \cite{gu2023learnability} proposes to fine-tune an LLM to distill the model with inference-time watermarks, making it a model-level watermark which is similar to our approach. However, the detection pipeline is still statistical tests rather than model-based detection. Furthermore, the method is often underperformed by KGW-based approaches.
 
\paragraph{RLHF.} Adding human feedback with reinforcement learning into language models has become an important topic~\cite{christiano2017deep,macglashan2017interactive}, popularized by RLHF~\cite{ouyang2022training}. 
Then apply an RL framework with the reward model to finetune the LLM. Researchers show that the traditional policy gradient method~\cite{mnih2016asynchronous} does not help the optimization goal well since the reward model is fragile to out-of-distribution samples~\cite{mckinney2023fragility}. Thus, it is common to use RL algorithms that do not drastically change model outputs, such as TRPO~\cite{schulman2015trust} and PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}. 



\paragraph{LLM Text Detection.} LLM text detection~\cite{wu2023survey} studies the problem of directly detecting whether a text is generated by LLMs or not, without changing any model training or text generation procedures. \cite{mitchell2023detectgpt} proposes to detect GPT-generated texts with curvature analysis on the text log probability function. \cite{wang2023detectgpt} shows that the previous work can be improved with self-masking prediction. \cite{wang2023seqxgpt} propose logit-based classifications. These works aim to detect general LLM texts and do not alter the model's training or generation. By comparison, our goal is to only detect texts generated by a specific (watermarked) model.


\section{Discussion}


\textbf{Model Update.} If an LLM iterates and its weights change, it is unclear if the old detector trained on the old weights can still detect the text generated by the new weights. In other words, can a detector paired with an LLM also detect other LLMs in the same family, trained on the same dataset, or iterated from the same pipeline? 

\textbf{Non-RLHF Finetuning.} Is it possible that one can generate watermarks in the same way as our framework but finetuning LLMs in a non-RL way, e.g. in the supervised learning style such as DPO~\cite{rafailov2023direct}? Since the RLHF still incurs high costs, finding a more efficient way of finetuning while achieving high detection accuracy can be appealing.

\textbf{Spoofing Attack.} Fixed-model approaches~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark} might be vulnerable to spoofing attack~\cite{sadasivan2023can}, i.e. generating fake watermarks that damage the reputation of the existing watermarks. In our approach, since the watermark is not generated algorithmically, which is transparent to the attacker, but generated in a data-driven way, so our method might be less vulnerable to spoofing attacks.



\section{Conclusion and Limitation}

We propose a model-based watermarking pipeline to track the outputs generated by LLMs. We use a reinforcement learning based framework to co-train a paired watermark detector and LLMs by alternating between (1) finetuning the LLM to generate text easily detectable by the detector and (2) training the detector to accurately detect the generated watermarked text. We empirically show that our watermarks are more accurate, robust, and adaptable to new attacks. It also supports open-sourcing. We hope our work can bring more effort into studying a broader watermark design.

\textbf{Limitation.} First, the need for finetuning might make our method more computationally costly than the fixed-model approach. Second, since our method is a data-driven approach, we require practitioners to collect relevant training data. Last, our detection is more costly than statistical tests in the fixed-model approach. Nevertheless, the first two issues can be mitigated if we integrate the watermarking process into the standard LLM alignment pipeline.
