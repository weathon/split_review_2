\title{Welfare Diplomacy: Benchmarking Language Model Cooperation}

\begin{abstract}
The growing capabilities and increasingly widespread deployment of AI systems necessitate robust benchmarks for 
measuring their cooperative capabilities. Unfortunately, most multi-agent benchmarks are either zero-sum or 
purely cooperative, 
providing limited opportunities for such measurements. We introduce a general-sum 
variant of the zero-sum 
board game Diplomacy---called Welfare Diplomacy---in which 
players must balance investing in military 
conquest and domestic welfare.
We argue that Welfare Diplomacy 
facilitates both a clearer assessment of and stronger training incentives for 
cooperative capabilities.
Our contributions are: (1) proposing the Welfare Diplomacy rules and implementing them via an open-
source Diplomacy engine; (2) constructing baseline agents 
using zero-shot prompted language models; 
and (3) conducting %
experiments where we find that baselines using state-of-the-art models attain high
social welfare but are exploitable. Our work aims to promote societal safety by aiding 
researchers in developing and assessing multi-agent AI systems
\end{abstract}

As foundation models become increasingly capable, we will likely see their integration into an ever-growing array of complex systems to assist multiple actors with varying interests. %
Ensuring that interactions in this multi-principal, multi-agent world lead to high social welfare 
is the goal of the emerging field of cooperative AI 
\citep{dafoe_open_2020,dafoe_cooperative_2021,conitzer_foundations_2023}. 
Achieving this goal will require the ability to measure the \textbf{cooperative capabilities} of powerful AI agents, which can be understood as skills that allow 
agents to better achieve the goals they share with other agents \citep{dafoe_open_2020}. 

The board game Diplomacy \citep{diplomacyboardgame}, where players control one of seven European powers in the early $20^{\text{th}}$ century to negotiate and compete over land, has recently been a focus of multi-agent language model (LM) research in open-ended environments \citep{paquette2019no,bakhtin2021no,bakhtin_human-level_2022,bakhtin_mastering_2022,kramar2022deepminddiplomacy}. 
Attesting to the possible use of the environment for developing 
AI assistants for high-stakes settings, the U.S. Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency has 
funded research on AI Diplomacy ``to inform and 
improve key elements of the diplomatic process, 
including strategic decision-making, collaboration, 
and deception'' \citep{shade2022}.
While Standard Diplomacy (SD) has 
features that make it interesting as an environment 
for cooperative AI research, it is zero-sum and incentivizes the 
development of cooperation-undermining capabilities, such as 
deception, betrayal, and collusion.

We thus propose Welfare Diplomacy (WD), a variant of Diplomacy in which 
players must make trade-offs between investing resources in military 
units 
and improving the welfare of their nations. In WD, players can build/disband to 
fewer 
units than their current supply center count in build turns, and the 
difference 
between the two each year cumulatively adds to their Welfare Points (WPs). The 
game 
ends after a fixed number of years. A player’s total utility is equal to their 
accumulated WPs at the end of the game; there is no single “winner”. 
In contrast to SD, WD is general-sum, as it is possible for players
to improve their welfare without reducing the welfare of others.
In this 
paper, we argue that 
WD leads to clearer evaluations of---and stronger selection pressures for---cooperative capabilities in AI systems.

Our contributions are as follows: \textbf{(1)} We introduce Welfare Diplomacy and provide an implementation in an open-source Diplomacy library; \textbf{(2)} We provide theoretical and empirical evidence for the benefits of WD relative to SD;  %
\textbf{(3)} We construct an LM scaffolding system to create competent zero-shot baseline agents for WD; %
\textbf{(4)} We benchmark a variety of state-of-the-art models, including GPT-4, on WD, %
measuring the welfare they obtain and their exploitability, 
for which we construct novel exploiter policies. 
Most of our agents attain high welfare by mutually demilitarizing but 
are highly exploitable, leaving much room for improvements in future work.  



\section{Welfare Diplomacy}


Here, we discuss 
the limitations of SD, introduce the rules of Welfare Diplomacy (WD), and argue for its benefits relative 
to Standard Diplomacy (SD) for measuring cooperative capabilities. %
\Cref{app:community_feedback} summarizes feedback on these rules we collected from the online Diplomacy community.
\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

Our motivation is to %
improve the 
cooperative capabilities of AI systems.
\textbf{Cooperative capabilities} are skills that allow 
agents to better achieve the goals they share with other agents, 
operationalized here as attaining high social 
welfare. 
Examples of cooperative capabilities
include the ability to identify Pareto-efficient
joint policies 
(e.g., \citealt{zheng2022ai}),
design contracts that 
incentivize agents to follow through on a mutually beneficial 
agreement 
(e.g., \citealt{hughes2020learning,christoffersen2022get}),
and resolve disagreements over Pareto-efficient agreements 
(e.g., \citealt{stastny2021normative}).
We focus on two criteria that we believe an environment for benchmarking cooperative capabilities should satisfy: 
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*})]
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
    \item[(A)] The environment should \textit{allow for significant global, rational cooperation}. 
First, it should be possible for all players to do better by
working together, and doing so should be 
(in some sense) individually
rational 
for all players. 
One operationalization of
this requirement is that there exist 
Nash equilibria (NEs) that are 
Pareto-dominated (i.e., at least
one player is better off, and none is worse off) by other NEs.\footnote{We use NE 
throughout as a lens for analyzing the
strategic dynamics of WD. This is largely for simplicity,
and we acknowledge that 
NE has a number
of shortcomings as a predictor of what skilled play will look
like. First, NE 
does not require
certain plausible constraints on play, such 
as subgame perfection
\citep{selten_reexamination_1975} 
or deterring deviations by coalitions (\citealt{bernheim_coalition-proof_1987}, though 
see our discussion of exploitability by coalitions in 
\Cref{sec:benchmarking}). Second, NE  unrealistically assumes that
players' policies are common knowledge (though see our discussion of 
equilibrium selection problems
in  
\Cref{sec:eq-selection}). Nevertheless we expect that 
our comparison of SD and WD 
would still apply under other operationalizations of our criteria \textbf{(A)}
which don't depend on NEs.}
Moreover, Pareto-efficient solutions should involve the 
significant exercise of cooperative capabilities. 
\item[(B)] \textit{Skilled play should be differentially globally 
cooperative}.
By this, we mean that skilled play requires capabilities that promote global 
cooperation (i.e., cooperation among \textit{all} players) more than other kinds of 
capabilities, thus allowing for ``differential progress'' on cooperation \citep{Sandbrink2022}.
One operationalization is 
the requirement that the socially optimal NEs of the environment should should involve limited use of the ability to betray others (which is not cooperative) or enter into collusive agreements at the expense of others (which is not \textit{globally} cooperative).

\item[(C)] The environment should exhibit \textit{bargaining problems}. 
A \textbf{bargaining problem} is a setting in which agents have 
conflicting preferences over Pareto-efficient and stable solutions (e.g., Nash equilibria), 
and there is a risk of reverting to a Pareto-dominated outcome if they fail to 
agree. 
This allows us to test 
agents' ability to negotiate compromises, as well as their robustness in cases where they are unable to agree on a 
Pareto-efficient outcome. 

\end{enumerate}

Although cooperation is sometimes useful in SD, players cannot cooperate indefinitely 
because
there is only one winner, except when a coalition is cooperating for a 
draw.
And all policy profiles are Pareto-efficient (no player can do better without another 
player doing worse), so there is no opportunity for global cooperation.
SD therefore fails to satisfy criterion \textbf{(A)}. 
As a consequence, 
measures of social welfare 
are of little or no use.\footnote{E.g., 
the commonly-used 
utilitarian social welfare---the sum of players' expected utilities---is constant.} 
Moreover, even if Pareto improvements 
are possible for subsets of players, it is unclear how 
to use this to create simple quantitative measures 
of cooperation.
Secondly, even temporary cooperation
between players comes at the expense of the others. 
Thus, 
it is reasonable to expect that a significant amount of the effort  
expended
by a skilled SD player goes towards deception and 
otherwise undermining other players' goals, rather than 
identifying ways of achieving shared goals. SD therefore also fails 
to satisfy criterion \textbf{(B)}.
Finally, because all policy profiles are Pareto-optimal in SD, 
there is no risk of reverting to a Pareto-inefficient outcome, 
and therefore no bargaining problem between all players, 
so SD fails to satisfy criterion \textbf{(C)}.
(Although there are bargaining problems between strict subsets of
players.) 
\subsection{The Rules of Welfare Diplomacy}
SD is a seven-player game in which 
players maneuver military units 
with the goal of capturing 
\textbf{supply centers (SCs)}. The game is divided into Spring, Fall, and Winter turns. 
In Spring and Fall, players issue commands to their military units, and 
in Winter, players may build new units if their SC count has
gone up over the preceding year, and must remove military units if their
SC count has gone down. The first player to capture
18 SCs wins. In the ``full-press'' version of 
SD, players have an opportunity to exchange private messages before
deciding on their moves each turn.
\par WD involves three changes to SD's rules:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
    \item After each Winter, players receive a number of \textbf{Welfare Points (WPs)}
    equal to the difference between the number of SCs they control 
    and their number of units on the board; 
    \item The game ends after a fixed number of years, not
    when one player captures 18 SCs;
    \item A player's objective is to maximize their own WPs. Unlike in SD, there is no ``winner''.
\end{enumerate}
Thus, players are incentivized to build as few units as possible, so 
as to accumulate more WPs. However, building fewer military units may tempt other players to take one's SCs. 
Players are therefore additionally incentivized to 
demilitarize in ways that
do not leave them vulnerable, and to identify punishments that adequately 
disincentivize defections from agreements.

\par Unlike SD, we should expect there to be NEs that 
Pareto-dominate others,
satisfying criterion \textbf{(A)} from \Cref{sec:motivation}. 
Moreover, we conjecture 
some NEs to involve 
all players
occupying neutral SCs
and then demilitarizing according to some schedule that
does not incentivize defection, so that all players get a high number of 
WPs. Such equilibria would not involve deception or domination
of some players by others, fulfilling our requirement \textbf{(B)}. As evidence for these conjectures, we construct such an equilibrium in a toy 
version of WD in \Cref{sec:demilitarization-eq}. 
In \Cref{sec:compare_sd_wd} we 
present empirical comparisons of our baseline agents in
SD and WD, showing that the rate of conflict is significantly 
higher in the former. %
Finally, because there are many ways of allocating SCs, WD likely 
exhibits bargaining problems (requirement \textbf{(C)}). 
We provide evidence for this conjecture in a toy problem in
\Cref{sec:eq-selection}.

\par WD has a number of other advantages
as an environment for cooperative AI research. 
For example, the fact that 
policy profiles differ in their social welfare also 
allows for studying the effects of adding a 
\textit{mediator} to the game (i.e., 
a player whose goal is to maximize social welfare), as well as 
to more easily measure relevant ``dispositions'' 
like inequity aversion 
\citep{fehr_theory_1999}. 

One downside of WD relative to SD is that
it lacks a base of skilled human players.  
This means that it will be harder to get high-quality 
human data, which was critical in the 
construction of human-level agents for SD
\citep{bakhtin_mastering_2022,bakhtin_human-level_2022}.
However, we would
like to eventually build AI systems that
are sufficiently cooperatively competent to perform well
with minimal human data. 
Finally, see our comparison with alternative scoring rules
for Diplomacy in \Cref{app:scoring}, and  
\Cref{sec:related-work} for discussion
of advantages of WD over 
multi-agent environments other than SD.



\section{Cooperative Equilibria in Welfare Diplomacy}

We support our arguments for WD as a cooperative AI benchmark 
by demonstrating certain NEs in a simplified version of WD. 
These equilibria all involve disbanding 
units 
to avoid conflict and obtain WPs, except for the punishment of deviators, 
demonstrating that these behaviors are possible for rational players. 
All proofs are in 
\Cref{sec:proofs}.

\subsection{Notation}
We let $\players$ be the set of $n$ players (indexed by $i$, where $-i$ denotes all 
players except $i$) and $T$ be the time horizon 
(with times indexed by $t$). %
 Informally, %
 a policy for player $i$ is a mapping 
$\policyi$ from histories of play to distributions over legal actions. 
We write the expected utility 
for player $i$
induced by policy profile $\policyprofile = (\policy_1,\ldots,\policy_n)$
as $\utilityi(\policyprofile)$. 

Let $\Pi_i$ be the set of policies for player $i$.
A \textbf{Nash equilibrium (NE)}
is a policy profile $\policyprofile$ such that, for each $i$, 
$\policyi \in \argmax_{\policyi' \in \Pi_i} \utilityi(\policyi', \policyprofileminusi)$.
A \textbf{social welfare function} $\welfare$  measures how socially good
policy profiles are. For example, %
the \textbf{Nash welfare}
is given by $\welfare^{\mathrm{Nash}}(\policyprofile) \coloneqq \prod_{i}
(\utility_i(\policyprofile)-d_i)$, for some ``disagreement points'' $d_i$
such that $\utilityi(\policyprofile)-d_i$ is always nonnegative. 
We say that a policy profile $\policyprofile$  
\textbf{Pareto dominates} $\policyprofile'$ if 
for all $i$, $\utilityi(\policyprofile) \geq 
\utilityi(\policyprofile')$, and for some $i$ 
this inequality is strict. If $\policyprofile$ is not
Pareto-dominated by any policy profile, we say it is 
\textbf{Pareto-efficient}. A desirable property of a social welfare
function $w$ is that, whenever $\policyprofile$ Pareto-dominates
$\policyprofile'$, we have $w(\policyprofile) > w(\policyprofile')$.

\subsection{Equilibrium Analysis}\label{sec:equilibria}
We first construct a class of equilibria for a toy version of WD, in which players acquire neutral SCs and then
demilitarize. We then give an example of a bargaining problem.
\subsubsection{Mutual Demilitarization}\label{sec:demilitarization-eq}
The board for the toy game with $n$ players 
is a graph $G_n$ consisting of a complete graph on $n$ vertices with an additional leaf on each vertex; $G_6$
is shown in \Cref{fig:toy_games} (left).
Each of the $n$ leaves is the single
home SC for one of the $n$ players, 
occupied by a unit at the beginning of the
game. The remaining vertices are 
provinces containing neutral SCs; 
we refer to the neutral SC adjacent to a 
player's home SC as ``their'' neutral SC. 
Let $\toygameNT$ be the corresponding game of
WD lasting $T$ years.
We construct NEs in which players disband
their units after a certain number of turns.  
For each $1\leq k \leq T$, let 
$\policyprofile^k$ be a policy profile that behaves as follows:
\textbf{(1)} every player claims their neutral SC on the first turn; 
\textbf{(2)} no further orders are submitted until the $k^{\text{th}}$ year; 
\textbf{(3)} in Winter of the $k^{\text{th}}$ year, all players disband all their 
units, and no further orders are submitted; 
\textbf{(4)} if a player 
deviates from the above, the other players retaliate by claiming the 
deviator's SCs such that they cannot gain from deviating.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{nashA}\label{thm:nashA} 

Let 
$\policyprofile^k$ be defined as above
and $n\geq6$. Then $\policyprofile^k$ is a NE of $\toygameNT$ for all $1\leq k \leq T$, $k\neq T-2$.\footnote{If $k=T-2$, it's possible for a player $i$ to make positive gains from deviation such that $\policyprofile^k$ is not a NE. The other players $-i$ do not have enough to time to retaliate before the game ends, and $i$ can claim enough of their undefended SCs by the end of $T$ to exceed the WPs $i$ would have gained under $\policyprofile^k_i$.}
Furthermore, $\policyprofile^{k}$ Pareto-dominates $\policyprofile^{k+1}$ for all $1\leq k \leq T-1$, and $\policyprofile^1$ is Pareto-efficient as long as $T\neq 3$.
\end{restatable}
It is particularly interesting to compare $\policyprofile^T$ with
$\policyprofile^k$ for $k < T$. In $\policyprofile^T$, players 
wait until the very last Winter to disband their units. Thus the
only possible deviations of interest are disbanding one's units
early, and such deviations are easily made unprofitable by the
other players occupying the deviating player's now-unoccupied
SC(s). But players are only able to accumulate WPs in the
very last round.
By contrast, in $\policyprofile^k$ with $k<T$,  players
disband before the last year, and so there is the possibility that
a deviator re-builds their units in an attempt to take
the other players' now-unoccupied SCs. 
Enforcing this equilibrium thus requires the additional  
ability of the other players to coordinate to punish the
deviator. But players are able to accumulate more WPs
by disbanding earlier. 
This is an instance where a Pareto-improving equilibrium 
requires greater cooperative capability.

\subsubsection{Bargaining Problems}\label{sec:eq-selection} 
We hypothesize that WD exhibits bargaining problems.
As evidence, we construct Pareto-efficient
NEs over which players have conflicting preferences in our toy setting.
We introduce a variation of the previous board by adjoining an additional home province to one of the neutral provinces, 
such that two players share an adjacent neutral province.
See \Cref{fig:toy_games} (center). 

Let $i$ and $j$ represent the players that share the neutral province and let $\policyprofile^i$ (respectively $\policyprofile^j$) represent the following policy profile: \textbf{(1)} all players move into their neutral province in the first turn, except for $i$ (respectively $j$); \textbf{(2)} all units disband in the first Winter; \textbf{(3)} no further orders are submitted, unless to punish a deviator.
This is similar to the previous mutual demilitarization policy profile, except that we must now choose to which player to allocate the shared province. The two allocations result in different total
WPs for the two players.

\begin{restatable}{theorem}{nashB}\label{thm:nashB} 
$\policyprofile^i$ (respectively $\policyprofile^j$) is a Pareto-efficient NE preferred by $j$ (respetively $i$).
\end{restatable}

The existence of separate Pareto-efficient equilibria preferred by different players allows us to study equilibrium selection. Players with high cooperative capabilities should be able to negotiate to select one of the equilibria, rather than fail to select any and thereby end up in an inefficient outcome. 




\section{Zero-Shot Language Model Diplomacy Agents}

Inspired by \citet{bakhtin_human-level_2022}, we develop 
our agents using prompting to enable LMs 
to play WD without fine-tuning.
A full prompt and subsequent model completion is composed of three components. A \textbf{system prompt} includes an explanation that the LM is an expert Diplomacy AI playing in an interactive environment with other players, the rules of our Welfare Diplomacy variation, and the desired JSON response format. A \textbf{user prompt} includes summaries of messages between the given player and all other players for previous turns, all such messages for the current turn, the orders submitted on the last three turns, the current board state consisting of abbreviated and unit ownerships, current counts of SCs, units, and WPs, and turn-specific instructions about what kinds of moves are legal. Finally, an \textbf{assistant response} is generated by the LM and includes first private reasoning, then a list of intended orders at the moment, and finally a list of messages to send to the other players. We refer to the resulting class of agents as \BotName. 

We refer to the agents obtained by applying this scaffolding to a model $M$ as \BotName($M$), and  
write the 
profile of policies obtained by using this scaffolding for each player 
and for LM $M$
as $\policyprofileBotName$. We use the same scaffolding but without WD-specific instructions in the system prompt 
to construct a class of agents for SD called \SDBotName.
More details on our prompting system and example prompts are in \Cref{app:scaffold_methodology}. The results of an 
ablation experiment of 11 prompt elements are in \Cref{sec:prompt_ablation}.




\section{Experimental Results}

In this section, we provide experimental results on the performance of LMs on WD. Unless otherwise 
specified, all games last ten years with three message rounds per turn. We complete five 
runs with different random seeds per experimental group, and error bars represent bootstrapped 95\% confidence intervals. We run 
experiments on \texttt{GPT-4-0613} \citep{openai_gpt-4_2023} (\textit{GPT 4 (RLHF)} in figures), \texttt{GPT-3.5-
turbo-16k-0613} \citep{ouyang_training_2022} (\textit{GPT-3.5}), \texttt{GPT-4-base} \citep{openai_gpt-4_2023}  (\textit{GPT-4 (Base)}), 
\texttt{Claude-2.0} \citep{anthropic_model_2023} (\textit{Claude 2.0)}, \texttt{Claude-instant-1.2} \citep{bai_constitutional_2022} (\textit{Claude 
1.2)}, and \texttt{Llama-2-70B-Chat} \citep{touvron_llama_2023} (\textit{Llama 2 (70B)}). 

\subsection{Benchmarking \BotName's Cooperative Capabilities}\label{sec:benchmarking}

We are primarily interested in measuring agents' ability to 
find solutions that lead to high social welfare and are
\textbf{stable}, meaning that they do not admit strong incentives to deviate. 
We thus focus on two metrics. First, we measure the 
{\textbf{Nash welfare} of $\policyprofileBotName$
(i.e., self-play using \BotName\ with model $M$), with
$d_i=0$.
This is given by $\welfare^{\mathrm{Nash}}(\policyprofile) \coloneqq \prod_{i}
\utility_i(\policyprofile)$
where $\utility_i$ is the cumulative
WPs for player $i$ 
divided by the number of years elapsed. Second, we provide a measure of the \textbf{exploitability} 
of $\policyprofileBotName$. We can define the exploitability of a policy profile 
$\policyprofile$
by coalitions of size $k$ as the minimum that any player can gain by deviating from
$\policyprofile$ as a member of a $k$-player coalition
(cf. \citealt{zinkevich2007regret}).\footnote{Notice that a policy profile
is a NE if and 
only if it its exploitability by coalitions of size one is zero.} Computing 
exploitability is a difficult optimization problem, however, so we instead 
construct a class of exploiter agents and estimate the amount that 
such agents can gain by deviating from $\policyprofileBotName$ in coalitions
of size one and two. 
We additionally report a \textbf{basic 
proficiency} score, 
defined as the mean of three values: 
the rate of model outputs that are valid JSON and thus able to be parsed 
without error, the rate of submitted orders that are valid possible 
orders, and the fraction of global SCs owned by any player and not left 
neutral. 

\paragraph{Nash Welfare measures cooperation in self-play games.}
In \Cref{fig:sp_benchmark}, we provide the 
root Nash welfare, $(\welfare^{\mathrm{Nash}})^{1/n}$
with $n=7$,
of 
$\policyprofileBotName$ for 
different models $M$. 
Since we are performing self-play evaluations, a high Nash welfare
means that an agent 
is capable of cooperating with itself. %
For comparison, we also include three other baseline policies, all playing against 
themselves. 
The ``Optimal Prosocial'' 
policy is hard-coded to expand to
a particular partition of neutral SCs
and then disband all units at the end of the 
first year, and gives an upper bound on Nash welfare
(see 
\Cref{app:optimal_prosocial_details}).
The ``Random'' policy 
randomly samples one of the possible actions on each turn. The ``Exploiter'' policy is described in the next section.

There is a substantial variation in Nash welfare, with many agents performing at or below the Random 
Policy. 
GPT-4 obtained the highest score, while Claude Instant 1.2 obtained the lowest. For the models that we 
tested, larger models (GPT-4, Claude 2) tended to achieve higher Nash welfare than smaller models (GPT-3.5, 
Claude Instant 1.2). 
Interestingly, plotting the average counts of units, SCs, and WPs over time 
reveals that while most models do 
demilitarize over time, they do so with 
wildly different policy profiles (see 
\Cref{sec:metrics_over_time}).
We hypothesize that cooperative capabilities may improve in general with model scale, 
but do not attempt to demonstrate this here
given the lack of basic proficiency for our less-capable models and the prohibitive computational costs that verifying this hypothesis 
would require. 

\paragraph{Exploitability reveals our agents don't deter deviators.}\label{sec:exploitability}

We construct our Exploiter agents as follows. We designate a 
coalition of one or two players to act as exploiters. The exploiters
make use of a policy trained using reinforcement
learning 
in no-press SD (``SD policy''; FPPI-2 trained by 
\citet{anthony_learning_2020}).\footnote{We initially tried prompting LMs to exploit, but found that this was 
significantly less effective than
incorporating a policy created using reinforcement learning.}

\section{Discussion}




\subsection{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

\paragraph{AI for Diplomacy.}
Diplomacy has a long history of study in AI \citep{kraus_diplomat_1988, hall_thoughts_1995}. Until 
recently, most Diplomacy agents used rule-based or search-based algorithms 
\citep{ferreira_dipblue_2015, de_jonge_d-brane_2017}. Advances in deep learning and game-playing AI 
have since led to progress in the \emph{no-press} version of Diplomacy \citep{paquette_no-press_2019, anthony_learning_2020, bakhtin_mastering_2022},
culminating in the recent success of \cite{bakhtin_human-level_2022}, whose CICERO agent was the 
first to reach to human-level performance in the \emph{full-press} version.%

\paragraph{Cooperation Benchmarks.} Several environments that
pose cooperation problems for AI agents have been studied extensively.
However, several of the most prominent environments involve
pure cooperation problems (e.g., 
StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge \citep{whiteson2019starcraft}, 
Hanabi \citep{bard2020hanabi}, 
Overcooked \citep{carroll2019utility,wang2020too}), and thus
do not test abilities that are critical for cooperation
in mixed-motive settings such as negotiation and commitment. 
An exception is Melting Pot \citep{leibo_scalable_2021,agapiou_melting_2022}, 
a suite of multi-agent scenarios set in partially observable
gridworlds
which includes a number of mixed-motive environments. However, 
compared to Welfare Diplomacy there is 
a limited role for communication and long-term strategic
planning in Melting Pot environments.
\paragraph{Language Model Benchmarks.}
Several benchmarks for evaluating the capabilities of large language models have been proposed, including testing for general natural language capabilities \citep{kiela_dynabench_2021}, the ability to autonomously execute tasks \citep{kinniment_evaluating_2023}, programming abilities \citep{chen_evaluating_2021}, instruction following \citep{efrat_turking_2020}, truthfulness \citep{lin_truthfulqa_2022}, and social skills \citep{choi_llms_2023}. Most closely to our work is research on evaluating the \textit{cooperative} capabilities of LMs \citep{aher_using_2022,chan_towards_2023,gandhi_strategic_2023,akata_playing_2023,horton_large_2023}, though these study only simple settings, such as the ultimatum game or finitely repeated matrix games, limiting our ability to thoroughly evaluate models.

\paragraph{Automated Negotiation.}
Beyond work on Diplomacy specifically, there is a substantial literature on multi-agent bargaining and negotiation both in the field of game theory \citep{nash_bargaining_1950, handgraaf_social_2003, forsythe_fairness_1994, guth_experimental_1982} and AI \citep{mell_towards_2018, baarslag_learning_2016, chawla_casino_2021, sunder_prosocial_2021}.
One recent line of work focuses on improving (usually through fine-tuning or search) and evaluating LM capabilities on negotiation tasks \citep{lewis_deal_2017, he_decoupling_2018, fu_improving_2023, verma_chai_2022, Abdelnabi_Gomaa_Sivaprasad_Schönherr_Fritz_2023_deliberation}. These works, however, only evaluate negotiation between two agents in relatively simple bargaining games.  
 

\subsection{Societal Impact}\label{sec:societal_impacts}

Our goal with introducing WD is to facilitate improvements in the cooperative capabilities of AI systems, which we consider essential for obtaining positive societal outcomes \citet{dafoe_open_2020,bertino_artificial_2020,crandall_cooperating_2018,conitzer_foundations_2023}. Much as previous machine-learning benchmarks motivated improvements in general capabilities (e.g., \citealt{deng_imagenet_2009,rajpurkar_squad_2016,bowman_large_2015,wang_glue_2019}), an ideal impact of our work would be to motivate similarly rapid improvements in the cooperative capabilities of AI systems.

There is increasing attention on evaluating risks---such as cooperation failures---not just from current models, but those from even more capable systems \citep{shevlane_model_2023,anderljung_frontier_2023,berglund_taken_2023,perez_discovering_2022,kinniment_evaluating_2023,lin_truthfulqa_2022,park_ai_2023,chen_evaluating_2021,khlaaf_hazard_2022}. While few works have considered multi-agent risks specifically, these risks may become increasingly important with capabilities scaling \citep{kaplan_scaling_2020, sorscher_beyond_2022, caballero_broken_2022} and AI deployment in high-stakes multi-agent situations---see, for example, Palantir's LM military planning assistant \citep{palantir_technologies_inc_aritificial_2023}.

Work to improve cooperative capabilities may have unintended, negative side effects. %
Overfitting to a benchmark may result in systems that appear more cooperative during evaluation than they are during deployment \citep{kiela_dynabench_2021}. Data leakage is similarly a concern, especially for foundation models. Moreover, it remains unclear how to build in cooperative capabilities while avoiding AI collusion against human overseers \citep{calvano_artificial_2020,beneke_artificial_2019}. %



\section{Conclusion}

We introduce Welfare Diplomacy (WD) as a benchmark for cooperative AI. In contrast to 
Standard Diplomacy (SD), the goal of WD is to maximize one's own Welfare Points 
through actions like demilitarization. Our environment permits positive-sum 
interactions 
that are more 
reflective of real-world dynamics. We argue for the benefits of WD as a benchmark by 
proving the existence of cooperative equilibria, providing qualitative feedback from 
the online Diplomacy community, and demonstrating that players in WD engage in fewer 
conflicts than players in SD. Using our prompt scaffolding system, our empirical zero-
shot evaluations in WD suggest that LMs can cooperate in WD, but are vulnerable to exploitation by defectors. 

This work has several limitations. 
First, even though we do not engage in fine-tuning, our experiments were still computationally expensive, as the long prompts summed to more than three million tokens for most games and limited us to only five games per experimental condition. %
Second, %
future work should try to distinguish between cooperation and collusion in a measurable way. 
Third, while we only consider metrics that are functions of players' utilities
(Nash welfare and exploitability),
systematic analysis of agent reasoning or messages could provide much greater insights.
Evaluations against distributions of different agents, in addition to self-play, should also be conducted.   
Fourth, future work should explore the factors that explain the variation in Nash welfare between different models, and how to develop agents that approach the optimal Nash welfare.
Finally, 
it is unclear how well insights from the study of 
Diplomacy transfer to real-world settings. %
Although we believe that WD is an improvement upon existing environments in
this regard,
we hope that it is a step towards even more realistic and diverse
evaluations for cooperative AI.
