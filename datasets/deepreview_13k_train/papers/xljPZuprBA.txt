\title{Exploring Edge Probability Graph Models Beyond Edge Independency: Concepts, Analyses, and Algorithms}

\begin{abstract}
Desirable random graph models (RGMs) should 
\textit{(i)} generate \textit{realistic} structures such as high clustering (i.e., high subgraph densities),
\revisenips{\textit{(ii)} generate \textit{variable} (i.e., not overly similar) graphs,}
and \textit{(iii)} remain \textit{tractable} to compute and control graph statistics.
A common class of RGMs (e.g., Erd\H{o}s-R\'{e}nyi and stochastic Kronecker) outputs edge probabilities, and we need to realize (i.e., sample from) the edge probabilities to generate graphs.
Typically, each edge's existence is assumed to be determined independently \revisenips{for simplicity and tractability}.
However, with edge independency, RGMs theoretically cannot \revisenips{produce high subgraph densities and high output variability simultaneously.}
In this work, we explore realization beyond edge independence that can produce more realistic structures while \revisenips{maintaining high tractability and variability}.
\revisenips{Theoretically,} we propose an edge-dependent realization framework called \textit{binding} \revisenips{that provably preserves output variability}, and derive \textit{closed-form} tractability results on subgraph (e.g., triangle) densities in generated graphs.
\revisenips{Practically,} we propose algorithms for graph generation with binding and parameter fitting of binding.
Our empirical results demonstrate that binding exhibits high tractability and generates realistic graphs with high clustering, significantly improving upon existing RGMs assuming edge independency.

	
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


	
Random graph models (RGMs) help us understand, analyze, and predict real-world systems~\citep{drobyshevskiy2019random},
\revisenips{with various practical applications, e.g., graph algorithm testing~\citep{murphy2010introducing}, statistical testing~\citep{ghoshdastidar2017two}, and graph anonymization~\citep{backstrom2007wherefore}.}
Desirable RGMs should generate graphs with \textit{realistic} patterns, such as high clustering,\footnote{\textit{High clustering} means high subgraph densities, as used by, e.g., \citet{newman2003properties} and \citet{pfeiffer2012fast}.} power-law degrees, and small diameters~\citep{chakrabarti2006graph}.
\revisenips{At the same time, the generated graphs should be \textit{variable}, i.e., not highly-similar or even near-identical, and the RGMs should be \textit{tractable}, i.e., we can compute and control graph statistics.}

Many RGMs output individual edge probabilities and generate graphs accordingly, e.g., 
the Erd\H{o}s-R\'{e}nyi model~\citep{erdos1959randomgraph1}, 
the Chung-Lu model~\citep{chung2002connected}, 
the stochastic block model~\citep{holland1983stochastic}, and
the stochastic Kronecker model~\citep{leskovec2010kronecker}.
To generate graphs from edge probabilities, we need realization (i.e., sampling), where edge independency (i.e., the edge existences are determined mutually independently) is widely assumed for the sake of simplicity and tractability.\footnote{\revisenips{Strictly speaking, some variants of such models involve weak edge dependency, e.g., the $G(n,M)$ version of the Erd\H{o}s-R\'{e}nyi model. However, such edge dependency is marginal and often can be ignored in practice.}}
\revisenips{Although edge-independent RGMs have high tractability and they may reproduce some realistic patterns (e.g., power-law degrees and small diameters),}
they empirically fail to preserve some other patterns, especially high clustering~\citep{moreno2018tied,seshadhri2013depth}.
Moreover, edge-independent RGMs theoretically cannot generate graphs with high triangle density \revisenips{and high output variability at the same time}~\citep{nips21powerOfEIGMs}.

Naturally, we ask:
Can we apply realization without assuming edge independency so that we can improve upon such RGMs \revisenips{to generate graphs that are both realistic and variable}, while still ensuring high tractability?
To address this question, in this work, we explore edge probability graph models (EPGMs) beyond edge independency, from both theoretical and practical perspectives.
Our key message is a positive answer to the question.
Specifically, our novel contributions are four-fold:
\begin{enumerate}    
    \item \textbf{Concepts (\cref{sec:prob_statement}):} We formally define EPGMs with related concepts,
    and theoretically show some basic properties of EPGMs, e.g., even with edge dependency introduced, \revisenips{the \textit{variability is maintained}} in the generated graphs as the corresponding edge-independent model.
    \item \textbf{Analyses (\cref{sec:binding}):} We propose \textit{realistic}, \textit{tractable}, and \textit{flexible} realization schemes called \textit{binding} to construct EPGMs with different levels of edge dependency, and derive tractability results on the \textit{closed-form} subgraph (e.g., triangle) densities.
    \item \textbf{Algorithms (\cref{sec:binding}):} We propose practical algorithms for graph generation with binding, and for efficient parameter fitting to control the graph statistics generated by EPGMs with binding.
    \item \textbf{Experiments (\cref{sec:exp}):} We use our binding and fitting algorithms to generate graphs. 
    Via experiments on real-world graphs, we show the power of edge dependency to preserve realistic graph patterns and validate the correctness of our theoretical analyses and practical algorithms.    
\end{enumerate}

\section{Preliminaries}


\smallsection{Graphs.}
A {node-labelled} \textit{graph} $G = (V, E)$ is defined by a \textit{node set} $V = V(G)$ and an \textit{edge set} \revisenips{$E = E(G) \subseteq \binom{V}{2} \coloneqq \set{V' \subseteq V \colon \abs{V'} = 2}$}.\footnote{In this work, we consider undirected unweighted graphs without self-loops following common settings for random graph models. {See \cref{app:discussion:general_graphs} for discussions on more general graphs.}}
For a node $v \in V$, the set of \textit{neighbors} of $v$ is $N(v; G) = \set{u \in V \colon (u, v) \in E(G)}$.
The \textit{degree} $d(v; G)$ of $v$ is the number of its neighbors, i.e., $d(v; G) = \abs{N(v; G)}$.
Given $V' \subseteq V$, the \textit{induced subgraph} of $G$ on $V'$ is $G[V'] = (V', E \cap \binom{V'}{2})$.
\smallsection{Random graph models (RGMs).}
Fix a node set $V = [n] = \set{1, 2, \ldots, n}$ with $n \in \bbN$.
Let $\calG(V) = \set{G = (V, E) \colon E \subseteq \binom{V}{2}}$ denote the set of all $2^{\binom{n}{2}}$ possible {node-labelled} graphs on $V$.
    A \textit{random graph model} (RGM) is defined as a probability distribution $f: \calG(V) \to [0, 1]$ with $\sum_{G \in \calG(V)} f(G) = 1$.
    For each graph $G \in \calG(V)$, $f(G)$ is the probability of $G$ being generated by the RGM $f$.
    For each node pair $(u, v)$ with $u, v \in V$, the (marginal) \textit{edge probability} of $(u, v)$ under the RGM $f$ is $\Pr_{f}[(u, v)] \coloneqq \sum_{G \in \calG(V)} f(G) \mathbf{1}[(u, v) \in E(G)]$, where $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ is the indicator function.

\smallsection{Edge independent graph models (EIGMs).}
Given edge probabilities, edge independency is widely assumed in many existing RGMs, resulting in the concept of edge independent graph models (EIGMs; also known as \textit{inhomogeneous Erd\H{o}s-R\'{e}nyi graphs}~\citep{klopp2017oracle}).
\begin{definition}[EIGMs \citep{nips21powerOfEIGMs}]\label{def:EIGMs}
    Given edge probabilities $\pfull$, the \textit{edge independent graph model} (EIGM) w.r.t. $p$ is the RGM $\feigm$ defined by
    $\feigm(G) = \prod_{(u, v) \in E(G)} p(u, v) \prod_{(u', v') \notin E(G)} (1 - p(u', v')), \forall G \in \calG(V)$.
\end{definition}



\section{Related work and background}


\subsection{Limitations of EIGMs}\label{sec:rel_wk:limit_eigms}
This work is motivated by the theoretical findings of 
\citeauthor{nips21powerOfEIGMs} \citeyearpar{nips21powerOfEIGMs} on the limitations of EIGMs \revisenips{and the power of edge (in)dependency.}
They defined the concept of \textit{overlap} to measure the variability of RGMs, where a high overlap value implies low variability.
Roughly speaking, the overlap of an RGM is the expected proportion of edges co-existing in two generated graphs.
\begin{definition}[Overlap \citep{nips21powerOfEIGMs}]\label{def:overlap}
    Given an RGM $f: \calG(V) \to [0, 1]$, the \textit{overlap} of $f$ is defined as
    $\overlap(f) = \frac{\bbE_{f} \abs{E(G') \cap E(G'')}}{\bbE_{f} \abs{E(G)}}$, where $G$, $G'$, and $G''$ are three mutually independent random graphs generated by $f$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:overlap_important}
High variability (i.e., low overlap) is important for RGMs~\citep{de2018molgan}, \revisenips{as generating overly similar graphs undermines RGMs' effectiveness in their common applications, e.g., graph algorithm testing, statistical testing, and graph anonymization (see \cref{sec:intro}).}
\end{remark}

\citet{nips21powerOfEIGMs} showed that EIGMs are unable to generate graphs with high triangle density (i.e., with many triangles) unless EIGMs memorize a whole input graph (i.e., have high overlap).
\begin{theorem}[Limited triangles by EIGMs \citep{nips21powerOfEIGMs}]\label{thm:EIGMs_limitation}    
    For any $\pfull$,
    $\bbE_{\feigm}[\triangle(G)]
    \leq \frac{\sqrt{2}}{3} \left(\overlap(\feigm) \sum_{(u, v) \in \binom{V}{2}} p(u, v)\right)^{3/2}$,
    where $\triangle(G)$ is the number of triangles in $G$.
\end{theorem}
\citeauthor{arxiv23edgeDpendency} \citeyearpar{arxiv23edgeDpendency} recently extended their theoretical results, showing triangle-density upper bounds w.r.t. overlap in different types of edge-dependent RGMs.\footnote{In EPGMs, the overlap is constant yet we can have different triangle densities. See \cref{thm:constant_degrees_overlap}.} 
{However, they did not
provide practical graph generation algorithms\footnote{Their graph generation algorithm is not practical since it relies on maximal clique enumeration, which is time-consuming~\citep{eblen2012maximum}. {See \cref{app:discussion:edgedep23} for more discussions.}} or detailed tractability results,}
while tractability results and practical graph generation are part of our focus in this work.

{There exist methods that shift edge probabilities by accept-reject~\citep{mussmann2015incorporating} or mixing different EIGMs~\citep{kolda2014scalable}, in order to improve upon existing EIGMs.
Such methods are essentially still EIGMs, and by \cref{thm:EIGMs_limitation}, they inevitably have high overlap (i.e., low variability).
See \cref{app:exp:high_overlap_eigms} for more discussion and empirical evaluation on such methods.}

\subsection{Edge dependency in RGMs}\label{sec:rel_wk:dependency}
\revisenips{Despite the popularity of EIGMs, edge dependency also widely exists in various RGMs,} e.g.,
preferential attachment models~\citep{barabasi1999emergence}, 
small-world graphs~\citep{watts1998collective},
copying network models~\citep{kleinberg1999web},
\revisenips{random geometric graphs~\citep{penrose2003random},
graphon models~\citep{lovasz2006limits},\footnote{\revisenips{In random geometric graphs and graphon models, edges are placed independently after the vectors associated with the nodes are fixed, but the whole process involves edge dependency.}}}
and exponential random graph models~\citep{lusher2013exponential}.
Some other models use additional mechanisms on top of existing models to introduce edge dependency by, e.g., directly forming triangles~\citep{pfeiffer2012fast,wegner2021atomic}.
Such models show the power of edge dependency, especially in generating graphs with high clustering.

\revisenips{As discussed above, we are not the first to consider edge dependency in RGMs. However, we provide a novel perspective on edge dependency. That is, we can decompose RGMs into (1) the marginal edge probabilities and (2) the realization of the probabilities (see \cref{def:EPGMs}). 
This new perspective provides a new way to construct RGMs, allowing us to ``stand on the shoulders of giants'', i.e., improve upon existing models that are based on marginal edge probabilities (mostly EIGMs), inheriting their strengths (e.g., variability and tractability; see \cref{thm:constant_degrees_overlap} and \cref{thm:local_binding_motif_probs}) and alleviating their weaknesses (e.g., inability to generate high clustering; see \cref{sec:exp}).}
\revisenips{Specifically, the closed-form tractability results on subgraph densities derived by us (\cref{thm:local_binding_motif_probs,thm:para_binding_motif_probs}) are usually unavailable for existing RGMs with edge dependency~\citep{drobyshevskiy2019random}.\footnote{\revisenips{Usually only \textit{asymptotic} results, as the number of nodes approaches infinity, are available for such models~\citep{ostroumova2017general,gu2013clustering,bhat2016densification}.}}}


\section{Edge probability graph models: Concepts and basic properties}

Given edge probabilities, EIGMs generate graphs assuming edge independency.
In contrast, we explore a broader class of edge probability graph models (EPGMs) going beyond edge independency.
\begin{definition}[EPGMs]\label{def:EPGMs}
    Given edge probabilities $\pfull$, the set $\calF(p)$ of \textit{edge probability graph models} (EPGMs) w.r.t. $p$ \revisenips{consists of all the RGMs satisfying the given marginal edge probabilities, i.e.,}
    $\calF(p) \coloneqq \set{f \colon \Pr_f[(u, v)] = p(u, v), \forall u, v \in V}$.
\end{definition}

The concept of EPGMs decomposes each RGM into two factors:
\textbf{(F1)} the marginal probability of each edge and
\textbf{(F2)} how the edge probabilities are realized (i.e., {sampled}), where (F2) has been overlooked by EIGMs \revisenips{and this decomposition introduces a novel way of imposing edge dependency}.
Below, we show some basic properties of EPGMs and discuss their meanings and implications.

\begin{property}\label{lem:eigms_are_epgms}
    {EIGMs are special cases of EPGMs w.r.t. the same edge probabilities.}
\end{property}
\begin{proof}

\end{proof}

\begin{property}\label{thm:EPGMs_general}
    {Each RGM can be represented as an EPGM (w.r.t its marginal edge probabilities).}
\end{property}

{While} \cref{thm:EPGMs_general} \revisenips{is an immediate result following the definition of EPGMs, %
{it}} shows the \textit{generality} of the concept of EPGMs, yet also implies the impossibility of exploring all possible EPGMs,
{which motivates us to find \textit{good subsets} of EPGMs.}
\revisenips{{Specifically,} \cref{thm:EPGMs_general} tells us that each RGM can be represented as an EPGM w.r.t. \textit{some} edge probabilities.}
What can we obtain for \textit{given} edge probabilities?
For this, we obtain the upper bounds of expected subgraph densities in the graphs generated by EPGMs with given edge probabilities.
\begin{property}[Upper bound of edge-group probabilities in EPGMs]\label{thm:EPPRGMs_co_existing_ub}
    For any $\pfull$ and any $P \subseteq \binom{V}{2}$, 
    $\Pr_{f}[P \subseteq E(G)] \leq \min_{(u, v) \in P} p(u, v), \forall f \in \calF(p)$.
\end{property}
\begin{remark}\label{rem:epgms_co_exist_ub}
    Later, we shall show that the upper bound in \cref{thm:EPPRGMs_co_existing_ub} is tight, i.e., we can find EPGMs achieving the upper bound (see \cref{lem:max_binding_max_coex_prob}).    
\end{remark}
\cref{thm:EPPRGMs_co_existing_ub} can be applied to obtain the upper bounds of the expected number of 
specific subgraphs, e.g., cliques and cycles.
Below is an example on the number of triangles (i.e., $\triangle(G)$).
\begin{corollary}\label{cor:EPPRGMs_triangle}
    For any $p$,
    $\bbE_{f}[\triangle(G)] \leq \sum_{\set{u,v,w} \in \binom{V}{3}} \min(p(u, v), p(u, w), p(v, w)), \forall f \in \calF(p)$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{property}[EPGMs have constant expected degrees and overlap]\label{thm:constant_degrees_overlap}
    For any $\pfull$, the expected node degrees and overlap (see \cref{def:overlap}) of all the EPGMs w.r.t. $p$ are constant.
\end{property}
\cref{thm:constant_degrees_overlap} implies that, \revisenips{for given edge probabilities,} compared to EIGMs, considering more general EPGMs neither changes expected degrees nor impairs the variability of the generated graphs.
\revisenips{Many EIGMs (e.g., Chung-Lu and Kronecker) can generate graphs with desirable degrees, and this property ensures that EPGMs can inherit such strengths (see \cref{fig:main_fig} for empirical evidence).}
{As discussed in \cref{rem:overlap_important}, high variability is important and desirable for RGMs.}

In this work, we explore EPGMs from both theoretical and practical perspectives, aiming to answer two research questions inspired by the basic properties of EPGMs above:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{(RQ1; Theory)} {How to find good subsets of EPGMs that are realistic, flexible, and tractable?}
    \item \textbf{(RQ2; Practice)} How to generate graphs using such EPGMs and fit the parameters of EPGMs?
\end{itemize}


\section{Binding: Realistic, flexible, and tractable EPGMs}


{We aim to construct EPGMs that are \textit{realistic} (specifically, high clustering) and
\textit{flexible} (i.e., different levels of dependency), in a \textit{tractable} (i.e., controllable graph statistics) way.}

\subsection{Binding: \revisenips{A general framework for}
realistic EPGMs with high clustering}\label{subsec:binding:binding}
\revisenips{As discussed in \cref{sec:intro}, desirable RGMs should generate graphs with realistic patterns, e.g., high subgraph densities, power-law degrees, and small diameters~\citep{chakrabarti2006graph}.
Many EIGMs (e.g., Kronecker) can already do well in the latter two aspects, while generating high subgraph densities is the empirical and theoretical (see \cref{thm:EIGMs_limitation}) bottleneck of EIGMs.
Hence, we focus on the bottleneck and aim to construct more \textit{realistic} EPGMs with high subgraph densities.}\footnote{\revisenips{Notably, later we shall also empirically show that binding maintains (or even improves) the realisticness w.r.t. several different graph metrics, including but not limited to degrees and diameters (see \cref{subsec:EPGMs_do_not_harm_others}).}}
To this end, we study and propose \textit{binding}, \revisenips{a general mathematical framework} that introduces {{positive dependency}} among edges, where multiple edges' (in)existences are determined together.

\input{ALG/binding_general}
Algorithmically, binding is the probabilistic process in \cref{alg:binding_general},
where we have a partition $\calP$ of all the pairs, and the node pairs in each group $P \in \calP$ are \textit{bound} together in that the (in)existences of the edges are determined \textit{dependently}.\footnote{\revisenips{See \cref{def:binding_formal} in \cref{app:proofs:general_binding} in for a formal definition of binding.}}
{{Note that, \revisenips{\cref{alg:binding_general} describes a general framework, while} our practical algorithms (\cref{alg:local_binding,alg:iid_binding}) do not need to choose an explicit partition $\calP$ beforehand.}}

There are two basic properties of binding:
\textit{(i)} binding is \textit{correct}, i.e., generates EPGMs, and
\textit{(ii)} binding improves the \textit{realisticness} w.r.t. subgraph densities upon EIGMs.

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:binding_preserve_probs}  \cref{alg:binding_general} with input $p$ (and any $\calP$) produces an EPGM w.r.t. $p$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:binding_higher_density}
    Binding produces higher or equal subgraph densities, compared to the corresponding EIGMs.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:lower_epgms}
    There are EPGMs with lower subgraph densities, which are against our motivation to improve upon EIGMs w.r.t. subgraph densities and are out of this work's scope.
    That said, they may be useful in scenarios where dense subgraphs are unwanted, e.g., disease control.
\end{remark}

With binding, we can construct EPGMs with different levels of edge dependency by different ways of binding the node pairs.
Let us first study two extreme cases.

\smallsection{Minimal binding.}
{EIGMs are the case with minimal binding, i.e., without binding, where the partition contains only sets of a single pair, i.e., $\calP = \set{\set{(u, v)} \colon u, v \in V}$.}

\smallsection{Maximal binding.}
Maximal binding corresponds to the case with $\calP = \set{\binom{V}{2}}$, i.e., all the pairs are bound together.
It achieves the upper bound of subgraph densities, i.e., the maximal edge-group probabilities in \cref{thm:EPPRGMs_co_existing_ub}, showing that the upper bound is tight, as mentioned in \cref{rem:epgms_co_exist_ub}.

\subsection{Local binding: Flexible and tractable spectrum between two extremes}\label{sec:binding:local_binding}
\revisenips{Building upon the general framework introduced in \cref{subsec:binding:binding}, we propose practical binding algorithms.}
Intuitively, the more pairs we bind together, the higher subgraph densities we have.
{Between minimal binding (i.e., EIGMs) and maximal binding that achieves the upper bound of subgraph densities, we can have a \textit{flexible} spectrum.}
However, the number of possible partitions of node pairs $\binom{V}{2}$ grows exponentially w.r.t. $\abs{V}$, which is hardly tractable.
{Hence, we propose to introduce edge dependency without explicit partitions.}
Specifically, we propose \textit{local binding},
where we repeatedly sample node groups,\footnote{We use independent \textit{node} sampling (yet still with \textit{edge} dependency), which is simple, tractable, and works empirically well in our experiments. See \cref{sec:disc:subset_sampling} for more discussions.\label{ftnt:indep_node_samp}} and
bind pairs between each sampled node group together.
Pairs between the same node group are structurally related, compared to pairs sharing no common nodes.

\smallsection{Real-world motivation.}
In social networks, each group ``bound together'' can represent a group interaction, e.g., an offline social event (meeting, conference, party) or an online social event (group chat, Internet forum, online game). 
In such social events, people gather together, and the communications/relations between them likely co-occur. 
At the same time, not all people in such events would necessarily communicate with each other, e.g., some people are more familiar with each other. This is the point of considering binding with various edge probabilities (instead of just inserting cliques).
\revisenips{In general, group interactions widely exist in graphs in different domains, e.g., social networks~\citep{felmlee2013interaction}, biological networks~\citep{naoumkina2010genomic}, and web graphs~\citep{dourisboure2009extraction}.}
{{See \cref{sec:disc:practical_binding} for more discussions.}}

In \cref{alg:local_binding}, we repeatedly sample a subset of nodes (\cref{alg:local_binding:sample_nodes}) and group the ungrouped pairs between the sampled nodes (\cref{alg:local_binding:add_group}).
We maintain $P_{rem}$ to ensure disjoint partitions (\cref{alg:local_binding:sample_pairs,alg:local_binding:update_P_rem}).
For practical usage, we consider a limited number (i.e., $R$) of rounds for binding (\cref{alg:local_binding:if_R_rounds,alg:local_binding:R_rounds_break}) otherwise it may take a long time to exhaust all the pairs.
\cref{alg:local_binding} is also a probabilistic process, and we use $\flb$ to denote the corresponding RGM, i.e.,
$\flb(G) = \Pr[$\cref{alg:local_binding} outputs $G$ with inputs $p$, $g$, and $R$].
{As a special case of binding, local binding is also correct, i.e., generates EPGMs.}

\begin{proposition}\label{thm:local_binding_EPPRGM}
    \cref{alg:local_binding} with input $p$ (and any $g$ and $R$) produces an EPGM w.r.t. $p$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{remark}\label{rem:local_binding_spectrum}
    We introduce node-sampling probabilities (i.e., $g$) to sample node groups with better tractability, without explicit partitions.
    With higher node-sampling probabilities, larger node groups are bound together, and the generated graphs are expected to have higher subgraph densities.
    Specifically, local binding forms a spectrum between the two extreme cases.
    When $g(v) \equiv 0$, local binding reduces to minimal binding, i.e., EIGMs.
    When $g(v) \equiv 1$, it reduces to maximal binding.
\end{remark}

    Given $\pfull$, $\gfull$, and $R \in \bbN$,
    $\flb$ generates a graph in $O(R \left(\sum_{v \in V} g(v)\right)^2 + \abs{V}^2)$ time with high probability,\footnote{That is, $\lim_{\abs{V} \to \infty} \Pr[\text{it takes $O(R \left(\sum_{v \in V} g(v)\right)^2 + \abs{V}^2)$}] = 1$.} with the worst case $O(R \abs{V}^2)$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Parallel binding: The parallelizable icing on the cake}\label{sec:binding:para_binding}
In local binding, the sampling order matters, i.e., later rounds are affected by earlier rounds.
Specifically, if one pair is already determined in an early round, even if it is sampled again in later rounds, its (in)existence cannot be changed.
This property hinders the parallelization of the binding process and the derivation of tractability analyses.
{This property also implies that each pair can only be bound together once, entailing less flexibility in the group interactions.}

We thus propose a {more flexible and} naturally \textit{parallelizable} binding algorithm, \textit{parallel binding}.
Specifically, we consider the probabilistic process in \cref{alg:iid_binding}, and let $\fiid$ denote the corresponding RGM defined by $\fiid(G) = \Pr[\text{\cref{alg:iid_binding} outputs $G$ with inputs $p$, $g$, and $R$}]$.

\input{ALG/iid_binding}
The high-level idea is to make each round of binding probabilistically equivalent (see Lines \ref{alg:iid_binding:for_each_round} to \ref{alg:iid_binding:add_edges_each_round}).
Specifically, in each round, we insert edges with low probabilities (compared to the ones in $p$) while maintaining the final individual edge probabilities, by the calculation of $r$ and $p_{rem}$ at \cref{alg:iid_binding:pair_prob_after_sampled,alg:iid_binding:pair_rem_prob}.
We can straightforwardly parallelize the rounds by, e.g., multi-threading.

Although parallel binding is algorithmically different from (local) binding (e.g., no partition is used), it shares many theoretical properties with local binding.
Specifically, 
\cref{thm:local_binding_EPPRGM},
\cref{rem:local_binding_spectrum},
\cref{thm:local_binding_time_gen},
\cref{thm:local_binding_motif_probs},
\cref{rem:local_binding_motif_prob_higher_order}, and
\cref{thm:local_binding_time}
also apply to parallel binding.
This implies that we maintain (or even improve; see \cref{rem:para_binding_can_isolated_nodes}) correctness, tractability, flexibility, and efficiency when using parallel binding instead of local binding.
See \cref{app:proof:para_binding} for the formal statements and proofs.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:para_binding_can_isolated_nodes}
We also derive tractability results of parallel binding on the expected number of (non-)isolated nodes.
It is much more challenging to derive such results for local binding due to the properties mentioned above, i.e., later rounds are affected by earlier rounds.
Since our main focus is on subgraph densities, 
see \cref{app:isolated_nodes} for all the analysis regarding (non-)isolated nodes.
\end{remark}
\subsection{Efficient parameter fitting with node equivalence}\label{sec:fitting}

Since our closed-form tractability results are functions involving only arithmetic operations on the parameters, differentiable optimization techniques (e.g., gradient descent) are possible for parameter fitting.
The fitting objective can be any function of $3$-motif probabilities.
{For example, one can fit the (expected) number of triangles ($\bbE_{\fxx}[\triangle(G)]$) to the ground-truth number in an input graph ($\triangle(G_{input})$) by minimizing the ``difference'' between them, e.g., 
$(1 - \frac{\bbE_{\fxx}[\triangle(G)]}{\triangle(G_{input})})^2$,
where 
$\bbE_{\fxx}[\triangle(G)] = \sum_{V' \in \binom{V}{3}} \Pr_{\fxx}[E(G[V']) = \binom{V'}{2}]$ are obtainable by the tractability results (\cref{thm:local_binding_motif_probs,thm:para_binding_motif_probs})
with $X \in \set{\text{LB}, \text{PB}}$ indicating the binding scheme (LB represents local binding and PB represents parallel binding).}

Efficient evaluation of the fitting objective is important.
{A key challenge is that the naive computation takes $O(\abs{V}^3)$ time in total by considering all $O(\abs{V}^3)$ different possible node groups $V'$ (see \cref{thm:local_binding_motif_probs,thm:para_binding_motif_probs})).}
We aim to improve the speed of computing the tractability results by considering \textit{node equivalence} w.r.t. motif probabilities in various edge-probability models.
Equivalent nodes form equivalent node groups, which reduces the number of distinct node groups to calculate.
\smallsection{Erd\H{o}s-R\'{e}nyi (ER) model.}
The ER model~\citep{erdos1959randomgraph1} outputs uniform edge probabilities, and all the nodes are equivalent.
Hence, we set all the node-sampling probabilities identical, i.e., $g(v) = g_0, \forall v \in V$ for a single parameter $g_0 \in [0, 1]$.

\begin{lemma}[Reduced time complexity with ER]\label{lem:fitting_er_time}
    For ER, the time complexities of computing 
    $3$-motif probabilities can be reduced 
    from $O(\abs{V}^3)$ to $O(1)$.
\end{lemma}

\smallsection{Chung-Lu (CL) model.}
The CL model~\citep{chung2002connected} outputs edge probabilities with expected degrees $D = (d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n)$,
and nodes with the same degree are equivalent.
We set node-sampling probabilities as a function of degree
with $k_{deg}$ parameters,
where 
$k_{deg} \coloneqq \abs{\set{d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n}}$.

\begin{lemma}[Reduced time complexity with CL]\label{lem:fitting_cl_time}
    For CL, the time complexities of computing 
    $3$-motif probabilities
    can be reduced 
    from $O(\abs{V}^3)$ to $O(k^3_{deg})$.
\end{lemma}

\smallsection{Stochastic block (SB) model}
The SB model~\citep{holland1983stochastic} outputs edge probabilities with each node assigned to a block (i.e., a group), and nodes partitioned in the same block are equivalent.
Hence, we set the node-sampling probabilities as a function of the block index, with the number of parameters equal to the number of blocks.
\begin{lemma}[Reduced time complexity with SB]\label{lem:fitting_SB_time}
    For SB, the time complexities of computing 
    $3$-motif probabilities
    can be reduced 
    from $O(\abs{V}^3)$ to $O(c^3)$, where $c$ is the total number of blocks.
\end{lemma}

\smallsection{Stochastic Kronecker (KR) model}
With a (commonly used $2$-by-$2$) seed matrix $\theta \in [0, 1]^{2 \times 2}$ and $k_{KR} \in \bbN$, the KR model~\citep{leskovec2010kronecker} outputs edge probabilities as the
$k_{KR}$-th Kronecker power of $\theta$.
In KR, each node $i \in [2^{k_{KR}}]$ is associated with a binary node label of length $k_{KR}$, i.e., the binary representation of $i - 1$.
Nodes with the same number of ones in their binary node labels are equivalent.\footnote{The equivalence in KR is slightly weaker than that in the other three models.
This is why the reduced time complexity is $O(k^7_{KR})$ instead of $O(k^3_{KR})$. See \cref{sec:proofs:KR} for more details.}
Hence, we set node-sampling probabilities as a function of the number of ones in the binary representation, with $k_{KR} + 1$ parameters.
\begin{lemma}[Reduced time complexity with KR]\label{lem:fitting_KR_time}
    For KR, the time complexities of computing 
    $3$-motif probabilities
    can be reduced 
    from $O(\abs{V}^3)$ to $O(k^7_{KR})$.   
\end{lemma}
\smallsection{Note.}
See \cref{app:proofs:fitting} for more details about parameter fitting, e.g., formal definitions of the models and the details of node equivalence.



\section{Experiments}


In this section, we empirically evaluate EGPMs with our binding schemes and show the superiority of realization schemes beyond edge independency.
Specifically, we show the following two points:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{(P1)} When we use our tractability results to fit the parameters of EPGMs, we improve upon EIGMs and reproduce high triangle densities, and thus produce high clustering which is a realistic pattern; this also validates the correctness of our tractability results and algorithms.
    \item \textbf{(P2)} We can reproduce other realistic patterns, e.g., power-law degrees and small diameters, especially when the corresponding EIGMs are able to do so; this shows that improving EIGMs w.r.t. clustering by binding does not harm the generation quality w.r.t. other realistic patterns.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental settings}
\smallsection{Datasets.}
We use six real-world datasets:
(1) social networks 
\textit{hamsterster (\hamster)} and \textit{facebook (\facebook)},
(2) web graphs \textit{polblogs (\polblog)} and
\textit{spam (\spam)}, and
(3) biological graphs \textit{CE-PG (\biopg)} and
\textit{SC-HT (\bioht)}.
{See \cref{tab:datasets} in \cref{app:exp:setting} for the statistics of the datasets.}

\smallsection{Models.}
We consider the four edge-probability models analyzed in \cref{sec:fitting}:
the Erd\H{o}s-R\'{e}nyi (ER) model,
the Chung-Lu (CL) model,
the stochastic block (SB) model, and
the stochastic Kronecker (KR) model.
\revisenips{Given an input graph, we fit each model to the graph and obtain the output edge probabilities (see \cref{app:proofs:fitting} for more details).}

\smallsection{Realization methods.}
We compare three realization methods:
EIGMs (\EI), and 
EPGMs with local binding (\LB) and with parallel binding (\PB).

\smallsection{Fitting.}
Since our main focus is to improve triangle densities,
in our main experiments, we use the number of triangles as the objective of the fitting algorithms.
We use gradient descent to optimize parameters.
\revisenips{In the main experiments, the edge probabilities are fixed as those output by the edge-probability models, while we also consider joint optimization of edge probabilities and node-sampling probabilities (see \cref{sec:exp:joint_optim}).}
{See \cref{app:exp:setting} for the detailed experimental settings.}

\input{TAB/main_tab}

\subsection{P1: EPGMs reproduce high clustering (\cref{tab:main_cluster})}\label{sec:app:p1_clustering}
EPGMs with binding reproduce realistic high clustering.
In \cref{tab:main_cluster}, for each dataset and each model, we compare three clustering-related metrics, the number of triangles ($\triangle$), the global clustering coefficient (GCC), and the average local clustering coefficient (ALCC), in the ground-truth (\GT) graph and the graphs generated with each realization method.
For each dataset and each model, we compute the ranking of each method according to the absolute error w.r.t. each metric, and show the average rankings (ARs) over datasets and models in \cref{tab:main_cluster}.
The statistics are averaged over $100$ generated graphs.
{See \cref{app:exp:cluster} for the full results with standard deviations.}

The number of triangles, which is the objective of our fitting algorithms, can be almost perfectly preserved by both \LB and \PB, showing the correctness and effectiveness of our algorithms.
Notably, as \cref{thm:EIGMs_limitation} imply, EIGMs often fail to generate graphs with enough triangles.
GCC and ALCC are also significantly improved (upon EIGMs) in most cases, while
\PB has noticeably higher ALCC than \LB.
In some rare cases, \PB generates graphs with exceedingly high GCC and/or ALCC and have higher absolute errors compared to EIGMs.

\subsection{P2: EPGMs generate realistic degrees and distances (\cref{fig:main_fig})}\label{subsec:EPGMs_do_not_harm_others}
EPGMs with binding (\LB and \PB) also reproduce other realistic patterns.
In \cref{fig:main_fig}, for each dataset (each column) and each model (each row), we compare the degree distributions and distance distributions in the ground-truth graph and the graphs generated with each realization method.
Specifically, for each realization method, we count the number of nodes with degree at least $k$ for each $k \in \bbN$ 
and count the number of pairs in the largest connected component with distance at least $d$ for each $d \in \bbN$ in each generated graph, and take the average number over $100$ generated graphs.
{See \cref{app:exp:deg_dist} for the formal definitions and full results.}

EPGMs with binding generate graphs with realistic patterns: power-law degrees and small diameters (i.e., small distances).
Both schemes (\LB and \PB) perform comparably well while \LB performs noticeably better with ER and \PB performs noticeably better with KR.
Importantly, when the edge probabilities output power-law expected degrees (e.g., CL and KR), the degree distributions are well preserved with binding.
Edge-independent ER cannot generate power-law degrees~\citep{bollobas2003mathematical}, and binding alleviates this problem.
\revisenips{Notably, with binding, the generated graphs are more realistic w.r.t. some other graph metrics (specifically, modularity and core numbers). See \cref{app:exp:deg_dist} for more details.}

\section{Conclusion and discussions}




In this work, we show that realization beyond edge independence can produce more realistic structures while ensuring high tractability and variability.
We formally define EPGMs and show their basic properties (\cref{sec:prob_statement}).
Notably, even with edge dependency, EPGMs maintain the same variability (\cref{thm:constant_degrees_overlap}).
We propose a realistic, tractable, and flexible realization framework called \textit{binding} (\cref{alg:binding_general}) with two practical variants: local binding (\cref{alg:local_binding}) and parallel binding (\cref{alg:iid_binding}).
We derive tractability results (\cref{thm:local_binding_motif_probs,thm:para_binding_motif_probs}) on the closed-form subgraph densities, and propose efficient parameter fitting (\cref{sec:fitting}; Lemmas~\ref{lem:fitting_er_time}-\ref{lem:fitting_KR_time}).
We conduct extensive experiments to show the empirical power of EPGMs with binding (\cref{sec:exp}). 

\smallsection{Limitations and future directions.}
EPGMs with binding generate more isolated nodes than EIGMs due to higher variance.
Fortunately, we can address the limitation by 
fitting and controlling the number of isolated nodes with the tractability results, as mentioned in \cref{rem:para_binding_can_isolated_nodes}.
\revisenips{The performance of EPGMs depends on both the underlying edge probabilities and the way to realize (i.e., sample from) them. In this work, we focus on the latter, while finding valuable edge probabilities is an independent problem. Notably, as shown in \cref{sec:exp:joint_optim}, it is possible to jointly optimize both edge probabilities and their realization.}
\revisenips{As discussed in \cref{rem:lower_epgms}, binding only covers a subset of EPGMs, and we will explore the other types of EPGMs (e.g., EPGMs with lower subgraph densities) in the future.
Combining binding with other mechanisms in existing edge-dependent RGMs to create even stronger RGMs is another interesting future direction.
