\title{Latent Space Chain-of-Embedding Enables Output-free LLM Self-Evaluation}

\begin{abstract}
LLM self-evaluation relies on the LLM's own ability to estimate response correctness, which can greatly improve its deployment reliability. 
In this research track, we propose the {\bf Chain-of-Embedding (CoE)} in the latent space to enable LLMs to perform output-free self-evaluation.
CoE consists of all progressive hidden states produced during the inference time, which can be treated as the latent thinking path of LLMs. We find that when LLMs respond correctly and incorrectly, their CoE features differ, these discrepancies assist us in estimating LLM response correctness.
Experiments in four diverse domains and seven LLMs fully demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. Meanwhile, its label-free design intent without any training and millisecond-level computational cost ensure real-time feedback in large-scale scenarios.
More importantly, we provide interesting insights into LLM response correctness from the perspective of hidden state changes inside LLMs
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\label{sec:intro}


Large Language Models (LLMs) have significantly enhanced their ability to generalize across diverse scenarios \colorcitep{brown2020language,achiam2023gpt,glm2024chatglm}.
However, their outputs can sometimes be unstable, leading to incorrect responses that may threaten social safety. Therefore, label-free LLM self-evaluation --- estimating the correctness of LLM responses fully through LLMs’ own capabilities --- has emerged as a crucial research area. It can provide real-time response monitoring and feedback in large-scale employments, enhancing the reliability of LLMs \colorcitep{sun2024trustllm}.

Popular self-evaluation research in the era of LLMs focuses more on output-based forms \colorcitep{zhang2023siren}.
Two typical paradigms that do not assess the internal states of LLMs involve directly asking LLMs to express confidence in their responses through well-designed prompts \colorcitep{lin2022teaching,tian2023just}, and generating multiple responses by perturbing prompts \colorcitep{gao2024spuq} or decoding sampling \colorcitep{wang2023self} to calculating the response consistency \colorcitep{xiong2024can}.
Besides the two types, other methods basically draw on uncertainty estimation concepts from the era of deep neural networks, leveraging output logits or probability distributions to gauge the confidence of model responses \colorcitep{malinin2020uncertainty,si2022prompting,huang2023look,kuhn2023semantic}.

Recently, some research has revealed that the latent space of LLMs contains a substantial amount of untapped hidden state information, they can largely reflect response correctness \colorcitep{azaria2023internal,liu2023cognitive,duan2024llms}, and are usually more interpretable than LLM output \colorcitep{li2024inference}.
However, these output-free research often require correctness labels 0/1 for training probing classifiers to extract features from hidden states \colorcitep{burns2022discovering,sky2024androids,su2024unsupervised}. This contradicts our goal of being ``label-free'' and limits the generalization capabilities on unseen data. {\it To further expand this research line}, we consider a challenging but valuable question: 

\begin{center}
{\it \textbf{How to solely utilize hidden states to estimate the LLM response correctness without any label?}}
\end{center}



To answer this question, we start from the perspective of human thinking:
In the cognitive theory, human thinking is accomplished collaboratively by intuitive thinking (system 1) and deliberative thinking (system 2) \colorcitep{evans2003two}: correct thinking tends to activate system 2 to produce more deliberative thinking paths, while incorrect thinking tends to be affected by system 1 to make more rapid and direct thinking paths \colorcitep{kahneman2011thinking}.
This cognitive phenomenon means that {\it human thinking paths may differ when responding correctly and incorrectly}.

Next, we draw an analogy about the latent thinking path from humans to LLMs:
Some research \colorcitep{peters2018dissecting,tenney2019bert,jawahar2019does,chen2020mixtext} has demonstrated that large Transformer-based \colorcitep{vaswani2017attention} language models generate text representations by first encoding morphological and syntactic information in the lower layers, then progressing to more complex semantic information in the higher layers.
This means that the hidden state changes can mirror the interpretable progressive thinking of LLMs in the latent space.
Moreover, some LLM mechanistic studies also utilize multiple hidden states to explore the thinking states at different stages of LLMs \colorcitep{ye2024physics}.
Therefore, {\it we can treat the progressive hidden states as the latent thinking path of LLMs}, which we term {\bf Chain-of-Embedding (CoE)}, as shown in Figure \colorref{img:first-figure}.


Based on these analyses, we boldly migrate human thinking patterns to LLMs and make the following assumption: {\it \textbf{CoE discrepancies may happen when LLMs generate correct and incorrect responses}}.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{figures/open-latent-space-3.pdf}  
  \caption{Chain-of-Embedding mirrors the latent thinking path of LLMs, which may reflect LLM response correctness during inference time.}
  \label{img:first-figure}
\end{wrapfigure}
Starting from this assumption, our paper structure and contributions are as follows:
In Section \colorref{sec:motivation}, we first explore the CoE discrepancy when LLMs respond correctly and incorrectly by quantifying its features to demonstrate this assumption.
In Section \colorref{sec:method}, we propose a comprehensive CoE-based metric for label-free LLM self-evaluation.
In Section \colorref{sec:experiment}, we verify the performance of our CoE method from four diverse domains that are popular in the LLM ability test: {\it Mathematics, Reasoning, Knowledge, and Understanding}, to demonstrate the effectiveness of CoE for self-evaluation.
In Section \colorref{sec:theoretical}, we conduct theoretical analyses further to present more insights about the effectiveness of our method.

\paragraph{Problem Statement.}
{\it Label-free LLM self-evaluation aims to estimate whether the LLM response to a given input question is correct or not \textbf{fully through LLMs' own capabilities without relying on any true label, external tool, and supervised trainer}} \colorcitep{chen2023quantifying,li2024think}.
We denote the language model as $f$. For each sample $(\bm{x}, \bm{y}) \leftrightarrow$ (question, true answer), the output of the language model is denoted as $\bm{\hat{y}} = f(\bm{x})$.
Then this sample is associated with a decision score $s = S(\bm{x}, \bm{\hat{y}}, f)$, where $S(\cdot)$ is a decision function derived solely from the question, LLM output, and language model information --- without reference to the true label $\bm{y}$. The domain of this function encompasses the entire sample and model space.
An ideal decision function aims to achieve the following goal: {\bf \textit{a higher score indicates a greater likelihood of a correct answer}}.

Therefore, the self-evaluation task can be formulated as a binary classification problem. Let $\gamma$ be the decision threshold, for a question, whether the LLM response is correct can be discriminated as the instruction function $\chi(\bm{\hat{y}}) = \mathsf{Correct} \text{ if } s > \gamma \text{ else } \mathsf{Incorrect}$. The core goal is to find an optimal threshold to improve the classification accuracy.



\section{Chain-of-Embedding Reflects Response Correctness}

\label{sec:motivation}

\subsection{Definition: Latent Space Chain-of-Embedding (CoE)}
\label{sec:definition}

\paragraph{Formalization.}
We start by formalizing the CoE under the language model $f$.
Assume the model has $L$ hidden layers, we can decompose $f$ into the following ordered sub-modules:
\begin{equation}
f = f_{\text{head}} \circ f_L \circ \cdots \circ f_l \circ \cdots \circ f_1 \circ f_{\text{emb}}.
\label{eq:decompose}
\end{equation}
In Eq.\colorref{eq:decompose}, $f_{\text{head}}: \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}$ is the final classification layer, $f_{\text{emb}}: \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$, which can also be denoted as $f_0$, is the embedding mapping layer (0-th layer), and each $f_l (1 \leq l \leq L): \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d$ is the intermediate hidden layer.
Here $d$ is the embedding dimensions and $\mathcal{V}$ is the model vocabulary.
Given a question $\bm{x}$ as input to $f$, the output $\bm{\hat{y}}$ consists of $T$ tokens $\hat{y}_1 \hat{y}_2 ... \hat{y}_T$. For the $t$-th token, we denote its hidden state at layer $l (0 \leq l \leq L)$, \emph{i.e.}, the $t$-th output embedding of function $f_l \circ \cdots \circ f_1 \circ f_0$, as $\bm{z}_l^t$.
Following the definitions in \colorcitet{ren2022out,wang2024trajectory}, we define the average embedding at layer $l$ as $\bm{h}_l = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \bm{z}_l^t$, which represents the $l$-th sentence hidden state.
Then, the CoE is expressed as a progressive chain $\bm{H}$ of all sentence hidden states formalized as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \bm{H} = \underbrace{\bm{h}_0}_{\text{Input State}} \rightarrow \underbrace{\bm{h}_1 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \bm{h}_l \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow \bm{h}_{L-1}}_{\text{Intermediate Hidden States}} \rightarrow \underbrace{\bm{h}_L}_{\text{Output State}} \tag{{\bf Chain-of-Embedding}}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Feature Definition.}
After formalizing the CoE, we need to quantify its features so that we can utilize them to validate the assumption proposed in Section \colorref{sec:intro}: ``{\it CoE discrepancies may happen when LLMs generate correct and incorrect responses}''.
We can create a continuous CoE trajectory in the latent space by performing segmented linear interpolation (simply connecting adjacent states) on the CoE.
To measure the trajectory feature, its geometric information is the most fundamental dimension \colorcitep{helland2009trajectory,rintoul2015trajectory}, which usually includes {\bf magnitude} and {\bf angle} that can reflect the distance and direction changes produced during the trajectory wandering.

We briefly examine the practical significance of these two features in measuring the LLMs' thinking path.
The magnitude feature is undoubtedly the direct feature of the thinking path curvature. 
In contrast, while the angle feature does not explicitly represent the thinking path curvature, the cosine value between two embeddings indicates semantic similarity \colorcitep{rahutomo2012semantic}. This suggests that the angle feature can indirectly reflect the thinking path feature at the semantic modeling level.

Now, we first define changes in magnitude and angle between each adjacent state pair $(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) (0 \leq l \leq L-1)$. The magnitude change $M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ is quantified using the L2-norm, while the angle change $A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ is derived by indirectly calculating the cosine value between the two vectors, and then transformed using the arc cosine function. The two measures are formalized as follows:
\begin{equation}
    M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) = ||\bm{h}_{l+1} - \bm{h}_l||_2, ~~~~~A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) = \arccos \left( \frac{\bm{h}_{l+1}^{\top} \bm{h}_l}{||\bm{h}_{l+1}||_2 \cdot ||\bm{h}_{l}||_2} \right).
\end{equation}
Subsequently, the magnitude and angle features of the whole CoE trajectory, denoted as $\mathrm{Mag}(\bm{H})$ and $\mathrm{Ang}(\bm{H})$,  can be defined as the average changes in magnitude and angle between each adjacent state pair. The two features are formalized as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Mag}(\bm{H}) = \frac{1}{L} \cdot \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \frac{M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})}{\mathcal{Z}_\mathrm{Mag}},~~~~~
    \mathrm{Ang}(\bm{H}) = \frac{1}{L} \cdot \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \frac{A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) }{\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{Ang}}}.
    \label{eq:change}
\end{equation}
In Eq.\colorref{eq:change}, to reduce potential sample biases, we set the range scaling factors $\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{Mag}} = M(\bm{h}_0, \bm{h}_{L})$ and $\mathcal{Z}_{\mathrm{Ang}} = A(\bm{h}_0, \bm{h}_{L})$ for the following reason:
The positions in the input space and output space of different samples may vary, if the input and output of one sample are far apart in the latent space, its trajectory naturally has a longer wandering distance.
By setting scaling factors, we convert the absolute magnitude and angle changes of each adjacent state pair into relative changes, specifically, the changes of $(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ is relative to the changes of the input-output states $(\bm{h}_0, \bm{h}_{L})$, thereby avoiding measurement noise caused by inherent differences between samples.




\subsection{How CoE Reflects Response Correctness}
\label{sec:pre-experiment}


\paragraph{Setup.}
After defining CoE and its features, we aim to explore the generalized impact of CoE on the LLM response correctness. Therefore, we focus on four popular domains: {\it Mathematics}, {\it Reasoning}, {\it Knowledge}, {\it Understanding}, {\bf with each domain set MATH, TheoremQA, MMLU, and Belebele as domain datasets} separately. Dataset details and citations can be found in Section \colorref{sec:setup}.
In each domain dataset, we divide the correct and incorrect samples into two sets, with each corresponding to a feature set, denoted as $V_+ = \{(\mathrm{Mag}^+_i, \mathrm{Ang}^+_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_+}$ and $V_- = \{(\mathrm{Mag}^-_i, \mathrm{Ang}^-_i)_{i=1}^{n_-}\}$.
Additionally, we use the {\bf Qwen2-7B-Instruct} \colorcitep{yang2024qwen2} model as the backbone.
These settings apply to all experimental analyses in this section (Figure \colorref{img:trajectory-distribution} and \colorref{img:trajectory}) and will not be repeated hereafter.




\paragraph{CoE Feature Distribution Discrepancy.}
Now, we quantify the CoE feature discrepancies between correct and incorrect sample sets for each domain.
Due to each sample having two CoE features, we employ 2D kernel density estimation to calculate the probability density function (PDF) $f_V$ for each feature set $V = \{(\mathrm{Mag}_i, \mathrm{Ang}_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, which represent the CoE feature distribution of $V$. We use Gaussian kernel for PDF estimation, expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    f_V(\mathrm{Mag}, \mathrm{Ang}) = \frac{1}{nh^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{1}{2\pi} \exp \left\{ -\frac{1}{2h^2} [(\mathrm{Mag} - \mathrm{Mag}_i)^2 + (\mathrm{Ang} - \mathrm{Ang}_i)^2] \right\},
    \label{eq:pdf}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the bandwidth, which we set to 1 to maintain consistency with the default value in the Python \texttt{sklearn} library \colorcitep{pedregosa2011scikit}. We implement Eq.\colorref{eq:pdf} using the \texttt{sklearn} library.
In each domain, we independently derive the PDFs $f_{V_+}$ and $f_{V_-}$ for $V_+$ and $V_-$ using Eq.\colorref{eq:pdf}, as illustrated in Figure \colorref{img:trajectory-distribution}.
We find that in all domains, the distributions of correct and incorrect samples do not overlap and show significant discrepancies, with conclusions described below:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=8px]
    \item {\it CoE magnitude feature} $\mathrm{Mag}$ of correct samples are more significant than that of incorrect samples, indicating that LLMs' latent thinking paths are more convoluted when providing correct answers.
    \item {\it CoE angle feature} $\mathrm{Ang}$ of correct samples are less significant than that of incorrect samples, indicating that LLMs' latent thinking paths at the semantic modeling level are more unstable when providing incorrect answers.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{CoE Visualization.}
These two conclusions directly reveal the essential discrepancies of CoE features. To demonstrate these more intuitively, we visualize CoE as illustrated in Figure \colorref{img:trajectory}:
Overall, the CoE trajectory is not inclined to choose the shortest path from the input space to the output space; instead, it passes through a semantic space that is quite distant from both the input and output.
In detail, the detour phenomenon of the CoE trajectory for correct samples is more pronounced than that for incorrect samples, which verifies its more significant CoE magnitude feature. 
Furthermore, the intermediate states of the incorrect samples are closer to the origin, resulting in a larger angle formed before and after the state transition, which verifies its more significant CoE angle feature. 


\section{CoE Score for Output-free LLM Self-evaluation}

\label{sec:method}
In Section \colorref{sec:motivation}, we have quantified CoE features and highlighted discrepancies between correct and incorrect samples. Next, we wish to create a self-evaluation metric combining the two features, so as to detect the LLM response correctness using a comprehensive CoE feature.
However, the feature combination is not simple because of their inconsistent magnitudes. We propose two ways as follows:

\paragraph{CoE-R: Real-Space Combination.}
A straightforward method is to calculate a numerical summation of the two features. Though adding the two real numbers may seem unmeaningful due to their differing magnitudes, our focus is on the metric relative trends rather than exact numbers, so this way can preserve the metric usability without considering the feature relevance.

In Section \colorref{sec:pre-experiment}, we have found that $\mathrm{Ang}(\bm{H})$ is inversely proportional to the response correctness, so for each adjacent state pair, we use $1 - A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) / A(\bm{h}_0, \bm{h}_{L})$ as the direction change measure, then add it with $M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) / M(\bm{h}_0, \bm{h}_{L})$. By removing extraneous constants, we derive $\text{CoE-R}(\bm{H})$ score by averaging all adjacent state pair changes with the real-space combination way:
\begin{equation}
    \text{CoE-R}(\bm{H}) = \frac{1}{L} \cdot \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \left( \frac{M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})}{M(\bm{h}_0, \bm{h}_{L})} - \frac{A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) }{A(\bm{h}_0, \bm{h}_{L})} \right). 
\end{equation}

\paragraph{CoE-C: Complex-Space Combination.}
However, while the exact values of the two features may not be critical, they can significantly interfere with each other, particularly if one is abnormally large. This interference can weaken the overall impact of another feature, resulting in the instability of the CoE-R metric. 
Therefore, we aim to combine the two features more seamlessly.

The magnitude and angle features enable a clear association of complex numbers in the complex plane, with each point uniquely represented by its complex magnitude and complex argument.
Therefore, for each adjacent state pair, We combine $M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ and $A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ into a new feature point $C(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ on the complex plane, where $M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ represents the complex magnitude and $A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ represents the complex argument. This feature point $C(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})$ can be expressed as:
\begin{small}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        C(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) &= M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) e^{i \cdot A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})} \\
        &= \underbrace{M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) \cos(A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}))}_{\text{Real Part:} ~\mathrm{Re}(C(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}))} ~+~ i \cdot \underbrace{M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) \sin(A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}))}_{\text{Imaginary Part:} ~\mathrm{Im}(C(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}))},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{small}

where $i$ is the imaginary unit. Each adjacent state pair corresponds to one feature point, we then average these $L$ feature points by separately averaging their real and imaginary parts. The magnitude of this averaged point yields the final $\text{CoE-C}(\bm{H})$ score with the complex-space combination way:
\begin{small}
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{CoE-C}(\bm{H}) 
        &= \sqrt{ \left( \frac{1}{L} \cdot \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \mathrm{Re}(C(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})) \right)^2 + \left( \frac{1}{L} \cdot \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} \mathrm{Im}(C(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})) \right)^2 } \\
        = \frac{1}{L} \cdot &\sqrt{ \left( \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) \cos(A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})) \right)^2 + \left( \sum_{l=0}^{L-1} M(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1}) \sin(A(\bm{h}_l, \bm{h}_{l+1})) \right)^2 }.
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\end{small}

This combination metric still tends to capture relative trends, while fundamentally aiming to establish more robust mutual constraints between the two features, in contrast to CoE-R.
First, the monotonicity of CoE-C and CoE-R remains consistent and does not contradict the conclusions of Section \colorref{sec:pre-experiment}.
Second, CoE-C exhibits less variation than CoE-R when confronted with outliers, allowing for fewer deviations from the feature distribution of the corresponding class. See Section \colorref{sec:theoretical} for concrete proofs.

 Higher scores indicate a greater likelihood of obtaining the correct response. 
In terms of {\bf Computational Complexity}, after the base LLM inference and the extraction of all hidden states, the computations for both CoE-R and CoE-C involve only $\mathcal{O}(Ld)$ operations for scalar addition and multiplication, along with $\mathcal{O}(L)$ operations for square roots and trigonometric computations.
Since these computations can be executed in parallel by the CPU, the overall computational burden is negligible. Section \colorref{sec:advantage} will provide a detailed efficiency analysis of the execution time in practice.

\section{Experimental Verification}

\label{sec:experiment}


\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Dataset.}
We select six datasets across four domains for our self-evaluation experiments. These domains reflect the four critical dimensions of LLM capabilities \colorcitep{zheng2024judging,huang2024c}:
(1) {\bf GSM8K} \colorcitep{cobbe2021training} and {\bf MATH} \colorcitep{hendrycks2measuring} for the {\it Mathematics} domain;
(2) {\bf CommonsenseQA} \colorcitep{talmor2019commonsenseqa} and {\bf TheoremQA} \colorcitep{chen2023theoremqa} for the {\it Reasoning} domain;
(3) {\bf MMLU} \colorcitep{hendrycks2020measuring} for the {\it Knowledge} domain; 
(4) {\bf Belebele} \colorcitep{bandarkar2023belebele} for the {\it Understanding} domain.
\paragraph{Language Model.}
We use {\it instruction-based} models due to their ability to follow instructions, effectively addressing diverse user needs. We mainly adopt 7B+ parameter models with the Zero-Shot-CoT generation paradigm \colorcitep{wei2022chain,kojima2022large}, including {\bf Llama2-7B} \colorcitep{touvron2023llama}, {\bf Llama3-8B} \colorcitep{llama3}, {\bf Qwen1.5-7B} \colorcitep{qwen15}, {\bf Qwen2-7B} \colorcitep{yang2024qwen2}, {\bf Mistral-7B} \colorcitep{jiang2023mistral}.
Additionally, to demonstrate the robustness of model parameter scaling, we also test two larger models with 70B+ parameters: {\bf Llama3-70B} \colorcitep{llama3} and {\bf Qwen2-72B} \colorcitep{yang2024qwen2}.


\paragraph{Baseline.}
We select {\it ten} label-free self-evaluation baselines for fair comparisons:
The first two represent typical paradigms that do not assess internal states:
(1) {\bf Verbal Confidence}, we select P(true) \colorcitep{kadavath2022language} for its versatility.
(2) {\bf Prompt-Sampling-Aggregation (PSA) Pipeline} \colorcitep{xiong2024can}, we refer to \colorcitet{gao2024spuq} to perturb input prompt with special tokens and aggregate sampling outputs based on {\it lexical similarity} \colorcitep{lin2022towards,kuhn2023semantic} with Rouge-L \colorcitep{lin2004rouge}; 
The remaining eight are centered on the idea of uncertainty estimation, which assesses the internal state:
(3) {\bf Maximum Softmax Probability};
(4) {\bf Perplexity} \colorcitep{si2022prompting};
(5) {\bf Entropy} \colorcitep{huang2023look};
(6) {\bf Temperature Scaling} \colorcitep{shih2023long};
(7) {\bf Energy} \colorcitep{liu2020energy};
(8) {\bf Monte-Carlo Dropout} \colorcitep{gal2016dropout};
(9) {\bf Length-normalized Entropy} \colorcitep{malinin2020uncertainty};
(10) {\bf Eigenscore} \colorcitep{chen2024inside}.

Among these, (3)-(7) require only single output distribution, (2) and (8)-(10) require multiple stochastic inferences for sampling, and (10) only utilize the hidden states.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
We select {\bf AUROC} \colorcitep{boyd2013area}, {\bf FPR95}, and {\bf AUPR} \colorcitep{manning1999foundations} metrics to evaluate performances.
AUROC focuses on the trade-off between $\mathrm{TPR}$ and $\mathrm{FPR}$ under different thresholds; FPR95 focuses on the rate of misclassifying samples when $\mathrm{TPR}$ reaches 95\%; AUPR focuses on the trade-off between $\mathrm{Precision}$ and $\mathrm{Recall}$, placing greater importance on the correct prediction of positive cases.
These three metrics complement each other and can reflect the classification performances from different perspectives \colorcitep{cen2021deep,hendrycks2022scaling}.
Additionally, we use the exact match to obtain the correctness labels 0/1 of LLM responses for evaluation.



\subsection{Main Results (Table \colorref{tab:mainresults})}

\paragraph{Method Comparisons.}
First, our methods {\bf achieve SOTA performances across almost all scenarios.}
{\bf In four domains, our CoE achieves an average improvement of 8.30\%, 5.55\%, and 5.52\% across three metrics compared to the optimal baseline}. Notably, an AUROC value below 60 denotes a deviation from ideal performance \colorcitep{xiong2024can}. {\bf Our CoE exceeds this threshold in most scenarios, underscoring its practical value}. In contrast, most baselines fall short of this standard, particularly in the last three domains.

In contrast to the consistently optimal performance of our CoE method across nearly all scenarios, we observe that the performances of other baselines lack stability, with its effectiveness varying significantly across different scenarios. Below, we provide an analysis of other methods:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=8px]
    \item First, we examine two typical paradigms (1-2) that do not assess internal states. Verbal confidence often exhibits poor performances, mainly due to a confirmed overconfidence issue \colorcitep{zhang2024self,xiong2024can}. PSA pipeline lacks stability, it can either perform optimally or underperform. This may stem from unresolved issues related to effective consistency metrics \colorcitep{manakul2023selfcheckgpt} and inadequate prompt robustness.
    \item Secondly, we examine the uncertainty estimation methods (3-10) that assess internal states. 
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=12px]
        \item Single-output-based methods (3-7) possess the highest stability. While they do not always achieve best performances, they seldom rank the worst in any scenario. However, on the other hand, their performance limitations align with the assertion by \colorcitet{liu2024uncertainty} that ``the traditional uncertainty estimation can be extremely challenging when applied to LLMs due to the output diversity''. Our chosen tasks include challenging datasets like MATH, where LLMs can generate solutions with thousands of tokens. This complexity greatly limits these methods.
        \item In contrast, multiple-output-based methods (8-10) exhibit greater instability. For example, the LN-entropy method does not consistently surpass its base entropy version and can sometimes perform the worst, highlighting the inherent uncertainties associated with sampling.
        \item We are especially interested in the performances of Eigenscore (10), as it also utilizes hidden state information. However, it falls short of ideal performance, particularly in the {\it Mathematical} domain.
        The motivation behind Eigenscore is the rich semantic information inherent in the embedding space can be utilized \colorcitep{chen2024inside}. However, \colorcitet{wang2024trajectory} has found that the embedding modeling is often inaccurate for mathematical tasks, which may result in underperformance.
        In contrast, we emphasize the dynamic changes of hidden states, focusing on their behaviors within the latent space rather than on the specific state representations.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metric Consistency.}
Among the three metrics, AUROC emphasizes the trade-off between $\mathrm{TPR}$ and $\mathrm{FPR}$, FPR95 emphasizes the $\mathrm{FPR}$, and AUPR emphasizes the prediction of positive cases. They fully simulate the various demands in real-life scenarios.
In our experiments, we find that in most cases, our CoE method maintains the best performance across all three metrics simultaneously, except in a few cases where the trends of AUROC and FPR95 are inconsistent. This suggests that our method may focus more on positive cases, \emph{i.e.}, correctly responded samples.
Overall, {\bf our CoE method can adequately adapt to various discrimination needs in real-world scenarios}.

\paragraph{Domain Robustness.}
In vertical comparisons between four domains, the average AUROC\footnote{Note that the ratio of positive to negative examples varies across different scenarios, as it depends on the model's accuracy for each specific task. Therefore, when performing a vertical comparison between various domains of the same model or method, AUROC is the most appropriate metric for it is insensitive to the ratio of positive to negative examples compared to the other two metrics.} performance improvements compared to the strongest baseline are {\bf 9.83\%, 8.36\%, 7.78\%, 7.23\%} from domains I to IV. Notably, {\bf no domain shows a significantly lower improvement, highlighting our domain robustness}.
More interestingly, the improvement in the {\it Mathematics} domain is noticeably greater, and it is exactly the most objective among the four domains, suggesting that our method is likely more effective in objective scenarios.
This phenomenon aligns with human intuition: compared to solving subjective problems, humans' thinking path when solving objective problems tends to be less influenced by subjective feelings and biases. This reduces the subjective noise and enhances path systematic \colorcitep{paul2019miniature}, thereby increasing the quantification precision of path features.

\paragraph{Model-scaling Robustness.}
In horizontal comparisons between 70B+ and 7B+ parameter models, we are surprised to find that even with a tenfold increase in parameters, our method still maintains its leading performance and even surpasses the 7B+ model performances in most cases.
As the demand for large-scale LLMs surges in the industry, {\bf the enhanced model scaling robustness allows our method for widespread deployment in real-world scenarios, ensuring its broad generalizability}.

\section{Theoretically Revisit CoE-C and CoE-R}

\label{sec:theoretical}
\paragraph{Monotonicity Analysis.}
In Section \colorref{sec:method}, we pointed out that CoE-R lacks physical significance as a metric. However, its linearity effectively captures the monotonicity of both magnitude and angle features. In contrast, the monotonicity of these two features of CoE-R is not as apparent.
We assume $n$ feature points, each represented as a pair of magnitude and angle $(L_i, \alpha_i)$ for $1 \leq i \leq n$, with $\bm{L} = [L_i]_{i=1}^n$ and $\bm{\alpha} = [\alpha_i]_{i=1}^n$. The final CoE feature is denoted as $\mathcal{F}(\bm{L}, \bm{\alpha})$.
We consider the increments $\Delta L$ applied to $L_i$ and $\Delta \alpha$ applied to $\alpha_i$, with the CoE feature increments being 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        &\Delta \mathcal{F}(L_i) = \mathcal{F}(L_i + \Delta L, \bm{L}_{-i}, \bm{\alpha}) - \mathcal{F}(L_i, \bm{L}_{-i}, \bm{\alpha}), \\
        &\Delta \mathcal{F}(\alpha_i) = \mathcal{F}(\alpha_i + \Delta \alpha, \bm{\alpha}_{-i}, \bm{L}) - \mathcal{F}(\alpha_i, + \Delta \alpha, \bm{\alpha}_{-i}, \bm{L})
    \end{aligned}
\label{eq:increment}
\end{equation}

For CoE-R, its final feature $\mathcal{F}_R = \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{L_j}{n} + \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\alpha_j}{n}$ ensures $\Delta \mathcal{F}_R(L_i) = \frac{\Delta L}{n} > 0$ and $\Delta \mathcal{F}_R(\alpha_i) = - \frac{\Delta \alpha}{n} < 0$.
In contrast, The situation on CoE-C is relatively complex, we formalize its final feature $\mathcal{F}_C$ and computer its feature increments $\Delta \mathcal{F}_C(L_i)$ and $\Delta \mathcal{F}_C(\alpha_i)$ by following Eq.\colorref{eq:increment} as below:
\begin{scriptsize}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_C(\bm{L}, \bm{\alpha}) = \sqrt{\left( \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n L_j \cos \alpha_j}{n} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\sum_{j=1}^n L_j \sin \alpha_j}{n} \right)^2} = \frac{1}{n} \sqrt{\sum_j L_j^2 + \sum_{k,t, k\neq t} 2L_kL_t \cos(\alpha_k - \alpha_t)}.
\end{equation}
\end{scriptsize}
\begin{scriptsize}
\begin{equation}
        \Delta \mathcal{F}_C(L_i) = \frac{\Delta L \left( 2L_i + \Delta L + \sum_{j, j \neq i} 2L_j \cos(\alpha_i - \alpha_j) \right)}{n^2 \mathcal{F}_C(L_i + \Delta L, \bm{L}_{-i}, \bm{\alpha}) + n^2 \mathcal{F}_C(\bm{L}, \bm{\alpha})}, ~~~
        \Delta \mathcal{F}_C(\alpha_i) = \frac{-\sum_{i,j,i\neq j} L_iL_j \sin \left(\alpha_i - \alpha_j + \frac{\Delta \alpha}{2} \right) \sin \left(\Delta \alpha \right)}{n^2 \mathcal{F}_C(\alpha_i + \Delta \alpha, \bm{\alpha}_{-i}, \bm{L}) + n^2 \mathcal{F}_C(\bm{L}, \bm{\alpha})}.
\label{eq:increment-coec}
\end{equation}
\end{scriptsize}

\colorref{eq:increment-coec}.
In practical inference, we statistically find that more than 98\% of the cases fall within the range of $\alpha_i$ between 0 and $\pi/2$, this can also be intuitive in Figure \colorref{img:trajectory}. Consequently, it is nearly always true that $\Delta \mathcal{F}_C(L_i) > 0$.
Additionally, the angle difference between correct and incorrect trajectories is often greater than the angle difference within a single trajectory, namely $|\alpha_i - \alpha_j|$. When $\Delta \alpha$ causes $\alpha_i$ to deviate from the current class feature, it tends to be sizable.
As a result, $\sin \left(\alpha_i - \alpha_j + \frac{\Delta \alpha}{2} \right)$ will be greater than 0, leading to $\Delta \mathcal{F}_C(\alpha_i) < 0$.

Therefore, in practical scenarios, the CoE-C monotonicity of both magnitude and angle features is consistent with CoE-R and satisfies conclusions drawn in Section \colorref{sec:pre-experiment}.



\paragraph{Why CoE-C is More Robust Than CoE-R?}
In Section \colorref{sec:method}, we pointed out that CoE-C may be more sensitive to outliers. This claim has been verified in the ablation study presented in Section \colorref{sec:advantage}. Here, we delve into the fundamental reasons behind the metric robustness from a theoretical perspective.

We already know that the magnitude changes of the CoE trajectory of a correct sample is more significant, which means that for an incorrect sample, if one $L_i$ of a feature point appears abnormally large, it will be easily misclassified as a correct sample.
Therefore, if one CoE feature can better control the increment when facing this situation, it will reduce the risk of misclassification. Formally, we compare $\Delta \mathcal{F}_R(L_i)$ and $\Delta \mathcal{F}_C(L_i)$, the smaller one CoE metric possesses stronger robustness.
We first deflate the lower bound of $\Delta \mathcal{F}_C(L_i)$ by fixing the principal element $L_i$:
\begin{small}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}_C(\bm{L}, \bm{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{n} \sqrt{\sum_j L_j^2 + \sum_{k,t,k \neq t} 2L_kL_t \cos(\alpha_k - \alpha_t)} \geq \frac{1}{n} \left( L_i + \sum_{j,j\neq i} L_j \cos(\alpha_i - \alpha_j) \right)
\end{equation}
\end{small}

Then, we use this deflation bound to further deflate the $\Delta \mathcal{F}_C(L_i)$ of Eq.\colorref{eq:increment}:
\begin{scriptsize}
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \mathcal{F}_C(L_i) \leq \frac{\Delta L \left( 2L_i + \Delta L + \sum_{j, j \neq i} 2L_j \cos(\alpha_i - \alpha_j) \right)}{n^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n} \left( L_i +\Delta L + \sum_{j,j\neq i} L_j \cos(\alpha_i - \alpha_j) \right) + n^2 \cdot \frac{1}{n} \left( L_i + \sum_{j,j\neq i} L_j \cos(\alpha_i - \alpha_j) \right)} = \frac{\Delta L}{n}
\label{eq:deflation}
\end{equation}
\end{scriptsize}
We find that the right side of Eq.\colorref{eq:deflation} is exactly $\mathcal{F}_R(L_i)$, which implies $\mathcal{F}_C(L_i) \leq \mathcal{F}_R(L_i)$, proving that CoE-C is more robust than CoE-R.


\section{Related Work and Discussion}

\label{sec:related-and-discussion}

Our research focuses on label-free self-evaluation, where the uncertainty estimation in deep neural networks \colorcitep{gal2016dropout,guo2017calibration} and their variants in the era of LLMs \colorcitep{huang2023look,kuhn2023semantic} are closely related to us, we categorize them as white-box methods. Additionally, the two typical paradigms that do not access internal states are classified as black-box methods \colorcitep{manakul2023selfcheckgpt,li2024think}. Among these, white-box and black-box research tracks are usually orthogonal to each other \colorcitep{li2024think}. 

From the perspective of research ideas, our research involves the usage of hidden state information.
Many existing studies typically utilize hidden states to train correctness-label-based probing classifiers. They can learn useful hidden state features of specific datasets or error types, but their generalization ability on out-of-distribution (OOD) data is unpredictable.
Despite the differing research intents, considering the overlap of research ideas, we also conduct simple comparisons with them.
We select two recent works:
(1) ITI \colorcitep{li2024inference}, which is trained on the TruthfulQA dataset \colorcitep{lin2022truthfulqa}; and (2) MIND \colorcitep{su2024unsupervised}, which is trained with hallucinated data sourced from Wikipedia. Both methods focus on detecting factual errors, making mathematics and reasoning tasks likely OOD for them.
We select four datasets: 
(1) TruthfulQA \colorcitep{lin2022truthfulqa} as it is in-distribution (ID) for both ITI and MIND and even fitted by ITI; and (2-4) GSM8k, MATH, and TheoremQA (used in our main experiments), they are OOD for both ITI and MIND.

\section{Conclusion}

In summary, we propose a lightweight self-evaluation method for LLMs.
It does not access the output text or probability distribution but utilizes the progressive chain of all hidden states in the latent space instead, which we term Chain-of-Embedding (CoE). 
Our method exhibits strong performance and robustness across various models, domains, task difficulties, and languages. 
Its low computational cost also ensures real-time deployments for large-scale feedback needs in practical scenarios.
