\title{Near-Optimal Policy Identification in Robust Constrained Markov Decision Processes via Epigraph Form}

\begin{abstract}
\looseness=-1
Designing a safe policy for uncertain environments is crucial in real-world control applications.
However, this challenge remains inadequately addressed within the Markov decision process (MDP) framework.
This paper presents the first algorithm capable of identifying a near-optimal policy in a robust constrained MDP (RCMDP), where an optimal policy minimizes cumulative cost while satisfying constraints in the worst-case scenario across a set of environments.
We first prove that the conventional Lagrangian max-min formulation with policy gradient methods can become trapped in suboptimal solutions by encountering a sum of conflicting gradients from the objective and constraint functions during its inner minimization problem.
To address this, we leverage the epigraph form of the RCMDP problem, which resolves the conflict by selecting a single gradient from either the objective or the constraints.
Building on the epigraph form, we propose a binary search algorithm with a policy gradient subroutine and prove that it identifies an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy in an RCMDP with $\tiO(\varepsilon^{-4})$ policy evaluations.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




\looseness=-1
Designing policies that satisfy safety constraints even in unforeseen environments is crucial for real-world decision-making, as real systems frequently encounter measurement or estimation errors and environmental disturbances \citep{taguchi1986introduction}. 
Within the Markov decision process (MDP) framework,  constraint satisfaction and environmental uncertainty have traditionally been addressed separately, through constrained MDPs (CMDP; e.g., \citet{altman1999constrained}) and robust MDPs (RMDP; e.g., \citet{iyengar2005robust}), respectively.
The former aims to minimize costs while satisfying constraints, whereas the latter aims to minimize the worst-case cost in an uncertainty set of possible environments. 
However, in practice, both robustness and constraint satisfaction are important. 
The recent robust constrained MDP (RCMDP) framework addresses this dual need by aiming to minimize the worst-case cost while robustly satisfying the constraints.
Despite the significant theoretical progress made in CMDPs and RMDPs (see \cref{sec:related work}), theoretical results on RCMDPs are currently scarce.
Even in the tabular setting, where the state and action spaces are finite, there exists no algorithm with guarantees to find a near-optimal policy in an RCMDP.

\looseness=-1
The difficulty of RCMDPs arises from the challenging optimization process, which simultaneously considers robustness and constraints. 
The dynamic programming (DP) approach, popular in unconstrained RMDPs, is unsuitable for constrained settings where Bellman's principle of optimality can be violated \citep{haviv1996constrained,bellman1957dynamic}.
Similarly, the linear programming (LP) approach, commonly used for CMDPs, is inadequate due to the nonconvexity of the robust formulation \citep{iyengar2005robust,grand2022convex}.
Consequently, the policy gradient method with the Lagrangian formulation is the primary remaining option \citep{russel2020robust,wang2022robust}. 
The Lagrangian formulation approximates the RCMDP problem $\min_\pi \brace*{f(\pi) \given h(\pi) \leq 0}$ by $\max_{\lambda \geq 0} \min_\pi f(\pi) + \lambda h(\pi)$, where $f(\pi)$ and $h(\pi)$ represent the worst-case cumulative cost---known as the (cost) return\footnote{We commonly use the term \emph{return} to refer specifically to the objective cost return. When discussing a return value in the context of RCMDP's constraints, we refer to it as the \emph{constraint return}.}---and the worst-case constraint violation of a policy $\pi$, respectively.
There have been a few attempts to provide theoretical guarantees for the Lagrangian approach \citep{wang2022robust,zhang2024distributionally}; however, no existing studies offer rigorous and satisfactory guarantees that the max-min problem yields the same solution as the original RCMDP problem.
This leaves us with a fundamental question:
\begin{center}
\emph{How can we identify near-optimal policies in an RCMDP?}
\end{center}

\looseness=-1
We address this question by presenting three key contributions, which are summarized as follows:

\paragraph{Gradient conflict in the Lagrangian formulation (\cref{sec:multi-robust}).}
We first show that solving the Lagrangian formulation is inherently difficult, even when its max-min problem can yield an optimal policy. 
Given the limitations of DP and LP approaches as discussed, the policy gradient method might seem like a viable alternative.
However, our \cref{theorem:non-gradient-dominance} reveals that policy gradient methods can get trapped in a local minimum during the inner minimization of the Lagrangian formulation.
This occurs when the gradients, $\nabla f(\pi)$ and $\nabla h(\pi)$, conflict with each other, causing their sum $\nabla f(\pi) + \lambda \nabla h(\pi)$ to cancel out, even when the policy $\pi$ is not optimal.
Consequently, the Lagrangian approach for RCMDPs may not reliably lead to a near-optimal policy.

\paragraph{Epigraph form of RCMDP (\cref{sec:alternative form}).}
\looseness=-1
We then demonstrate that the \emph{epigraph form}, commonly used in constrained optimization literature \citep{boyd2004convex,beyer2007robust,rahimian2019distributionally}, entirely circumvents the challenges associated with the Lagrangian formulation.
The epigraph form transforms the RCMDP problem into $\min_{y} \brace*{y \given \min_{\pi} \max\brace*{f(\pi) - y, h(\pi)} \leq 0}$, introducing an auxiliary minimization problem of $\min_{\pi} \max\brace*{f(\pi) - y, h(\pi)}$ and minimizing its threshold variable $y$.
Unlike the Lagrangian approach, which necessitates summing $\nabla f(\pi)$ and $\nabla h(\pi)$, policy gradient methods for the auxiliary problem update the policy using either $\nabla f(\pi)$ or $\nabla h(\pi)$, thanks to the maximum operator in the problem. 
As a result, the epigraph form avoids the problem of conflicting gradient sums, preventing policy gradient methods from getting stuck in suboptimal minima (\cref{theorem: Phi gradient dominance}).

\looseness=-1
\paragraph{A new RCMDP algorithm (\cref{sec:double-loop}).}
Finally, we propose an RCMDP algorithm called \textbf{Epi}graph \textbf{R}obust \textbf{C}onstrained \textbf{P}olicy \textbf{G}radient \textbf{S}earch (\textbf{\proposal}, pronounced as ``Epic-P-G-S'').
The algorithm employs a double-loop structure:
the inner loop verifies the feasibility of the threshold variable $y$ by performing policy gradients on the auxiliary problem, while the outer loop employs binary search to determine the minimal feasible $y$.
\proposal is guaranteed to find an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy\footnote{The definition of an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy is provided in \cref{def:eps-optimal-policy}} with $\tiO(\varepsilon^{-4})$ robust policy evaluations (\cref{corollary:DLPG-epigraph}), where $\tiO(\cdot)$ represents the conventional big-O notation excluding polylogarithmic terms.
Since RCMDP generalizes plain MDP, CMDP, and RMDP, our \proposal is applicable to all these types of MDPs, ensuring a near-optimal policy for each. 
\cref{tb:approach-summary} compares existing approaches in various MDP settings.

\looseness=-1
Notably, \proposal does not rely on the rectangularity assumption of the uncertainty set (see, e.g., \citet{iyengar2005robust}), which often leads to overly conservative policies \citep{goyal2023robust}.
\proposal remains effective as long as policy evaluation under the worst-case environment is possible (see \cref{assumption:uncertainty set,assumption:eval-oracle,assumption:grad-approx}), making it applicable to a wide range of settings.
Additionally, \proposal converges in a last-iterate sense. 
Many Lagrangian-based algorithms for CMDPs guarantee performance for the average of past policies \citep{miryoosefi2019reinforcement,chen2021primal,li2021faster,liu2021learning}, but they encounter difficulties in scenarios where policy averaging is impractical, such as in deep RL applications. In contrast, \proposal does not require policy averaging and ensures that the final policy is near-optimal (\cref{corollary:DLPG-epigraph}). We discuss the limitations and potential future directions of our approach in \cref{sec:conclusion}. 




\section{Related Work}

\looseness=-1
This section reviews existing approaches for RCMDPs.
Additional related work on CMDPs and RMDPs can be found in \cref{sec:related work}.

\looseness=-1
\citet{russel2020robust,mankowitz2020robust} proposed heuristic algorithms for RCMDPs, but their approaches lack theoretical guarantees for convergence to near-optimal policies. 
\citet{wang2022robust} introduced a Lagrangian approach with convergence guarantee to a stationary point. 
However, they do not ensure the optimality of this stationary point. 
Moreover, their method is heavily dependent on the restrictive $R$-contamination set assumption \citep{du2018robust,wang2021online,wang2022policy}.

\looseness=-1
\citet{sun2024constrained} applied a trust-region method to RCMDPs. The policy is updated to remain sufficiently similar to the previous one, ensuring that performance and constraint adherence do not degrade, even in the face of environmental uncertainty. However, while they ensure that each policy update step maintains performance, convergence to a near-optimal policy is not guaranteed.

\looseness=-1
\citet{ghoshsample} employed a penalty approach which considers the optimization problem of the form $\min_{\pi} f(\pi) + \lambda \max \brace{h(\pi), 0}$, where $f$ and $h$ are defined in \cref{sec:intro}. 
While this approach can yield a near-optimal policy for a sufficiently large value of $\lambda > 0$, the author does not provide a concrete optimization method for the minimization and instead assumes the availability of an oracle to solve it.
As we will demonstrate in \cref{sec:multi-robust}, this minimization is intrinsically difficult, making the practical implementation of such an oracle challenging.

\looseness=-1
Finally, \citet{zhang2024distributionally} tackled RCMDPs using the policy-mixing technique \citep{miryoosefi2019reinforcement,le2019batch}. In this technique, a policy is sampled from a finite set of deterministic policies according to a sampling distribution at the start of each episode, and it remains fixed throughout the episode. 
However, even if a good sampling distribution is determined, there is no guarantee that the resulting expected policy will be optimal due to the non-convexity of the return function with respect to policies \citep{agarwal2021theory}. 
We discuss the limitations of the policy-mixing technique in \cref{sec:mixing-policy-notes}.
Additionally, \citet{zhang2024distributionally} assume an $R$-contamination uncertainty set, limiting its applicability similarly to the work of \citet{wang2022robust}.

\looseness=-1
Although the RCMDP problem remains unsolved, the control theory community has long studied the computation of safe controllers under environmental uncertainties. Notable methods include robust model predictive control \citep{bemporad2007robust} and $H_\infty$ optimal control \citep{anderson2019system,zames1981feedback,doyle1982analysis}. These approaches are specifically tailored for a specialized class of MDPs, known as the linear quadratic regulator (LQR, \citet{du2021bilinear}). However, because LQR and tabular MDPs operate within distinct frameworks, these control methods are unsuitable for tabular RCMDPs.
Given that most modern reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms, such as DQN \citep{mnih2015human}, are based on the tabular MDP framework, our results bridge the gap between the RL and control theory communities, laying the foundation for the development of reliable RL applications in the future.



\section{Preliminary}

\looseness=-1
We use the shorthand $\R_+ \df [0, \infty)$.
Without loss of generality, every finite set is assumed to be a subset of integers.
The set of probability distributions over $\cS$ is denoted by $\probsplx(\cS)$.
For two integers $a \leq b$, we define $\bbrack{a,b}\df \brace*{a,\dots, b}$.
If $a > b$, $\bbrack{a, b} \df \emptyset$.
For a vector $x \in \R^N$, its $n$-th element is denoted by $x_n$ or $x(n)$, and we use the convention that $\|x\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_i{x_i^2}}$ and $\|x\|_\infty=\max_i\abs{x_i}$.
For two vectors $x, y \in \R^N$, we denote $\langle x, y\rangle = \sum_i x_i y_i$.
We let $\bzero \df \paren{0, \ldots, 0}^\top$ and $\bone \df \paren{1, \ldots, 1}^\top$, with their dimensions being clear from the context.
All scalar operations and inequalities should be understood point-wise when applied to vectors and functions.
Given a finite set $\cS$, we often treat a function $f: \cS \to \R $ as a vector $f \in \R^\cS$. 
Both notations, $f: \cS \to \R$ and $f \in \R^\cS$, are used depending on notational convenience.
Finally, $\partial f(x)$ and $\nabla f(x)$ denote the (Fr\'{e}chet) subgradient and gradient of $f: \cX \to \R$ at a point $x$, respectively. 
Their formal definitions are deferred to \cref{def:subgradient}.

\subsection{Constrained Markov Decision Process}

\looseness=-1
Let $N \in \Z_{\geq 0}$ be the number of constraints.
An infinite-horizon discounted constrained MDP (CMDP) is defined as a tuple $(\S, \A, \gamma, P, \cC, b, \mu)$, where
$\S$ denotes the finite state space with size $\aS$,
$\A$ denotes the finite action space with size $\aA$,
$\gamma \in (0, 1)$ denotes the discounted factor,
and $\mu \in \probsplx(\S) $ denotes the initial state distribution.
For notational brevity, let $H\df (1-\gamma)^{-1}$ denotes the effective horizon.
Further, $b \df (b_1, \dots, b_N)\in [0, H]^N$ denotes the constraint threshold vector, where $b_n$ is the threshold scalar for the $n$-th constraint,
$\cC\df\brace*{c_n}_{n\in \bbrack{0,N}}$ denotes the set of cost functions, where
$c_n: \SA \to [0, 1]$ denotes the $n$-th cost function and $c_n(s, a)$ denotes the $n$-th cost when taking an action $a$ at a state $s$.
$c_0$ is for the objective to optimize and $\brace{c_1, \dots, c_N}$ are for the constraints. 
$P: \SA \to \probsplx(\S)$ denotes the transition probability kernel, 
which can be interpreted as the environment with which the agent interacts. $P\paren{s' \given s, a}$ denotes the state transition probability to a new state $s'$ from a state $s$ when taking an action $a$.

\subsection{Policy and Value Functions}
\looseness=-1
A (Markovian stationary) policy is defined as $\pi \in \R^{\SA}$ such that $\pi \paren{s, \cdot} \in \probsplx(\A)$ for any $s \in \S$. $\pi(s, a)$ denotes the probability of taking an action $a$ at state $s$.
The set of all the policies is denoted as $\Pi$, which corresponds to the \emph{direct parameterization} policy class presented in \citet{agarwal2021theory}.
Although non-Markovian policies can yield better performance in general RMDP problems \citep{wiesemann2013robust}, for simplicity, we focus on Markovian stationary policies in this paper. 
With an abuse of notation, for two functions $\pi, g \in \R^{\SA}$, we denote $\langle \pi, g\rangle = \sum_{s, a \in \SA} \pi(s, a) g(s, a)$.

\looseness=-1
For a policy $\pi$ and transition kernel $P$, let $\occ{\pi}_P: \S \to \R_+$ denote the occupancy measure of $\pi$ under $P$. 
$\occ{\pi}_P(s)$ represents the expected discounted number of times $\pi$ visits state $s$ under $P$, such that
$
\occ{\pi}_P(s) = 
(1-\gamma)
\E\brack*{
\sum_{h=0}^\infty
\gamma^{h}
\mathbbm{1}\brace*{s_h=s} \given s_0 \sim \mu, \pi, P}
$.
Here, the notation means that the expectation is taken over all possible trajectories, where $a_h \sim \pi \paren{s_h, \cdot}$ and $s_{h+1} \sim P \paren{\cdot \given s_h, a_h}$.

\looseness=-1
For $\pi \in \R^{\SA}$ and $n$-th cost $c_n$, let $\qf{\pi}_{n, P}: \SA \to \R$ be the action-value function such that 
\footnote{We do not restrict the domain of the value function to $\Pi$. This allows the policy gradient $\nabla J_{n, P}(\pi)$ to be well-defined for any $\pi \in \Pi$, as demonstrated in \cref{lemma:policy gradient}.}
$$
\qf{\pi}_{n, P}(s, a) = c_n(s, a) + \gamma \sum_{s' \in \S}P\paren*{s'\given s, a}\sum_{a' \in \A}\pi(s', a')\qf{\pi}_{n, P}(s', a')\quad \forall (s, a) \in \SA\;.
$$
\looseness=-1
Let $\vf{\pi}_{n, P} : \S \to \R$ be the state-value function such that $\vf{\pi}_{n, P} (s) = \sum_{a \in \A} \pi(s, a)\qf{\pi}_{n, P}(s, a)$ for any $s \in \S$.
If $\pi \in \Pi$, $\vf{\pi}_{n, P}(s)$ represents the expected cumulative $n$-th cost of $\pi$ under $P$ with an initial state $s$.
We denote the (cost) return function as $J_{n, P}(\pi) \df \sum_{s, a \in \SA} \mu(s) \vf{\pi}_{n, P}(s)$.

\paragraph{Policy gradient method.}
\looseness=-1
For a problem $\min_{\pi \in \Pi} f(\pi)$ where $f: \Pi \to \R$ is differentiable at a policy $\pi \in \Pi$, policy gradient methods with direct parameterization update $\pi$ to a new policy $\pi'$ as follows \citep{agarwal2021theory}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Psi-pol-grad}
\begin{aligned}
\pi' \df \proj_{\Pi}\paren*{\pi - \alpha \nabla f(\pi)}
\numeq{=}{a} \argmin_{\pi' \in \Pi}
\left\langle 
\nabla f(\pi), \pi' - \pi
\right\rangle + \frac{1}{2\alpha} \norm*{\pi' - \pi}^2_2\;,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\looseness=-1
where $\alpha > 0$ is the learning rate and $\proj_{\Pi}$ denotes the Euclidean projection operator onto $\Pi$.
The equality (a) is a standard result (see, e.g., \citet{parikh2014proximal}). 
Note that when $\widebar{\pi} \in \Pi$ is a stationary point, meaning it satisfies $\min_{\pi \in \Pi}\langle \pi - \widebar{\pi}, \nabla f(\barpi) \rangle = 0$, \cref{eq:Psi-pol-grad} outputs the same $\widebar{\pi}$.

\looseness=-1
The following lemma is a well-known result that provides the gradient of the return function for the direct parameterization policy class $\Pi$ (e.g., \citet{bhandari2024global}). 
\begin{lemma}[Policy gradient theorem]\label{lemma:policy gradient}
For any $\pi \in \Pi$, transition kernel $P:\SA \to \probsplx(\S)$, and $n \in \zeroN$, the gradient is given by
$
\paren*{\nabla J_{n, P}(\pi)}(s, a) = 
H\occ{\pi}_{P}(s) \qf{\pi}_{n, P}(s, a) \quad\forall (s, a) \in \SA\;.
$
\end{lemma}

\subsection{Robust Constrained Markov Decision Process}

\looseness=-1
An infinite-horizon discounted robust constrained MDP (RCMDP) is defined as a tuple $(\S, \A, \gamma, \U, \cC, b, \mu)$, where $\U$ is a compact set of transition kernels, known as the uncertainty set. 

\looseness=-1
For each $n \in \zeroN$, let $J_{n, \U} (\pi) \df \max_{P \in \U} J_{n, P}(\pi)$ denote the worst-case (cost) return function, which represents the $n$-th return of $\pi$ under the most adversarial environment within $\U$.

\looseness=-1
Let $\Pisafe \df \brace*{\pi \in \Pi \given \max_{n \in \oneN} J_{n, \U}(\pi) - b_n \leq 0}$ be the non-empty set of all the feasible policies.
An optimal policy in an RCMDP minimizes the robust objective return while satisfying all the constraints. 
We denote an optimal policy as $\pi^\star$ and its objective return as $\heartsuit$ such that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RCMDP problem}
    \pi^\star \in \argmin_{\pi \in \Pisafe} J_{0,\U}(\pi) \; \text{ and } \; \heartsuit \df J_{0, \U}(\pi^\star) \;.
\end{equation}

\looseness=-1
We define a near-optimal policy in the following sense\footnote{
To simplify analysis and notation, we allow an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy to violate constraints by up to $\varepsilon$. 
Designing a near-optimal policy that strictly adheres to the constraints is straightforward by using a slightly stricter threshold $b' \df b - \varepsilon$ and assuming the existence of a feasible policy under the new threshold $b'$.
}.
\begin{definition}[$\varepsilon$-optimality]\label{def:eps-optimal-policy}
\looseness=-1
A policy $\pi \in \Pi$ is $\varepsilon$-optimal if it satisfies
$J_{0, \U}(\pi) - \heartsuit \leq \varepsilon$
and
$\max_{n \in \oneN}J_{n, \U}(\pi) - b_n \leq \varepsilon$. 
\end{definition}


\section{Challenges of Lagrangian Formulation}


\looseness=-1
To motivate our formulations and algorithms presented in subsequent sections, this section illustrates the limitations of using the conventional Lagrangian formulation for RCMDPs. 
By introducing Lagrangian multipliers $\lambda\df \paren{\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N} \in \R^N_+$, the optimization problem of RCMDP is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RCMDP-problem}
(\textbf{RCMDP})\quad \heartsuit =
\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \max_{\lambda \in \R^N_+} J_{0, \U}(\pi) + \sum_{n =1}^N\lambda_n \paren*{J_{n, \U}(\pi) - b_n} \;.
\end{equation}

\looseness=-1
As this min-max problem is hard to solve, \citet{russel2020robust,mankowitz2020robust,wang2022robust} swap the min-max and consider the following Lagrangian relaxed formulation \footnote{This is relaxed since $\clubsuit \leq \heartsuit$ holds due to the min-max inequality \citep{boyd2004convex}.}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Lagrangian-problem}
\begin{aligned}
(\textbf{Lagrange})\quad 
\clubsuit \df
\max_{\lambda \in \R^N_+} 
\min_{\pi \in \Pi} 
\Lag{\lambda}{\pi}\;
\text{ where }\;
\Lag{\lambda}{\pi} \df 
J_{0, \U}(\pi) + \sum_{n=1}^N\lambda_n \paren*{J_{n, \U}(\pi) - b_n}\;.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
\looseness=-1
To identify an optimal policy $\pi^\star$ using \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem}, two key questions must be addressed: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[$\mathrm{(i)}$] Does \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem} yield $\pi^\star$ as its solution?
    \item[$\mathrm{(ii)}$] Is it tractable to identify a pair of $(\lambda, \pi)$ that solves \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem}?
\end{enumerate}
However, answering these questions affirmatively is challenging due to the following issues:

\paragraph{Strong duality challenge $(\mathrm{i})$.}
\looseness=-1
Deriving $\pi^\star$ from \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem} hinges on establishing strong duality, i.e., $\heartsuit = \clubsuit$. 
Once strong duality is confirmed, solving \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem} can indeed yield $\pi^\star$ since $\pi^\star \in \argmin_{\pi \in \Pi} L_{\lambda^\star}(\pi)$, where $\lambda^\star \in \argmax_{\lambda \in \R^N_+} \min_{\pi \in \Pi}  \Lag{\lambda}{\pi}$  \citep{boyd2004convex}.

\looseness=-1
In the CMDP setting where $\U = \brace{P}$, a well-established approach to proving strong duality is to reformulate \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem} as $\max_{\lambda \in \R^N_+} \min_{d \in \cD_P} \langle c_0 + \lambda \paren*{c_1 - b_1 / H}, d\rangle$, where $\cD_P$ denotes the set of state-action occupancy measures in the environment $P$
\citep{altman1999constrained,paternain2019constrained,paternain2022safe}.
Since the set $\cD_P$ is convex \citep{borkar1988convex}, with a mild assumption of the existence of a strictly feasible policy, Sion's minimax theorem immediately establishes strong duality.

\looseness=-1
However, \textbf{Lemma 1} in \citet{wang2022robust} demonstrated that this strategy cannot be directly applied to RCMDPs. When robustness is incorporated, the set of occupancy measures can become non-convex, hindering the application of the minimax theorem.
Consequently, it is challenging to guarantee that \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem} will yield $\pi^\star$.

\paragraph{Gradient conflict challenge $(\mathrm{ii})$.}
\looseness=-1
Unfortunately, even if strong duality holds and $\pi^\star$ can be found by $\pi^\star \in \argmin_{\pi \in \Pi} L_{\lambda^\star}(\pi)$, solving this minimization remains challenging.
Since the sum of robust returns in $\Lag{\lambda^\star}{\pi}$ excludes the use of DP and convex-optimization approaches \citep{iyengar2005robust,altman1999constrained,grand2022convex}, the policy gradient method such as \cref{eq:Psi-pol-grad} is the primary remaining option to solve $\min_{\pi \in \Pi} L_{\lambda^\star}(\pi)$.
However, the following \cref{theorem:non-gradient-dominance} shows that policy gradient methods can be trapped in a local minimum that does not solve $\min_{\pi\in \Pi} \Lag{\lambda^\star}{\pi}$:
\begin{theorem}\label{theorem:non-gradient-dominance}
\looseness=-1
For any $\gamma \in (0, 1)$, there exist a $\widebar{\lambda} > 0$, a policy $\barpi \in \Pi$ and an RCMDP with $\mu> \bzero$ satisfying the following condition: There exists a positive constant $R > 0$ such that, for any $b_1 \in \R$, 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:non-grad-dom}
\Lag{\widebar{{\lambda}}}{\barpi} < \Lag{\widebar{{\lambda}}}{\pi}\;\; \forall \pi \in \brace*{\pi \in \Pi \given \norm*{\pi - \barpi}_2 \leq R,\; \pi \neq \barpi}\;\;
\text{ but }\;\; 
\Lag{\widebar{\lambda}}{\barpi} - \min_{\pi \in \Pi}\Lag{\widebar{\lambda}}{\pi} \geq \frac{3 \gamma H}{16}\;.
\end{equation}
Moreover, there exists a $b_1 \in (0, H)$ where $\widebar{\lambda}$ satisfies $\widebar{\lambda} \in \argmax_{\lambda \in \R^N_+} \min_{\pi \in \Pi}  \Lag{\lambda}{\pi}$.
\end{theorem}

\looseness=-1
The detailed proof is deferred to \cref{subsec:proof-of-non-gradient-dominance}. Essentially, the proof constructs a simple RCMDP where \emph{the policy gradients for the objective and the constraint are in conflict}.

\begin{example}\label{example:RCMDP}
\looseness=-1
Consider the RCMDP with $\U=\brace{P_1, P_2}$ presented in \cref{fig:non-grad-CMDP} with $\delta = 0$ and set $\lambda =1$ for simplicity. 
Let ${\color{blue}\pi_1}$ and ${\color{red}\pi_2}$ be policies that select ${\color{blue}a_1}$ and ${\color{red}a_2}$ for all states, respectively. 
For both policies, the objective worst-case is $P_1$ and the constraint worst-case is $P_2$ (see \cref{subsec:proof-of-non-gradient-dominance}).
Hence, switching from policy ${\color{red}\pi_2}$ to taking action ${\color{blue}a_1}$ decreases the objective return under $P_1$ but increases the constraint return under $P_2$.
This conflict causes the gradients of ${\color{red}\pi_2}$ for the objective $(\nabla J_{0, P_1}({\color{red}\pi_2}))$ and for the constraint $(\nabla J_{1, P_2}({\color{red}\pi_2}))$ to sum to a constant vector, i.e., 
\begin{equation*}
\paren*{\nabla \Lagnot{1}({\color{red}\pi_2})}(s, \cdot) = \paren*{\nabla J_{0, P_1}({\color{red}\pi_2}) + \nabla J_{1, P_2}({\color{red}\pi_2})}(s, \cdot) = \mathrm{constant} \cdot \bone\quad \forall s \in \S\;,
\end{equation*}
showing that ${\color{red}\pi_2}$ is a stationary point. 
However, ${\color{red}\pi_2}$ cannot solve $\min_{\pi \in \Pi} L_{1}(\pi)$ because ${\color{blue} \pi_1}$ would clearly result in a smaller $L_{1}(\pi)$.
This stationary point becomes a strict local minimum with a positive $\delta > 0$, where ${\color{red} \pi_2}$ slightly prefers ${\color{red} a_2}$ over ${\color{blue} a_1}$ (see \cref{subsec:proof-of-non-gradient-dominance} for details).

\looseness=-1
\cref{fig:grad-conflict-graph} computationally illustrates this negative result by plotting the landscape of $\Lag{1}{\pi}$ in the RCMDP example across all possible policies for $(\gamma, \delta) = (0.4, 0.09)$. 
We set $b_1 = 0$ as $b_1$ does not influence the landscape of $\Lag{1}{\pi}$. 
In this example, ${\color{red}\pi_2}$ becomes a local minimum that attracts the policy gradient but fails to solve $\min_{\pi \in \Pi}\Lag{1}{\pi}$, as ${\color{blue}\pi_1}$ achieves $\Lag{\lambda}{{\color{blue}\pi_1}} < \Lag{\lambda}{{\color{red}\pi_2}}$.
\end{example}

\looseness=-1
This negative result underscores the difficulty of RCMDPs in contrast to CMDPs.
In CMDP, where $\U=\brace{P}$, for any $\lambda\in \R$, the problem $\min_{\pi\in \Pi} \Lag{\lambda}{\pi}$ reduces to solving a standard MDP with a cost function $c_0 + \lambda c_1$ in the environment $P$.
As a result, the well-known policy gradient analysis by \citet{agarwal2021theory} guarantees that the policy gradient method (\cref{eq:Psi-pol-grad}) converges to stationary points that solve $\min_{\pi\in \Pi} \Lag{\lambda}{\pi}$.
However, the negative result presented above reveals that there exist RCMDPs where \cref{eq:Psi-pol-grad} fails by encountering a local minimum.



\section{Alternative RCMDP Formulation}

\looseness=-1
This section introduces the epigraph form of RCMDP, which overcomes the challenges discussed in \cref{sec:multi-robust}.
For any constrained optimization problem of the form $\min_{x}\brace*{f(x) \given h(x) \leq 0}$ with $x \in \R^n$ and $f, h: \R^n \to \R$, its epigraph form is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:epigraph-optimize}
\min_{x, y} y \;\suchthat\; f(x) \leq y \; \text{ and }\; h(x) \leq 0
\end{equation}
with variables $x \in \R^n$ and $y \in \R$.
It is well-known that $(x, y)$ is optimal for \cref{eq:epigraph-optimize} if and only if $x$ is optimal for the original problem and $y = f(x)$ (see, e.g., \citet{boyd2004convex}).

\subsection{Epigraph Form of RCMDP}\label{subsec:epigraph-formulation}

\looseness=-1
Based on \cref{eq:epigraph-optimize}, the RCMDP problem can be equivalently written as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:epigraph-pre}
\heartsuit = 
\min_{b_0 \in [0, H], \pi\in \Pi} b_0 \;\suchthat\; 
\epivio{b_0}{\pi} \leq 0\;.
\end{equation}
\looseness=-1
where we define $\epivionot{b_0}: \R^{\SA} \to \R$ such that
$\epivio{b_0}{\pi} = \max_{n \in \zeroN} J_{n, \cU}(\pi) - b_n$.
$\epivio{b_0}{\pi}$ represents the maximum violation of the constraints $\max_{\bbrack{1,N}} J_{n, \mathcal{U}}(\pi) - b_n \leq 0$ of the policy $\pi$, with the additional constraint $J_{0, \mathcal{U}}(\pi) - b_0 \leq 0$.
By moving $\min_{\pi \in \Pi}$ to the constraint, \cref{eq:epigraph-pre} can be further transformed as follows:
\begin{theorem}\label{theorem:epigraph is RCMDP-zero}
\looseness=-1
Let $\epivios{b_0} \df \min_{\pi \in \Pi}\epivio{b_0}{\pi}$. Then,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:epigraph problem-zero}
({\normalfont\textbf{Epigraph Form}})\quad \heartsuit = \min_{b_0 \in [0, H]} b_0 \; \text{ such that }\; \epivios{b_0} \leq 0 \;.
\end{equation}
\looseness=-1
Furthermore, if $b_0=\heartsuit$, any policy $\pi \in \argmin_{\pi \in \Pi}\epivio{b_0}{\pi}$ is optimal.
\end{theorem}
\begin{wrapfigure}[14]{r}{0.3\textwidth}
  \begin{center}
    \vspace*{-6mm}
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{figs/concept.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \vspace*{-4mm}
  \caption{\looseness=-1 \small 
  Behavior of $\epivios{b_0}$ in the RCMDP from \cref{example:RCMDP} with $(\gamma, \delta) = (0.1, 0)$ and $b_1 = 2/3$. Adjusting $b_0$ by increasing it when $\epivios{b_0} \geq 0$ and decreasing it otherwise leads to convergence at $b_0 = \heartsuit$, where $\epivios{b_0} = 0$.
}
  \label{fig:algo-concept}
\end{wrapfigure}
\looseness=-1
The proof is provided in \cref{subsec:proof-epigraph is RCMDP-zero}.
Instead of \cref{eq:epigraph-pre}, we call \cref{eq:epigraph problem-zero} the epigraph form of RCMDP.
Intuitively, the epigraph form seeks the smallest threshold value $b_0$ such that there exists a feasible policy $\pi \in \Pisafe$ that also satisfies $J_{0, \cU}(\pi) \leq b_0$.
If $\epivios{b_0} \leq 0$, such a feasible policy exists; if $\epivios{b_0} > 0$, no feasible policy $\pi \in \Pisafe$ achieves $J_{0, \cU}(\pi) \leq b_0$.

\looseness=-1
Since the epigraph form provides $\heartsuit$ and $\pi^\star$, the remaining question is whether \cref{eq:epigraph problem-zero} can be easily solved. 
To address this, the following properties of $\epivios{b_0}$ are useful:
\begin{lemma}\label{lemma: phi-properties}
$\epivios{b_0}$ is monotonically decreasing in $b_0$ and $\epivios{\heartsuit}=0$.
\end{lemma}
The proof is provided in \cref{subsec:proof of phi-properties}.
Given the monotonicity of $\epivios{b_0}$, if $\epivios{b_0}$ can be efficiently computed, a line search over $b_0 \in [0, H]$ will readily yield $b_0 \approx \heartsuit$. 
Increase $b_0$ if $\epivios{b_0} \geq 0$, and decrease it if $\epivios{b_0} \leq 0$, as illustrated in \cref{fig:algo-concept}.

\subsection{On Computing \texorpdfstring{$\epivios{b_0}$}{Lg}}\label{subsec:epigraph-grad-dominance}

\looseness=-1
To compute $\epivios{b_0}$, we need to solve the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:min-Phi}
\epivios{b_0} = \min_{\pi \in \Pi}\epivio{b_0}{\pi} = \min_{\pi \in \Pi}
\max_{n \in \zeroN} \max_{P \in \U} J_{n, P}(\pi) - b_n\;.
\end{equation}
Clearly, the right-hand side of \cref{eq:min-Phi} is an RMDP problem with added robustness over the set of modified cost functions $\cC_{b_0} \df \brace*{c_n - b_n / H}_{n \in \zeroN}$. 
Since $\cC_{b_0}$ is neither $s$-rectangular nor $(s, a)$-rectangular (see, e.g., \citet{iyengar2005robust}), DP approaches are inadequate for this problem.
Therefore, we employ the policy gradient method to compute $\epivios{b_0}$ by assuming the following general structure on the uncertainty set:
\begin{assumption}\label{assumption:uncertainty set}
$\cU$ is either ($\mathrm{i}$) a finite set or ($\mathrm{ii}$) a compact set such that, for any $\pi \in \Pi$, $\nabla J_{n, P}(\pi)$ is continuous with respect to $P \in \U$.
\end{assumption}

\looseness=-1
Then, the subgradient $\partial \epivio{b_0}{\pi}$ is represented as follows, with the proof provided in \cref{subsec: proof of weak-convexity}:

\begin{lemma}\label{lemma:Phi-subgrad}
\looseness=-1
For any $b_0 \in 0$ and $\pi \in \Pi$, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:worst set}
\subgrad_{b_0}(\pi) \df 
\brace*{\nabla J_{n, P}(\pi) \given 
n, P \in \cW_{b_0}(\pi)
}
\;\text{ where }\;
\cW_{b_0}(\pi) \df \argmax_{n \in \zeroN, P \in \cU} J_{n, P}(\pi) - b_n\;.
\end{equation}
\looseness=-1
Let $\conv B$ denote the convex hull of a set $B \subset \R^{\SA}$.
Under \cref{assumption:uncertainty set}, for any $\pi \in \Pi$ and $b_0 \in \R$, the subgradient of $\epivio{b_0}{\cdot}$ at $\pi$ is given by
$
\partial \epivio{b_0}{\pi}= \conv \subgrad_{b_0}(\pi)\;.
$
\end{lemma}
\looseness=-1
Here, $\argmax$ returns the set $\cW_{b_0}(\pi)$, which contains all cost and environment pairs that achieve the worst-case for $\pi$, while $\subgrad_{b_0}(\pi)$ denotes the set of policy gradients corresponding to $\cW_{b_0}(\pi)$.
Roughly speaking, while $\partial \Lag{\lambda}{\pi}$ involves summing policy gradients from return functions across different environments (see \cref{example:RCMDP}), $\partial\epivio{b_0}{\pi}$ can focus on the policy gradient of a single worst-case environment by taking $\max_{n \in \zeroN}$.
Consequently, $\epivionot{b_0}$ avoids the sum of conflicting policy gradients, thereby circumventing the \textbf{gradient conflict challenge} discussed in \cref{sec:multi-robust}.

\looseness=-1
To formally guarantee that any stationary point of the policy gradient is globally optimal, we introduce the following assumption regarding the coverage of the initial distribution:
\begin{assumption}\label{assumption:init-dist}
The initial distribution $\mu \in \probsplx(\S)$ satisfies $\mu > \bzero$.    
\end{assumption}
Similar assumptions are used in policy gradient literature for MDPs \citep{agarwal2021theory}, CMDPs \citep{ying2022dual}, and RMDPs \citep{wang2023policy,li2022first}. 
Additionally, we remark that the Lagrangian approach performs poorly even under \cref{assumption:init-dist} as \cref{theorem:non-gradient-dominance} demonstrates.

\looseness=-1
\cref{assumption:init-dist} allows the epigraph form to enjoy the following \emph{gradient-dominance} property  (see, e.g., \citet{agarwal2021theory}), which ensures that any stationary point is globally optimal:
\begin{theorem}[Gradient dominance]\label{theorem: Phi gradient dominance}
\looseness=-1
Under \cref{assumption:Phi-oracle,assumption:uncertainty set}, for any $\pi \in \Pi$ and $b_0 \in \R$,
$$
\epivio{b_0}{\pi} - \min_{\pi' \in \Pi}\epivio{b_0}{\pi'} 
\leq 
D H \max_{\pi' \in \Pi}\left\langle \pi - \pi', \grad \right\rangle \quad \forall \grad \in \partial \epivio{b_0}{\pi}\;,
$$
where $D \df \max_{n, P \in \zeroN\times \cU}\norm*{\occ{\pi^\star_{n, P}}_{P} / \mu}_\infty$ with $\pi^\star_{n, P} \in \argmin_{\pi' \in \Pi}J_{n,P}(\pi')$.
\end{theorem}
\looseness=-1
The detailed proof can be found in \cref{subsec:proof of grad-dominance}. 
Our proof is similar to \textbf{Theorem 3.2} in \citet{wang2023policy}, but it is more rigorous and corrects a crucial error that can invalidate their result\footnote{For example, their proof around \textbf{Equation (32)} incorrectly bounds a positive value by a negative value.}. 
Moreover, while their proof is limited to cases where $\argmax_{P \in \U} J_{0, P}(\pi)$ is finite, ours is not.
We leverage Sion's minimax theorem \citep{sion1958general} for this refinement.



\section{Algorithm}


\looseness=-1
This section introduces a double-loop algorithm to solve the epigraph form (\cref{eq:epigraph problem-zero}) using a subroutine algorithm that approximately solves $\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \epivio{b_0}{\pi}$.
Throughout this section, we assume access to the following oracle that approximately evaluates the value of $\epivio{b_0}{\pi}$.
\begin{assumption}[Evaluation oracle]\label{assumption:eval-oracle}
\looseness=-1
We have an oracle $\sE: \R_+\times \Pi \to \R_+$ that takes a value $b_0 \geq 0$ and a policy $\pi \in \Pi$, and returns a value $\estvio \geq 0$ such that $\abs*{\estvio - \epivio{b_0}{\pi}} \leq \epsest$, where $\epsest \geq 0$ is an unknown value.
\end{assumption}
\looseness=-1
This assumption is mild and easy to meet in practice. 
For example, if the uncertainty set incorporates a structural assumption like $s$-rectangularity or $(s, a)$-rectangularity, this evaluation oracle can be efficiently implemented using robust DP methods \citep{iyengar2005robust,kumar2022efficient,kumar2024policy}.

\looseness=-1
Note that solving an RMDP is generally NP-hard when the uncertainty set satisfies only \cref{assumption:uncertainty set} \citep{wiesemann2013robust}. 
The hardness primarily stems from evaluating $J_{0, \U}(\pi)$.
\cref{assumption:eval-oracle} mitigates this hardness by abstracting the evaluation step, avoiding the need for additional structural assumptions on $\U$.

\begin{algorithm}[t!]
    \caption{Projected Policy Gradient Subroutine}\label{algo:Min-Phi}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \STATE \textbf{Input: } Threshold parameter $b_0 \geq 0$, learning rate $\alpha > 0$, evaluation oracle $\sE$ (see \cref{assumption:eval-oracle}), gradient estimator $\sG$ (see \cref{assumption:grad-approx}), and iteration length $T \in \N$
    \STATE Set an arbitrary initial policy $\pi^{(0)} \in \Pi$
    \FOR{$t = 0, \cdots, T-1$}
        \STATE Evaluate policy: $\estvio^{(t)} = \sE(b_0, \pi^{(t)})$
        \STATE Compute policy gradient: $\grad^{(t)} = \sG(b_0, \pi^{(t)})$
        \STATE Compute new policy: $\pi^{(t+1)} = \proj_{\Pi}(\pi^{(t)} - \alpha \grad^{(t)})$
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $\pi^{(t^\star)}$ where $t^\star \in \argmin_{t \in \bbrack{0,T-1}} \estvio^{(t)}$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t!]
\caption{\looseness=-1 Double-Loop Optimization with $\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \epivio{b_0}{\pi}$ Subroutine\\
(also referred to as \proposal when using \cref{algo:Min-Phi} as the subroutine)}\label{algo:double-loop}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \STATE \textbf{Input: }Evaluation oracle $\sE$ (see \cref{assumption:eval-oracle}), subroutine algorithm $\sA$ (see \cref{assumption:Phi-oracle}), and iteration length $K \in \N$
    \STATE Initialize the search space: $i^{(0)}=0 $ and $j^{(0)}=H$
    \FOR{$k = 0, \cdots, K-1$}
        \STATE Compute policy: $\pi^{(k)} = \sA(b_0^{(k)})\;$ where $\;b_0^{(k)} = (i^{(k)} + j^{(k)})/2$
        \STATE Evaluate policy: $\estvio^{(k)} = \sE(b_0^{(k)}, \pi^{(k)})$
        \STATE Compute $i^{(k+1)}$ and $j^{(k+1)}$ by \cref{eq:binary update}
    \ENDFOR
    \RETURN $\pi_{\mathrm{ret}}$ computed by $\sA(j^{(K)})$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Subroutine Algorithm to Solve \texorpdfstring{$\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \epivio{b_0}{\pi}$}{Lg}}\label{subsec:solve phi}
\looseness=-1
The subroutine algorithm updates policies via a policy gradient method. 
Starting from an arbitrary initial policy $\pi^{(0)} \in \Pi$, let $\pi^{(1)}, \dots, \pi^{(T)}$ be the updated policies where $T \in \N$ is the iteration length.

\looseness=-1
Recall that $\cG_{b_0}(\pi)$, defined in \cref{lemma:Phi-subgrad}, is a subset of the subgradient $\partial \epivio{b_0}{\pi}$.
We assume access to an oracle that can approximate a subgradient element in $\cG_{b_0}(\pi)$.
\begin{assumption}[Gradient estimator]\label{assumption:grad-approx}
\looseness=-1
Let $\epsgrd \in \R_+$ be an unknown value.
We have an oracle $\sG: \R_+\times \Pi \to \R^{\SA}$ that takes a value $b_0 \in \R_+$ and a policy $\pi \in \Pi$, and returns a vector $\grad \in \R^{\SA}$ such that 
$\min_{\grad'\in \cG_{b_0}(\pi^{(t)})}
\norm*{
\grad - \grad'
}_2 \leq \epsgrd
$.
\end{assumption}
Similar to \cref{assumption:eval-oracle}, this oracle can be efficiently implemented within uncertainty sets with certain structural assumptions \citep{kumar2024policy,wang2022policy}.

\looseness=-1
Using the gradient oracle $\sG$ and a learning rate $\alpha > 0$, we update the policy according to the following projected policy gradient, which is similar to \cref{eq:Psi-pol-grad}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proj-policy-grad}
\begin{aligned}
&\pi^{(t+1)}\in  
\argmin_{\pi \in \Pi}
\left\langle 
\grad^{(t)}, \pi - \pi^{(t)}
\right\rangle + \frac{1}{2\alpha} \norm*{\pi - \pi^{(t)}}^2_2
\; \text{ where }\; \grad^{(t)} = \sG(b_0, \pi^{(t)})\;.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\looseness=-1
We summarize the pseudocode of the policy gradient procedure in \cref{algo:Min-Phi}.
The following theorem demonstrates that \cref{algo:Min-Phi} finds a near-minimum point of $\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \epivio{b_0}{\pi}$.
\begin{theorem}\label{theorem:policy grad convergence}
Suppose \cref{assumption:uncertainty set,,assumption:init-dist,,assumption:eval-oracle,,assumption:grad-approx} hold.
Then, there exist problem-dependent constants $C_\sG, C_\alpha, C_T > 0$ that do not depend on $\varepsilon$ such that, when \cref{algo:Min-Phi} is run with $\alpha= C_\alpha \varepsilon^2$ and $T = C_T \varepsilon^{-4}$, if the gradient estimation is sufficiently accurate such that $\epsgrd = C_\sG \varepsilon^2$, \cref{algo:Min-Phi} returns a policy $\pi^{(t^\star)}$ satisfying
$$
\epivio{b_0}{\pi^{(t^\star)}} - \min_{\pi \in \Pi} \epivio{b_0}{\pi} \leq \varepsilon + 2\epsest\;.
$$
\end{theorem}
We provide the proof and the concrete values of $C_\sG, C_\alpha$ and $C_T$ in \cref{sec:policy-grad-convergence-proof}. 
\subsection{Binary Search with \texorpdfstring{$\min_{\pi \in \Pi} \epivio{b_0}{\pi}$}{Lg} Subroutine}\label{sec:find b0}

\looseness=-1
Our double-loop algorithm employs a binary search method to solve the epigraph form, supported by a subroutine algorithm that satisfies the following assumption.

\begin{assumption}[Subroutine algorithm]\label{assumption:Phi-oracle}
\looseness=-1
We have a subroutine algorithm $\sA: \R_+ \to \Pi$ that takes a value $b_0 \geq 0$ and returns a policy $\pi = \sA(b_0)$ such that $\epivio{b_0}{\pi} \leq \min_{\pi' \in \Pi}\epivio{b_0}{\pi'} + \epsopt$ with an unknown $\epsopt \geq 0$.
\end{assumption}
As we have shown in \cref{theorem:policy grad convergence}, \cref{assumption:Phi-oracle} can be realized by \cref{algo:Min-Phi}.

\looseness=-1
Let $K \in \N$ be the number of iterations of the binary search.
For each iteration $k$, let $[i^{(k)}, j^{(k)}] \subseteq [0, H]$ be the search space where $i^{(k)}  < j^{(k)}$.
We set $i^{(0)}=0$ and $j^{(0)} = H$.
We denote $b_0^{(k)} \df (i^{(k)} + j^{(k)})/2$.
Additionally, given $b_0^{(k)}$, we denote the returned policy from $\sA$ as $\pi^{(k)} \df \sA(b^{(k)}_0)$ and its value evaluated by $\sE$ as $\estvio^{(k)} \df \sE(b^{(k)}_0, \pi^{(k)})$.

\looseness=-1
Our binary search aims to identify the minimum $b_0$ such that $\epivios{b_0}=0$, as such $b_0$ satisfies $b_0=\heartsuit$.
Following the strategy illustrated in \cref{fig:algo-concept}, we increase $b_0^{(k)}$ if $\estvio^{(k)} > 0$; otherwise, we decrease it.
More concretely, our binary search iteratively narrows down the search space as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:binary update}
i^{(k+1)} \df 
\begin{cases}
b_0^{(k)} & \text{ if } \;\estvio^{(k)} > 0 \\
i^{(k)} & \text{ otherwise } 
\end{cases}
\quad\text{ and }\quad
j^{(k+1)} \df 
\begin{cases}
j^{(k)} & \text{ if } \; \estvio^{(k)} > 0 \\
b_0^{(k)} & \text{ otherwise } 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\looseness=-1
We summarize the pseudocode of the algorithm in \cref{algo:double-loop}.
The following \cref{theorem:binary-search-performance} ensures that \cref{algo:double-loop} returns a near-optimal policy.
We provide the proof in \cref{subsec:proof-of-binary-search}.
\begin{theorem}\label{theorem:binary-search-performance}
Suppose that \cref{algo:double-loop} is run with oracles $\sE$ and $\sA$ that satisfy \cref{assumption:eval-oracle,,assumption:Phi-oracle}.
Then, \cref{algo:double-loop} returns an $\tilde{\varepsilon}$-optimal policy, where $\tilde{\varepsilon} \df 2(\epsopt+\epsest)+2^{-K}H$.
\end{theorem}

\looseness=-1
We refer to \cref{algo:double-loop} with \cref{algo:Min-Phi} subroutine as 
\textbf{Epi}graph \textbf{R}obust \textbf{C}onstrained \textbf{P}olicy \textbf{G}radient \textbf{S}earch  (\textbf{\proposal}).
By applying \cref{theorem:policy grad convergence} to \cref{theorem:binary-search-performance}, the following corollary shows that \proposal finds an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy by making $\tiO(\varepsilon^{-4})$ queries to the oracles $\fE$ and $\fG$.

\begin{corollary}\label{corollary:DLPG-epigraph}
\looseness=-1
Assume \cref{assumption:uncertainty set,,assumption:init-dist} holds.
Suppose we have sufficiently accurate oracles $\sE$ and $\sG$ that satisfy \cref{assumption:eval-oracle} and \cref{assumption:grad-approx} with $\epsest \leq \varepsilon / 24$ and $\epsgrd \leq \varepsilon / 8$, respectively.
Set \cref{algo:Min-Phi} as the subroutine algorithm $\sA$ with parameters $(\sE, \sG, \alpha, T)$, where we set $\alpha = C_\alpha \varepsilon^2/ 64$, $T=C_T \varepsilon^{-4} / 4096$, using $C_\alpha$ and $C_T$ from \cref{theorem:policy grad convergence}.
Then, given inputs $\sE$ and $\sA$, \cref{algo:double-loop} returns an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy after $K=\lfloor \log \paren*{2 H\varepsilon^{-1}}\rfloor$ iteration.
\end{corollary}

\looseness=-1
Finally, we remark that \proposal converges in a \emph{last-iterate} sense. 
While many Lagrangian-based algorithms for CMDPs provide certain performance guarantees for the average of past policies \citep{miryoosefi2019reinforcement,chen2021primal,li2021faster,liu2021learning}, they become problematic in scenarios where policy averaging is impractical, such as in deep RL. 
In contrast, \cref{corollary:DLPG-epigraph} does not require policy averaging and ensures that the final policy output is near-optimal.

\looseness=-1
This section empirically compares our \proposal algorithm to a Lagrangian counterpart, which aims to solve the problem $\max_{\lambda \in \R^N_+} \min_{\pi \in \Pi} \Lag{\lambda}{\pi}$ in \cref{eq:Lagrangian-problem} by performing gradient ascent on $\lambda$ while using a policy gradient subroutine to solve $\min_{\pi \in \Pi}\Lag{\lambda}{\pi}$.
We refer to this Lagrangian-based algorithm as the ``Lagrangian Formulation Policy Gradient Search (\lagrange).''
\lagrange abstracts some existing Lagrangian-based algorithms for RCMDPs (e.g., \citep{russel2020robust,wang2022robust}).
The detailed implementation of \lagrange is provided in \cref{sec:experiment details}.

\looseness=-1
Since Lagrangian-based algorithms typically require averaging the optimization variables obtained during updates \citep{miryoosefi2019reinforcement,chen2021primal,li2021faster,liu2021learning}, we also report the performance of the averaged policies from \lagrange, where the $k$-th policy is set as $\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=0}^k \pi^{(k)}$.
We refer to this averaging algorithm as \lagrangeavg.
\looseness=-1
\cref{fig:RCMDP-experiment-double-loop} shows the objective return and constraint violation for algorithms averaged over 20 randomly generated simple RCMDPs with finite uncertainty sets. 
The details of the environmental setup are provided in \cref{sec:experiment details}.
For each $k$-th outer iteration, we report the objective return minus the return of the uniform policy, i.e., $J_{0, \cU}(\pi^{(k)}) - J_{0, \cU}(\pi_{\mathrm{unif}})$, where $\pi_{\mathrm{unif}}$ represents a uniform policy. 
This subtraction accounts for variations in the minimum return across different RCMDPs.

\paragraph{Results.}
\looseness=-1
The \textbf{left} and \textbf{middle} figures in \cref{fig:RCMDP-experiment-double-loop} demonstrate that \proposal rapidly converges to a policy that not only satisfies the constraints but also achieves a low objective return.
In contrast, \lagrange exhibits oscillatory returns and constraint violations.
Even when averaging its policies (\lagrangeavg), the objective return remains worse than \proposal and continues to exhibit constraint violations, indicating suboptimal performance.
Additionally, the \textbf{right} figure of \cref{fig:RCMDP-experiment-double-loop} reveals that any other algorithm does not find the feasible and low-return policy identified by \proposal ({\color{Orchid} \CIRCLE} in the figure).
These findings empirically validate that \proposal yields a near-optimal policy, contrasting with the conventional Lagrangian-based algorithm's inability to do so.


\section{Conclusion and Limitations}

\looseness=-1
In this work, we propose \proposal, the first algorithm guaranteed to find a near-optimal policy in an RCMDP (\cref{corollary:DLPG-epigraph}). 
At the core of \proposal is the use of the epigraph form for RCMDP.
Remarkably, the epigraph form produces the optimal policy $\pi^\star$ (\cref{subsec:epigraph-formulation}) and supports a policy gradient algorithm to find it (\cref{subsec:epigraph-grad-dominance}). 
These features effectively address the optimization challenges encountered in the conventional Lagrangian formulation (\cref{sec:multi-robust}).

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
\looseness=-1
A double-loop algorithm like \proposal is often impractical when the inner problem requires high computational cost \citep{lin2024single}.
Developing a single-loop algorithm based on the epigraph form is a promising direction for future research. 
To support future RCMDP studies, we discuss the potential difficulty in designing such an algorithm in \cref{sec:single-loop}.

\looseness=-1
Another area for future research involves improving iteration complexity. It is known that with a rectangularity assumption, the natural policy gradient method can find an $\varepsilon$-optimal policy in RMDP with $\tiO(\varepsilon^{-2})$ iterations \citet{li2022first}. Investigating whether our $\tiO(\varepsilon^{-4})$ complexity in RCMDP can be improved is a promising area for future research.

\looseness=-1
Finally, we leave for future work the exploration of removing the coverage assumption on the initial distribution (\cref{assumption:init-dist}). 
Although this assumption can be readily satisfied in robust policy design using simulators by assigning negligible initial probabilities to all states, it may violate the theoretical guarantees of the designed policy, as optimal policies in CMDP depend on the initial state distribution \citep{altman1999constrained}.
