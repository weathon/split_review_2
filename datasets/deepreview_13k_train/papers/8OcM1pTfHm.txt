\title{Is Free Self-Alignment Possible?}

\begin{abstract}
Aligning pretrained language models (LMs) is a complex and resource-intensive process, often requiring access to large amounts of ground-truth preference data and substantial compute. Are these costs necessary?
That is, \textit{it is possible to align using only inherent model knowledge and without additional training?} We tackle this challenge with $\SYSNAME$, a novel approach that uses (1) self-generated preference data and (2) representation editing to provide nearly cost-free alignment.
During inference, $\SYSNAME$ modifies LM representations to reduce undesirable and boost desirable components using subspaces identified via self-generated preference pairs. Our experiments reveal that this nearly cost-free procedure significantly narrows the gap between base pretrained and tuned models by an average of 31.6\%, observed across six datasets and three model architectures. Additionally, we explore the potential of using $\SYSNAME$ as a means of \emph{expediting} more expensive alignment procedures.  Our experiments show that $\SYSNAME$ improves DPO models tuned only using a small subset of ground-truth preference data. Lastly, we study the conditions under which improvement using $\SYSNAME$ is feasible, providing valuable insights into its effectiveness.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

 

Large language model (LMs) alignment involves the use of complex and expensive pipelines \cite{schulman2017proximal, ouyang2022training, rafailov2024direct}. Usually at least two critical components are needed: (1) collecting human preference data, and (2) modifying pretrained model weights to better align with these preferences. Some pipelines involve more complexity (e.g., RLHF trains a reward model on the human preference data and uses it for PPO-based model optimization). 
Such approaches face substantial scalability challenges: collecting human preference data is costly and time-intensive, and as model sizes increase, the computational requirements for fine-tuning are likely to become prohibitive. 

A prospective way to bypass the need for human preference data is to exploit knowledge \textbf{\emph{already contained}} in the pretrained model weights.
This idea is motivated by evidence suggesting that alignment merely reveals knowledge and capabilities acquired during pretraining \cite{zhou2024lima, lin2023unlocking}. 
This notion has led to a growing body of literature achieving impressive results using signal contained in pretrained models for fine-tuning \cite{franken2024self, wang2022self, NEURIPS2023_0764db11, sun2024principle}, largely or totally sidestepping human annotation.

Next, to achieve free alignment, we must additionally obviate the need for fine-tuning. 
Instead, we propose to replace it with a form of \textbf{\emph{representation editing}} that does not require computing gradients or even optimizing a proxy loss at all.
Existing representation editing approaches \cite{zou2310representation, wu2024reft, li2024inference} rely on access to ground truth data, which does not account for the unique challenges of using only signals from pretrained models. These signals are often noisier and more limited compared to human-annotated data \cite{bender2021dangers, bommasani2021opportunities, kenton2021alignment,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2102-02503}, necessitating a more tailored approach.% to effectively harness the inherent knowledge within the models.

This work puts together these two pieces to \textit{explore the feasibility of free self-alignment}. We align pretrained LMs to human preferences using only the knowledge from the model itself, without additional training or fine-tuning. We introduce $\SYSNAME$, a novel approach designed for this setting. Using the pretrained model's own generated preference pairs, $\SYSNAME$ identifies the subspaces within the model's embedding spaces that correspond to helpful and non-helpful responses. During inference, we surgically modify the model's embeddings by boosting the components from the helpful subspaces and neutralizing those from the non-helpful ones.

With this nearly cost-free procedure, we effectively narrow the performance gap between pretrained and aligned models by 31.6\% across three model architectures and six datasets. Additionally, we explore the potential of $\SYSNAME$ to expedite more expensive alignment processes. Our experimental results demonstrate that $\SYSNAME$ improves upon models trained using DPO \cite{rafailov2024direct} with only a small subset of ground-truth preference data. In summary, our contributions include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We introduce 
    $\SYSNAME$, a nearly cost-free approach that leverages preference data generated by the pretrained LM to modify its embeddings, aligning  outputs to human preferences.
    \item Our experiments show that $\SYSNAME$ significantly narrows the gap between the base model and its counterparts aligned with traditional expensive methods by 31.6\% across three model architectures and six datasets.
    \item We demonstrate that $\SYSNAME$ can \emph{expedite} more expensive methods like DPO by improving models trained with DPO using only a small subset of ground truth preference data, by 2.2\% on average.
    \item We demonstrate a simple method to possibly predict conditions when free self-alignment using $\SYSNAME$ is possible, as a function of the quality of self-generated preference pairs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Our work tackles alignment and sits at the intersection of self-generated synthetic data and efficient model editing. We give a (necessarily) compressed introduction to these areas.

\paragraph{LM Alignment.} The standard approach to aligning LMs with human values and preferences relies on human-annotated preference data. This data is used either to (i) train a reward function and subsequently fine-tune the LM to maximize this reward using reinforcement learning objectives, as in methods like RLHF \cite{ouyang2022training, christiano2017deep}, or (ii) optimize a proxy loss to maximize the margin between preferred and not preferred outputs, as in methods like DPO \cite{rafailov2024direct}. While these methods achieve remarkable performance, they are challenging to implement due to their complex pipelines, the high cost of computing resources, and the limited scalability of acquiring human-preference data.

\paragraph{Self-Improvement.} The difficulty of obtaining human-annotated data has led to significant efforts to bypass this requirement. Methods such as those proposed by \cite{wang2022self, sun2024principle, mcintosh2023google} use manually crafted seed prompts to generate high-quality synthetic datasets from pretrained LMs, which are then used for fine-tuning or training reward models. \cite{guo2024human} uses retrieval-augmented generation to remove reliance on manually designed prompts. Another approach, \cite{li2023self}, leverages instruction-tuned models to assist in generating synthetic datasets. The work most similar to our approach is \cite{franken2024self}, which emphasizes \textit{maximizing the use of knowledge from the pretrained model being aligned}. Our work takes this further by exploring whether self-alignment can be made even more cost-effective by replacing fine-tuning with representation editing, dramatically accelerating the alignment process. 

\paragraph{Representation Editing.} A parallel line of work seeks to modify model behavior without fine-tuning---doing so by solely editing the model's representations. For vision-language models like CLIP, \cite{adila2023zero} and \cite{chuang2023debiasing} show that removing spurious or unwanted concept subspaces from embeddings boosts model accuracy on rare class predictions. \cite{limisiewicz2023debiasing} shows that doing so in LLM architectures reduces gender bias in generated sentences without degrading model performance in other tasks. \cite{zou2310representation, li2024inference, han2023lm} demonstrate that modifying embeddings during inference to steer them towards certain traits (e.g., honesty, truthfulness, sentiment) can effectively enhance these traits in the generated outputs. Similarly, \cite{wu2024reft} \textit{learns} the appropriate embedding modification, acting as a form of fine-tuning. \emph{These methods assume access to ground-truth} preference datasets. Our work differentiates itself by designing an intervention technique that can handle the noisier signal from synthetic data generated by LMs.

\section{$\SYSNAME$: (Almost) Free Alignment of Language Models}

\label{sec:method}
We are ready to describe the $\SYSNAME$ algorithm. First, we query a base pretrained LM to generate its own preference data (Figure \ref{fig:generate_pref_data}). Our intuition is that, while noisy, base models have learned, from pretraining data, sufficient signal to aid in alignment. 
Using this self-generated data, the identify the subspaces in the LM's embedding spaces that correspond to helpful and harmful directions for alignment. During inference, we modify the LM embeddings using these identified subspaces, steering the model to generate outputs that better align with human preferences (Figure \ref{fig:editing}).

First, we describe the self-generated preference data extraction pipeline in Section \ref{sec:generate_pref_data}. Next, we explain how $\SYSNAME$ identifies helpful and non-helpful subspaces in Section \ref{sec:finding_direction}. Finally, we detail the embedding editing operation in Section \ref{sec:embedidng_editing} and the layer selection procedure for intervention in Section \ref{sec:selct_layers}.

\subsection{Self-generated Preference Data}
\label{sec:generate_pref_data}
First, we extract the human preference signal from the base LLM by querying it to generate its own preference data.  Given a dataset $D$ of $N$ queries, for each query $q_i$, we first ask the base LM (denoted as $\omega$) to describe characteristics of answers from a helpful agent ($c_i^{help}$) and a malicious agent ($c_i^{harm}$). Next, we pair each query with its corresponding characteristics: $(c_i^{help},q_i)$ and $(c_i^{harm},q_i)$. We then prompt the LM to generate responses conditioned on these characteristics, resulting in self-generated preference pairs for each query, denoted as $(p_i^{help},p_i^{harm})$. By applying this procedure to all $N$ samples in the dataset, we obtain self-generated preference data pairs $P^{help}$ and $P^{harm}$. Note that we do not perform any prompt tuning, instead relying on a fixed set of prompt templates.

Critically, we note that the base models for generating the preference data are \textbf{not aligned or instruction-tuned}. Consequently, the resulting preference pairs may not always align with the conditioning characteristics, introducing noise into the self-preference data. To address this challenge, we tailor the embedding intervention in $\SYSNAME$ to accommodate this condition.

\subsection{Finding Preference Directions} 
\label{sec:finding_direction}
Next, using the noisy self-generated preference data, we identify the directions in the model embedding space that correspond with human preferences. These directions, represented as vectors $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ within $\omega$'s latent space, can either (i) align with the \textit{helpful} preferences $P^{help}$, facilitating alignment of the model's generated sentences, or (ii) align with the \textit{harmful} preferences $P^{harm}$, leading to adverse effects on alignment \cite{adila2023zero} \cite{dalvi2022discovering}. We denote these directions as $\theta^{help}$ and $\theta^{harm}$, respectively. We explore several ways to identify them.  

\input{tables/main_algorithm}

\paragraph{SVD-Based Identification.} Our first approach for identifying these directions involves using singular value decomposition (SVD) on the preference data embeddings. We extract the first eigenvector $\theta$. Intuitively, we view $\theta$ as the direction that best captures the underlying concepts. Let $\Phi_l$ represent the function that maps an input sentence to the LM embedding space at layer $l$. For each pair $(p_i^{help}, p_i^{harm})$, we obtain their corresponding representations $\Phi_{l}(p_i^{help})$ and $\Phi_{l}(p_i^{harm})$, which we abbreviate as $\Phi_{i,l}^{help}$ and $\Phi_{i,l}^{harm}$, respectively. To begin, we construct an embedding matrix for helpful preferences, denoted as $\textbf{H}_{l}^{help}$, using these representations:
\[
\textbf{H}_{l}^{help} := \left [ \Phi_{i,l}^{help} \middle| \ldots \middle| \Phi_{N,l}^{help}\right ]^T.
\]
Similarly, we create the harmful preferences embedding matrix $\textbf{H}_{l}^{harm}$. Then, we proceed to identify the helpful direction as follows:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:svd}
& \textbf{H}_{l}^{help} = \textbf{U}\Sigma\textbf{V} \nonumber \\
& \theta_l^{help} := \textbf{V}_{0,*}.
\end{align}
Here, $\textbf{U}$ and $\textbf{V}$ represent the left and right unitary matrices produced by running SVD, respectively, and $\Sigma$ is the diagonal matrix of singular values. We define $\theta_l^{help}$ as the first row of $\textbf{V}$, corresponding to the first eigenvector of $\textbf{H}_{l}^{help}$. The harmful direction $\theta_l^{harm}$ is defined similarly.

\paragraph{CCS-Based Identification \cite{burns2022discovering}.} Another approach for identifying these directions is by finding a hyperplane in the latent space that separates helpful data embeddings from harmful ones. Typically, this is achieved by training lightweight probes $\theta_l$ that maps $\Phi_{i,l}^{help}$ and $\Phi_{i,l}^{harm}$ to their respective classification labels \cite{li2024inference}. However, we face the challenge of avoiding overfitting to the noise inherent in self-generated data, which limits the applicability of supervised classifier loss in our context. To mitigate this issue, we employ the unsupervised Contrast-Consistent Search (CCS) loss $\mathcal{L}_{CCS}$ proposed in \cite{burns2022discovering}. Adapting the definition from \cite{burns2022discovering} to our notations, $\mathcal{L}_{CCS}$ can be expressed as:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:ccs}
& \mathcal{L}_{consistency} := [\theta_l(\Phi_{i,l}^{help})-(1-\theta_l(\Phi_{i,l}^{harm}))]^2 \nonumber \\
& \mathcal{L}_{confidence} := min\{\theta_l(\Phi_{i,l}^{help}), \theta_l(\Phi_{i,l}^{harm})\} \nonumber \\
& \mathcal{L}_{CCS} := \EX{\left [ \mathcal{L}_{consistency} + \mathcal{L}_{confidence} \right ]}.
\end{align}
Training $\theta_l$ with the $L_{CCS}$ objective aims to find a separating hyperplane without fitting any labels with $\mathcal{L}_{consistency}$ and concurrently promoting maximum separation with $\mathcal{L}_{confidence}$. Unlike the SVD approach, the hyperplane identified by this method can be used as either $\theta_l^{harm}$ or $\theta_l^{help}$, depending on which cluster it maps to class `1'. Specifically, we assign $\theta_{l}$ as $\theta_{l}^{harm}$ if it maps the majority of samples in $\textbf{H}_{l}^{harm}$ to class 1.

\paragraph{Hybrid Identification.} After exploring both methods, we find that the best results come from combining the two approaches. Specifically, we use SVD to identify $\theta_l^{help}$ and CCS to determine $\theta_l^{harm}$. This combined approach leverages the strengths of both techniques: SVD's ability to capture the primary direction of helpful embeddings and CCS's effectiveness in identifying the hyperplane that best separates harmful embeddings from helpful ones. We describe $\SYSNAME$ subspace identification in Algorithm \ref{tab:algorithm}
\subsection{Alignment with Embedding Editing.} 
\label{sec:embedidng_editing}
With the harmful and helpful subspaces $\theta_l^{harm}$ and $\theta_l^{help}$ identified, we proceed to modify the LM embeddings during inference. Given $x_l$ as the output of the MLP of layer $l$, the $\SYSNAME$ editing process proceeds as follows:
\begin{align*}
 \hat{x_l} \leftarrow x_l - \frac{\ip{x_l}{\theta_l^{harm}}}{\ip{\theta_l^{harm}}{\theta_l^{harm}}} \theta_l^{harm} \quad \text{ and } \quad
 \hat{x_l} \leftarrow \hat{x_l} + \frac{\ip{\hat{x_l}}{\theta_l^{help}}}{\ip{\theta_l^{help}}{\theta_l^{help}}} \theta_l^{help}.
\end{align*}

In the first step, we use vector rejection to remove the influence of $\theta_l^{harm}$ from $x_l$. In the second step, we adjust the embedding by steering it towards the helpful direction $\theta_l^{help}$. We perform the edit at every generation time-step. We illustrate $\SYSNAME$'s representation editing step in Figure \ref{fig:editing}.

\subsection{Selecting Layers for Intervention.} 
\label{sec:selct_layers}
The last piece of the puzzle is determining which layers of the LM to apply our embedding editing to. Intuitively, we want to intervene in the layers where the embeddings of $X^{harm}$ and $X^{help}$ \emph{are most separable},  maximizing the effectiveness of $\SYSNAME$. To accomplish this, we select the layers for intervention by identifying the subset of layers with the lowest average $\mathcal{L}_{CCS}$ loss. This ensures that our alignment interventions are targeted at the most impactful layers of the model.

\section{Experiments}

We evaluate the following claims about $\SYSNAME$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reduces alignment gap (Section \ref{sec:exp_main}).} $\SYSNAME$ significantly reduces the performance gap between the base model and aligned model without any additional fine-tuning and access to ground-truth preference data.
    \item \textbf{Expedites  alignment (Section \ref{sec:exp_dpo}).} $\SYSNAME$ \emph{expedites DPO alignment} by improving models that have been DPOed on \emph{only a small} subset of ground-truth preference data.
    \item \textbf{Compatible with prompting techniques (Section \ref{sec:exp_urial}).} $\SYSNAME$ is compatible with and can be used in combination with prompt engineering-based  alignment methods \cite{lin2023unlocking} (Section \ref{sec:exp_urial}).
    \item \textbf{Predicts when self-alignment is possible? (Section \ref{sec:exp_micro}).} Self-generated data provides a signal about the model's ability to self-align with $\SYSNAME$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Metrics.} We follow the most popular standard for automatic alignment evaluation, using GPT-4 as a judge to compare a pair of responses \cite{zheng2024judging} and calculate the win rate (Win \%) and lose rate (Lose \%). To ensure a more nuanced and unbiased evaluation, we employ the \emph{multi-aspect evaluation technique} proposed in \cite{lin2023unlocking}. Rather than evaluating the overall quality of the generated text, we ask GPT-4 to assess it across five aspects: \textbf{Engagement (E)}, \textbf{Helpfulness (H)}, \textbf{Factuality (F)}, \textbf{Depth (D)}, and \textbf{Clarity (C)}. We use the same prompt template as \cite{lin2023unlocking} and measure the following metrics:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Net Win\%} $=$ Win\% $-$ Lose\%: A model that produces meaningful improvement over the base model will exhibit a higher win rate than lose rate, resulting in a positive net win percentage.

\item \textbf{Relative Improvement\%}.
\[\frac{\text{Net Win}{\hspace{.5em}ours-base}}{\text{Net Win}{\hspace{.5em}aligned-base}} \times 100.
\]
This metric evaluates how much $\SYSNAME$ improves alignment of the base pretrained model, relative to the aligned model. A value of 0\% means $\SYSNAME$ offers no improvement over the base model, while 100\% means $\SYSNAME$ matches the performance of the aligned model. Positive percentages between 0\% and 100\% indicate that $\SYSNAME$ narrows the performance gap between the base and aligned models, and a negative percentage indicates a performance decline from the base model. Excitingly, we additionally sometimes observe AlignEZ performance beyond the aligned model.

\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Datasets.} To evaluate $\SYSNAME$'s generalization capability across diverse tasks and topics while keeping evaluation affordable, we use the helpfulness slice of the \textbf{\texttt{just-eval-instruct}} dataset \cite{lin2023unlocking}. This dataset is a diverse collection of queries created by sampling and merging several datasets. Specifically, we use the helpfulness slice, which combines (1) $\texttt{AlpacaEval}$ \cite{li2023alpacaeval} (including \texttt{helpful-base}, \texttt{koala}, \texttt{vicuna}, \texttt{open-assistant (oasst)}, and \texttt{self-instruct}), and (2) $\texttt{MT-Bench}$ \cite{zheng2024judging}. We report $\SYSNAME$'s performance on these individual slices.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare $\SYSNAME$ against several base models: (1) $\texttt{Mistral-7B-v0.1}$ \cite{jiang2023mistral}, (2) $\texttt{Llama-2-7B}$ \cite{touvron2023llama}, and (3) $\texttt{Llama3-8B}$ \cite{llama3modelcard}. As an upper bound, we also compare these base models to their aligned versions. For Llama2 and Llama3, we use $\texttt{Llama-2-7b-Chat}$ and $\texttt{Llama-3-8B-Instruct}$, which are RLHF versions of the base models \cite{touvron2023llama, metaIntroducingMeta}. For Mistral, we use $\texttt{Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1}$, a version of the base model fine-tuned with instruction tuning datasets \cite{jiang2023mistral}. We report results using the Mistral instruction-tuned model because our experiments show it outperforms the open-source Mistral DPO \cite{tunstall2023zephyr} on our evaluation datasets.

While we do not expect $\SYSNAME$ to consistently outperform the aligned models, we anticipate a positive \textbf{Relative Improvement\%} metric. This would indicate that $\SYSNAME$ effectively brings the base model's performance closer to that of the aligned model without incurring additional costs.

\subsection{Reducing Alignment Gap}
\label{sec:exp_main}
First, we assess how effectively $\SYSNAME$ brings the performance of the base pretrained model closer to that of its aligned version.

\paragraph{Setup.} All experiments use frozen LLM weights, with no additional training of these weights. We only train lightweight probes to identify $\theta_l^{harm}$ using $L_{CCS}$ (see Section \ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Results.} Our results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:main_exp}. We observe consistent positive Relative Improvement\% across datasets and model architectures. \textbf{This validates our claim that $\SYSNAME$ reduces the alignment gap between base models and their aligned versions}, occasionally even surpassing the performance of the aligned models. Remarkably, these improvements are achieved without access to ground truth preference data or any additional fine-tuning. In cases where $\SYSNAME$ does not yield improvements, such as with the Llama2 model on the \texttt{vicuna} dataset, we investigate the essential conditions for improvement in Section \ref{sec:exp_micro}.

Figure \ref{fig:main_exp} also reveals an interesting insight: $\SYSNAME$ shows more significant improvements in aspects like \textbf{Helpfulness} and \textbf{Factuality} compared to Engagement and Depth. This suggests that $\SYSNAME$ primarily enhances utility-related aspects of the base model, while its impact on stylistic aspects is comparatively limited. This indicates potential areas for further improvement in the self-generated data process. For example, generating preference data based on multiple aspects rather than a single differentiating category (e.g., helpful vs. non-helpful, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:generate_pref_data}) might lead to improved overall performance.

\subsection{Expediting Alignment}
\label{sec:exp_dpo}
Next, we evaluate $\SYSNAME$'s ability to expedite more expensive alignment techniques like DPO. Specifically, we test whether $\SYSNAME$ can improve models trained with DPO using only a smaller subset of ground-truth preference data.

\paragraph{Setup.} We perform DPO fine-tuning on the \texttt{Mistral-7b-base} model using the UltraFeedback-binarized dataset \cite{cui2023ultrafeedback, tunstall2023zephyr} and do evaluation on the test set.

\paragraph{Results.} Our results are shown in Table \ref{tab:dpo}. $\SYSNAME$ enhances the alignment of models tuned using DPO on a small subset of ground truth preference data, indicated by the positive Net Win\%. \textbf{This confirms our claim that $\SYSNAME$ expedites DPO alignment}. In Figure \ref{fig:dpo}, we observe that the improvement provided by $\SYSNAME$ diminishes as the percentage of training data increases, which is expected since the benefit from DPO itself grows with more training data. This result highlights $\SYSNAME$'s potential to provide additional alignment gains when only a limited amount of ground-truth preference data is available.

We also investigate the adaptability of $\SYSNAME$ when combined with other cost-effective alignment techniques, such as prompting \cite{lin2023unlocking}.

\subsection{When is Self-Alignment Possible?}
\label{sec:exp_micro}
We study whether the quality of self-generated data can predict if using $\SYSNAME$ leads to model improvement. To assess the data quality, we measure the generalization ability of classifiers trained on the self-generated data.

\paragraph{Setup.} We train logistic regression classifiers on the embeddings of the self-generated data to predict the labels associated with the data and record the test performance. %To address class imbalance, we adjust the test accuracy by subtracting the class balance from the raw accuracy. 
Additionally, we use an off-the-shelf sentence embedder to remove the influence of model embedding quality. The reported values are averaged across five independent runs.

\paragraph{Results.} Figure \ref{fig:micro} shows that the average Net Win\% achieved by $\SYSNAME$ generally correlates with the adjusted classifier accuracy. \textbf{This supports our claim that self-generated data provides a signal about the model's ability to self-align}. This correlation is particularly strong for the Mistral model. For the Llama3 and Llama2 models, the trend is mostly consistent, with some exceptions being the \texttt{koala} dataset on Llama3 (leftmost point) and the \texttt{self-instruct} dataset on Llama2 (rightmost point). 

Extending this approach may offer a quick and effective method for selecting data suitable for alignment. This is crucial, as extensive research has shown that the composition and quality of training data are critical to the resulting model's performance \cite{xie2023data, lee2021deduplicating, hoffmann2022empirical}.



\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations and Future Work.}
$\SYSNAME$ presents several limitations and avenues for future exploration. First, we perform embedding editing  at every generation time step. However, it remains uncertain whether selecting specific time steps for intervention could yield further improvements. Second, while we see promising indications in Section \ref{sec:exp_micro} that the quality of self-generated data correlates with $\SYSNAME$ improvement, refining this characterization by developing a specialized metric for predicting the model's ability to self-align would be useful. Similarly useful would be to conduct an analysis to gauge the steerability of the base model based on the quality of its pretrained model embeddings. Additionally, our technique needs to be adapted for red-teaming scenarios, where the goal is to have the model refuse to answer certain questions instead of providing information.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} We introduce $\SYSNAME$, a novel approach for aligning pretrained LMs with human preferences without access to human-annotated data and fine-tuning. By leveraging the inherent knowledge within pretrained models, $\SYSNAME$ modifies model embeddings during inference to produce outputs that better align with human preferences. We empirically show that $\SYSNAME$ consistently enhances the alignment of the base model across multiple evaluation aspects, occasionally surpassing the performance of their aligned counterparts.  Additionally, we show that $\SYSNAME$ can expedite more costly alignment techniques like DPO.

This work takes an initial step toward achieving truly cost-free alignment and paves the way for the development of techniques in exciting new domains like real-time dynamic alignment and fast model personalization -- areas currently beyond the reach of standard alignment methods.
