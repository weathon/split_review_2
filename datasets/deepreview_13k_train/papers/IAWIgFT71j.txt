\title{Assessing Large Language Models on Climate Information}

\begin{abstract}
As Large Language Models (LLMs) rise in popularity, it is necessary to assess their capability in critically relevant domains. We present a comprehensive evaluation framework, grounded in science communication research, to assess LLM responses to questions about climate change. Our framework emphasizes both presentational and epistemological adequacy, offering a fine-grained analysis of LLM generations spanning 8 dimensions and 30 issues. Our evaluation task is a real-world example of a growing number of challenging problems where AI can complement and lift human performance. We introduce a novel protocol for scalable oversight that relies on AI Assistance and raters with relevant education. We evaluate several recent LLMs on a set of diverse climate questions. Our results point to a significant gap between surface and epistemological qualities of LLMs in the realm of climate communication.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



As concerns around \emph{climate change} intensify~\citep{Poushter-2022, WHO-2021}, more and more people turn to digital media as their primary source of information~\citep{DigNews-2020}. However, in spite of ubiquitous access to information, there remains a considerable gap in climate literacy, exacerbated by the spread of mis- and disinformation~\citep{six-americas-2021}. The challenge of conveying climate data arises also from the nature of scientific communication: science, as an evolving domain, is laden with specialized knowledge, complexity, and inherent uncertainties~\citep{moser-2016-comm}. The digital media landscape, characterized by soaring amounts of AI-generated content~\citep{thompson2024shocking}, limited attention spans and adversarial dynamics, further compounds these challenges~\citep{pearce-2019-some}.

While AI's promise in addressing global climate challenges is evident through its applications in climate modeling, energy optimization, and disaster management~\citep{CCAI}, its intersection with Natural Language Processing (NLP) is still under-explored. Given recent advancements in LLMs~\citep{gpt-3, palm, openai2023gpt4,geminiteam2023gemini} there is hope that generative AI will also help addressing climate information challenges.
However, using LLMs to address science-related information raises factuality concerns~\citep{ethical-and-social-risks-of-lms}.
Eloquence and advanced dialogue behaviors are trusted by users, even in the absence of trustworthy information \citep{Chiesurin23}. This makes evaluating LLMs difficult.
Research on evaluating systems that may achieve or exceed human abilities, or \emph{scalable oversight}~\citep{Amodei16} is so far mostly theoretical \citep{Irving18, Leike18, Christiano18}, with some recent more practical advances \citep{michael23}. 

We introduce a framework based on
Science Communication research~\citep{oxford-sciencecomm}, to begin evaluating LLMs' responses within the climate change context in a principled way.\footnote{To aid reproducibility, we provide the exact evaluation protocols and all prompts used to generate data.} The evaluation relies on raters with relevant educational background. We assess {\bf presentational} properties such as \emph{style}, \emph{clarity}, linguistic \emph{correctness}, and \emph{tone}. More importantly, we also assess {\bf epistemological} issues: \emph{accuracy}, \emph{specificity}, \emph{completeness}, and \emph{uncertainty}. 
To test the relevance of the evaluation, we run an empirical study on a diverse set of 300 climate change-related questions involving some of the most recent and prominent LLMs. 

\begin{itemize}
    \item To increase the recall of detected issues and improve rating quality, it is crucial to introduce scalable oversight protocols that use grounded AI Assistance (cf.\ \cref{fig:sys-design}). However, while AI assistance demonstrably improves rating quality, its influence on raters extends beyond this enhancement. Understanding and mitigating these broader effects remains an open question for future research.
    \item Results suggest that the epistemological quality of responses on climate information of current LLMs is substantially lower than the presentational quality.
    \item We find preliminary evidence that summarizing the evaluation dimensions in the prompt can improve performance on the epistemological dimensions. 
    \item We notice trade-offs between dimensions. Most notably, there seems to be a trade-off between epistemological and presentational quality.
    \item We analyze the relation of our evaluation and attribution-based evaluations of LLMs~\citep{rashkin2022measuring} and find that they emerge as mostly orthogonal and complementary.
\end{itemize}



\section{Evaluative Dimensions}

\label{sec:dimensions}
Scholarship on science communication -- originating from disciplines such as communication science, sociology, psychology, human geography, and education, among others \citep{Trench.2021,Nisbet.2018,oxford-sciencecomm} -- offers conceptual arguments and empirical evidence for appropriately disseminating scientific information, e.g., on climate change, to the general public \citep{koenig_2023,Lewis_Neil_2021}. Building on this knowledge, we distinguish between two basic dimensions. (1) \emph{Presentational} features of the message that address its comprehensibility \citep{Lang.2000}. (2) \emph{Epistemological} features aiming to capture the degree to which the conveyed information represents current scientific knowledge adequately and comprehensively, while being specific and appropriately communicating associated uncertainties \citep{Fahnrich.2023}.

\subsection{Presentational Adequacy}
\label{sect:presentational-adequacy}
An adequate \emph{presentation} should comply with three criteria~\citep{oxford-sciencecomm}: (1) be comprehensible, (2) aid understanding through layout and visualizations, and (3) use appropriate sources and references. Here we focus primarily on \emph{comprehensibility}, evaluated along four criteria: style, clarity, linguistic correctness, and tone.

\textbf{Style.} The language should not be too informal or colloquial~\citep{Mazer.2008}, as this can undermine the credibility of information~\citep{Scharrer.2012}. Answers should not be too short or too long: brief snippets of information can lead to a ``feeling of knowing''~\citep{Leonhard.2020}, while long texts require motivation and cognitive resources that readers may not want to invest~\citep{Lang.2000}. In addition, we borrow some stylistic dimensions from the Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM) framework for  the evaluation of translations~\citep{mqm-lommel-2013}. 

\textbf{Clarity.} Responses should be concise and clearly formulated \citep{Maibach.2023}.  
The use of jargon and difficult technical content should be avoided~\citep{BaramTsabari.2013,BaramTsabari.2020}.

\textbf{(Linguistic) Correctness.} As in MQM, messages should adhere to linguistic conventions, i.e., the correct use of punctuation, spelling, and grammar.\footnote{ \url{https://themqm.info/typology}.} Violations can damage perceived credibility~\citep{Berger.2020,Mollick.2014}

\textbf{Tone.} The tone of a message concerns its perceived neutrality, its persuasiveness and its positivity or negativity. Science communication, especially climate-related, can be more effective if it doesn't lean towards a certain valence, worldview, or ideological conviction~\citep{Blanton.2019,yuan2020s,Kerr.2022,MunozCarrier.2020}. Likewise, messages should not use too positively or negatively valenced language, particularly if the goal is to convey factual information~\citep{palm2020don}.

\subsection{Epistemological Adequacy}
\label{sect:epistemic-adequacy}
The epistemological adequacy of climate-related messages is of greatest importance. This entails several aspects: (1) accuracy, (2) specificity, (3) completeness, (4) the degree of (un)certainty, and (5) the presentation of methods and methodology. Here we focus on the first four.

\textbf{Accuracy.} Scientific information should be \emph{accurate}~\citep{Kelesidou.2021}. This is crucial, considering known issues of LLMs such as \emph{hallucination}~\citep{Schafer.2023, Ji.2023}. We identify issues that deal with \emph{incorrect, wrong,} or \emph{self-contradictory} information, as well messages that take scientific findings, or anecdotal evidence, out of context~\citep{Hinnant.2016}. 

\textbf{Specificity.} Information that is \emph{relevant} to the audience should not be missed, while ignoring irrelevant information. Responses should address the \emph{spatial} and \emph{temporal} context; as specific, local information leads to higher perceived relevance~\citep{lee2015predictors,Leiserowitz.2017,Holmes.2020b}. In the absence of a specific time frame, the answer should generally be based on up-to-date knowledge.

\textbf{Completeness.} Rather than only referring to a part of the question posed, answers should be formulated in a way that addresses all aspects of the question in full~\citep{Leiserowitz.2017,bergquist2022information}. At the same time, the information given should reflect the depth and breadth of relevant scientific knowledge available regarding the topic(s) addressed~\citep{Kelesidou.2021}.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} Communicating the level of agreement and confidence regarding scientific findings, and supporting evidence, can be crucial to adequately informing the audience~\citep{howe2019acknowledging,budescu2012effective,Keohane.2014}. This is particularly important in climate communication~\citep{Maertens.2020,chinn2021effects,goldberg2022communicating}, scientific consensus on climate change has been found to function as a ``gateway belief'' and motivate public action~\citep{vanderLinden.2015}. 

\subsection{Aggregation of scores across dimensions}
In this paper we don't address the important question of how individual dimensions should be combined in a single metric, e.g., for model selection and benchmarking. This is a complex topic which requires assigning a value to each individual dimension. We also believe that the combination of these scores will vary by application.



\section{Human Evaluation Study}

\label{sec:experiments}
We test our evaluative dimensions in a human rating study. The rating task involves evaluating an answer based on the presentational  (\cref{sect:presentational-adequacy}) and epistemological dimensions (\cref{sect:epistemic-adequacy}).

Each answer is assessed by three human raters. We don't discourage brief consultations of external sources to clarify specific points but advise against extensive research.

\subsection{Question and Answer Data}
\label{sect:data}

\subsubsection{Questions}

A comprehensive evaluation would ideally cover a broad spectrum of information needs, including the basics of climate science, mitigation and adaptation, as well as context-specific issues; e.g., to address the concerns of vulnerable or under-resourced communities~\citep{amini2023ai}. 
However, no standardized tests exist to assess climate-related knowledge; in contrast to e.g. the medical domain~\citep{singhal2023large}. Hence, we begin by creating a diverse set of $300$ questions about topics that are either popular among search users, controversial or context-specific.

We collect questions from three different sources. 
For the first set, we use Google Trends, which provides data on public interest in specific search topics.\footnote{\url{https://trends.google.S., for the topics `Climate Change' and `Global Warming' for 2020-2022.
For the second set, we turn to Skeptical Science, a website that publishes authoritative information about climate science. We take the list of debated \emph{myths}\footnote{\url{https://skepticalscience.php}} and manually rephrase them as questions.
Lastly, we use \gptfour{} to generate synthetic questions from the English Wikipedia.
We manually select a list of articles related to climate change (e.g., "Global Dimming", "Polar Amplification"), or discuss the topic in specific locations (e.g., "Climate Change in [COUNTRY]"), for a total of 139 articles. Then we split the documents in paragraphs and ask \gptfour{} to generate questions that can be answered by the paragraph. We apply several filters to assure that the Wikipedia questions are not overly dependent on the context and are therefore answerable only from the given paragraph. See \cref{sec:synthetic-questions} for more details and a discussion of filtering choices.

We post-process all questions to remove duplicates, questions that are not related to climate change, or taken out of context. Finally, we sample $100$ questions from each set. 

\subsubsection{Answers}

Generated answers can display a great deal of variation depending on prompt engineering, reasoning schemes, in-context learning, etc. However, a direct question is the most common way for users to get answers from LLMs. As such, a plain question provides a valuable baseline, reducing variance due to individual LLM's skills and optimization effort, and limiting confounding factors. To obtain answers we use a simple prompt consisting of the instruction: \textit{You are an expert on climate change communication. Answer each question in a 3-4 sentence paragraph.} We include the answer length information to anchor the expected response to an objective value. 

\subsection{Auxiliary Data}
\label{subsec:aux_data}
We support raters with AI Assistance, consisting of a model-generated critique for each evaluated dimension. For epistemological dimensions the assistance is grounded in verbatim evidence from relevant passages extracted from Wikipedia articles. To produce all necessary auxiliary data we carefully design a simple, robust baseline system (\cref{fig:sys-design}), which relies on a single LLM.  For consistency, we always use \gptfour{} for this purpose. Besides testing our evaluation we also run a comparison with an attribution-based evaluation (AIS)~\citep{rashkin2022measuring}, on the same data.

\textbf{Keypoints.} To find supporting evidence for an answer, for AI Assistance and AIS evaluation (\cref{sect:ais}), we extract keypoints from each answer. To do so, we instruct \gptfour{} to examine all the statements in the answer, and identify one to three key statements that are made in answering the question. We find this to provide better signal to retrieve evidence (see the next paragraph) than either using the whole answer or all sentences individually~\citep{liu2023evaluating}.

\textbf{Evidence.} For each keypoint we fetch evidence from Wikipedia. Given the question and the answer, we first ask \gptfour{} to provide the URL of a Wikipedia article that supports the answer. See \cref{tab:prompts} for the exact prompt. We limit evidence to Wikipedia because \gptfour{} is fairly consistent in generating relevant, valid Wikipedia URLs, while the quality is lower for the unrestricted web. Furthermore, Wikipedia is uniform in style and quality as it adheres to established guidelines.\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Policies_and_guidelines}.} While random web pages can vary significantly in content and presentation quality.

We break down the relevant article into its paragraphs. For each keypoint, we ask the model to score the paragraphs based on their relevance to the keypoint and the question. We pick the $3$ highest scoring ones as evidence (cf. \cref{tab:ais-example} for an example). We find that using keypoints, in combination with URL generation and evidence selection, is a simple and effective solution. In particular, we find this to work better than off-the-shelf sparse or dense retrieval (e.g., using BM25/GTR~\citep{gtr-ni-etal-2022-large}) over Wikipedia passages.

\textbf{AI Assistance.} To assist human raters, we use \gptfour{} to critique the answer along the dimensions introduced in \cref{sec:dimensions}. For each dimension, we ask the model to express its agreement or disagreement that the information is presented well according to that dimension. For epistemological dimensions, we also provide the retrieved evidence and instruct the model to quote the evidence verbatim to support its disagreement (if any).

Please refer to \cref{tab:prompts} for a complete list of prompts used to generate the data, and to \cref{sec:answer-stats} for some statistics of the generated answers.

\section{Experimental Results}

Here we present the findings from the experiments using \cref{fig:all_results} as a summary. Full results tables, including confidence intervals, are reported in \cref{tab:main_results_presentational} and \cref{tab:main_results_epistemological}. We also report pairwise LLM t-tests in \cref{tab:pairwise_presentational,tab:pairwise_epistemological}.  We compute pairwise distance and Krippendorff's alpha agreement metrics for all experiments in \cref{sec:agreement}, including an analysis of rating timing \cref{sec:timing}. Accurate rating of climate information is challenging, but we find the main conclusion proposed below to be adequately supported.

\textbf{LLMs.} We evaluate the following models: \gptfour{}~\citep{openai2023gpt4}, \gptthreex{}, \gptturbo{}, \gptthree{}, \gpttwo{}\footnote{\url{https://platform.openai.}, as well as \palm{}~\citep{anil2023palm} and ~\falcon{}\footnote{\url{https://falconllm.tii.ae/falcon.html}.}. This data was collected in the months of September and October 2023.

\subsection{Performance Results}
\textbf{Presentational Results.} 
Overall, except for \gpttwo{}, LLMs produce clear, fluent, linguistically correct text. 
This confirms how far LLMs have come in terms of surface form quality, seemingly thanks to RLHF~\citep{instruction-tuning-ouyang2022training}. \textbf{Epistemological Results.} 
Compared to presentation, the epistemological evaluation reveals lower performance across all models and dimensions. Results are consistently low for the last three: \textit{specificity}, \textit{completeness} and \textit{uncertainty}. 
We note that these dimensions may be difficult to satisfy in short 3-4 sentence answers. Being comprehensive in such a short space may be harder than being accurate.

 Thus, space constraints alone do not seem sufficient to explain the result.
Overall, on climate information, current top-of-the-line LLMs have significant headroom for improvement. For examples, please see Tables~\ref{tab:example_accuracy} to \ref{tab:example_uncertainty}.

\subsection{Resolution and Range.}
The results suggest that the evaluation possesses sufficient resolution to distinguish models on specific dimensions, indicate where they differ and suggest trends. For instance, \chatgpt{} is the best on all presentation dimensions, except \emph{tone}, but ranks between position 4 and last on the epistemological scores. This indicates the possibility of complex dependencies between presentational and epistemological properties.
As a matter of fact, \gptfour{} is always better than \chatgpt{} across the epistemological dimensions but worse on most presentational ones.

The difference between the best LLM and the worst in specific dimensions -- e.g., \falcon{} and \gpttwo{} on the epistemological ones, and, respectively, \chatgpt{} and \gpttwo{} on the first three presentational ones--is large compared to the estimated standard deviation, providing evidence that the evaluation has sufficient dynamic range.
Noticeably, the most competitive model--at the time of writing--on the epistemological dimensions is an open model, \falcon{}. This LLM's performance may be related to its large size, but we can only speculate as this information is not generally available for all models. 

\subsection{Influence of Question Source.}
Comparing the rating outcome by source of the question – Skeptical Science, GTrends, and synthetic questions from Wikipedia-–we find no major differences, except for slightly lower scores for Wikipedia questions. In particular, we see no evidence that \gptfour{} performs better on questions that were generated with \gptfour{} compared to the other sources. Similarly, the topic of the question does not show a strong correlation with answer quality. See \cref{sec:rating_by_type} for additional discussion and figures.

LLMs often fill parts of the answer with somewhat relevant but unspecific, cautionary and sometimes preachy reminders. The example in \cref{tab:example-style} illustrates the point.
Due to the highlighted part, the response may result in sub-optimal \emph{cooperative conversation}, in a Gricean's sense~\citep{levinson_1983}. For instance, one could argue that the \emph{maxim of quantity} is being violated ('do not provide more information than required') as most of what follows the first sentence is strictly speaking unnecessary.
The \emph{maxim of manner} ('be relevant') may also be violated: comments on extreme weather and rising sea levels are only loosely related to the question. That space could be used to provide more specific information.

Furthermore, the answer relies generically on the notion of \emph{scientific consensus}, which happens relatively frequently in our data. Besides the possibility of being superficially interpreted as an `argument from authority', research suggests \citep{orchinik23} that the `consensus' argument can be surprisingly ineffective due to complex belief system underlying how such arguments are processed.  
\citet{orchinik23} argue that perceived scientists credibility, which in turn may depend on general worldview, affects how consensus-based messages are received and receptiveness to future messaging. 
This presentation style may not appeal to the different audiences of science communication and possibly lead to diminished interest and fatigue~\citep{schaefer-et-al-2018}. To further complicate the picture, recent work points out that after conversing with AI on climate change, people with a skeptical stance end up dissatisfied but also more supportive of scientific consensus~\citep{chen24}
In these respects, LLMs answers differ from some human experts' answers to similar questions.\footnote{E.g., from \url{https://climatefeedback.org/} or \url{https://skepticalscience.} The latter tend to rely on direct and specific scientific evidence; e.g., in the case of the question above, an expert may cite land, atmospheric and ocean data for temperature trends, from multiple scientific sources.\footnote{Human experts' answers also tend to include images summarizing quantitative data.}
Our framework captures some of these aspects in dimensions like \emph{tone} and \emph{specificity}, but the pragmatics aspects of Generative AI should probably be investigated more directly in the future.

\subsection{Role of AI Assistance.}
We expect human raters to identify more (real) issues with assistance,
because it makes them aware of them. We find supporting evidence in two separate experiments.
\begin{figure}%
    \centering
    \includesvg[width=\columnwidth]{figures/issue-assistance.svg}
    \caption{Number of issues detected depending on\\AI Assistance exposure.}
    \label{fig:critique-exposure-a}
\end{figure}

\cref{fig:critique-exposure-a} reports the number of issues detected for each dimension on \gptfour{} answers in three different settings, each with a different degree of the raters' exposure to assistance. 'Without AI Assistance' refers to a setting where a specific pool of raters is never exposed to rating with AI Assistance. 
'Without AI Assistance, but previous exposure' refers to a setting where no assistance was shown, but the raters have worked on previous studies that included assistance.\footnote{We do make sure that the raters have not worked on the same examples before and have never seen assistance for the specific examples they are working on.}
Lastly, 'With AI Assistance' denotes the standard setting where assistance is shown anytime is available.

Results suggest that the presence of assistance is key for detecting more issues. 
This is consistent with the results from \citet{saunders2022selfcritiquing}.
Raters with previous exposure to assistance are in a ``middle'' position: They detect more issues than the assistance-unaware group, but less than the group provided with assistance for the experiment. This suggests that raters learn from repeated exposure to assistance, and show improved performance even when no assistance is present.

Further evidence of the usefulness of AI Assistance comes from our validation experiments (cf. \cref{sect:validation-details} for more details).  Similar to \citet{saunders2022selfcritiquing}, we want to determine if assistance helps surface real issues, without general access to gold truth in our data. To do this, the authors manually generated $30$ different examples, each exhibiting a particular issue. We found that the majority of three raters detected $77\%$ of issues when shown assistance, while the majority of three raters only detected $60\%$ of the issues when not shown assistance.

The data suggests that when raters do not find assistance helpful, they give higher ratings (see \cref{fig:critique-exposure-b}). This indicates that the raters can think critically about the assistance and do not follow it blindly. %
These experiments provide evidence that the AI Assistance helps the raters find real issues that they would not have otherwise been reported.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

\label{sect:limitations-future-work}
While our agreement analysis (\cref{sec:agreement}) suggests that the evaluation is robust at the system level, the rating dimensions inherently have a subjective component, introducing noise when evaluating at the answer-level. As we do not have access to gold ratings, calibration of raters remain an open issue, as reflected by the medium inter-rater agreement discussed in \cref{sec:agreement}. Future work should consider explicitly addressing this subjectivity in the data collection process (cf. \citet{rottger22}).

AI Assistance is an essential part of our evaluation, because it helps raters identify issues in the answers, particularly for the epistemological dimensions. As \cref{tab:main_results_presentational,tab:main_results_epistemological} shows, raters would fail to recognize many issues without the AI Assistance ('\gptfour{} no assistance'). However, the assistance may also influence the raters beyond enhancing discovery. It may only help in the discovery of some issues but not others. There may also be errors caused by models falsely pointing out issues and wrongly convincing the raters. The issues identified will likely vary by model. There is definitely a need to better understand these issues and to identify mitigation strategies. This links this research to the broader AI alignment field and will be one of the main focuses of our future work.
A related topic is the role of LLMs as raters. We found that, as with humans, LLMs benefit from AI Assistance and that humans and LLM raters tend to agree on major points.

 

Ideally, an answer would be tailored towards the audience, and take into account their specific attributes ~\citep{Hendriks.2016,Klinger.2021}. Unless specifically prompted, LLMs do not do this and the evaluation of such setting would introduce additional challenges.
Another important area for future work concerns multimodal responses.
Research provides abundant evidence on the importance of supplementing textual information with visual aids. %
\citep{flemming2018emotionalization, Brown.2011}. Visual complements can be especially useful for understanding quantitative data~\citep{Fagerlin.2011} and in the case of limited literacy~\citep{Wolf.2010}. The abstract nature of climate change, and its distant implications, makes visualization particularly challenging~\citep{Schafer.2020}.


\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Evaluating LLMs.} While LLMs can generate fluent text, responses are not always adequately grounded, attributable to reliable sources, and complete. For instance, \citet{liu2023evaluating} assess four generative search engines and report that, although responses are perceived as high quality, only half are fully supported. Their findings reveal an inverse correlation between fluency/utility and evidential support. \citet{xu2023critical} advocate for expert-level human evaluations in question answering, cautioning against over-reliance on single metrics instead of comprehensive assessments.

Another domain that needs expert-level evaluation is the medical domain. \citet{singhal2023large} propose Med-PaLM, an LLM for medical information, and introduces a clinical evaluation framework which covers criteria like alignment with scientific consensus, potential harm, and comprehension. Evaluating LLMs on climate information is another domain that can benefit from expert-level evaluation. However, prior work mainly focused on text classification tasks~\citep{climatefever, climatext, cards,pirav1,climatebert,cheaptalk,climabench,climatex}. This study aims to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive evaluation framework for generative climate information.

\textbf{Scalable Oversight.} This area, introduced by \citet{Amodei16}, studies the question of how to scale human oversight, especially in the setting where evaluating (or supervising) models becomes increasingly difficult.
Contributions have initially focused on theoretical proposals for how AI can help humans supervise models that exceed their abilities \citep{Irving18, Leike18, Christiano18}. 
Following \citet{Irving18}, one can see our AI Assistance as a single-turn debate, where the human annotator is shown the answer proposed by the model and a single response to that answer.\footnote{In the setting of \citet{Irving18}, this corresponds to the second level of the polynomial hierarchy $\Sigma_2^P$.}

Two recent studies provide interesting proofs of concepts for AI Assistance:
\citet{bowman2022measuring} study \emph{sandwiching}, an approach where non-experts align a model with the help of a model while experts provide validation. They show that non-expert raters perform better on an (artificially) difficult multiple-choice task when interacting with a dialogue agent.
Several studies also evaluated short debates in this setting with mixed results \citep{parrish23a, parrish23b, michael23}.
\citet{saunders2022selfcritiquing} report that human raters of summarization tasks produce more critiques when given the opportunity to accept or edit critiques written by a model. Our work contributes a study of a \emph{scalable oversight} protocol to improve rating quality in a realistic setting.

\textbf{AI Ratings.} Recent studies explore the feasibility of evaluations performed by AI.
\citet{gemba} indicate that LLMs can perform state-of-the-art quality assessment of translations, even without references. Their work has been extended to automatic MQM annotation by \citet{fernandes2023devil}. \citet{gilardi} reports that ChatGPT has a higher agreement with expert-level raters than with less qualified ones. \citet{chiang2023can} argue that humans and LLMs ratings are correlated but point out LLM's factuality and bias limitations. Instead of replacing human raters entirely, in our work we demonstrate the effectiveness of using AI Assistance to aid educated raters.


\section{Conclusion}

We introduce an evaluation framework informed by science communication research and assess LLMs on a first set of climate information needs. The task is difficult for human raters. To support them, an important part of our framework relies on a novel and practical protocol for scalable oversight that leverages AI Assistance. It is important to realize that these are the first results of this kind and more research is needed. In particular, while there is evidence that AI Assistance is valuable, we need to develop a framework to understand and mitigate undesired influence on the raters. Overall, our results suggest that, while presentationally adequate, current LLMs have much room for improvement regarding the epistemological qualities of their outputs. More research is needed to understand and improve these aspects of LLMs. %
