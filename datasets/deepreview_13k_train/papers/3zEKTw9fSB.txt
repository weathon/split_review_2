\title{Generative Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning}

\begin{abstract}
We present \underline{G}enerative Parameter-Eff\underline{I}cient \underline{F}ine-\underline{T}uning (GIFT) for adapting pretrained Transformer backbones on downstream tasks.  GIFT learns to generate the fine-tuned weights for a layer directly  from its pretrained weights. The GIFT network is parameterized in a minimally-simple way by two linear layers (without bias terms), and is shared by different pretrained layers selected for fine-tuning (e.g., the Query layers), which result in significantly fewer trainable parameters compared to the layer-specific methods like Low-Rank Adapter (LoRA). We also show this formulation bridges parameter-efficient fine-tuning and representation fine-tuning. We perform comprehensive experiments on natural language tasks (commonsense and arithmetic reasoning, instruction tuning,  and sequence classification) and computer vision tasks (fine-grained classification). We obtain the best performance and parameter efficiency among baselines on commonsense and arithmetic reasoning, and instruction following using the Llama family of models and on visual recognition benchmarks using Vision Transformers. Notably, compared to LoRA, we obtain 5.7\% absolute increase in average accuracy with 14 times reduction of parameters on Commonsense170k using {\tt Llama-3 (8B)}, and  5.4\% absolute increase in the win rate with 4 times reduction of parameters using {\tt Llama-2 (7B)} during instruction tuning. Our GIFT also obtains a slightly higher win rate on instruction tuning than {\tt GPT 3.5 (Turbo 1106)}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\label{sec:intro}
\subsection{Motivation and Objective}
\label{sec:motivation}

Fine-tuning pretrained deep neural networks (DNNs) as feature backbones for downstream tasks, often known as transfer learning, has been an important and challenging research topic in deep learning literature since the resurgence of DNNs pioneered by the AlexNet~\citep{alexnet} trained on the ImageNet-1k dataset~\citep{ImageNet}.  %
While straightforward, finetuning all the parameters of a model may not yeild the best results on a downstream task, as subtle details in terms of exploiting the pretrained model and exploration for the new task can affect the performance~\citep{lee2022surgical}. This raises questions about which parts of a pretrained model to fine-tune, and how they should be trained. In recent years, large feature backbones with open weights like Llama~\citep{llama,llama2,llama3}, termed foundation models~\citep{bommasani2021opportunities}, have become ubiquitous. Training such models from scratch is impossible with limited resources, and fine-tuning them entirely may also be costly. However, considering the exploration-exploitation tradoff mentioned above, it may be unnecessary to fine-tune the full pretrained model. Hence, parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) becomes necessary and remains an active research topic. 

In this paper, we focus on PEFT for pretrained Transformer backbone models~\citep{transformer}. Prior approaches used for fine-tuning isotropic pretrained Transformer backbones such as Llama and Vision Transformers \citep{vit} include prompt-based methods~\citep{prefix-tuning,vpt}, adapters \citep{houlsby-adapter}, and bias fine-tuning \citep{zaken2022bitfit}. In recent years, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)~\citep{lora}, which learns weight-residuals in the low-rank form (i.e., $A^l$ and $B^l$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:gift-lora} (a)) has become the \textit{de facto} method with many follow-up variants~\citep{adalora,qlora,relora,facct,vera,dft-tune,dora}. This formulation can achieve strong empirical performance, but the learnable weight-residuals do not have direct ``information exchange'' with the pretrained weights. Recently, \cite{loreft} proposed Representation Fine-Tuning (ReFT), an alternative to PEFT, that can achieve strong performance at a lower parameter cost. Motivated from a causal intervention mechanism \citep{dii}, ReFT steers the pretrained model towards a task by editing the representations of a few tokens in a low-dimensional subspace. 

Despite these advances, we still lack an intuitive and holistic understanding of the relationship between frozen pretrained models and their fine-tuned models by PEFT beyond the residual structure, and between the representations of the frozen pretrained weights and their intervened representations by ReFT. Furthermore, while ReFT is proposed as a distinct paradigm, is there a way relate PEFT and ReFT? Motivated by these observations, we ask two questions: 
\begin{highlightbox}[mysubtitle]{}
  \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item[(Q1)] {Can we induce an explicit and direct mapping between the  fine-tuned model and the frozen pretrained model, i.e., learn the fine-tuned weights directly from the pretrained weights?} \label{main_question1}
    \item[(Q2)] {How simple can the enforced relationship be while allowing the fine-tuned model to match or outperform LoRA and remain memory-efficient, and bridge PEFT and ReFT?}\label{main_question2}
\end{enumerate}
\end{highlightbox}

Addressing these two questions entails a new angle for PEFT. We present a simple yet effective formulation that provides affirmative answers to the two questions in this paper, which we briefly overview in the next sub-section.

\subsection{Method Overview: \underline{G}enerative Parameter-Eff\underline{i}cient \underline{F}ine-\underline{T}uning (GIFT)}
\label{sec:simple-gift}

Our proposed GIFT is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:gift-lora} (b). Let $\omega^l\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$ denote the pretrained weights of a layer $l$. The fine-tuned weights $\hat{\omega}^l$ are expressed as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}= \omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} \cdot P_{d_{in}\times d_{in}} =\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} \cdot (\mathbb{I}+\Theta_{d_{in}\times d_{in}}),    \label{eq:linear_ft}
\end{equation} 
where $P_{d_{in}\times d_{in}}=\mathbb{I}+\Theta_{d_{in}\times d_{in}}$, $\mathbb{I}$ is an identity matrix and $\Theta_{d_{in}\times d_{in}}$ a projection matrix to be learned for a downstream task. \textbf{$\Theta$ is \textit{not layer-specific} and can be shared by different layers selected for fine-tuning}, reducing the trainable parameters significantly compared to layer specific methods such as LoRA. We specify layer-sharing GIFTs in Section \ref{sec:experiments}. $\Theta_{d_{in}\times d_{in}}$ is further defined by a low-rank structure as
\begin{equation}
    \Theta_{d_{in}\times d_{in}} = \phi_{d_{in}\times r} \cdot \psi_{r\times d_{in}}, \label{eq:linear_ft_factorization}
\end{equation}
where the rank $r$ is a hyper-parameter, similar to LoRA. Let $L$ be the total number of layers selected for finetuning, and let $\Omega_{L\times d_{out} \times d_{in}}$ denote the weights of all the $L$ layers. Our proposed GIFT is formulated as,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Omega}_{L\times d_{out} \times d_{in}} = \text{GIFT}(\Omega_{L\times d_{out} \times d_{in}}; \Theta_{d_{in}\times d_{in}}) = \Omega + \text{Linear}(\text{Linear}(\Omega; \phi); \psi) . \label{eq:gift-overview}
\end{equation}
\textit{This parameterization shows that the relationship between the fine-tuned model and the frozen pretrained model is enforced to be linear, the simplest mapping relationship.} 

Hence, Eqn \ref{eq:gift-overview} provides an answer for (Q1). We show that this simple GIFT: two linear projections of the pretrained weights shared across all the layers $L$ is surprisingly effective, also providing an answer to (Q2). In Section \ref{sec:gift-formulation}, we also show that GIFT bridges PEFT and ReFT~\citep{loreft}.  For the proposed GIFT formulation above, we emphasize two aspects:%
\begin{itemize} [leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item Although $\Theta$ is shared, the output fine-tuned weights, $\{\hat{\omega}^l: l \in L\}$, are still layer-specific in Eqn.~\ref{eq:linear_ft}. 
    \item It is straightforward to learn GIFT along the $d_{out}$ dimension by writing Eqn.~\ref{eq:linear_ft} as $(\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}})^{\top}=  (\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}})^{\top} \cdot P_{d_{out}\times d_{out}}$ . Unless otherwise specified, we will henceforth focus our description of GIFT only on the $d_{in}$ dimension for simplicity.
\end{itemize}

 

\textbf{Performance Overview of Our GIFT}: 
To show the effectiveness of our proposed GIFT in Eqn.~\ref{eq:gift-overview}, we briefly summarize the performance of fine-tuning the family of Meta's three Llama models~\citep{llama,llama2,llama3} on three tasks on the bottom of Fig.~\ref{fig:gift-lora} with experimental details presented in Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}: (i) \textit{Instruction Following with Llama-2.} Our GIFT$^{16}_{\alpha}$ obtains higher Win Rate than a recently proposed  representation fine-tuning method, LoReFT \citep{loreft}. With slightly increased parameters (still 4 times less than LoRA), our GIFT$^{128}_{\alpha}$ can even outperform  {\tt GPT 3.5 Turbo 1106}. 
(ii) \textit{Arithmetic Reasoning with LLaMa-1.} Our GIFT$_{\beta}^{64}$ outperforms VeRA~\citep{vera} significantly using the same fine-tuning parameter budget. 
(iii) \textit{Commonsense Reasoning with three Llama Models}: our GIFT$_{\gamma}^{64}$ obtains 6\% absolute increase in average accuracy with 53 times less parameters using {\tt Llama-3} compared to LoRA. It is also consistently better than LoReFT~\citep{loreft}, but using about half the number of parameters.


\textbf{Why Does the Simple GIFT Work?} Based on the effectiveness of our GIFT, we can draw intuitive understanding of PEFT using pretrained Transformer backbones: Pretrained Transformer backbones ``distill'' general and diverse knowledge from a large-scale pretraining dataset, encoded in the pretrained weights. When fine-tuning them at a downstream task, to ``absorb'' new information in the training data of the downstream task, \textit{one of the simplest updates that minimally ``distorts'' and maximally ``preserves'' the pretrained knowledge} is defined by Eqn.~\ref{eq:linear_ft} and Eqn.~\ref{eq:linear_ft_factorization}, thanks to the low-rank factorized linear projection in the parameter space. The newly ``absorbed'' information from the downstream task is also linearly expressed in the space spanned by the pretrained weights $\omega_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$ via $\omega_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}\cdot \Theta_{d_{in}\times d_{in}}$. 
When pretrained Transformer backbones are sufficiently expressive, which is often assumed in PEFT, simpler parameterization methods such as our proposed GIFT should be more generalizable and better under the principle of Occam's razor. For example, on commonsense reasoning in Fig.~\ref{fig:gift-lora}, the performance of GIFT consistently increases when the backbone in use is more expressive from LLaMa-1, to Llama-2 and to Llama-3. 


\textbf{Our Contributions}: (i) We present Generative Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (GIFT) that is conceptually different from LoRA variants in terms of how the fine-tuning weight-residuals are learned. Our GIFT learns fine-tuned weights directly from the pretrained weights, with its parameters shared by different layers selected in fine-tuning, making it even more parameter-efficient than LoRA while retaining the memory efficiency of LoRA.  (ii) We present a simple parametrization composed of two linear layers (without bias terms) with projected low-rank structures  for GIFT, which induces linear relationships between the learned weight-residuals and the original pretrained weights. The simple GIFT bridges parameter-efficient and representation-efficient fine-tuning methods. (iii) The two-linear-layer GIFT is tested with pretrained Transformer backbones on a variety of tasks including Natural Language Generation (instruction following, commonsense reasoning, and arithmetic reasoning), Natural Language Understanding, and Visual Recognition. Our GIFT outperforms previous PEFT methods while using approximately 15 times less parameters and surpasses previous ReFT approaches with half of the parameters.

\section{Approach}

\subsection{Background on Efficient Fine-Tuning}

For a self-contained context for our proposed GIFT, we first review both parameter-efficient fine-tuning popularized by LoRA~\citep{lora} and representation-efficient fine-tuning (ReFT) recently proposed by~\citep{loreft}.

As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:gift-lora} (a), consider a pretrained layer with the weights $\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$.  LoRA learns the layer-specific weight-residual with a low-rank parameterization scheme,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} = \omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} +\Delta \omega^l = \omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} + B^l_{d_{out}\times r }\cdot A^l_{r\times d_{in}}, \label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the predefined rank ($r\ll \min(d_{out}, d_{in})$, e.g., $r=16$), which is often specified in a layer-agnostic way.  LoRA is efficient in terms of both the number of learnable parameters and the memory footprint in training. Many follow-up works modify LoRA by introducing more inductive biases and/or heuristics in parameterizing the weight-residual $\Delta\omega^l$ in addition to low-rank, leading to trade-off between parameter and memory efficiency. We briefly discuss two recent variants.

\textbf{DoRA}~\citep{dora} 
exploits the magnitude and direction decomposition and formulates the fine-tuned weights as $\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} =M^l_{1\times d_{in}}\cdot \frac{\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} + B^l_{d_{out}\times r}\cdot A^l_{r\times d_{in}}}{||\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}+B^l_{d_{out}\times r}\cdot A^l_{r\times d_{in}}||_c}$, where $||\cdot||_c$ represents vector-wise norm. Compared to LoRA, DoRA introduces extra parameters $M^l$ and  increases the computation cost and memory footprint due to the directional component.
\textbf{VeRA}~\citep{vera} uses fixed random matrices $A^l_{r \times d_{in}}$ and $B^l_{d_{out}\times r}$, and learns scaling vectors $d \in \mathbb{R}^r$ and $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}}$. VeRA learns the weight-residual as $\Delta\omega^l = \Lambda_b B^l \Lambda_d A^l$, where $\Lambda_b$ and $\Lambda_d$ are diagonal matrices with their diagonals as $b$ and $d$ respectively. Due to fixed random projection matrices, compared with the vanilla LoRA, although VeRA can reduce the number of learnable parameters significantly, it needs a large rank $r$ (typically equal to the model dimension) to achieve good performance, hence increasing the computational costs and training time. Additionally, adding fixed random matrices with learnable scales in PEFT makes the relationship between fine-tuned models and frozen pretrained models less intuitive. 
\textbf{ReFT}~\citep{loreft} does not fine-tune pretrained models as in PEFT, and instead intervenes the representations/activations, i.e., the token sequence in the hidden layers by learning how to edit them in a low-dimensional subspace in a layer-specific way. Without loss of generality, consider the representation of a token $y^l \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times 1}$ at a certain layer $l$, two variants of ReFT are proposed: \textit{LoReFT} learns an edited representation in the projected $r$-dimensional subspace via $\hat{y}^l = y^l + (R^l_{r\times d_{out}})^\top \cdot (W^l_{r\times d_{out}}\cdot y^l + b^l_{r\times 1} - R^l_{r\times d_{out}}\cdot y^l)$ where $R^l$ has orthonormal rows. 
\textit{DiReFT} relaxes the orthonormal constraint and learns $\hat{y}^l = y^l + (W^l_2)^\top\cdot (W^l_1 \cdot y^l + b^l)$ where both $W^l_2, W^l_1\in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{out}}$. 
To apply the invertentions, ReFT needs to search for a dedicated  hyperparameter configuration to decide how many interventions to learn and which layers and token positions to apply each intervention on. ReFT has shown to be a very strong alternative to LoRA. Unlike LoRA-based PEFT methods which do not incur any extra inference cost after training, ReFT methods increase inference cost slightly. Furthermore, token-level interventions lack a holistic understanding of the relationship between ReFTed models and frozen pretrained models.

\subsection{Our Proposed GIFT}
\label{sec:gift-formulation}

We now provide a deductive perspective of deriving and understanding our proposed GIFT (Fig.~\ref{fig:gift-lora} (b)) to consolidate the overview in Sec.~\ref{sec:simple-gift} and present the detailed formulation.

While they show good performance, the prior PEFT (LoRA, DoRA and VeRA) and ReFT (LoReFT and DiReFT) methods fail to address the two motivating questions in Section \ref{sec:motivation}. Moreover, \textbf{they overlook an intuitive yet important method of improving efficiency, that is to facilitate sharing learnable parameters across layers} selected for fine-tuning a pretrained backbone. However, simply sharing the weight residuals $\Delta\omega$ across layers as model parameters does not make intuitive sense. 
To address this dilemma, one solution is to exploit hierarchical modeling, that is to use a generator network to generate the weight residuals across different layers: the learnable parameters of the generator networks are shared, while its outputs are layer-specific. The question then is about what the input of the weight-residual generator network should be. The most intuitive and simple answer will be directly using the pretrained weights as the input. In sum, for the $L$-layer pretrained weights $\Omega_{L\times d_{out}\times d_{in}}$, our proposed GIFT is formulated by, 
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\Omega}_{L\times d_{out} \times d_{in}} = \text{GIFT}(\Omega_{L\times d_{out}\times d_{in}};\Theta) = \Omega_{L\times d_{out}\times d_{in}} + \mathcal{G}(\Omega_{L\times d_{out}\times d_{in}};\Theta),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta\Omega = \mathcal{G}(\Omega_{L\times d_{out}\times d_{in}};\Theta)$ is the weight-residual generator network parameterized by $\Theta\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times d_{in}}$ and shared across the $L$ layers. \textit{This formulation addresses the first motivating question (Q1)}. We recruit a network $\mathcal{G}()$ to by-pass different inductive biases such as the weight-decomposition in DoRA~\citep{dora} and the fixed random matrices in VeRA~\citep{vera}. Intuitively, we can see the generator network  $\mathcal{G}()$ provides more flexibility in modeling the weight-residuals $\Delta\Omega$. 

\textit{To address the second motivating question (Q2)}, we enforce a low-rank structure for the weight-residual generator network $\mathcal{G}()$ following widely adopted practices in efficient fine-tuning methods:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{G}(\Omega_{L\times d_{out}\times d_{in}};\Theta) = \text{Linear}(g(\text{Linear}(\Omega_{L\times d_{out}\times d_{in}};\phi));\psi),
\end{equation}
where we have,

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\itemsep0em
    \item $\text{Linear}(\Omega;\phi)$ projects the input dimension to a lower dimension (or rank) $r$ with learnable weights $\phi\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times r}$ without bias terms. Denote by $\Omega_1\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{out}\times r}$ the output of this layer. 
    \item $\text{Linear}(\cdot;\psi)$ is an output dimension-recovery projection with learnable weights $\psi\in \mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{in}}$ and no bias term. %
    It outputs the learned weight-residuals, $\Delta \Omega\in \mathbb{R}^{L\times d_{out}\times d_{din}}$.
    \item $g(\cdot;\theta)$ is the low-dimensional generator network, which can be realized by any suitable network specifications. We consider the following schema in this paper: 
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \itemsep0em
        \item \textit{Transformer}: We treat $\Omega_1$ as a batch of $L$ sequences each consisting of $d_{out}$ tokens in $r$-dim space. We then apply a single Transformer block~\citep{transformer,vit}. 
        \item \textit{MLP-Mixers}: Similar to vanilla Transformers, we apply a single MLP-Mixer~\citep{tolstikhin2021mlp} block.   
        \item \textit{Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs)}: e.g., $g(\Omega_1;\theta)=\text{Linear}(\text{GELU}(\text{Linear}(\Omega_1;\theta_1)); \theta_2)$, where $\theta_1\in \mathbb{R}^{m\cdot r\times r +m\cdot r}$ and $\theta_2\in \mathbb{R}^{r\times m\cdot r + r}$ consist of weights and bias terms of the two linear layers with the MLP latent dimension ratio $m$ (e.g., $m=2$).
        \item \textit{Element-wise non-linearity functions} without learnable parameters (i.e., $\theta=\emptyset$): e.g.,  $g(\Omega_1)=\text{Sigmoid}(\Omega_1)$ or $g(\Omega_1)=\text{GELU}(\Omega_1)$.
        \item \textbf{The identity operation:} $g(\Omega_1;\theta)=\text{Identity}(\Omega_1)=\Omega_1$ with no learnable parameters $\theta=\emptyset$, which leads to the simple two-layer linear parameterization of GIFT (Eqn.~\ref{eq:linear_ft}, Eqn.~\ref{eq:linear_ft_factorization} and Eqn.~\ref{eq:gift-overview}). 
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Through ablation studies (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}), we show that the two-layer linear parameterization of GIFT is surprisingly effective, and thus our focus in this paper. For a pretrained layer with weights $\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$, our GIFT finetuned weights are, 
\begin{equation}
\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} =\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} + \omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} \cdot  \phi_{d_{in}\times r}\cdot \psi_{r\times d_{in}},    \label{eq:gift}
\end{equation} 
which we can make intuitive connections with LoRA~\citep{lora} and ReFT~\citep{loreft}.
\begin{itemize} [leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Our GIFT can be treated as the sharing-oriented LoRA}, where we have $B^l_{d_{out}\times r}=\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}} \cdot  \phi_{d_{in}\times r}$ conditioned on the pretrained weights and retained to be layer-specific, and $A^l_{r\times d_{in}}=\psi_{r\times d_{in}} (\forall l)$ relaxed to be layer-agnostic.
    \item \textbf{Our GIFT bridges PEFT and ReFT.} To show this, consider a linear layer with pretrained weights $\omega^l\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$ and the bias term  $b^l_{d_{out}}$, the representation/activation for its input $x^l_{N\times d_{in}}$ is,
    \begin{equation}
        y^l_{N\times d_{out}} = x^l_{N\times d_{in}} \cdot (\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}})^{\top} + b^l_{d_{out}} .
    \end{equation}
    With the GIFT weights $\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$ (Eqn.~\ref{eq:gift}), we have,
    \begin{align}   
   \nonumber \hat{y}^l_{ N\times d_{out}} &= x^l_{ N\times d_{in}} \cdot (\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}})^{\top} + b^l_{d_{out}} \\
   \nonumber  &= (x^l_{ N\times d_{in}} + x^l_{ N\times d_{in}}\cdot \phi_{d_{in}\times r}\cdot \psi_{r\times d_{in}}) \cdot (\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}})^{\top} + b^l_{d_{out}} \\
   &= \hat{x}^l_{N\times d_{in}} \cdot (\omega^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}})^{\top} + b^l_{d_{out}},   
    \label{eq:gift_linear_output} 
\end{align}
where $\hat{x}^l_{N\times d_{in}}=x^l_{ N\times d_{in}} + x^l_{ N\times d_{in}}\cdot \phi_{d_{in}\times r}\cdot \psi_{r\times d_{in}}$ is the ``GIFTed'' input activation, the counterpart of the ``GIFTed'' weights $\hat{\omega}^l_{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$ (Eqn.~\ref{eq:gift}). So,
\textbf{our GIFT can be equivalently applied to the input activation, rather than the pretrained weights, to achieve the same fine-tuning effect}, maintaining the memory and compute efficiency of LoRA in implementation. Unlike the ReFT~\citep{loreft} that entails a dedicated search for where the representation interventions should apply at the token level, our GIFT (Eqn.~\ref{eq:gift_linear_output}) eliminates the need of search. 
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Gradient Analyses of GIFT}

Without loss of generality and for the clarity of presentation, consider a toy isotropic MLP with 2 hidden layers and no bias terms as the pretrained backbone: 
\begin{align}
    x^1 = \sigma\left(x^0{\omega^1}^\top\right), x^2 = \sigma\left(x^1{\omega^2}^\top\right), x^3 = x^2{\omega^3}^\top , 
    \label{eq:toy_mlp}
\end{align}
where $x^0,x^1,x^2,x^3 \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ represents $N$-token sequence in the $d$-dim space, $\omega^1, \omega^2, \omega^3 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out} \times d_{in}}$ denote the pretrained weights, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ denotes the activation function. For simplicity, we assume that $d_{out}=d_{in}=d$. For large pretrained models, it is often sufficient to fine-tune only a subset of all the layers. For the toy MLP, assume that layers 1 and 3 will be fine-tuned.

During fine-tuning, the derivatives to the learnable parameters $B^l$ and $A^l$ in LoRA and those to $\phi$ and $\psi$ in GIFT are also significantly different. Let $\ell$ be the scalar loss (e.g., the cross-entropy loss) computed using one data point for simplicity. In the toy MLP example (Eqn.~\ref{eq:toy_mlp}), for LoRA, we have,
\begin{align}
    \underbrace{\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial A^1}}_{r \times d} = {B^1}^{\top} \cdot \boxed{{\frac{\partial \ell }{\partial x^1}}^{\top} \cdot  x^0 }, \qquad \underbrace{\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial B^1}}_{d \times r} =  \boxed{\underbrace{{\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial x^1}}^{\top}}_{d \times N} \cdot \underbrace{x^0}_{N \times d} } \cdot \underbrace{{A^1}^{\top}}_{d\times r}
    \label{eq:lora_gradident}
\end{align}
Similarly, for $\psi$ and $\phi$ in our GIFT, 
\begin{align}
    {\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \psi}}
    &= \phi^\top \cdot \boxed{{\omega^1}^\top \cdot {\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial x^1}}^\top \cdot x^0} + \phi^\top \cdot \boxed{{\omega^3}^\top \cdot {\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial x^3}}^\top \cdot x^2}, \label{eq:gift_gradident_1} \\
   {\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \phi}} &=  \boxed{{\omega^1}^\top \cdot {\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial x^1}}^\top \cdot x^0} \cdot {\psi}^\top + \boxed{{\omega^3}^\top \cdot {\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial x^3}}^\top \cdot x^2} \cdot {\psi}^\top, \label{eq:gift_gradident_2}
\end{align}
where $\psi$ and $\phi$ gather information from all the selected layers since they are shared by them.  Specifically, in terms of the gradient updates, $\psi$ and $\phi$ ``accumulate'' information distilled from data significantly different from, yet more informative than, how $B$ and $A$ in LoRA do. For example, $B^1$ and $A^1$ ``accumulate'' gradient modulated input activation (${\frac{\partial \ell }{\partial x^1}}^{\top} \cdot  x^0 $'s in Eqn.~\ref{eq:lora_gradident}). $\psi$ and $\phi$ not only ``accumulate'' information from all (shared) layers, but also utilize the gradient modulated input activation projected onto the space of the pretrained weights (${\omega^1}^\top \cdot {\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial x^1}}^\top \cdot x^0$ in Eqn.~\ref{eq:gift_gradident_1} and Eqn.~\ref{eq:gift_gradident_2}). 


 


\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiments}

\textbf{Naming Convention and choice of finetuning layers}: We mainly follow the prior works of selecting layers of pretrained backbones to be fine-tuned on different tasks for fair comparisons. We use the subscript to denote layers to be fine-tuned and the superscript for the rank $r$, i.e., \textbf{GIFT}$^r_{\text{layers}}$.  We index different components in a Transformer block using their initials: Query ($Q)$, Key ($K$), Value ($V$), and Output projection ($O$) in the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) block, and Up  ($U$), Gate ($G$) and Down ($D$) projections in the MLP block. In the subscript, we use comma (`,') to split layers to indicate the sharing patterns, underline (e.g. $\underline{Q}$) to indicate that GIFT is applied to the $d_{in}$ dimension, and overline (e.g. $\overline{Q}$) to indicate that GIFT is applied to the $d_{out}$ dimension.
We experiment with four variants:
\begin{itemize} [leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{GIFT}$^r_{\underline{Q},\underline{V}}$ represents that we use two GIFTs applied to $d_{in}$ dimension, one is shared by all Query layers and the other by all Value layers, and both using the rank $r$ (e.g., $r=16$). Similarly, \textbf{GIFT}$^r_{\underline{Q},\underline{K},\underline{V},\underline{U},\underline{D}}$ represents that we use five GIFTs. These two are used for fair comparisons with different variants of LoRA. 
    \item \textbf{GIFT}$^r_{\overline{O},\overline{D}}$ represents that we use two GIFTs along the $d_{out}$ dimension for the projection Output layers in MHSA blocks and the Down layers in the MLP blocks. This is used for fair comparisons with ReFT~\citep{loreft}.
    \item \textbf{GIFT}$^r_{\underline{QKV},\overline{O},\underline{UG},\overline{D}}$ represents a block-wise sharing pattern we test in experiments. Each Transformer block has its own GIFTs,  where we share one GIFT each for \underline{$QKV$}, \underline{$UG$}, $\overline{O}$, and $\overline{D}$.    
\end{itemize}

We conduct experiments across Natural Language Generation, Natural Language Understanding, and Visual Recognition and compare our two-linear-layer parameterized GIFT with various other PEFT methods. We also conduct ablation studies on the different parameterization schemes of GIFT.

\begin{align}
 H^l_{N\times r} = &\hat{y}^l_{N\times d_{out}} \cdot C^l_{d_{out}\times r} .
\end{align}
We normalize the $r$ heatmaps to $[0,1]$ individually and use 0.5 as the threshold to generate the visualizations (Figure~\ref{fig:cluster-interpretability}).

\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:relatedwork}

\noindent\textbf{Parameter Efficient Fine-tuning (PEFT).} The goal of PEFT methods is to reduce the computational resources (memory footprint, wall time, etc.) required for fine-tuning large models such as Transformers \citep{transformer} and Vision Transformers (ViTs) \citep{vit}. Prompt-based methods either append prompts to the input tokens \citep{prompt-tuning,vpt}, or the intermediate layers \citep{prefix-tuning,prefix-tuning-v2,llama-adapter}. Early work on PEFT used sequential/parallel learnable adapters added after the Multi-Head Self Attention and/or FFN blocks \citep{houlsby-adapter,bapna-adapter,adapter-fusion,invertible-adapters,adapter-drop,compacter,adaptformer}. LoRA \citep{lora} and its variants \citep{adalora,qlora,relora,facct,vera,dft-tune,dora} learn residuals to the pretrained weight matrices in the form of low-rank factorization, removing the added inference cost in adapter based methods. BitFit~\citep{zaken2022bitfit} fine-tunes all the bias terms in a pretrained backbone.

\textbf{Hypernetworks.} \cite{hypernetworks} introduced Hypernetworks, i.e., neural networks that generate the parameters for other neural networks, in language modeling tasks by generating the weights of an LSTM \citep{lstm}. Hypernetworks have previously been applied for few-shot classification \citep{meta-learning-via-hypernetworks,hypertransformer}, transfer learning \citep{requeimaFastFlexibleMultiTask2019} and continual learning \citep{cl-with-hypernetworks,sylph}. Similar to our proposed approach, \citep{requeimaFastFlexibleMultiTask2019} learns to adapt a global feature extractor through an adaptation network. In a few shot continual learning setup, \citep{continual-hypertransformer} uses a hyper-Transformer to generate the parameters for a separate Convolutional Neural Network (ConvNet), which use as inputs both a support set of images of the current task and the ConvNet parameters generated for the previous tasks. HyperFormer++ \citep{hyperformer} uses a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to generate the parameters from layer embedding and a latent vector for Adapters \citep{houlsby-adapter} introduced across layers of a pretrained model in a multitask setting. Unlike \citep{hyperformer}, we directly use the weights of the frozen pretrained model, thus eliminating the need for embeddings. 

\textbf{Neural Functionals}: Our approach is related to neural functionals that aim to learn deep neural networks acting on the weights of other neural networks. For toy problems, equivariant architectures have been explored for tasks like classifying implicit neural representations \citep{equivariant-deep-weight-spaces,penn, nfn, gnn-equi}, adapting model architectures to new domains \citep{equivariant-deep-weight-spaces}, predicting model generalization performance \citep{penn, nfn, gnn-equi, gnn-meta}, and learned optimizers \citep{unf}. However, our work is the first to explore fine-tuning of a model using it's own weights. We do not use equivariant architectures, but note that this direction of work is orthogonal to ours, and can be further explored in the future.



\section{Conclusion}

We present Generative Parameter Efficient Fine-Tuning (GIFT) for adapting pretrained Transformer backbones on downstream tasks. Our GIFT learns to generate the fine-tuning weight-residuals for layers selected in fine-tuning directly from their frozen pretrained weights using a neural network. We show that a simple design - where GIFT consists of two linear layers without bias terms - can achieve strong performance, which provides a novel angle for formulating PEFT methods. We further show that the simple GIFT bridges PEFT and ReFT methods. 
We conduct experiments across various tasks, including Natural Language Generation (instruction following, commonsense reasoning, and arithmetic reasoning), Natural Language Understanding, and Visual Recognition. GIFT outperforms previous PEFT methods while using approximately 15 times fewer parameters and surpasses previous ReFT approaches with half the parameters.
