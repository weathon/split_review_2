\title{Feature Responsiveness Scores: Model-Agnostic Explanations for Recourse}

\begin{abstract}
Machine learning models are often used to automate or support decisions in applications such as lending and hiring. 
In such settings, consumer protection rules mandate that we provide a list of ``principal reasons'' to consumers who receive adverse decisions.
In practice, lenders and employers identify principal reasons by returning the top-scoring features from a \emph{feature attribution} method.
In this work, we study how such practices align with one of the underlying goals of consumer protection -- \emph{recourse} -- i.e., educating individuals on how they can attain a desired outcome.
We show that standard attribution methods can mislead individuals by highlighting \emph{reasons without recourse} -- i.e., by presenting consumers with features that cannot be changed to achieve recourse.
We propose to address these issues by scoring features on the basis of \emph{responsiveness} -- i.e., the probability that an individual can attain a desired outcome by changing a specific feature.
We develop efficient methods to compute responsiveness scores for any model and any dataset under complex actionability constraints.
We present an extensive empirical study on the responsiveness of explanations in lending, and demonstrate how responsiveness scores can be used to construct feature-highlighting explanations that lead to recourse and to mitigate harm by flagging instances with fixed predictions.

\end{abstract}

Machine learning models are now routinely used to automate or support decisions about people in domains such as employment~\citep[][]{bogen2018help,raghavan2020mitigating}, consumer finance~\citep[][]{hurley2016credit}, and public services~\citep{wykstra2020government,eubanks2018automating,gilman2020poverty}. In such applications, explanations are often seen as an essential tool to protect consumers who are adversely affected by the predictions of a machine learning model~\cite{AIBOR, OnlineCivilRightsAct, selbst2018intuitive, barocas2020hidden}. Existing and proposed laws and regulations include provisions that require lenders or employers to provide explanations to individuals in such situations~\citep{cfpb2024regB, AIBOR, OnlineCivilRightsAct, EUAIAct}. In the United States, for example, the adverse action notice requirement in the Equal Credit Opportunity Act mandates that lenders provide ``principal reasons'' explaining why individuals are denied credit~\cite{cfpb2024regB}. In the European Union, Article 86 of the AI Act \citep{EUAIAct} grants individuals a right to obtain explanations to describe the ``main elements'' of their decision in high-risk applications regarding employment, education, financial systems, government benefits, law enforcement, and border control~\citep[see Annex III][for a definition of ``high risk'']{EUAIAct}. 

The use of explanations in such settings reflects widespread beliefs about the effectiveness of transparency for consumer protection~\citep{edwards2017slave} -- i.e., that revealing information can protect and empower consumers~\citep[][]{selbst2018intuitive}. For example, the adverse action notice is motivated by the fact that presenting consumers with ``principal reasons'' can: (1) promote \emph{anti-discrimination} by highlighting potential bias; (2) facilitate \emph{rectification}, by allowing individuals to identify and correct data errors, and; (3) support \emph{recourse} by educating individuals on how to improve future outcomes~\citep[][]{taylor1980meeting}. Regulators provide model deployers with substantial flexibility in complying with these requirements. In practice, model owners comply with these regulations using feature attribution methods such as LIME and SHAP \cite{FinRegLab2023}. These are general-purpose methods that can explain the predictions of a model after it has been trained and generate explanations that can be communicated to consumers. These methods output scores that reflect the importance of each feature for a given prediction. Given these scores, model deployers can retrieve the top scoring features and present them to consumers as a list of ``principal reasons'' or ``main elements'' (see \cref{Fig::ResponsivenessOverview}).

In this work, we study how to explain model predictions in a way that can achieve one of the main goals of consumer protection: \emph{recourse}. We focus on achieving recourse through the use of feature attribution -- techniques that are widely used in practice. Our work is motivated by the fact that regulations seek to achieve multiple goals; we claim that it is useful to align the design of an explanatory method with the goals it seeks to achieve. To this end, we study how well existing approaches for feature attribution methods support recourse, and develop an approach tailored to communicating with respect to this goal. 
Our main contributions include:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0.1em]

\item We present a feature attribution method to measure the responsiveness of predictions from a model. The \emph{responsiveness score} measures the probability of changing the prediction of a model by intervening on a given feature. Our approach highlights features that can be changed to receive a better model outcome, and identifies instances that may lead to harm. 

\item We develop model-agnostic methods to compute feature responsiveness scores using \emph{reachable sets}. Our methods can evaluate scores for any model, paired with theoretical guarantees that support our ability to flag harm, and can be readily adapted to achieve other goals.

\item We conduct a comprehensive empirical study on the responsiveness of feature attribution in consumer finance. Our results demonstrate that common feature attribution methods output \emph{reasons without recourse} by highlighting features that do not provide recourse, and underscore the benefits of our approach. 

\item We include Python source code to measure feature responsiveness available at the \repourl{}.
 
\end{enumerate}

We focus on methods for feature attribution, which are designed to evaluate the importance of feature for a given prediction. Our work shows how feature attribution methods can inflict harm in such cases by highlighting \emph{reasons without recourse} -- i.e., by highlighting features, when acted upon, do not change the prediction -- sometimes to consumers who are assigned fixed predictions. This is a failure mode that affects a broad class of local explainability techniques -- adding to a growing literature on how local explanations are prone to manipulation~\cite[e.g., ][]{lakkaraju2020fool,slack2020fooling,slack2021counterfactual,aivodji2019fairwashing,goethals2023manipulation}, and indeterminate~\citep[e.g., due to multiplicity][]{marx2020multiplicity,watsondaniels2022predictive,brunet2022implications,black2022model}. Our work complements impossibility results on recourse and feature attribution showing that attribution methods that satisfy completeness and linearity (e.g., SHAP) perform no better than random guessing when inferring model behavior (e.g., recourse) ~\citep[see e.g.,][]{bilodeau2024impossibility,fokkema2023attribution}. Here, we establish the prevalence of this effect empirically and develop a principled approach to mitigate it.

Our approach is related to a stream of work on algorithmic recourse~\citep[][]{ustun2019actionable,karimi2020algorithmic}. The vast majority of work on this topic develops algorithms for recourse provision -- i.e., to present consumers with \emph{actions} that can change the prediction a specific model~\citep[see e.g.,][]{karimi2021survey}. Our goal is to highlight features that can be reliably changed to achieve recourse. To this end, responsiveness scores measure the number of actions on a \emph{single} feature. Our approach builds on a line of work that elicits and enforces complex actionability constraints~\citep[][]{ustun2019actionable,kothari2024prediction}.
Here, we use this machinery to represent actionability constraints at an instance level, and to generate a set of all points that a person can reach under a set of actionability constraints~\citep[][]{kothari2024prediction}. 
Our approach outputs feature responsiveness scores that can be used with any model, and can be adapted to address practical challenges in providing recourse -- e.g., robustness~\citep{nguyen2023distributionally,pawelczyk2023probabilistically,upadhyay2021towards} and causality~\citep[][]{karimi2021interventions,dominguez2022adversarial,galhotra2021explaining}. 



\section{Problem Statement}

\label{Sec::ProblemStatement}

We formalize the problem of explaining the predictions of a machine learning model through feature attribution. 
We consider a standard classification task where we wish to predict a label $y \in \Y = \{0,1\}$ from a set of $d$ \emph{features} $\xb = [x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_d] \in \X \subseteq \R^d$. 
We assume that we given a model $\clf{}: \X \to \Y$ where each instance represents a person, and their features $\xp \in \X$ encode semantically meaningful characteristics for the task at hand (e.g., $\textfn{age}$ or $\textfn{income}$).
We assume that the feature values are bounded so that $x_j \in [l_j, u_j]$ and $\vnorm{\xb} \leq B$ for all $\xb \in \X$ and $B$ sufficiently large.
\footnote{This assumption holds for most semantically meaningful features \citep[see][]{ustun2019actionable}. Some features have bounds by construction (i.e. binary features). In other cases, we can set loose bounds (e.g., for \textfn{income}).}

We consider a task where we provide explanations to individuals who are adversely affected by the prediction of a given model (see e.g., \citep{cfpb2024regB,EU2024AIActArticle86e}). We assume that $\clf{\xp} = 1$ represents a \emph{target prediction} that is desirable -- e.g., $\clf{\xb_i} = 1$ if applicant $i$ is predicted to repay their loan within 2 years -- and thus will explain the predictions for individuals where $\clf{\xb_i} = 0.$  

\paragraph{Feature-Highlighting Explanations}
Our goal is to construct explanations where each feature is \emph{responsive} -- i.e., can be changed independently to achieve recourse. 

The standard practice of explaining predictions is to use \emph{feature-highlighting explanations}~\citep[see e.g.][]{barocas2020hidden}. These explanations consist of a list of ``most important'' features using a given method that we convert into a natural language description~\citep[e.g., a reason code][]{FinRegLab2023}. 

\paragraph{Feature Attribution Methods}

\newcommand{\intrange}[1]{[#1]}
\newcommand{\impscore}[2]{\phi_{#1}({#2})}
\newcommand{\impscorevec}[1]{\bm{\phi}({#1})}
\newcommand{\rscore}[2]{\mu_{#1}({#2})}
\newcommand{\rbscore}[2]{\nu_{#1}({#2})}

The standard approach to construct feature-highlighting explanation is to use feature attribution method~\citep{FinRegLab2023}.
\begin{definition}
Given a model $\clf{}: \X \to \Y$ and its training dataset $\data{} = \{(\xp, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, a \emph{feature attribution method} for point $\xp$ is a function 
$\impscorevec{\xp \mid \clf{}, \data{}} : \X \to \R^d$, where the $j$th element of the output, $\impscore{j}{\xp \mid \clf{}, \data{}}$ is the attribution for feature $j \in \intrange{d}$.
\label{Def::FeatureExplainer}
\end{definition}
In what follows, we write $\impscorevec{\xp}$ instead of $\impscorevec{\xp \mid \clf{}, \data{}}$ when $\clf{}$ and $\data{}$ are clear from context. This function captures the behavior of several methods that are used to explain the prediction of a model in terms of its features:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0.5em,topsep=0.0em]

    \item \emph{Local Linear Explainers}~\citep[see e.g.,][]{ribeiro2016should,zhou2021s,zafar2019dlime,elshawi2019ilime}: 
    Given a model $\clf{}$ and a point $\xp$, these methods fit a linear model $g: \R^d \to \R$ to approximate the decision boundary surrounding $\xp$ such that $g(\xb^{\prime}) = \dotprod{\bm{\phi}(\xp)}{\xb^{\prime}}$. The resulting attribution for each feature is its weight in $g$.
    
    \item \emph{Shapley Value Methods}~\citep[see e.g.,][]{lundberg2017unified,jethani2021fastshap,fumagalli2024shap}: Given a model $\clf{}$ and a point $\xp$, these methods cast features as players in a cooperative game, and estimate $\impscore{j}{\xp}$ as the marginal contribution of feature $j$ to the prediction $\clf{\xp}$ under basic axioms of social choice~\citep{shapley1953value}. 
    
\end{itemize}
Given a model $\clf{}$ and its training dataset $\data$, the scores $\bm{\phi}(\xp)$ capture how each feature captures the prediction of a model at the $\xp$ in different ways. In all cases, the scores satisfy the following properties:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=0.5em,topsep=0.0pt]

\item \emph{Relevance}: A feature with an attribution score $\impscore{j}{\xp} = 0$ is not relevant to the prediction for $\xp$ -- i.e., it can be changed arbitrarily without changing the prediction~\cite[see e.g., the ``missingness'' axiom in][]{lundberg2017unified}.

\item  \emph{Strength}: Features with larger attribution scores have larger impact on the prediction -- i.e., if $|\impscore{j}{\xp}| > |\impscore{j'}{\xp}|$, then feature $j$ has a stronger contribution to the prediction than feature $j'$. 

\end{itemize}
These properties allow model developers to comply with consumer protection rules, but can promote misinterpretation among consumers~\cite{kaur2020interpreting}.

One failure mode of machine learning in consumer-facing applications is that models can assign \emph{fixed predictions} -- i.e., predictions that cannot be changed by their decision subjects (see e.g., \cref{Tab::ToyExample}). In lending, for example, fixed predictions inflict harm through \emph{preclusion} -- i.e., permanently barring consumers from access to credit. Fixed predictions are a recently-discovered failure mode~\citep[][]{kothari2024prediction}, which must be mitigated through changes in feature construction, model development, or implementation (e.g.,using a separate model for re-applicants who are assigned fixed predictions). In practice, these instances are often left undetected. We can mitigate harm if instead of providing misleading explanations of fixed predictions they are flagged to model owners or auditors.

These issues stem from an oversight of \emph{actionability}\todo{what is "oversight of actionability"? rephrase} -- how we can change the features of a model. On the one hand, models assign fixed predictions because they use features that can only be changed in specific ways. \todo{rephrase} On the other, we are unable to detect these instances through feature attribution methods because they are designed to explain a prediction, rather than how it can be changed. 

\paragraph{Accounting for Actionability}

Given these challenges, we introduce machinery to capture how features can be changed at the instance level. For example, a change in one feature might necessitate a change in another; this makes strictly independent changes to certain features infeasible. 

\newcommand{\nullaction}{\bm{\emptyset}}

\begin{definition}
An \emph{action} is a vector $\ab = [a_1, \ldots, a_d] \in \R^d$ that a person can perform to change their features from $\xp$ to $\xp + \ab = \xq \in \X$. Given a point $\xp \in \X$, the \emph{action set} $\Aset{\xp}$ contains all possible actions for $\xp$. We assume that every action set contains the \emph{null action} $\nullaction \in \Aset{\xp}$. 
\end{definition}
Action sets captures how we can change features from a given point as a set of \emph{actionability constraints}. As shown in \cref{Table::ActionabilityConstraintsCatalog}, we can elicit complex constraints from human experts in natural language, and convert them into equations that we can embed into an optimization problem. In this way, we can enforce actionability in -- for example -- algorithms to find recourse actions~\citep[see e.g.,][]{ustun2019actionable,kothari2024prediction}.

To highlight features that are responsive, we must assign a score to features that accounts for actionability constraints. In practice, the actionability constraints for a given feature will include constraints that pertain to the feature as well as other features. We refer to the features that may change as a result of interventions on feature $j$ as \emph{downstream features}, $\MCF{j}$.
\begin{definition}
\label{Def::SingleFeatureActionSet}
    Given an action set $\Aset{\xp}$ for a point $\xp \in \X$, the \emph{action set} for feature $j \in \intrange{d}$ is:
    \begin{align*}
    \Ajset{\xp}{j} := \{ \ab \in \Aset{\xp} ~|~ \ab_j \neq 0 \wedge \ab_k  = 0, k \in \intrange{d} \setminus \MCF{j} \}.
    \end{align*}
    Here, the downstream set $\MCF{j} := \{k \in \intrange{d} \setminus \{j\} ~|~ \ab_j \neq 0 \implies \ab_k \neq 0 ~~ \forall \ab \in \Aset{\xb} \}$ is the subset of all features that must change as a result of interventions on feature $j$.
\end{definition}
\cref{Def::SingleFeatureActionSet} captures cases where actions on a feature can induce changes in other features. Such cases can stem from deterministic causal relationships -- e.g., increasing $\textfn{years\_of\_account\_history}$ should lead to a commensurate change in $\textfn{age}$. In general, they can capture dependencies that would not be included in a traditional causal graph -- e.g., changing a categorical attribute will require switching a binary feature ``off'' while turning another binary feature ``on'' (so that $x_j = 1 \to 0 \implies x_j' = 0 \to 1$). 



\section{Measuring Feature Responsiveness}

\label{Sec::ResponsivenessScores}

In this section, we introduce our main technical contribution -- the \emph{responsiveness score}. We first define the responsiveness score, then discuss its interpretation and computation.

\subsection{Responsiveness Scores}
Our goal is to measure the \emph{responsiveness} of the prediction of a model at a point $\xp$ with respect to the set of feasible actions on specific features. We propose to measure the sensitivity for each feature through the \emph{feature responsiveness score}.
\begin{definition}
\label{Def::ResponsiveScore}
Given a model $\clf{}:\X\to\Y$, a point $\xp$ with action set $\Aset{\xp}$ and feature $j\in [d]$, the \emph{responsiveness score} for feature $j$ is defined as:
\begin{align*}
    \rscore{j}{\xp \mid \clf{}, \Aset{\xp}} := \Pr{\clf{\xq} = 1 \mid \xq = \xp + \ab, \ab \in \Ajset{\xp}{j}}
\end{align*}
\end{definition}
The responsive score for a feature $j$ captures the proportion of single-feature actions on feature $j$ that change the prediction of a model $\clf{}$ at $\xp$. In what follows, we write $\rscore{j}{\xb}$ instead of $\rscore{j}{\xb \mid \clf{}, \Aset{\xp}}$ when $\clf{}$ and $\Aset{\xp}$ are clear from context. Given a feature where $\rscore{j}{\xp} = p$, we know that $100(p)\%$ of the single-feature actions on $j$, $\ab \in \Ajset{\xp}{j}$ will change the prediction of the model. Thus, all actions to a feature where $\rscore{j}{\xp} = 0$ would not change the prediction while all actions on a feature where $\rscore{j}{\xp} = 1$ would result in a different prediction. 

These interpretations are contingent on the actionability constraints used to compute the responsiveness score. In the simplest case, actionability constraints encode indisputable constraints on how a feature can be changed (e.g., feature encoding or physical limits) and so the responsiveness score for a given feature represent an upper bound on responsiveness: ``at most $100\rscore{j}{\xp}$\% of single-feature actions on feature $j$ attain a desired prediction.'' Such constraints let us flag undeniable instances of harm.
More generally, actionability constraints encode information about how other features are expected to vary when a single feature is changed. For example, if a model has a feature indicating the \textfn{job rank} of an individual, we can create actionability constraints that encode the expectation that if job rank increases, so does \textfn{income}.

\paragraph{Safeguards for Consumer Protection}

One benefit of responsiveness scores is that we can reliably use them to detect when consumers are assigned fixed predictions, and when feature-based explanations can provide recourse.
\begin{remark}
    \label{Rem::Recourse}
    Given a model $\clf{}: \X \to \Y$, let $\rscore{1}{\xp},\ldots, \rscore{d}{\xp}$ denote the responsiveness scores of $\xp \in \X$ with respect to the action set $\Aset{\xp}.$ 
    \begin{enumerate}[label={\alph*)}, itemsep=0pt, leftmargin=*]
        \item If $\rscore{j}{\xp} > 0$ for some feature $j\in \intrange{d}$, then $\clf{}$ can provide recourse to $\xp$ through a single-feature action on $j$. \label{SG::GreenLight}
        \item If $\rscore{j}{\xp} = 0$ for all features $j\in \intrange{d}$, then either: 
        \begin{enumerate*}[label={(\roman*)}]
        \item $\clf{}$ assigns a fixed prediction to $\xp$, or \label{SG::RedLightFixed}
        \item $\clf{}$ can only provide recourse to $\xp$ through actions that alter two or more features. 
        \label{SG::RedLightJoint}
        \end{enumerate*}
        \label{SG::RedLight}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{remark}
\cref{Rem::Recourse}\ref{SG::GreenLight} states that every person ($\xp$) who receives a positive responsiveness score for at least one feature has recourse. This implies that when we construct feature-highlighting explanations using the top-$k$ responsiveness scores, we will \emph{only} provide explanations to individuals who have recourse.
\cref{Rem::Recourse}\ref{SG::RedLight} also illustrates how the responsiveness scores can flag for potential harm when $\rscore{j}{\xp} = 0$ and allows us to mitigate harm on a case by case basis. In case \ref{SG::RedLightFixed} -- where a person is assigned fixed predictions -- we would refrain from providing explanations to avoid misleading consumers, and flag the issue so that model development can be potentially revisited. In case \ref{SG::RedLightJoint} -- where a person is assigned predictions that can change through multiple actions -- we could provide explanations that highlight subsets of responsive features, include explicit warning against presumptions of feature independence, or proceed in a similar manner to case \ref{SG::RedLightFixed}.

\end{figure}

We compute responsiveness scores using a \emph{reachable set}:
\begin{definition}
\label{Def::ReachableSet}
Given a point $\xp$ and its action set $\Aset{\xp}$, we refer to the set of all points that are attainable through actions in $\Aset{\xb}$ as the \emph{reachable set}:
$\Rset{\xp} := \{ \xp + \ab \mid \ab \in \Aset{\xp} \}$.
We refer to the subset of points that are reachable through actions on feature $j \in \intrange{d}$ as the \emph{reachable set} for feature $j$ and denote it as:
$\Rjset{\xp}{j} := \{\xp + \ab' \mid \ab' \in \Ajset{\xp}{j} \}$.
\end{definition}
Reachable sets represents an alternative way to store and process information about actionability at the instance level. In particular, a reachable set $\Rset{\xp}$ encodes this information as a set of feature vectors that can be reached through feasible actions. Given reachable sets for each feature $\Rjset{\xp}{j}$ for $j \in \intrange{d}$, we can calculate responsiveness scores (see \cref{Def::ResponsiveScore}) for a model by querying its predictions (see \cref{Fig::ResponsivenessComputation}). This has the benefits that: (1) it can work with any model; (2) we only need to compute the reachable set once; and (3) it can allow us to evaluate other notions of responsiveness.
 
\paragraph{Enumeration for Discrete Features}

When dealing with discrete feature spaces, we can obtain a complete reachable set through enumeration following an approach of \citet{kothari2024prediction}. Given a point $\xp$ and its action set $\Aset{\xp}$, this approach returns a set of all reachable points. Given a complete reachable set, we can calculate the responsiveness score of feature $j$ by evaluating the model $\clf{}$ on points reachable via single-feature actions on $j$ (see \cref{Fig::ResponsivenessComputation}). 
This approach can certify when an individual cannot change the prediction of a model. Unfortunately, the reachable set grows exponentially with the number of actionable features, which can lead to practical challenges in storage and compute.\footnote{\citet{kothari2024prediction} propose to enumerate reachable sets for subsets of features that can be altered independently. In practice, this can allow construction of reachable set for real-world classifcation datasets with discrete features. However, it may require considerable compute and storage. For example, on the \textds{heloc} dataset which contains 31 actionable features and 5,616 distinct points, enumerating all reachable sets requires roughly 1 hour and storing them requires 18.8GB}

\paragraph{Sampling for Continuous Features}

\newcommand{\rscorehat}[2]{\hat{\mu}_{#1}({#2})}
\newcommand{\rscoretilde}[2]{\tilde{\mu}_{#1}({#2})}
\newcommand{\success}[0]{S}
\newcommand{\sampsize}[0]{N}

The enumeration technique described above is infeasible when we wish to evaluate the responsiveness of a continuous feature, or when a feature has downstream effects on continuous features. In such cases, we estimate the responsiveness score for feature $j$ by drawing a uniform sample of reachable points from $\Rjset{\xp}{j}.$ 
Given a feature without downstream effects -- i.e., without downstream features so that $|\MCF{j}| = 0$ -- we can sample values from $[l_j, u_j]$. When features have  downstream effects, we can apply a rejection sampling procedure where we first sample values for all features from $[l_j, u_j]$ and reject values that violate actionability constraints.

\begin{definition}
\label{Def::EstimatedRScore}
    Given a point $\xp \in \X$, let $\Rjhat{\xp}{j}$ denote a sample of $\sampsize{}$ points drawn uniformly from the reachable set for feature $j$, $\Rjset{\xp}{j}$.
    Given any model $\clf{}: \X \to \Y$, we can estimate the responsiveness score for feature $j$ as 
    \begin{align*}
    \rscorehat{j}{\xp} := \frac{1}{\sampsize} \sum_{\xq \in \Rjhat{\xp}{j}} \indic{\clf{\xq} = 1}
    \end{align*}
\end{definition}
When the samples are drawn uniformly from $\Rjset{\xp}{j}$, the number of responsive predictions $\success \sim \textrm{Bin}(\sampsize, \rscore{j}{\xp})$. We can then quantify the uncertainty associated with our estimate $\rscorehat{j}{\xp}$:
\begin{proposition}
\label{Prop::CI}
    Given a level of significance $\alpha \in (0,1)$, the $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval for $\rscore{j}{\xp}$ is:
    \begin{align*}
        \rscoretilde{j}{\xp} \; \pm \; \kappa \sqrt{\frac{1}{\tilde{\sampsize}} \rscoretilde{j}{\xp}(1-\rscoretilde{j}{\xp})}
    \end{align*}
    Here: $\kappa := \Phi^{-1}(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2})$, $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the Normal CDF,
    and $\rscoretilde{j}{\xp} := \frac{1}{\sampsize + \kappa^2} \left( S + \frac{\kappa^2}{2}\right)$ is the corrected estimator.
\end{proposition}
We can alter two parameters to adjust the uncertainty: the level of significance $\alpha$ and the sample size $\sampsize$. For a fixed $\sampsize$, a larger $\alpha$ will yield a narrower confidence interval at a lower confidence level. Similarly, with a fixed $\alpha$, we can adjust $\sampsize$ to attain a desired level of certainty for the estimate $\rscorehat{j}{\xp}$. For example, given $\alpha = 0.05$, $\sampsize > 42$ would ensure that the width of the confidence interval surrounding $\rscorehat{j}{\xp}$ is at most 0.1 when we don't observe any points with the target prediction -- i.e. $\success = 0$. Note that we use a correction upon the standard binomial confidence interval formulation to improve coverage when $\rscore{j}{\xp} = 0$ or $1$~\citep[see][]{brown2001interval}.

This is a general-purpose approach that can compute responsiveness scores for features that are continuous or discrete. In settings where we are computing the responsiveness score of a discrete feature, we can reap the benefits of responsiveness using a small sample of reachable points. This approach avoids the computation and storage costs of enumeration but sacrifices the ability to identify individuals with fixed predictions with complete certainty.

\paragraph{Discussion}

One of the benefits of working with reachable sets is that they can readily be extended to handle other desiderata by weighing and filtering reachable points. As an example, we can highlight features that are robust to changes through a \emph{robust} variant defined below.
\begin{definition}
\label{Def::RobustScore}
Given a model $\clf{}:\X\to\Y$, a point $\xp$ with action set $\Aset{\xp}$ and feature $j\in [d]$, the \emph{robust responsiveness score} for feature $j$ measures the probability that an action on feature $j$ will flip the prediction regardless of the actions on other features:
\begin{align*}
    \rbscore{j}{\xp \mid \clf{}, \Aset{\xp}} := 
    \Pr{\clf{\xq} = 1 \mid \xq = \xp + \ab, \ab_j \neq 0, \ab \in \Aset{x}}
\end{align*}
\end{definition}
This variant can measure the prevalence of actions that would change a prediction even as other features change~\citep[see e.g.,][]{nguyen2023distributionally}. We can calculate the robust responsiveness score by enumerating a subset of the entire reachable set $\Rset{\xp}$ containing points reachable by all actions that alter feature $j$.

\paragraph{Results}

We summarize the viability of promoting recourse using feature-highlighting explanations in \cref{Table::DatasetModelMetrics}, and the responsiveness of explanations from each method in \cref{Table::MainMetrics}. We evaluate explanations built using the top-4 scoring features from each method, which reflects the recommended number of reasons to include in an adverse action notice required by the U.S. Equal Credit Opportunity Act~\citep[see][]{cfpb2024regBcomment,barocas2020hidden}.

Our results in \cref{Table::DatasetModelMetrics} show that models admit features that allow \emph{some} individuals to change them to attain a desired prediction (29.8\% to 93.2\% across models and datasets). At the same time, they reveal their potential to mislead individuals who are assigned fixed predictions (i.e., 0.2\% to 49.1\% across all models and datasets).
For example, given the \LR{} model for the \textds{heloc} dataset, we would present an explanation to 56.1\% of individuals who are a denied loan. Among them, 44.4\% can achieve recourse through single-feature actions; 35.6\% can only achieve recourse through joint actions; and 19.1\% have no path to recourse because they receive a fixed prediction.

\paragraph{On Responsiveness Scores}
Our results in \cref{Table::MainMetrics} show how our approach can support consumers by presenting responsive features and by flagging instances where explanations may be misleading. Explanations are only provided to individuals who can achieve recourse through a single-feature action, and are given to \emph{all} such individuals (the values for \emph{\% Presented with Reasons} in \cref{Table::MainMetrics} match the values for \emph{\% 1-D Rec} in \cref{Table::DatasetModelMetrics}). When we construct feature-based explanations using responsiveness scores, we present individuals with explanations that only contain responsive features, achieving 100\% on the \emph{\% All Reasons Responsive} metric across datasets and models. This may result in explanations that highlight fewer reasons on average—for example, individuals receiving explanations from the \LR{} model on \textds{german} receive 1.9 out of 4 reasons on average. This behavior can mitigate harm as we avoid presenting explanations to individuals with fixed predictions or those who require joint actions to change their outcomes.

\paragraph{On Feature Attribution Scores}

Our results show how standard methods for feature attribution can output explanations that are ineffective and potentially misleading. For example, under the \LR{} model for the \textds{heloc} dataset, we find that 82\% and 75.6\% of explanations from \lime{} and \shap{} include 4/4 unresponsive features respectively. This behavior arises as a result of algorithm design, as the scores do not account for responsiveness nor actionability. This results in two key problems:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0pt, label={},topsep=0pt]

\item \emph{Low Scores for Responsive Features:} Methods can assign low scores to responsive features. On the \textds{heloc} dataset, for example, 44.4\% of denied individuals by the \LR{} model can achieve recourse by altering a single feature. However, explanations built using \lime{} and \shap{} fail to include them since their scoring mechanisms do not account for feature responsiveness. For instance, an individual could achieve recourse by acting on \textfn{NumRevolvingTrades}, but a feature-based explanation produced by \lime{} does not include it, as it assigns higher scores to four other features that are unresponsive. We also observe this phenomenon beyond the top-4 features in \cref{Fig::Rank}.

\item \emph{Reasons without Recourse:} Methods provide explanations to individuals with fixed predictions. On the \textds{heloc} dataset, the \LR{} model assigns a fixed prediction to 19.1\% of denied individuals. In such cases, \lime{} and \shap{}, and their variants offer explanations, even though it is impossible for them to achieve recourse. These explanations may mislead individuals by highlighting features that are salient to the prediction and could be changed, but would not lead to recourse. For example, an explanation from \shap{} for an individual with a fixed prediction includes \textfn{AvgYearsInFile} and \textfn{NetFractionRevolvingBurden} -- both of which are mutable but not actionable.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{On Adapting Existing Methods}

Seeing how responsiveness is inherently tied to actionability, we study the potential to improve responsiveness through \emph{action-aware} variants of \shap{} and \lime{} -- \shapaa{} and \limeaa{}. 
We consider a simple post-processing strategy where we only construct explanations using features that are mutable. This reflects a common belief surrounding actionability is that we can find ways to account for it by post-processing~\citep[e.g.,][]{mothilal2020explaining,karimi2020model}. 
Our results show this approach can improve the responsiveness of explanations – as we observe marginal improvements when switching from \shap{} and \lime{} to \shapaa{} and \limeaa{} for all models and datasets in \cref{Table::MainMetrics}.
For example, when we provide explanations for \LR{} model on \textds{heloc}, switching from \shap{} to \shapaa{} improves the proportion of explanations that contain at least one responsive feature from $24.4\%$ to $35.3\%.$ As a result, more consumers could achieve recourse based on explanations with at least one responsive feature.
We observe \shapaa{}'s increased tendency to return responsive features at higher ranks in \cref{Fig::Rank} as well: the distribution of the \% of times the feature was responsive at each rank shifts slightly upward when we adapt \shap{} to \shapaa{}.
Nevertheless, \shapaa{} and \limeaa{} explanations still contain unresponsive reasons. In the \textds{heloc} dataset, \shapaa{} and \limeaa{} returned explanations where every reason is responsive 0.2\% of the time under the \LR{} model. In other words, 98\% of the explanations given to denied consumers contained at least one unresponsive feature. This occurs because \limeaa{} and \shapaa{} still suffer from the same drawbacks as their original counterparts, albeit to a lesser extent. They attribute scores to features that are not responsive when there are other responsive features or have exhausted the list of such features. Overall, these results show that they still fall short in providing responsive explanations.

\section{Concluding Remarks}

\label{Sec::ConcludingRemarks}

Explanations are often seen as a strategy to protect individuals from harm when machine learning models are applied in domains like lending and hiring. Our work reveals how this strategy can backfire by highlighting unresponsive features and overlooking fixed predictions. We find that common feature attribution methods exhibit both of these failure modes, leading to situations where consumers are given reasons without recourse. Our work addresses these limitations by developing a feature attribution method that measures \emph{responsiveness}—i.e., the probability that a feature can be changed in a way that leads to recourse. These scores can readily replace the scores currently used to comply with regulations. In doing so, we can strengthen consumer protection by highlighting features that enable recourse when possible and flagging instances where recourse is unattainable. Our results demonstrate the benefits of developing standalone methods to address specific goals—whether for recourse, rectification, or anti-discrimination. By adopting specialized approaches, we can achieve more effective consumer protection.

\paragraph{Limitations}
The main limitations of our work stem from assumptions about actionability and responsiveness. Our approach relies on the validity of actionability assumptions within an action set. When defining this set to encode indisputable constraints, as in \cref{Sec::Experiments}, responsiveness scores can flag individuals with fixed predictions. However, presented features may not achieve recourse due to individual constraints. To mitigate this, we can highlight features achieving a threshold responsiveness or elicit constraints from decision subjects~\citep[see e.g.,][]{esfahani2024exploiting,de2022personalized,koh2024understanding}.
A broader limitation is that our machinery only represents a subset of constraints considered in causal algorithmic recourse literature. It can represent cases with deterministic causal effects but excludes scenarios where interventions induce probabilistic effects on downstream features~\citep{karimi2021interventions,dominguez2022adversarial,von2022fairness}. In principle, our approach can incorporate such assumptions: given an individual probabilistic graphical model, we can compute a responsiveness score reflecting the expected recourse rate. The key challenge lies in validating causal assumptions at an individual level. This reflects a practical bottleneck that requires further study and may require an approach to measure responsiveness in a way that is robustness to misspecifcation.
