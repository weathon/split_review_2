\title{How the Level Sampling Process impacts Zero-Shot Generalisation in Deep Reinforcement Learning}

\begin{abstract}
A key limitation preventing the wider adoption of autonomous agents trained via deep reinforcement learning (RL) is their limited ability to generalise to new environments, even when these share similar characteristics with environments encountered during training. In this work, we investigate how a non-uniform sampling strategy of individual environment instances, or levels,  affects the zero-shot generalisation (ZSG) ability of RL agents, considering two failure modes: overfitting and over-generalisation. As a first step, we measure the mutual information ($\mut$) between the agent's internal representation and the set of training levels, which we find to be well-correlated to instance overfitting. In contrast to uniform sampling, adaptive sampling strategies prioritising levels based on their value loss are more effective at maintaining lower $\mut$, which provides a novel theoretical justification for this class of techniques. We then turn our attention to unsupervised environment design (UED) methods, which adaptively \textit{generate} new training levels and minimise $\mut$ more effectively than methods sampling from a fixed set. However, we find UED methods significantly \textit{shift} the training distribution, resulting in over-generalisation and worse ZSG performance over the distribution of interest. To prevent both instance overfitting and over-generalisation, we introduce \textit{self-supervised environment design} (SSED). SSED generates levels using a variational autoencoder, effectively reducing $\mut$ while minimising the shift with the distribution of interest, and leads to statistically significant improvements in ZSG over fixed-set level sampling strategies and UED methods.

\end{abstract}

\section{introduction}




A central challenge facing modern reinforcement learning (RL) is learning policies capable of zero-shot transfer of learned behaviours to a wide range of environment settings. Prior applications of RL algorithms \citep{rubiks,CLrobloc,fastCLrobloc} indicate that strong zero-shot generalisation (ZSG) capabilities can be achieved by employing an adaptive sampling strategy over the set of environment instances available during training, which we refer to as the set of training \textit{levels}. However the relationship between ZSG and the level sampling process remains poorly understood. In this work, we draw novel connections between this process and the minimisation of an upper bound on the generalisation error derived by \cite{instance_invariant}, which depends on the \textit{mutual information} (MI) between the agent's internal representation and the identity of individual training levels.

To build an understanding of the relationship between MI and ZSG, consider the minimal gridworld navigation example in \Cref{fig:CMDPex}. A ``shortcut'' exists in level (a), and a model with high MI is able to first predict the level identity from its initial observation and follow a level-specific policy, which is optimal over the training set. When deployed on (c) the model will predict it is in (a) since under the agent's restricted field of view (a) and (c) share the same initial observation. As a result the agent will attempt to follow the (a)-specific policy, which will not transfer. An agent learning level-specific policies implies high MI between its internal representation and the level identities, and in general, will not transfer zero-shot to new levels. We discover that the reduced generalisation error achieved by adaptive level sampling strategies over uniform sampling can be attributed to their effectiveness in reducing the MI between the agent's internal representation and the level identity. In particular, we find that strategies de-prioritising levels with low value loss, as first proposed in prioritised level replay \citep[PLR,][]{PLR}, effectively minimise mutual information by avoiding training on levels in which the value can be accurately estimated through level identification.

While adaptive sampling strategies help reduce mutual information, our experiments indicate that they are ultimately limited by the number of training levels. A natural extension is to \textit{augment} the set of training levels to further reduce the mutual information and the generalisation error upper bound. We consider the setting in which we are provided with a set of environment parameters $X$, each $\vx \in X$ instantiating a level within a parametrisable simulator. These parameters may consist of a set of values, a configuration file or any other modality specific to the simulator. For example, these may be numerical arrays describing the gridworld layouts in \Cref{fig:CMDPex} or 3D scans of indoor environments \citep{OpenRooms}. The later is likely to be expensive to collect and thus limited in supply, as in most other practical applications. It is also likely to be high dimensional, highly structured data, and would make random or unsupervised generation methods ill-suited for augmenting this supply (and we see in our experiments that these methods can be ill-suited even in settings as simple as the aforementioned gridworld). Instead, we introduce the \textit{Self-Supervised Environment Design} (SSED) framework, which uses the information present in $X$ to generate the augmented set of level parameters $\tilde{X}$. 
While an augmented set effectively minimises the mutual information, it may not result in better generalisation performance if $\tilde{X}$ and $X$ are drawn from different distributions. In fact, we show it can induce a form of \textit{over-generalisation}, in which the agent learns to solve levels incompatible with the targeted task, and performs poorly at test time. There is therefore a trade-off between augmenting $X$ to prevent \textit{instance-overfitting}, i.e. not learning level-specific policies, and ensuring that $\tilde{X}$ and $X$ come from similar distributions to avoid distributional shift and over-generalisation. In our experiments, we show that SSED is able to strike this trade-off more effectively than when training over the original level set, even adaptively, or when training on augmented sets obtained using previously proposed environment design methods. We demonstrate that SSED leads to statistically significant improvements in the agent's ZSG capabilities, reaching 125\% of the returns achieved by the next best baseline on held-out test levels, and a 200\% to 300\% improvement on levels with an increased difficulty with respect to the training distribution.


\section{related work}

\textbf{Buffer-free level sampling strategies.} Domain randomisation \citep[DR,][]{DR, DR1997}, is one of the earliest proposed methods for improving the generalisation ability of RL agents by augmenting the set of available training levels, and does so by sampling uniformly between manually specified ranges of environment parameters. Subsequent contributions introduce an implicit prioritisation over the generated set by inducing a minimax return \citep[robust adversarial RL][]{Pinto2017RobustAdversarialRL} or a minimax regret \citep[unsupervised environment design (UED),][]{PAIRED} game between the agent and a \textit{level generator}, which are trained concurrently. These adversarial formulations prioritise levels in which the agent is currently performing poorly and aim to result in robust generalisation over the sampled set, with UED achieving better theoretical guarantees at equilibrium. However UED and adversarial RL require a distinct, domain-specific RL environment for task generation, and suffer from poor sample efficiency and from training instability due to their adversarial training procedure, leading them to be outperformed by well tuned DR implementations \citep{Robust-PLR}. CLUTR \citep{CLUTR} improves the sample efficiency of the original UED algorithm by having the level generator operate within the latent space of a VAE pre-trained on randomly sampled level parameters. However \cite{CLUTR} acknowledge that CLUTR remains outperformed by DR and buffer-based UED methods based on random level generation, and we will show that this is not the case for SSED.

\textbf{Buffer-based level sampling strategies.} Prioritised Level Replay \cite{PLR} focuses exclusively on the level sampling strategy, empirically demonstrating $\ell_1$-value loss prioritisation over a \textit{level buffer} to be the most effective in minimising the generalisation gap and improving test scores. Robust PLR \citep[RPLR,][]{Robust-PLR} extends PLR to the UED setting and propose the \textit{Dual Curriculum Design} (DCD) framework, in which the agent is exclusively trained on levels sampled from the PLR buffer, where new levels are periodically proposed by a level generator and only included in the buffer if they score higher than its lowest scoring level. RPLR employs DR as its level generation mechanism, whereas ACCEL \citep{ACCEL}, its direct extension and the current state-of-the-art, gradually evolves new levels by performing random edits on high scoring levels in the buffer. SAMPLR proposes to maintain a prioritised level distribution while eliminating curriculum-induced covariate shift over the ground truth distribution of level parameters. SAMPLR is therefore close to SSED in its scope, but employs a different approach, resampling individual \textit{transitions} using a second simulator, in order to remain consistent with the ground truth. Due to the different approaches taken, SSED and SAMPLR are best viewed not as competitors but as \textit{complementary} methods: while SSED learns a model of the level parameter distribution and minimises distributional shift induced by the level generation phase, SAMPLR assumes full access to the ground truth level parameter distribution, and eliminates the covariate shift induced by the prioritisation strategy, by modifying \textit{individual transitions} using a second simulator that runs in parallel. Lastly, SAMPLR is only applicable to settings in which the ground truth and training distributions share the same support, making it not easily applicable without privileged access to the ground truth distribution. 

\textbf{Mutual-information minimisation in RL.} In prior work, mutual information has been minimised in order to mitigate instance-overfitting, either by learning an ensemble of policies \citep{instance_invariant,E-POMDP}, performing data augmentation on observations \citep{raileanu2021UCB-DrAC,Kostrikov2021ImageAllYouNeed}, an auxiliary objective \citep{mhairiCMID} or introducing information bottlenecks through selective noise injection on the agent model \citep{vib_actor_critic,coinrun}. In contrast, our work is the first to consider mutual-information minimisation through adaptive level sampling and generation strategies.



\section{preliminaries}

We model an individual level as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) $\langle A, O, S, \mathcal{T}, \Omega, R, p_0, \gamma \rangle$ where $A$ is the action space, $O$ is the observation space, $S$ is the set of states, $\mathcal{T} : S \times A \rightarrow \Delta (S)$ and $\Omega: S \rightarrow \Delta (O)$ are the transition and observation functions (we use $\Delta (\cdot)$ to indicate these functions map to distributions in the stochastic setting), $R:S\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is the reward function, $p_0(s)$ is the initial state distribution and $\gamma$ is the discount factor. We employ the episodic RL setting, in which the agent attempts to learn a policy $\pi$ maximising the expected discounted return $V^\pi (s_t) = \mathbb{E}_\pi[\sum^T_{\bar{t}=t}\gamma^{t - \bar{t}} r_t]$ over an episode terminating at timestep $T$, where $s_t$ and $r_t$ are the state and reward at step $t$. We use $V^\pi$ to refer to $V^\pi (s_0)$, the expected episodic returns taken from the first timestep of the episode. In this work, we focus on on-policy actor-critic algorithms \citep{A3C,DDPG,PPO} representing the agent policy $\pi_\vtheta$ and value estimate $\hat{V}_\vtheta$ with neural networks (we use the $\vtheta$ subscript to refer to their respective weights). The policy and value networks usually share an intermediate state representation $b_\vtheta(o_t)$ (or for recurrent architectures $b_\vtheta(H^o_t)$, $H^o_t = \{o_0,\cdots,o_t\}$ being the history of observations $o_i$).

Following \cite{kirk2023survey}, we model the set of environment instances we aim to generalise over as a Contextual-MDP (CMDP) $\mathcal{M} = \langle A, O, S, \mathcal{T}, \Omega, R,  p_0(s|\rvx), \gamma, X_C, p(\rvx)\rangle$, $p(\rvx)$ being a distribution defined over the context set $X_C$, where each element $\vx \in X_C$ instantiates a \textit{level} $\lvl_\vx$ of the CMDP with initial state distribution $p_0(s|\rvx)$. For clarity, we will refer to a set of levels as $L$ and to their associated level parameters as $X$. The CMDP may be viewed as a POMDP, except that the reward, transition and observation functions now also depend on $X_C$, that is $\mathcal{T} : S \times X_C \times A \rightarrow \Delta (S)$, $\Omega: S \times X_C \rightarrow \Delta (O)$, $R:S \times X_C \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. In the ZSG setting we wish to obtain the policy $\pi^* \in \argmax_\pi \mathbb{E}_{\rvx \sim p(\rvx)} [V^\pi_{\lvl_\rvx}]$, with $V^\pi_{\lvl_\vx}$ being the expected return of $\pi$ in level $\lvl_\vx$  without the agent observing $\rvx$. As in the UED setting, we assume access to a parametrisable simulator to instantiate individual CMDP levels, or in other words we assume $X_C \subset \sX$, $\sX$ being the level parameter space of the simulator. We distinguish $\sX$ from the subset instantiating \textit{solvable} levels $\sX^* = \{\vx \in \sX | \exists \pi \in \Pi \text{ s.t } V^\pi_{i_\vx} = V^*_{i_\vx}\}$, $\Pi$ being the policy space and $V^*_{i_\vx}$ the maximum possible return in level $\lvl_\vx$. While the UED framework assumes $X_C = \sX^*$, that is any level in $\sX^*$ belongs to the target CMDP, we consider the more general setting in which there may be more than one CMDP within $\sX^*$, and we aim to solve a specific target CMDP with $X_C \subseteq \sX^*$. We refer to levels with parameters $\vx \in X_C$ as \textit{in-context} and to levels with parameters $\vx \in \sX^* \setminus X_C$ as \textit{out-of-context}. As we show in our experiments, out-of-context levels may cause the agent to learn a different policy than the optimal CMDP policy if they get included in the training set, causing a form of over-generalisation.

We start training with access to a limited set of levels $X_{\text{train}}\subset X_C$ sampled from $p(\rvx)$, and we evaluate the agent's ZSG capabilities on a set of held-out levels $X_{\text{test}}$, also sampled from $p(\rvx)$. As such, we can obtain a formulation for the generalisation gap reminiscent of supervised learning
\begin{equation} \label{eq:GenGap}
    \text{GenGap}(\pi) \coloneqq \frac{1}{|X_{\text{train}}|} \sum_{\vx \in X_{\text{train}}} V^\pi_{\lvl_\vx} - \frac{1}{|X_{\text{test}}|}\sum_{\vx \in X_{\text{test}}} V^\pi_{\lvl_\vx}.
\end{equation}
Using this formulation, \cite{instance_invariant} extend generalisation results in the supervised setting \citep{MI_gengap_SL} to derive an upper bound for the generalisation gap in the ZSG setting. 
\begin{theorem}\label{th:gen_gap}
  For any CMDP such that $|V^\pi (s)| \leq D/2, \forall s, \pi$, then for any set of training levels $L$, and policy model $\pi$ we have
\begin{equation}
   \text{GenGap}(\pi) \leq \sqrt{\frac{2D^2}{|L|} \times \mut(L, \pi)}.
\end{equation}   
\end{theorem}
With $\mut (L, \pi) = \sum_{\lvl\in L} \mut(\lvl, \pi)$ being the mutual information between $\pi$ and the identity of each level $\lvl \in L$. In this work, we test the hypothesis that minimising the bound in \Cref{th:gen_gap} is an effective surrogate objective for reducing the generalisation gap. We first investigate how to minimise $\mut (i, \pi)$ through an adaptive sampling strategy over $L$. To do so, we study the class of techniques derived from PLR \citep{PLR}, which compute individual level scores from rollout trajectories $\tau_i$ using a scoring function $\textbf{score}(\tau_i, \pi)$. Level scores are then used to define an adaptive sampling distribution over a level buffer $\Lambda$, with
\begin{equation}
    P_\Lambda = (1 - \rho) \cdot P_S + \rho \cdot P_C,
\end{equation}
where $P_S$ is a distribution parametrised by the level scores and $\rho$ is a staleness coefficient, mixing $P_S$ with a staleness distribution $P_C$ prioritising levels replayed less recently. \cite{PLR} experiment with different scoring functions, and find that selecting a scoring function based on the $\ell_1$-value loss $S^V_i = \textbf{score}(\tau_i, \pi) = 1 / T \sum_{t=0}^T |\hat{V}_t - V_t |$, and defining $P_S$ using rank prioritisation over the scores $S^V_i$, incurs a significant reduction in the generalisation gap at test time. However, \cite{PLR} do not provide a theoretical connection between their prioritisation strategy and the generalisation gap. In the remaining sections, we will draw connections between this particular prioritisation strategy and the minimisation of $\mut (L, \pi)$. We then introduce SSED, a level generation and sampling framework training the agent over an augmented set of levels. SSED minimises the generalisation bound surrogate more aggressively, as it reduces $\mut (L, \pi)$ while increasing the size of $L$.



\section{mutual-information minimisation under a fixed set of levels}




We first investigate how to minimise the bound in \Cref{th:gen_gap} when we are restricted to sample levels $L$ with parameters $\vx \in X_{\text{train}}$. In other words $L$ remains fixed and we can only act on the bound by minimising $\mut (L,\pi)$, using a sampling distribution $P_\Lambda$ of our choosing over $L$. The following lemma enables us to derive an approximation for an upper bound for $\mut (L,\pi)$.

\begin{lemma} \label{th:mi_fixed_L}

    \begin{align}
        \mut (L,\pi) \leq \mut (L,b) &= \mathcal{H}(p(i)) + \sum_{i\in L} \int dh p(h, i) \log p(i|h) \\
        &\approx \mathcal{H}(p(i)) + \frac{1}{|B|} \sum_{(i, H_t^o) \in B} \log p(i|b(H_t^o)) \label{eq:empirical_approx2}
    \end{align}
    where $\mathcal{H}(p)$ is the entropy of distribution $p$ and $B$ is a batch of trajectories collected from individual levels $i \in L$.
\end{lemma}

To remain consistent with the CMDP we must set $p(i)$ to $p(\rvx)$, making the entropy $\mathcal{H}(p(i))$ in Lemma \ref{th:mi_fixed_L} a constant. However, the second term in \Cref{eq:empirical_approx2} depends on the learned representation function of our agent and is therefore determined by the training process. We can formulate a hypothesis that an uniform training distribution over $L$ is unlikely to systematically minimise the mutual information, whereas there should exist adaptive distributions capable of minimising this second term more effectively. We can reformulate the second term of \Cref{eq:empirical_approx2} as 
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{(i, H_t^o) \in B} \log p(i|b(H_t^o)) = \sum_{i\in L} \sum_{H_t^o \in \tau_i} \log p(i|b(H_t^o)).
\end{equation}
We can therefore approximates $\mut (L,b)$ (up to a constant) as the sum of negative cross-entropies of a classifier attempting to predict the level identity across a trajectory $\tau_i$. Sampling levels associated with highly negative classifier cross-entropies therefore results in less mutual information about the level identity being passed through the agent's representation for a batch of trajectories $B$.

We hypothesise that value loss prioritisation strategies induce a curriculum that maintains low levels of mutual information with the training set, which would explain the high test returns and low generalisation gap over uniform sampling reported by \cite{PLR}. The value loss employs the level-specific $V^\pi_i$ for its targets, which can be expressed as $V^\pi_i (s) = V^\pi (s) + v^\pi_i (s)$, where $v^\pi_i$ is a level-specific function, and therefore requires high $\mut (i, b)$ to estimate. As levels with high value loss have a significant prediction error for at least one of $V^\pi$ or $v^\pi_i$, the level scoring function $S^V$ will prioritise levels with either a high $V^\pi$ error, inducing a curriculum for the agent, and/or with low $\mut (i, b)$, minimising the generalisation gap. In the later stages of training, levels where $|V^\pi_i - \hat{V}^\pi|\approx 0$, %
are de-prioritised by $S^V$, preventing the agent from training further on levels its representation has already overfitted to.

To verify our hypothesis, we first introduce a secondary scoring strategy $S_2$ with associated distribution $P_{S_2}$, letting us \textit{mix} different sampling strategies and study their interaction. $P_\Lambda$ then becomes
\begin{equation} \label{eq:buffer_dist}
    P_\Lambda = (1 - \rho) \cdot ((1-\eta) \cdot  P_S + \eta \cdot P_{S_2}) + \rho \cdot P_C,
\end{equation}
with $\eta$ being a mixing parameter. We then compare different sampling strategies in Procgen \citep{procgen}, a benchmark of 16 games designed to measure generalisation in RL. We use the PPO \citep{PPO} baseline employed in the original publication, which uses a non-recurrent intermediate representation $b_\vtheta (o_t)$.

In \Cref{fig:procgen_results}, we compare the original $\ell_1$-value loss scoring strategy ($S=S^V, S_2=\varnothing$), uniform sampling ($P_S=\mathcal{U}(\cdot),S_2=\varnothing$), $\mathcal{U}(\cdot)$ being the uniform distribution, and a mutual information minimisation strategy ($S=S^{\mut}, S_2=\varnothing$) following the scoring strategy $S^{\mut}_i=\textbf{score}(\tau_i, \pi) = \sum_{t=0}^T \log p_\vtheta(\lvl|b(o_t))$, where $p_\vtheta$ is a linear classifier. 
We also compare $S^V$ with mixed strategies ($S=S^V, S_2=S^\mut$) and ($S=S^V, P_{S_2}=\mathcal{U}(\cdot)$). While ($S=S^V, P_{S_2}=\mathcal{U}(\cdot)$) results in a non-negligible increase in the generalisation gap and reduction in the test score, ($S=S^V, S_2=S^\mut$) does not change performance significantly. $S=S^V$ therefore appears to strike a good balance between sample efficiency and mutual information minimisation, without requiring an additional strategy explicitly minimising mutual information. On the other hand, $S^\mut$ is most effective at reducing the generalisation gap, demonstrating a statistically significant improvement over other methods across Procgen games, but achieves subpar performance on the training set, and only performs about as well as random sampling on the test set.



\section{self-supervised environment design}

We have established that certain adaptive sampling strategies effectively minimise mutual information between the agent's learned representation and the identity of training levels, which in turn reduces the generalisation gap. However our experiments in \Cref{sec:procgen} indicate that significant levels of mutual information remain when training the agent over a fixed level set, even with a sampling strategy specifically designed to minimise said information. We now introduce SSED, a framework designed to more aggressively minimise the generalisation bound in \Cref{th:gen_gap} by jointly minimising $\mut (L, \pi)$ and increasing $|L|$. SSED does so by generating an augmented set of training levels $\tilde{L}\supset L$, while still using a prioritised sampling strategy over this new augmented set. 

SSED shares UED's requirement of having access to a parametrisable simulator allowing the specification of levels through environment parameters $\vx$. In addition, we assume that we start with a limited set of levels $X_\text{train}$ sampled from $p(\rvx)$. SSED consists of two components: a \textit{generative phase}, in which a variational autoencoder (VAE) pre-trained on $X_\text{train}$ is employed as a generative model to obtain an augmented set $\tilde{X}$, and a \textit{replay phase}, in which we use an adaptive distribution $P_\Lambda$ to sample levels from $\tilde{L}$, a set containing levels instantiated by both sets $\tilde{X}$ and $X_\text{train}$. We alternate between the generative and replay phases, and only perform gradient updates on the agent during the replay phase, while the VAE weights remain fixed throughout training. \Cref{alg:SSED} describes the full SSED pipeline, and we provide further details on each phase below.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\centering
\caption{Self-Supervised Environment Design}\label{alg:SSED}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Input Pre-trained VAE encoder and decoder networks $\psi_{\vtheta_E}$, $\phi_{\vtheta_D}$, level parameters $X_\text{train}$, number of pairs $M$, number of interpolations per pair $K$
\State Initialise agent policy $\pi$ and level buffer $\Lambda$, adding level parameters in $X_\text{train}$ to $\Lambda$ 
\State Update $X_\text{train}$ with variational parameters $(\vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)_n \gets \psi_{\vtheta_E}(\vx_n)$ \textbf{for} $\vx_n$ in $X_\text{train}$
\While{\textit{not converged}} 
    \State Sample batch $X$ using $P_\Lambda$ \Comment{Replay phase} %
    \For{$\vx$ in $X$}
        \State Collect rollouts $\tau$ from $i_\vx$ and compute scores $S, S_2$
        \State Update $\pi$ according to $\tau$
        \State Update scores $S, S_2$ of $\vx$ in $\Lambda$
    \EndFor
    \State Randomly sample $2M$ $(\vx, \vmu, \vsigma)$ from $X_\text{train}$ and arrange them into $M$ pairs. 
    \For {$((\vx, \vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)_{i}, (\vx, \vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)_{j})$ in pairs} \Comment{Generative phase}
        \State Compute $K$ interpolations $\{(\vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)\}_{K}$ between $((\vx, \vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)_{i}, (\vx, \vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)_{j})$
        \For {$(\vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)_k$ in $\{(\vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)\}_{K}$}
            \State Sample embedding $\vz \sim \mathcal{N}(\vmu_\rvz, \vsigma_\rvz)$
            \State $\tilde{\vx} \gets \phi_{\vtheta_D}(\vz)$
            \State Collect $\pi$'s trajectory $\tau$ from $\vx$ and compute scores $S, S_2$
            \State Add $\langle \vx, S, S_2 \rangle$ to $\Lambda$ at $\argmin_{\{\Lambda \setminus X_\text{train}\}} S_2$ \textbf{if} $S_2 > \min_{\{\Lambda \setminus X_\text{train}\}} S_2$
        \EndFor
\EndFor
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{the generative phase}
A VAE \citep{VAE, VAE-pmlr-v32-rezende14} models the observed data distribution $p(\rvx)$ %
as stochastic realisations of a latent distribution $p(\rvz)$ via a generative model $p(\rvx \mid \rvz)$.
The model is optimised by jointly maximising the variational ELBO
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ELBO}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{ELBO}} = \mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\vx\sim p(\vx)}\left\{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\vz \sim q(\rvz|\rvx; \psi_{\vtheta_E})}[\log p(\rvx \mid \rvz; \phi_{\vtheta_D})] - \beta \KL(q(\rvz \mid \rvx;\psi_{\vtheta_E})\mid\mid p(\rvz))\right\},
\end{equation}
where $q(\rvz \mid \rvx; \psi_{\vtheta_E})$ is a variational approximation of an intractable model posterior distribution $p(\rvz \mid \rvx)$ and $\KL(\cdot \mid\mid \cdot)$ denotes the Kullback--Leibler divergence, which is balanced using the coefficient $\beta$, as proposed by \cite{betaVAE}. 
The generative $p(\rvx \mid \rvz; \phi_{\vtheta_D})$ and variational $q(\rvz \mid \rvx ; \psi_{\vtheta_E})$ models are parametrised via encoder and decoder networks $\psi_{\vtheta_E}$ and $\phi_{\vtheta_D}$.%

We use $p(\rvx; \phi_{\vtheta_D})$ to generate augmented level parameters $\tilde{\vx}$. As maximising \Cref{eq:ELBO} fits the VAE such that the marginal $p(\rvx; \phi_{\vtheta_D}) = \int p(\rvx \mid \rvz; \phi_{\vtheta_D}) p(\rvz) \dif \rvz$ approximates the data distribution $p(\rvx)$, sampling out-of-context levels and shifting the training distribution from the CMDP context distribution is less likely than if we were sampling directly from $\sX$. We show in \Cref{sec:minigrid} that this aspect is key in enabling SSED-trained agents to outperform UED-agent in ZSG. %
To improve the quality of the generated $\tilde{\vx}$, we interpolate in the latent space between the latent representations of pair of samples $(\vx_i, \vx_j) \sim X_\text{train}$ to obtain $\rvz$, instead of sampling from $p(\rvz)$, as it was proposed by \cite{white2016sampling}.
We evaluate the agent (without updating its weights) on the levels obtained from a batch of levels parameters $\tilde{X}$, adding to the buffer $\Lambda$ any level scoring higher than the lowest scoring generated level in $\Lambda$. We provide additional details on the architecture, hyperparameters and pre-training process in \Cref{app:vae_imp}.
\subsection{the replay phase}
We define $P_\Lambda$ as in \Cref{eq:buffer_dist}, mixing two score-based distributions $P_S$ and $P_{S_2}$ with a staleness distribution $P_C$. We initialise the buffer $\Lambda$ to contain levels $L$ obtained from $X_\text{train}$ and gradually add levels obtained during the generative phase over the course of training. We do so by restricting the support of $P_S$ and $P_C$ to $L$, whereas $P_{S_2}$ supports the entire buffer. We set both $S_1$ and $S_2$ to score levels according to the $\ell_1$-value loss, with $S_2$ being set to 0 if the level has never been solved by the agent, to prevent unsolvable levels being included in $\Lambda$. We assume that all levels in $X_\text{train}$ are in-context, whereas the generated levels, being obtained from an approximation of $p(\rvx)$, do not benefit from as strong of a guarantee. Our experiments suggest that it is particularly important to reduce the number of out-of-context levels during the early stages of training, which we control by linearly increasing the mixing parameter $\eta$ from 0 to 1 as training progresses, so that the agent starts training exclusively on $L$ and finishes training on the full augmented set $\tilde{L}$.


\section{experiments}

We demonstrate the effectiveness of SSED in Minigrid, a gridworld navigation domain \citep{gym_minigrid}. Minigrid levels can be instantiated via a parameter vector describing the locations, starting states and appearance of the objects in the grid. Despite its simplicity, Minigrid qualifies as a parametrisable simulator, in contrast to Procgen, in which level instantiation is only possible through a random seed. We wish to explore a problem setting in which only a limited subset of the level space corresponding to specific level parameter semantics will yield levels of the CMDP of interest. With this objective, we define the context space of the target CMDP as the set of layouts in which the location of green moss tiles and red lava tiles are respectively positively and negatively correlated to their distance to the goal location. 
We employ procedural generation to obtain a set $X_\text{train}$ of 512 level parameters, referring the reader to \Cref{fig:layouts_dataset} for a visualisation of levels from $X_\text{train}$, and to \Cref{app:cmdp_levelset} for extended details on the CMDP specification and level set generation process.

As the agent only observes its immediate surroundings and does not know the goal location a priori, the optimal CMDP policy is one that exploits the semantics shared by all levels in the CMDP, exploring first areas with high perceived moss density and avoiding areas with high lava density. Our CMDP coexists alongside a multitude of other potential CMDPs in the level space and some correspond to incompatible optimal policies (for example levels in which the correlation of moss and lava tiles is reversed). As such, it is important to maintain consistency with the CMDP when generating new levels.

We compare SSED to multiple baselines, sorted in two sets. The first set of baselines is restricted to sample from $X_\text{train}$, and consists of uniform sampling ($\mathcal{U}$) and PLR using the $\ell_1$-value loss strategy. The second set of baselines incorporate a generative mechanism, providing them access to the full level space, and as such are more similar to SSED. We consider domain randomisation (DR) \citep{DR} which generates levels by sampling uniformly between pre-determined ranges of parameters, RPLR \citep{Robust-PLR}, which combines PLR with DR used as its generator, and the current UED state-of-the-art, ACCEL \cite{ACCEL}, an extension of RPLR replacing DR by a generator making local edits to currently high scoring levels in the buffer. All experiments share the same PPO \citep{PPO} agent, which uses the LSTM-based architecture and hyperparameters reported in \cite{ACCEL}, training over 27k updates.
\subsection{generalisation performance}
As shown in \Cref{fig:zsg_test_set_and_edge_cases} SSED achieves statistically significant improvements in its IQM (inter-quantile mean), mean score, optimality gap and mean solved rate over other methods on held-out levels from the CMDP. In particular, SSED's ZSG performance on held-out levels from $X_\text{train}$ demonstrate SSED remains consistent with the target CMDP as it augments the level training set. This is thanks to its generative model effectively approximating $p(\rvx)$, and to its mixed sampling strategy ensuring many training levels originate from $X_\text{train}$, which are guaranteed to come from the correct distribution. We next investigate whether SSED's level generation possesses enough diversity make it robust when faced with rarely encountered edge cases. We generate additional level sets using different procedural generation parameters in order to produce levels that match the CMDP semantics but are out-of-distribution with $X_\text{train}$ in respect to their layout structures and tile type densities. We do so to evaluate the agents robustness to in-context \textit{edge-cases} which would have a low likelihood of being in $X_\text{train}$ in a real setting. We find that SSED is particularly dominant is this setting, achieving with an IQM four times higher the next highest performing baseline, $\mathcal{U}$, a solved rate nearly twice as high and a mean score that is 1.6 times the next best baseline for that metric, PLR.

SSED is therefore capable of introducing additional diversity in the level set while still producing levels that are semantically consistent with the CMDP. To test how far SSED can go in this respect, in \Cref{fig:zsg_hard_and_ablation} we evaluate it on a set of layouts 9 times larger in area than $X_\text{train}$ levels, and which are not instantiable during training. We find that SSED still performs over twice as well as the next best method in this task. 

To better understand the importance of the VAE model and disentangle its benefits from the one provided by the mixed sampling strategy, we introduce SSED-EL, a version of SSED replacing the VAE with ACCEL's level editing strategy. SSED-EL may be viewed as an SSED variant of ACCEL that effectively leverages a dataset using a non-parametric generative method, or equivalently as an ablation of SSED that does not approximate $p(\rvx)$ and is therefore less grounded to the target CMDP. In \Cref{fig:zsg_hard_and_ablation} we compare the two methods across level sets, and we find that SSED improves more significantly over its ablation for level sets that are most similar to the original training set. This highlights the significance of being able to approximate $p(\rvx)$ through the VAE to avoid distributional shifts, which we discuss next.

Despite their poor test scores, the UED methods tested generally achieve small generalisation gaps, as they perform poorly on both the test set and on $X_\text{train}$. Yet the fact they tend to perform well on the subset of $\sX$ spanning their respective training distributions means that they have over-generalised to an out-of-context set. As such, we cannot qualify their poor performance on $X_C$ simply as a lack of capability but instead as a form of misgeneralisation the generalisation gap does not quantify, and which we find reminiscent of failure modes reported in \cite{goal_misgen_DRL, goal_misgen_deepmind}. Instead, we propose to use the \textit{over-generalisation gap} as a complementary metric, which we define as
\begin{equation} \label{eq:OverGenGap}
    \text{OverGap}(\pi) \coloneqq \sum_{\tilde{\vx} \in \Lambda} P_\Lambda(i_{\tilde{\vx}}) \cdot V^\pi_{\lvl_{\tilde{\vx}}} - \frac{1}{|X_{\text{train}}|} \sum_{\vx \in X_{\text{train}}} V^\pi_{\lvl_\vx}.
\end{equation}
Note that $\text{OverGap}$ compares the agent's performance with $X_\text{train}$ \footnote{ using $X_\text{test}$ would make $\text{OverGap}\equiv\text{GenGap}$ if $P_\Lambda = \mathcal{U}(X_\text{train})$, whereas it should be 0.} and is only applicable to measure over-generalisation induced by distributional shift. Based on further analysis conducted in \Cref{app:dist-shift}, high $\text{OverGap}$ tends to coincide with shifted level parameter sampling distributions, and we find that SSED is the only level generation method tested able to maintain both low distributional shift and $\text{OverGap}$.



\section{conclusion}

In this work, we investigated the impact of the level sampling process on the ZSG capabilities of RL agents. We made a distinction between instance-overfitting, characterised by the generalisation gap; and over-generalisation due to distributional shift, which we quantified by introducing the over-generalisation gap. To mitigate instance-overfitting, we proposed to employ an upper bound on the generalisation gap as a surrogate minimisation objective. We initially investigated training over a fixed level set, in which minimising this bound is equivalent to minimising the mutual information between the agent model and the set of training levels. We derived a method to estimate the mutual information and we demonstrated that our estimate strongly correlates with the generalisation gaps induced by different sampling strategies, across multiple environments. In doing so, we uncovered a connection between the $\ell_1$-value loss level sampling prioritisation strategy and mutual information minimisation. 

We then investigated the setting in which the set of training levels is not fixed and where the generalisation bound can be more effectively minimised by training over an augmented set. We proposed SSED, a level generation and sampling framework restricting level generation to an approximation of the underlying distribution of a starting set of level parameters. We showed that this restriction lets SSED avoid over-generalisation failure modes experienced by UED methods. By jointly minimising the generalisation and over-generalisation gaps, we demonstrated that SSED achieves strong generalisation performance on in-distribution test levels, while also being robust to in-context edge-cases. %

There are high costs associated with collecting or prescribing training scenarios and environments in many RL domains, which include robotics and autonomous driving. SSED demonstrates potential in alleviating these costs by generating augmented sets of training scenarios from a small starting set. In future work, we plan to investigate how well SSED scales to more complex environments, which is becoming possible thanks to the advent of large scale datasets of realistic environments \citep{OpenRooms,argoverse2}.

Lastly, we are interested in exploring the synergies that exist between SSED and other mutual information minimisation frameworks. We point to the substantial improvements achieved by \cite{PLR} in combining PLR with UCB-DrAC \citep{raileanu2021UCB-DrAC}, a data augmentation method acting on the agent's observation, which may be viewed as combining two different mutual information minimisation strategies. We expect similar improvements to be achievable by combining SSED with bottlenecks placed at different points of the level-to-agent information chain, which is an investigation we leave for future work.



\section{reproducibility statement}

We believe parametrisable simulators are better suited to benchmark ZSG than procedural environments, as they provide a fine degree of control over the environment and are more consistent with a realistic application setting, as argued by \cite{kirk2023survey}. For example, our study of over-generalisation would not have been possible in Procgen, due to each game supporting a singular and non-parametrisable level distribution. Having access to the level parameters used in experiments also facilitates the reproducibility of ZSG research, and we make the train and evaluation sets of level parameters used in this work, as well as the code for running experiments, publicly available.\footnote{Available upon de-anonymisation of this publication} To encourage this practice in future work, we open-source our code\footnotemark[\value{footnote}] for specifying arbitrary CMDPs in Minigrid and generate their associated level sets, describing the generation process in detail in \Cref{app:cmdp_levelset}.
We also provide a dataset of 1.5M procedurally generated minigrid base layouts to facilitate level set generation.\footnotemark[\value{footnote}]
