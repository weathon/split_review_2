\title{Double Equivariance for Inductive Link Prediction for Both New Nodes and New Relation Types}

\begin{abstract}
The task of inductive link prediction in \update{knowledge graphs} (KGs) generally focuses on test predictions with solely new nodes but not both new nodes and new relation types. 
In this work, we formally define the  concept of {\em double permutation-equivariant representations} 
that are equivariant to permutations of both node identities and edge relation types.
\update{We then show how double-equivariant architectures are able to self-supervise pre-train on distinct KG domains and zero-shot predict links on a new KG domain (with completely new entities and new relation types).}
We also introduce the concept of {\em distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings} designed to perform the same task.
\update{Finally, we empirically demonstrate the capability of the proposed models against baselines on a set of novel real-world benchmarks. More interestingly, we show that self-supervised pre-training on more KG domains increases the zero-shot ability of our model to predict on new relation types over new entities on unseen KG domains.}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\label{sec:intro}
This work studies what we call a {\em doubly inductive} (node and relation) {\em link prediction} task to predict missing links in unseen {\ourgraph}\update{s} with completely new nodes and new relation types in test (i.e. none of them are seen in training).
\update{{\Ourtask} can be seen as zero-shot meta-learning task, where training on knowledge graphs from domains A, B, and C allows us to zero-shot predict never-seen-before relations in a different domain D at test time without side information or fine-tuning.}
\update{We note in passing that the outlined methodology could be applicable in other areas such as multilayer~\citep{coscia2013you} and heterogeneous~\citep{chen2021graph} network data.}
{\em The main contribution of our work is a general theoretical framework for {\bf \ourtask} on {\ourgraph}\update{s} and a blueprint to create equivariant neural networks for this task (both from structural representations and from positional embeddings).}
We will introduce the concept of double equivariant graph models and distributionally equivariant positional graph embedding models, which are equivariant to the overgroup of permutations of nodes and permutations of relations (we review the necessary group theory concepts in \Cref{sec:theory}).
\update{The essence of double equivariance is to force the model to abstract and generalize across various domains (e.g., distinct knowledge graph domains such as Education, Health, Sports, Taxonomy, etc.), which traditional KG models are unable to do (\Cref{sec:related} gives more details).}
{\bf Contributions.} 
This work makes the following {\em three} contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Our work provides the first formal definition of the {\em \ourtask task}, the concept of {\em double equivariance}, and that of {\em distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings} for knowledge graph models, whose node and pairwise representations are equivariant to the action of the permutation overgroup composed by the permutation subgroups of node identities, and edge types (relations). 
    \item \update{Our work introduces ISDEA+, a fast and general {\em double equivariant} graph neural network model that is capable of performing {\em \ourtask}. ISDEA+ is able to work on train and test knowledge graphs with disjoint sets of relations of distinct sizes}. %
     We also introduce an approximately double equivariant representation built from distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings. %
    \item Our work introduces two novel tasks in two real-world benchmark datasets%
    : \ourdata and WikiTopics. \update{These are pre-trained zero-shot meta-learning tasks, where the model is initially trained on a (diverse set of) KG(s) from distinct domains (e.g., Education, Health, Sports). The essence of this training is to imbue the model with the ability to abstract and generalize across domains. In test, the model is evaluated zero-shot on a KG of a completely new domain (e.g. Taxonomy). This setup challenges the model to apply its learned meta-knowledge to a new, unseen domain, demonstrating its capacity for cross-domain generalization and adaptation without prior exposure to the specific domain of the test KG.
     Our experiments show that \OurNewModel zero-shot performance increases with the number of self-supervised training KGs on diverse domains, to the point that it is sometimes better than {\em transductive training on the test KG domain itself} (e.g., the state-of-the-art baseline trains on Taxonomy KG to predict held out links on Taxonomy KG itself).}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Doubly (Node \& Relation) Inductive Link Prediction}

In what follows, we introduce the \ourtask task and compare it with the traditional inductive link prediction task using two examples. We then proceed to theoretically describe the task in a general setting and propose our double equivariant modeling framework to handle \ourtask task using structural representations and positional embeddings.

\subsection{Doubly inductive link prediction examples}
\label{subsec:example}
We now introduce \ourtask over both new nodes and new relation types and explain the difference between the traditional inductive link prediction task in \Cref{fig:example-link}. The traditional inductive link prediction task focuses solely on predicting new nodes in the test. To this end, standard graph neural networks (GNNs)~\citep{xu2018powerful,Morris2019WL} \update{and their KG counterparts (e.g.~\citep{hamilton2017inductive,galkin2021nodepiece,chenrefactor,zhu2021neural,teru2020inductive})} force the neural network to learn structural node representations~\citep{srinivasan2020on}, which ---if used appropriately--- allows GNNs for KGs to perform {\em inductive link prediction over new nodes}.
\update{As shown in~\Cref{fig:example-link}(b), these models are capable of learning the patterns in the gray box of~\Cref{fig:example-link}(b), but these patterns cannot extrapolate zero-shot to new relation types in test, because they rely on the relation type labels.} %

In other words, the equivariance in GNNs is not enough to perform the doubly inductive link prediction task in \Cref{fig:example-link}(a). %
Specifically, to be able to {\em inductively} predict the \update{``employee''} %
relation on the test graph by learning from the %
\update{``racing driver''}
relation on the training graph, the equivariance property needs to go beyond just node permutations. To be able to represent the structural properties of the nodes and relations with respect to the structural properties of other nodes and relations, our work defines an equivariance also in relations. %
For instance, via double equivariance (we will define the concept in \Cref{sec:def}) it is possible to perform the task of predicting \update{``employee''} using the node and relation structural pattern \update{shown at the bottom of \Cref{fig:example-link}(a) in the gray box}. Additional examples and a more detailed analysis of a family of logical statements implied by double equivariance can be found in \Cref{appx:logic,appd:add-example}.
\subsection{Formalizing the doubly inductive link prediction task}
\label{subsec:taskdef}
We now introduce notations and definitions used throughout this paper.
First, we formally define our inductive link prediction task for both new nodes and new relation types, i.e., \ourtask, over \ourgraph\update{s}. We denote $[n] := \{ 1, \dots, n \}$ for any $n\in \sN$.
Let $\cG\suptr = (\cV\suptr, \cR\suptr, \adj\suptr)$ be the training \ourgraph, where $\cV\suptr$ is the set of $N\suptr$ training nodes, $\cR\suptr$ is the set of $R\suptr$ training relation types.
We also define two associated bijective mappings $v\suptr_{\cdot}:[N\suptr]\rightarrow \cV\suptr, r\suptr_{\cdot}:[R\suptr]\rightarrow \cR\suptr$ that enumerate the nodes and relation types in training.
The tensor $\adj\suptr \in \{0, 1\}^{N\suptr \times R\suptr \times N\suptr}$ defines the adjacency  of the training graph such that $\forall (i,k,j)\in [N\suptr]\times [R\suptr]\times [N\suptr], \adj\suptr_{i, k, j} = 1$ indicates that the triplet $(v\suptr_i, r\suptr_k, v\suptr_j)$ is present in the data (%
we denote $(i,k,i)$ as the $k$-th attribute of node $i$). To simplify notation, we further refer to the collection of all \ourgraph\update{s} of any sizes as $\sA \coloneqq \cup_{N=1}^\infty\cup_{R=2}^\infty \{0, 1\}^{N \times R \times N}$. 

\begin{definition}[\Ourtask task]\label{def:task-double}
\update{The task of \ourtask learns a model on a set of $K$ training graphs $\{\cG\suptr_1,\ldots,\cG\suptr_K\}$ (often in a self-supervised fashion by masking links) and inductively applies it to predict missing links in a test graph $\cG\supte = (\cV\supte, \cR\supte, \adj\supte)$ with \textit{completely} new nodes and new relation types, i.e., $\cV\supte \cap \cV\suptr = \emptyset, \cR\supte \cap \cR\suptr = \emptyset$, without extra context given to the model.}
\end{definition}
While some real-world tasks may have overlapping relation types between training and test, \Cref{def:task-double} forces the model to not rely on potential overlaps.
In what follows, we use the superscript $(*)$ as a wildcard to describe both train and test data. For example, $\adj^{(*)}$ is a wildcard variable for referring 
to either $\adj\suptr$ or $\adj\supte$. %
And since there are bijections $v^{(*)}_\cdot,r^{(*)}_\cdot$ between indices and nodes and relation types, we represent the triplet $(v^{(*)}_i,r^{(*)}_k,v^{(*)}_j)\in \small \cV^{(*)}\times \cR^{(*)}\times \cV^{(*)}$ with indices $(i,k,j)\in[N^{(*)}]\times[R^{(*)}]\times[N^{(*)}]$, and mainly use $\adjwild$ to denote the \ourgraph.  %

Without additional context such as textural description embeddings for the new relations or graph ontology (thoroughly discussed in \Cref{sec:related}), it is essential for our model to differentiate nodes and relations based only on their structural relationships in $\adjwild$, rather than their labels in $\cV^{(*)}, \cR^{(*)}$, in order to make accurate predictions in \ourtask as discussed in \Cref{subsec:example}.
Thus, we develop the double equivariant representations for {\ourgraphease}\update{s} as follows. 
\subsection{Double equivariant representations for \ourgraphease\update{s}}
\label{sec:def}
In what follows, we provide definitions and theoretical statements of our proposed double equivariant \ourgraphease representations in the main paper while referring all proofs to \Cref{sec:proof}.
The proposal starts with defining the permutation actions on {\ourgraphs} as:%
\begin{definition}[Node and relation permutation actions on {\ourgrapheases}]
\label{def:rel-graph-perm}
For any \ourgraphease $\adjwild \in \sA$ with number of nodes and relations $\nodesizewild,\relsizewild$, a node permutation $\phi \in \sS_\nodesizewild$ is an element of the symmetric group $\sS_\nodesizewild$, a relation permutation $\tau \in \sS_\relsizewild$ is an element of the symmetric group $\sS_\relsizewild$, and the operation $\phi \circ \tau \circ \adjwild$ is the action of $\phi$ and $\tau$ on $\adjwild$, defined as $\forall (i, k, j) \in [\nodesizewild]\times[\relsizewild]\times[\nodesizewild], (\phi \circ\tau\circ \adjwild)_{\phi \circ i, \tau\circ k, \phi \circ j} = \adjwild_{i,k,j}$ where $\phi \circ i = \phi_i$ and $\tau\circ k = \tau_k$. The node and relation permutation actions on $\adjwild$ are commutative, i.e., $\phi\circ\tau\circ \adjwild = \tau\circ\phi\circ \adjwild$.
\end{definition}
To learn structural representation for both nodes and relations, %
we first design triplet representations that are invariant to the two permutation actions on nodes and relations, as shown below.
\begin{definition}[Double invariant triplet representations]
\label{def:trip-repr}
For any \ourgraphease $\adjwild \in \sA$ with number of nodes and relations $\nodesizewild,\relsizewild$, a double invariant triplet representation %
is a function $\trifunc{\Gamma}: \cup_{N=1}^\infty\cup_{R=2}^\infty([N]\times[R]\times[N])\times \sA \rightarrow \sR^{d}, d \geq 1$, such that $\forall (i, k, j) \in [\nodesizewild]\times[\relsizewild]\times[\nodesizewild], \forall \phi \in \sS_\nodesizewild, \forall \tau \in \sS_\relsizewild$, $\trifunc{\Gamma}((i, k, j), \adjwild) = \trifunc{\Gamma}((\phi \circ i, \tau \circ k, \phi \circ j), \phi \circ \tau \circ \adjwild)$.
\end{definition}
To understand the property of our double invariant triplet representations, we first introduce the notion of \ourgraph isomorphism and triplet double isomorphism.
\begin{definition}[\Ourgraphease isomorphism and Triplet isomorphism]
\label{def:rel-graph-iso}
We say two {\ourgrapheases} $\adj^{(G)}, \adj^{(H)} \in \sA$ with number of nodes and relations $N^{(G)},R^{(G)}$
and $N^{(H)}, R^{(H)}$ respectively, are isomorphic (denoted as ``$\adj^{(G)} \simeq_\text{RL} \adj^{(H)}$'') if and only if $\exists \phi\in\sS_{N^{(G)}}, \exists\tau\in \sS_{R^{(G)}}$, such that $\phi \circ \tau \circ \adj^{(G)} = \adj^{(H)}$. %
And we say two triplets $\left( i^{(G)}, k^{(G)}, j^{(G)} \right)\in [N^{(G)}]\times[R^{(G)}]\times[N^{(G)}]$, $\left( i^{(H)}, k^{(H)}, j^{(H)} \right) \in [N^{(H)}]\times[R^{(H)}]\times[N^{(H)}]$ are isomorphic triplets (denoted as ``$\left( \left( i^{(G)}, k^{(G)}, j^{(G)}  \right), \adj^{(G)} \right) \simeq_\text{TRI} \left( \left(  i^{(H)}, k^{(H)}, j^{(H)}\right), \adj^{(H)}\right)$'') if and only if $\exists \phi\in\sS_{N^{(G)}}, \exists\tau\in \sS_{R^{(G)}}$, such that $\phi \circ \tau \circ \adj^{(G)} = \adj^{(H)}$ and $\left( i^{(H)}, k^{(H)}, j^{(H)} \right) = \left( \phi \circ i^{(G)}, \tau \circ k^{(G)}, \phi \circ j^{(G)} \right)$.
\end{definition}
For example, in \Cref{fig:example-link}(a), \update{the training triplet (Red Bull Racing, racing driver, Sergio Pérez)} and \update{the test triplet (Google, employee, Jeff Dean)} are isomorphic triplets by \Cref{def:rel-graph-iso}. %
It is clear that our double invariant triplet representations are able to output the same representations for these isomorphic triplets, enabling \ourtask, \update{where the model trained to predict the missing (Red Bull Racing, racing driver, Sergio Pérez) in the training graph is able to predict the missing (Google, employee, Jeff Dean) in test}. The connection between \Cref{def:trip-repr} and logical reasoning can be found in \Cref{appx:logic}.
In what follows, we define the structure \ourequiv representations for the whole \ourgraphease $\adjwild$ (akin to how GNNs provide representations for a whole graph). %
\begin{definition}[\Ourequiv \ \ourgraphease representations]
\label{def:rel-graph-repr}
For any \ourgraphease $\adjwild \in \sA$ with  number of nodes and relations $\nodesizewild, \relsizewild$, a function $\grafunc{\Gamma}: \sA \rightarrow \cup_{N=1}^\infty\cup_{R=2}^\infty \sR^{N \times R \times N \times d}, d \geq 1$ is \ourequiv w.r.t.\ arbitrary node $\phi \in \sS_\nodesizewild$ and relation $\tau \in \sS_\relsizewild$ permutations, if $\grafunc{\Gamma}(\phi \circ \tau \circ \adjwild) = \phi \circ \tau \circ \grafunc{\Gamma}(\adjwild)$.
Moreover, valid mappings of $\Gamma_\text{gra}$ must map a domain element to an image element with the same number of nodes and relations.
\end{definition}
Finally, we connect \Cref{def:trip-repr,def:rel-graph-repr} by showing how to build double equivariant graph representations from double invariant triplet representations in \Cref{thm:inv-and-equiv}, and vice-versa.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{thmone}
\label{thm:inv-and-equiv}
For all $\adjwild \in \sA$ with number of nodes and relations $\nodesizewild,\relsizewild$, given a \ourinv triplet representation $\trifunc{\Gamma}$, we can construct a \ourequiv graph representation as $\left( \grafunc{\Gamma}(\adjwild) \right)_{i,k,j} := \trifunc{\Gamma}((i,k,j), \adjwild)$, $\forall (i,k,j) \in [\nodesizewild]\times [\relsizewild]\times [\nodesizewild]$, and vice-versa.
\end{restatable}
Next, we consider positional graph embeddings that are equivariant in distribution.

\subsection{
Distributionally double equivariant positional graph embeddings}
\label{sec:positional}
To the best of our knowledge, InGram~\citep{ingram} is the first and only existing work capable of performing our \ourtask task~(\Cref{def:task-double}), but it does so with what we now define as {\em distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings}, which are permutation sensitive, as we will show in \Cref{subsec:de-ingram}:

\begin{definition}[Distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings]
\label{def:pos-trip-repr}
For any \ourgraphease $\adjwild \in \sA$ with number of nodes and relations $\nodesizewild,\relsizewild$, the distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings of $\adjwild$ are defined as joint samples of random variables $\tZ|\adjwild \sim p(\tZ|\adjwild)$,  where the tensor $\tZ $ is defined as $\tZ_{i,k,j}\in\sR^d, d\geq 1, \forall (i,k,j) \in [\nodesizewild]\times[\relsizewild]\times[\nodesizewild]$, where we say $p(\tZ|\adjwild)$ is a double equivariant probability distribution on $\adjwild$ defined as $\forall \phi\in \sS_\nodesizewild,\forall \tau\in \sS_\relsizewild,  p(\tZ |\adjwild) = p(\phi \circ \tau \circ  \tZ| \phi \circ \tau \circ \adjwild)$.
\end{definition}

Prior work on (standard) link prediction tasks has shown the advantages of equivariant representations over positional embeddings~\citep{zhang2021labeling}.
Moreover, \citet{srinivasan2020on} establishes the equivalence between positional embeddings and structural representations for simple graphs by proving that representations based on an expectation of the positional embeddings 
are equivariant to node permutations.
In what follows, we extend this result to the double equivariant setting:

\begin{restatable}[From distributional double equivariant positional embeddings to double equivariant representations]{theorem}{thmthree}
\label{thm:equivalence} For any \ourgraphease $\adjwild \in \sA$, %
the average $\mathbb{E}_{p(\tZ|\adjwild)}[\tZ|\adjwild]$ is a double equivariant \ourgraphease representation (\Cref{def:rel-graph-repr}) for any distributional double equivariant positional embeddings $\tZ|\adjwild$ (\Cref{def:pos-trip-repr}). %
\end{restatable}
Later in \Cref{subsec:de-ingram}, we use the result in \Cref{thm:equivalence} to introduce DEq-InGram, a double equivariant representation that builds upon  InGram's distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings (\Cref{def:pos-trip-repr}) that is shown to significantly outperforms the original InGram in \Cref{sec:exp}.


\section{Double Equivariant Neural Architecture}

\label{sec:model}
This section introduces two double equivariant neural architectures based on \Cref{sec:def,sec:positional}. 
First, \Cref{subsec:isdea} introduces an Inductive Structural Double Equivariant Architecture \update{Plus (\OurNewModel)}, a model guaranteed to produce double equivariant representations~(\Cref{def:rel-graph-repr}). Then,~\Cref{subsec:de-ingram} introduces a Monte Carlo estimate of a double equivariant representation built from a distributionally double equivariant positional graph  embedding~\citep{ingram}. 

\subsection{Inductive Structural Double Equivariant Architecture \update{Plus (\OurNewModel)}}
\label{subsec:isdea}
We start revisiting \Cref{def:rel-graph-iso}. 
Consider an arbitrary \ourgraph $\adjwild \in \sA$ with number of nodes and relations $\nodesizewild,\relsizewild$, and denote $A^{(*,k)}$ as the adjacency matrix such that $A^{(*,k)}_{i,j} \coloneqq \adjwild_{i,k,j}$, $\forall (i, k, j) \in [\nodesizewild]\times[\relsizewild]\times[\nodesizewild]$. 
For each adjacency matrix $A^{(*,k)}$, it will correspond to a graph without edge relation types, thus we can also consider $A^{(*,k)}$ as an unattributed graph containing only edges with relation type $r^{(*)}_k$.
Then, the \ourgraphease $\adjwild$ can be equivalently expressed as a collection of  unattributed graphs $A^{(*)} \coloneqq \bigldcbrace A^{(*,1)}, \ldots, A^{(*,\relsizewild)} \bigrdcbrace$. Since the actions of the two permutation groups $\sS_\nodesizewild$ and $\sS_\relsizewild$ commute, the double equivariance of $\adjwild$ (\Cref{def:rel-graph-iso}) can be described as two (single) equivariances: A (graph) equivariance $\phi \in \sS_\nodesizewild$ over each graph $A^{(*,k)}, k = 1, \ldots, \relsizewild$, and a (set) equivariance $\tau \in \sS_\relsizewild$  (over the set of graphs). 
Hence, our double equivariance can make use of the general framework using DSS layers on learning sets of symmetric elements proposed by \citet{maron2020learning}. %
We first define a \ourequiv layer composed by a Siamese layer \citep{bromley1993signature} as follows, $L :\sA \rightarrow \cup_{N=1}^\infty\cup_{R=2}^\infty \sR^{N \times R \times N \times d}$, for each $k = 1, ..., \relsizewild$:
\begin{equation}
\small
\label{eq:dsslin}
\left( L \left( \adjwild \right) \right)_{:, k} = L_1 \left( A^{(*,k)} \right) + L_2 \left( \text{AGG}_{k'\neq k}^{\relsizewild} \left( A^{(*,k')} \right) \right),
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the output dimension, $L_1, L_2: \sA \rightarrow \cup_{N=1}^\infty\sR^{N \times N \times d}$  can be any (node) equivariant layers that output pairwise %
representations~\citep{zhang2018link,zhu2021neural,zhang2021labeling}, and the aggregation term $\text{AGG}_{k'\neq k}^{\relsizewild}$ can be any set aggregators such as sum, mean, max, DeepSets~\citep{zaheer2017deep}, etc.. 
Note that the proposed layer is similar to the $H$-equivariant layer proposed by 
\citet{bevilacqua2021equivariant} for increasing the expressiveness of GNN using sets of subgraphs (a markedly different task than ours).
We create our \ourequiv \ \update{ model with} double equivariant layers.
\subsubsection{Implementation Details}
We use GNN layers for constructing $L_1,L_2$.
Since most-expressive pairwise representations are computationally expensive, we propose Inductive Structural Double Equivariant Architecture \update{ Plus (\OurNewModel)} and trade-off expressivity in the implementation of \Cref{eq:dsslin} for speed and memory by using node representation GNN layers \citep{xu2018powerful,velivckovic2017graph,Morris2019WL}. Specifically, for a \ourgraphease $\adjwild$ with 
number of nodes and relations $\nodesizewild,\relsizewild$, 
at each iteration $t = 1, ..., T$, \update{for each relation type $k\in [\relsizewild]$}, all nodes $i \in [\nodesizewild]$ are associated with \update{two} learned vectors \update{$h_{i,k}^{(t)} \in \sR^{d_t}, h_{i,\neg k}^{(t)} \in \sR^{d_t}, d_t \geq 1$}.
If there are
no node attributes, we initialize \update{$\forall k \in [\relsizewild],h_{i,k}^{(0)} = h_{i,\neg k}^{(0)} = \mathbbm{1}$}. %
Then we recursively compute the update, 
$\forall i \in [\nodesizewild], \forall k \in [\relsizewild]$,
\update{
\begin{equation*}
\scriptstyle
    \begin{aligned}
    \scriptstyle
         h_{i, k}^{(t + 1)} & \scriptstyle= \text{GNN}^{(t)}_1 \Big( h_{i, k}^{(t)}, \bigldcbrace h_{j, k}^{(t)} \big\vert j \in \mathcal{N}_k(i) \bigrdcbrace \Big) , \quad \quad\quad \ \ h_{i, \neg k}^{(t + 1)} = \text{GNN}^{(t)}_2 \Big( h_{i, \neg k}^{(t)}, \bigldcbrace h_{j, \neg k}^{(t)} \big\vert j \in \bigcup\limits_{k' \neq k}\mathcal{N}_k(i) \bigrdcbrace \Big),  \quad\ \ \ \text{if } t = 0, \\
        \scriptstyle
         h_{i, k}^{(t + 1)} & \scriptstyle= \text{GNN}^{(t)}_1 \Big( h_{i, k}^{(t)}, \bigldcbrace h_{j, k}^{(t)} \big\vert j \in \bigcup\limits_{k' \in [\relsizewild]}\mathcal{N}_{k'}(i) \bigrdcbrace \Big) , h_{i, \neg k}^{(t + 1)} = \text{GNN}^{(t)}_2 \Big( h_{i, \neg k}^{(t)}, \bigldcbrace h_{j, \neg k}^{(t)} \big\vert j \in \bigcup\limits_{k' \in [\relsizewild]}\mathcal{N}_{k'}(i) \bigrdcbrace \Big) , \ \text{if } t > 0,
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation*}}%
where $\text{GNN}^{(t)}_1$ and $\text{GNN}^{(t)}_2$ denote two GNN layers %
and $\cN_k(i) \coloneqq \left\{ j \middle| \adjwild_{j, k, i} = 1 \right\}$ denotes the neighborhood set of node $i$ with relation $k$ in the unattributed graph $A^{(*,k)}$.
\update{To get the final representation $X_{i,k}$ for the node $i$ with respect to relation $k$. We define $h_{i,k} = h^{(0)}_{i,k}\Big\|h^{(1)}_{i,k}\Big\|\cdots \Big\|h^{(T)}_{i,k}$, $h_{i,\neg k} = h^{(0)}_{i,\neg k}\Big\|h^{(1)}_{i,\neg k}\Big\|\cdots \Big\|h^{(T)}_{i,\neg k}$, and combine the two embeddings as illustrated in \Cref{eq:dsslin},
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:finalMLP}
    X_{i,k} = \text{MLP}_1(h_{i,k}) + \text{MLP}_2(h_{i,\neg k}), \forall i\in[\nodesizewild], \forall k\in [\relsizewild],
\end{equation}
where $\text{MLP}_1, \text{MLP}_2$ are two multi-layer perceptrons, $\|$ as the concatenation operation.}

As shown by \citet{srinivasan2020on,you2019position}, structural node representations %
are not most expressive for link prediction in unattributed graphs.
Hence, we concatenate $i$ and $j$ (\ourequiv) node representations with the shortest distance between $i$ and $j$ in the observed graph as our triplet representations (appending distances is also adopted in the representations of prior work~\citep{teru2020inductive,galkin2021nodepiece}).
Finally, we obtain the triplet representation,
\update{
\begin{equation}
\small
\label{eq:dssrepr}
\Gamma_\text{\OurNewModel}((i,k,j), \adjwild) = \Big( X_{i, k} \Big\| X_{j, k} \Big\| d(i, j) \Big\| d(j, i) \Big), \forall (i, k, j) \in [\nodesizewild]\times[\relsizewild]\times[\nodesizewild],
\end{equation}}where we denote $d(i, j)$ as the length of shortest path from $i$ to $j$ without considering $(i,k,j)$.
Since our graph is directed, we concatenate them in both directions. \update{For more implementation details and complexity analysis of \OurNewModel, please refer to \Cref{appx:detailed_design}.}
\begin{restatable}{lemma}{lemthree}
\update{$\Gamma_\text{\OurNewModel}$} in \Cref{eq:dssrepr} is a \ourinv triplet representation as per \Cref{def:trip-repr}. %
\end{restatable}
 
As in %
\citet{yang2015embedding,schlichtkrull2018modeling,zhu2021neural}, we use negative sampling in our training with the difference that we account for both predicting missing nodes and relation types~(\Cref{def:task-double}).
Specifically, for each positive training triplet $(i,k,j)$ such that $\adj\suptr_{i,k,j}=1$, we first randomly corrupt either the head or the tail $n_\text{nd}$ times to generate the negative (node) %
examples $(i,k,j')$.
Additionally, %
we also want 
our model to learn the correct relation type $(i,?,j)$ between a pair of nodes.  %
Thus, we corrupt relation $n_\text{rl}$ times to generate negative (relation) examples $(i,k',j)$.
In our training, $n_\text{nd} = n_\text{rl} = 2$; while in evaluation, $n_\text{nd} = 50, n_\text{rl} = 0$ for node evaluation, and $n_\text{nd} = 0, n_\text{rl} = 50$ for relation evaluation.
Following \citet{schlichtkrull2018modeling}, we use cross-entropy loss 
to encourage the model to
score positive examples higher than corresponding negative examples:
\begin{equation}
\small
\label{eq:loss}
\mathcal{L}\! =\! - \!\!\sum_{(i, k, j) \in \mathcal{S}} \left( \log \left( \trifunc{\Gamma}((i,k,j), \adj\suptr) \right) + \frac{1}{n_\text{nd} + n_\text{rl}} \sum_{p = 1}^{n_\text{nd} + n_\text{rl}} \log \left( 1 -\trifunc{\Gamma}\left( \left(i'_p, k'_p, j'_p \right), \adj\suptr \right) \right) \right),
\end{equation}
where $S = \left\{ (i, k, j) \middle| \adj\suptr_{i, k, j} = 1 \right\}$, and $\left( i'_p, k'_p, j'_p \right)$ are the $p$-th negative node or relation example corresponding to $(i, k, j)$.

\subsection{Double Equivariant InGram (\OurOtherModel)}
\label{subsec:de-ingram}
\update{\OurNewModel} directly obtains double equivariant representations for {\ourgrapheases}. Alternatively, one can build these double equivariant representations from distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings (\Cref{thm:equivalence}).
To this end, we investigate obtaining double equivariant representations from the positional embeddings of InGram~\citep{ingram}, as discussed in \Cref{sec:positional}.

InGram~\citep{ingram} constructs a \textit{relation graph} as a weighted graph consisting of relations and a heuristic to construct affinity weights between them. %
It then employs a GNN on the relation graph to generate relation embeddings, %
which are then %
fed into another GNN on the original \ourgraphease to generate node embeddings.
Finally, InGram uses a variant of DistMult~\citep{yang2015embedding} to compute triplet scores from the node and relation embeddings. These embeddings, however, are permutation sensitive due to their reliances on  Glorot initialization~\citep{glorot2010understanding} in each training epoch and test-time inference. %
\begin{restatable}{lemma}{lemfour}
The triplet representations generated by InGram~\citep{ingram} output distributionally double equivariant positional embeddings (\Cref{def:pos-trip-repr}). %
\end{restatable}
\Cref{thm:equivalence} suggests that averaging InGram's positional embeddings can be used to construct double equivariant \ourgraphease representations. Hence, we propose a Monte Carlo method to estimate these double equivariant graph representations and denote it as \OurOtherModel. Specifically, given InGram's triplet score function $\mathbf{Z}_{\text{InGram}}((i, k, j), \adj\supte, \mV^{(0)}, \mR^{(0)})$ over a test \ourgraphease $\adj\supte$, the initial random node embeddings $\mV^{(0)} \in \sR^{N\supte \times d}$, and the initial random relation embeddings $\mR^{(0)} \in \sR^{R\supte \times d'}$ (where $d$ and $d'$ are the dimension sizes), %
our \OurOtherModel produces the following triplet scores:
\begin{align}
    \Gamma_{\text{\OurOtherModel}}((i, k, j), \adj\supte) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M \mathbf{Z}_{\text{InGram}}((i, k, j), \adj\supte, \mV^{(0)}_m, \mR^{(0)}_m)
    \label{eq:de-ingram}
\end{align}
where $\{\mV^{(0)}_m\}_{m=1}^M$ and $\{\mR_m^{(0)}\}_{m=1}^M$ are $M$ i.i.d. samples drawn from the distribution of initial node and initial relation embeddings respectively (via Glorot initialization). 



\section{Related Work}

A more comprehensive discussion of related work can be found in \Cref{sec:appd-rel}.

\paragraph{Transductive link prediction.}
In transductive link prediction task, missing links are predicted over a fixed set of nodes and relation types as in training~\citep{bordes2013translating, yang2015embedding, trouillon2016complex}. %
These (positional) embeddings can be made inductive via~\cite{srinivasan2020on}'s theory but are not designed for predicting new relation types.

\paragraph{Inductive link prediction over new nodes (but not new relations).}
Rule-induction methods~\citep{yang2015embedding,yang2017differentiable,meilicke2018fine,sadeghian2019drum} are inherently node-independent which aim to extract First-order Logical Horn clauses from the attributed multigraph.
Recently, with the advancement of GNNs, %
various works~\citep{schlichtkrull2018modeling,teru2020inductive,galkin2021nodepiece,zhu2021neural,chenrefactor} have applied the idea of GNN in relational prediction to learn structural node/pairwise representation. Although all these methods can be used to perform {\em inductive link prediction over solely new nodes}, they can not handle new relation types in test.


\paragraph{Inductive link prediction over both new nodes and new relations (with extra context).}
Existing methods for querying triplets involving both new nodes and new relations generally assume access to extra context, such as generating language embedding for textual descriptions of unseen relation types~\citep{qin2020generative,geng2021ontozsl,Zha_Chen_Yan_2022,wang2021relational}, 
a shared background graph connecting seen and unseen relations (e.g., test graph has training relations~\citep{csr2022,chen2021topology,MaKEr}), 
or access to graph ontology~\citep{geng2023relational}. Hence, these methods cannot be directly applied to test graphs that neither contain meaningful descriptive information of the unseen relation types (e.g., url links) nor connection with 
nodes and relation types seen in training.

\paragraph{Inductive link prediction over both new nodes and new relations (no extra context).} We focus on this most general \ourtask task without additional context data (just the test graph structure is available during inference). To the best of our knowledge, InGram~\citep{ingram} is the first and only existing method capable of performing this task. The connection between InGram and our work has been described in \Cref{sec:positional,subsec:de-ingram}.

 In this section, we aim to answer two questions: 
{\bf Q1}: Can double equivariant models (\update{\OurNewModel} and \OurOtherModel) perform \ourtask over {\ourgrapheases} more accurately (and faster) than InGram~\citep{ingram}? %
{\bf Q2}: 
\update{We introduce a self-supervised zero-shot meta-learning task, where we pre-train (in a self-supervised fashion) on an increasing number of KGs from different domains (e.g., Education, Health, Sports) and then ask the model to zero-shot predict links on a test KG from an unseen domain (e.g. Taxonomy) with {\em completely new entities and relationship types}. Will the performance of ISDEA+ improve as the number of pre-training KG domains increase?}
{\bf Datasets.}
To the best of our knowledge, there are no existing real-world benchmarks that are specially designed to test a model's extrapolation capability for \ourtask task with training and test graphs coming from distinct domains with distinct characteristics. %
Existing datasets %
such as NL-100, WK-100, and FB-100 from~\citet{ingram} are typically created by randomly splitting a larger graph (e.g., NELL-995~\citep{xiong2018one}, Wikidata68K~\citep{gesese2022raild}, FB15K237~\citep{toutanova2015observed}) into disjoint node and relation sets, implying that the test and training graphs still come from the same distribution. %
In contrast, we purposefully create two \ourtask benchmark datasets: \ourdata and WikiTopics, sampled respectively from the OpenEA library~\citep{OpenEA} and WikiData-5M~\citep{wang2021kepler}, where by design, the test and training graphs are either from different domains, and are likely to possess different characteristics to fully test model's capability for \ourtask. %

{\bf Baselines.} 
To the best of our knowledge, InGram~\citep{ingram} is the first and only work capable of performing \ourtask without needing significant modification to the model. 
We also run RMPI~\citep{geng2023relational}, which is capable of reasoning over new nodes and new relations but requires extra context at test time (test graphs either contain training relations or ontology about unseen relations). 
In addition, we consider the state-of-the-art link prediction model NBFNet~\citep{zhu2021neural} capable of generalizing to new nodes but not new relations and modify its architecture to work with new relations at test time (following \citet{ingram}'s approach). We also compare our models with message-passing GNNs, including GAT~\citep{velivckovic2017graph}, GIN~\citep{xu2018powerful}, GraphConv~\citep{Morris2019WL}, which treats the graph as a homogeneous graph by ignoring the relation types. 
For fair comparisons, we add distance features as in \Cref{eq:dssrepr} to increase the expressiveness of these GNNs. 
Additional baseline details are in \Cref{sec:appd-supexp}.

{\bf Relation and Node Prediction Tasks.} We report the Hits@10 performances over 5 runs of different random seeds for all models on both the relation prediction task of $(i, ?, j)$ and the more traditional node prediction task of $(i, k, ?)$. For each task, we sample 50 negative triplets for each ground-truth positive target triplet during test evaluation by corrupting the relation type or the tail node respectively. %
{\em Further experiment details on synthetic tasks, additional datasets from \citet{ingram}, baseline implementations, ablation studies, and other metrics (e.g., MRR, Hits@1) can be found in \Cref{sec:appd-supexp}.


\subsection{\OurTask over \ourdata Dataset} %
The OpenEA library~\citep{OpenEA} contains multiple {\ourgrapheases} of relational databases (i.e., knowledge graphs) from different domains on similar topics, 
such as DBPedia~\citep{lehmann2015dbpedia} in different languages (English, French and German), YAGO~\citep{rebele2016yago} and Wikidata~\citep{vrandevcic2014wikidata}.
We create a new dataset \textbf{\ourdata} (details in \Cref{appx:exp-pediatype}) by sampling from the OpenEA library~\citep{OpenEA}, including pairs of {\ourgrapheases} such as English-to-French DBPedia (denoted as EN-FR), DBPedia-to-YAGO (denoted as DB-YG), etc.. 
In each graph, triplets are randomly divided into 80\% training, 10\% validation, and 10\% test. 
We then train and validate the model on one of the graphs (e.g., EN) and directly apply it to another graph (e.g., DE), which has completely new nodes and new relation types.

\Cref{tab:pediatypes-relation} shows the results on the relation prediction task, and \Cref{tab:pediatypes-node} shows the node prediction task on \ourdata. Across all scenarios on both tasks, our models, \update{\OurNewModel} and \OurOtherModel, obtain significantly better average performance, achieving up to \update{50.48\% absolute} improvement in relation prediction and up to \update{23.37\% absolute} improvement in node prediction compared to the best-performing baseline. 
Furthermore, \update{\OurNewModel} tends to have smaller standard deviations than \OurOtherModel, and both demonstrate much smaller standard deviations than InGram in almost all scenarios, 
corroborating our theoretical predictions in~\Cref{sec:theory} that a model directly producing double equivariant representations will be more stable than positional embeddings, which are only double equivariant in expectation. 

Interestingly, we observe that in the node prediction task, the message-passing GNNs (GAT, GIN, and GraphConv) achieve quite excellent performances, even though \textit{they completely disregard the information carried by different relation types and treat the \ourgraphease as a homogeneous graph.} This observation corroborates with the conclusions of~\citet{jambor2021exploring}. Indeed, only 4 out of 8 scenarios did InGram outperform the message-passing GNNs on this task, suggesting the node prediction task might be too easy because a homogeneous link prediction model can do decently well.

\section{Conclusion}

This work formally introduced the \ourtask task defined over both new nodes and new relation types in the test data. It also defined {\em double equivariant models} and {\em distributionally double equivariant positional embedding} models for this task.
We showed that, similar to how node equivariances impose learning structural node representations in unattributed graphs, double (node and relation) equivariances impose relational structure learning for {\ourgrapheases}.
\update{
We then introduced ISDEA+, a fast, accurate, and consistent double equivariant architecture that outperforms baselines in nearly all tasks, and our theory allowed us to improve an existing baseline via Monte Carlo averaging. %
 Finally, we proposed two real-world \ourtask benchmarks, and empirically verified the ability of our proposed approach to perform zero-shot meta-learning tasks where pre-training on more KG topics increases the zero-shot ability of our model to predict on new relation types over new entities on unseen KG topics.}
