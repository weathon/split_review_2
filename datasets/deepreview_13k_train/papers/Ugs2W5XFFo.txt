\title{Information Theoretic Text-to-Image Alignment}

\begin{abstract}

Diffusion models for \gls{T2I} conditional generation have seen tremendous success recently. Despite their success, accurately capturing user intentions with these models still requires a laborious trial and error process. This challenge is commonly identified as a model alignment problem, an issue that has attracted considerable attention by the research community. 
Instead of relying on fine-grained linguistic analyses of prompts, human annotation, or auxiliary vision-language models to steer image generation, in this work we present a novel method that relies on an information-theoretic alignment measure. In a nutshell, our method uses self-supervised fine-tuning and relies on point-wise mutual information between prompts and images to define a synthetic training set to induce model alignment.
Our comparative analysis shows that our method is on-par or superior to the state-of-the-art, yet requires nothing but a pre-trained denoising network to estimate \acrshort{MI} and a lightweight fine-tuning strategy. %to substantially improve \acrshort{T2I} model alignment. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Generative models used for \acrfull{T2I} conditional generation~\cite{rombach2022, ramesh2022hierarchical, saharia2022, balaji2022, gafni2022, podell2024} have reached impressive performance. In particular, diffusion models~\cite{song2019, ho2020, kingma2021, song2020, song2021a, dhariwal2021} produce images obtained by specifying a natural text prompt, that acts as a guiding signal~\cite{ho2022classifier, nichol2022glide, rombach2022} for the generative process. Such synthetic images are of extremely high-quality, diversity and realism.
However, a faithful translation of the prompt into an image that contains the intended semantics has been shown to be problematic~\cite{conwell2022, feng2023, wang2023}. Issues include catastrophic neglecting --- one or more elements of the prompt are not generated, incorrect attribute binding --- attributes such as color, shape, and texture describing elements are missing or wrongly assigned, incorrect spatial layout --- generated elements do not follow a specified positioning, and more generally, difficulty in handling complex prompts.
Such \textit{model alignment} problems have attracted a lot of attention recently. Addressing \gls{T2I} alignment is challenging, and a wide range of methods have been studied, including heuristics for prompt engineering~\cite{witteveen2022investigating, liu2022design, wang2023} and negative prompting~\cite{hfnegative, mahajan2023prompting, ogezi2024optimizing}, which shift the alignment problem to users. Alternatively, other approaches advance that \gls{T2I} alignment can be improved by an appropriate seed selection~\cite{Samuel2023SeedSelect, karthik2023if}, or prompt rewriting~\cite{mañas2024OPT2I}. More generally, the bulk of \gls{T2I} alignment research develops along two main lines: inference-time and fine-tuning methods.

For inference-time methods, the key intuition is that the generative process can be optimized by modifying the reverse path of the latent variables. Some works~\cite{chefer2023, li2023, rassin2023} mitigate failure cases by refining on-the-fly the cross-attention units \cite{tang2023} of the denoising network of \gls{SD}~\cite{rombach2022}, to attend to all subject tokens in the prompt and strengthen their activations. Many other inference-time methods, which focus on individual failure cases, have been proposed~\cite{agarwal2023, liu2022, kang2023counting, dahary2024, meral2024, feng2023Structured, kim2023, wu2023}. The common trait of inference-time approaches is that they require a linguistic analysis of prompts, leading to specialized solutions that rely on auxiliary models for prompt understanding. Due to the extra optimization costs incurred during sampling, inference-time methods result in longer image generation time.

Alternatively, fine-tuning can also be used for \gls{T2I} alignment. Some works~\cite{wu2023better, lee2023aligning} require human annotation, which is used to prepare a fine-tuning set. Other methods \cite{fan2023dpok, wallace2023diffusion, clark2024directly} rely on reinforcement learning, \gls{DPO} or a differentiable reward function to steer model behavior. Additionally, recent methods~\cite{yuan2024selfplay, xu2023imagereward, sun2023dreamsync, wang2023tokencompose, ma2023subjectdiffusionopen} use self-playing, auxiliary models such as \gls{VQA}~\cite{li2023blip2} or segmentation maps~\cite{kirillov2023ICCV} in a semi-supervised fine-tuning setting. Most fine-tuning methods either require human annotation, which is subjective, costly, and does not scale well, or auxiliary models that must be instructed to provide alignment scores. However, fine-tuning methods do not incur in additional costs at image generation time.

Finally, various works~\cite{hu2023tifa, gordon2023mismatch, grimal2024tiam} define \gls{T2I} alignment metrics that rely on \gls{VQA} and \glspl{LLM} to measure and explain misalignment problems. In particular, a recent work \cite{huang2023t2icompbench} introduces a comprehensive benchmark suite, which can be used to assess alignment w.r.t. several categories, such as attribute binding, spatial attributes and prompt complexity. 

In this paper we take an information theoretic perspective on \gls{T2I} alignment by using \gls{MI} to quantify the non-linear relationship between prompts and the corresponding images. In particular, we focus on the estimation of \textit{point-wise} \gls{MI} using neural estimators~\cite{belghazi2018mine, song2019understanding, brekelmans2023improving, franzese2024minde, kong2024interpretable}, and show that \gls{MI} can be interpreted as an extremely general measure of \gls{T2I} alignment, which does not require linguistic analysis of prompts, nor does it rely on auxiliary models or heuristics. 
Our method, which we call \acrshort{MITUNE}, unfolds as follows. We build upon the work in~\cite{franzese2024minde} and extend it to compute point-wise \gls{MI}.
We then proceed with a self-supervised fine-tuning approach, whereby we use point-wise \gls{MI} to construct an information-theoretic aligned fine-tuning set using synthetic data generated by the \gls{T2I} model itself. We then use the recent adapter presented in~\cite{liu2024dora} to fine-tune a small fraction of weights of the \gls{T2I} model denoising network. 
In summary, our work presents the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a new alignment metric that relies on \gls{MI} between natural prompts and corresponding images (\Cref{sec:preliminaries}). We show that \gls{MI}-based alignment is compatible with the most advanced techniques available today, which are based on auxiliary \gls{VQA} or human preference models (\Cref{sec:mialign}). Pairwise \gls{MI} is an extremely simple and general measure of similarity between arbitrary distributions, and we leverage it to propose a new \gls{T2I} alignment method.
    \item We design a self-supervised fine-tuning method that uses a small number of fine-tuning samples to align a pre-trained \gls{T2I} model with no inference-time overhead (\Cref{sec:finetuning}).
    \item We perform an extensive experimental campaign (\Cref{sec:experiments}) using a recent \gls{T2I} benchmark suite~\cite{huang2023t2icompbench}, and obtain extremely competitive results for a variety of alignment categories. Previous work require an accurate analysis of which misalignment problem to address, either through linguistic analysis and prompt decomposition, or through costly human-annotation campaigns. Instead, our approach relies on a theoretically sound measure of alignment, and it is lightweight, as it does not require large amounts of fine-tuning data.
\end{itemize}


\section{Preliminaries}



\subsection{Diffusion models}\label{sec:diffmodel}

Denoising diffusion models \cite{ho2020, sohl-dickstein15} are generative models which are characterized by a forward process, that is fixed to a Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise to the data according to a carefully selected variance schedule $\beta_t$, and a corresponding discrete-time reverse process, that has a Markov structure as well. Intuitively, diffusion models rely on the principle of iterative denoising: starting from a simple distribution $\mbx_T \sim \cN(\mathbf{0}, \mbI)$, samples are generated by iterative applications of a denoising network $\mbepsilon_\mbtheta$, that removes noise over $T$ denoising steps.
A simple way to learn the denoising network $\mbepsilon_\mbtheta$ is to consider a re-weighted variational lower bound of the marginal likelihood:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lsimple}
    \cL_{\text{simple}}(\mbtheta) = 
    \E_{t \sim U(0,T), \mbx_0 \sim p_{\text{data}}, \mbepsilon \sim \cN(\mathbf{0}, \mbI)}
    \left[
        ||
            \mbepsilon - \mbepsilon_\mbtheta (\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} \mbx_0 + (\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}) \mbepsilon, t)
        ||^2
    \right],
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t$, $\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s$. For sampling, we let $\sigma_t^2 = \beta_t$. A similar variational objective can be obtained by switching perspective from discrete to continuous time \cite{song2021a}, whereby the denoising network approximates a score function of the data distribution. For image data, the denoising network is typically parametrized by a \acrshort{UNET} \cite{ronneberger2015, rombach2022}.

This simple formulation has been extended to conditional generation \cite{ho2021classifierfree}, whereby a conditioning signal $\mbp$ injects ``external information'' in the iterative denoising process. This, requires a simple extension to the denoising network such that it can accept the conditioning signal: $\mbepsilon_\mbtheta(\mbx_t, \mbp, t)$. Then, during training, a randomized approach allows to learn both the conditional and unconditional variant of the denoising network, for example by assigning a null value to the conditioning signal. At sampling time, a weighted linear combination of the conditional and unconditional networks, such as $\tilde{\mbepsilon}_\mbtheta(\mbx_t, \mbp, t) = \mbepsilon_\mbtheta(\mbx_t, \emptyset, t) + \gamma (\mbepsilon_\mbtheta(\mbx_t, \mbp, t) - \mbepsilon_\mbtheta(\mbx_t, \emptyset, t))$ can be used. % to generate the image. %MAX: to ... 

In this work, we use pre-trained diffusion models~\cite{rombach2022}, which operate on a learned projection of the input data $\mbx_0$ into a corresponding latent variable $\mbz_0$, which is lower dimensional compared to the original data. Moreover, the conditioning signal $\mbp$ is obtained by a text encoder such as \acrshort{CLIP} \cite{radford2021}. 

\subsection{MI estimation}\label{sec:estimation}

\gls{MI} is a central measure to study the non-linear dependence between random variables~\cite{shannon1948mathematical, mackay2003information}, and has been extensively used in machine learning for representation learning~\cite{bell1995information, stratos2018mutual, belghazi2018mine, oord2019representation, hjelm2019learning}, and for both training~\cite{alemi2019deep, chen2016infogan, zhao2018information} and evaluating generative models~\cite{alemi2019gilbo, huang2020evaluating}. 

For many problems of interest, precise computation of \gls{MI} is not an easy task~\cite{mcallester2020formal, paninski2003estimation}. Consequently, a wide range of techniques for \gls{MI} estimation have flourished. In this work, we focus on realistic and high-dimensional data, which calls for recent advances in \gls{MI} estimation~\cite{papamakarios2017masked, belghazi2018mine, oord2019representation, song2019understanding, rhodes2020telescoping, letizia2022copula, brekelmans2023improving, kong2024interpretable}. 
In particular, we capitalize on a recent method~\cite{franzese2024minde}, that relies on the theory behind continuous-time diffusion processes \cite{song2021a} and uses the Girsanov Theorem \cite{oksendal2003stochastic} to show that score functions can be used to compute the \gls{KL} divergence between two distributions, as well as the (conditional) entropy of a random variable. 
In what follows, we use a simplified notation, and gloss over several mathematical details to favor intuition over rigor. 
Here we consider discrete-time diffusion models, which are equivalent to the continuous-time counterpart under the variational formulation, up to constants and discretization errors \cite{song2021a}. 

We begin by considering the two arbitrary random variables $\mbz, \mbp$ which are sampled from the joint distribution $p_{\text{latent},\text{prompt}}$, where the former corresponds to the distribution of the projections in a latent space of the image distribution, and the latter to the distribution of prompts used for conditional generation. 
Then, following the approach in~\cite{franzese2024minde}, with the necessary adaptation to the discrete domain (see \Cref{app:minde} for details), point-wise \gls{MI} estimation can be obtained as follows %according to:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mi}
    \textsc{I}(\mbz,\mbp) = 
    \E_{t,\mbepsilon\sim \cN(\mathbf{0}, \mbI)}
    \left[
    \kappa_t
    ||
        \mbepsilon_\mbtheta(\mbz_t, \mbp, t) - \mbepsilon_\mbtheta(\mbz_t, \emptyset, t)
    ||^2
    \right],\,\,\, \kappa_t = \frac{\beta_t T}{2\alpha_t (1- \bar{\alpha}_t)}.
\end{equation}


Given a pre-trained diffusion model, we compute an  expectation (over diffusion times $t$) of the scaled squared norm of the difference between the conditional and unconditional networks, which corresponds to an estimate of the point-wise \gls{MI} between an image and a prompt. This is not only a key ingredient of our information-theoretic alignment method, but it also represents a competitive advantage, as it enables a self-contained approach to alignment, which relies on the \gls{T2I} model alone and does not need auxiliary models, nor human feedback.




\section{Our method: \acrshort{MITUNE}


The \gls{T2I} alignment problem arises when user intentions, as expressed through natural text prompts, fail to materialize in the generated image. 
Our novel approach aims to address alignment using a theoretically grounded \gls{MI} estimation, that applies across various contexts. While our focus in this work is on \gls{T2I} alignment, our framework remains extensible to other modalities.
To improve model alignment, we introduce a self-supervised fine-tuning method. Leveraging the same T2I model, we estimate \gls{MI} and generate an information-theoretic aligned fine-tuning dataset.

\subsection{Self-supervised fine-tuning with \acrshort{MITUNE}}\label{sec:finetuning}

In a nutshell, given a pre-trained diffusion model such as \gls{SD}~\cite{rombach2022} (or any variant thereof), we leverage our point-wise \gls{MI} estimation method to  select a small fine-tuning dataset set of information-theoretic aligned examples. Our method unfolds as follows.

Our self-supervised objective is to rely solely on the pre-trained model to produce a given amount of fine-tuning data, which is then filtered to retain only prompt-image pairs with a high degree of alignment, according to pair-wise \gls{MI} estimates. We begin with a set of fine-tuning prompts $\mathcal{P}$, which can be either manually crafted, or borrowed from available prompt collections~\cite{wang2023, huang2023t2icompbench}. Ideally, fine-tuning prompts should be conceived to stress the pre-trained model with challenging attribute and spatial binding, or complex rendering tasks.

\input{algos/mitune}
As described in \Cref{algo:mitune}, for each prompt $p^{(i)}$ in the fine-tuning set $\mathcal{P}$, we use the pre-trained model to generate a fixed number $M$ of synthetic, paired images. Given prompt-image pairs $(p^{(i)} ; z^{(j)}),\, j \in [1,M]$, we estimate pair-wise \gls{MI} and select the top $k$ pairs, which will be part of the model fine-tuning dataset $\mathcal{S}$. 
Finally, we augment the pre-trained model with adapters~\cite{hu2021lora, liu2024dora}, and proceed with fine-tuning, according to several variants. We study the impact of the adapter choice, and whether only the denoising network, or both the denoising and text encoder networks should be fine-tuned. Moreover, we measure the impact of the number of fine-tuning rounds $R$ to apply to the pre-trained model, i.e., we renew the fine-tuning dataset $\mathcal{S}$, and fine-tune the last model generation. %We also study the impact of fine-tuning regularization terms, to steer the model to not diverge too much from the original pre-trained model. 

\input{algos/miestimate}
Our efficient implementation combines latent generation, and point-wise \gls{MI} computation, as shown in \Cref{algo:miestimate}. Indeed, since \gls{MI} estimation involves computing an expectation over diffusion times $t$, it is easy to combine generation and estimation in the same loop. Moreover, this function can be easily parallelized, to gain significant performance boost in the construction of the fine-tuning set $\mathcal{S}$.



\section{Experimental evaluation}



We now delve into the details of our experimental protocol, to assess the performance of our proposed method \gls{MITUNE} in comparison to existing approaches.

\subsection{Baselines}
We begin with an overview of the recent \gls{T2I} alignment methods we use in our comparative analysis.

\noindent \textbf{Base model.}  We use a pre-trained \gls{T2I} model called \gls{SD}-2.1-base, which works at $512\times512$ resolution. For fairness in our comparative analysis, we adapt all competitors to the same base model.

\noindent \textbf{Inference-time methods.} These methods intervene at sampling time, and optimize the latent variables $\mbz_t$ throughout the numerical integration used to generate the (latent) image. This is done through an auxiliary loss function that uses attention maps to steer model alignment, based on a fine-grained linguistic analysis of the prompt. In this category, we consider: \gls{AE}~\cite{chefer2023attendandexcite}, \gls{SDG}~\cite{feng2023Structured} and \gls{SCG}~\cite{salimans2022distillation}.

\noindent \textbf{Fine-tuning methods.} Given a pre-trained model, these methods fine-tune it using adapters~\cite{hu2021lora}. Several variants exist, such as those based on reinforcement learning, and those based on supervision. In this category, we consider: \gls{DPOK}~\cite{fan2023dpok}, \gls{GORS}~\cite{huang2023t2icompbench} and \gls{HN}~\cite{krojer2023HNitm}.

    

\subsection{Benchmark and metrics}
We evaluate the capability of \gls{T2I} models to integrate and compose various concepts into a detailed and consistent scene based on textual prompts, which are generated with either predefined rules or ChatGPT~\cite{chatgpt} using \gls{T2I}-CompBench~\cite{huang2023t2icompbench}. This benchmark suite involves combining attributes with objects, assembling different objects with defined interactions and spatial relationships, and creating intricate compositions.
In this work we use the 6 categories defined in \gls{T2I}-CompBench, which are: attribute binding (including color, shape, and texture categories), object relationships (including spatial and non-spatial relationship), and complex compositions.

Evaluating compositional properties of \gls{T2I} is difficult as it requires a detailed cross-modal understanding for a given prompt-image pair. Despite several metrics have been used in the past, including \acrshort{CLIP}~\cite{hessel2021-clipscore, radford2021}, \textsc{miniGPT-4}~\cite{zhu2024minigpt}, and of course human evaluation, in our work we use \acrshort{BLIP} and \gls{HPS}. \acrshort{BLIP} has been consistently used across the literature, and allows for an additional comparison for approaches that rely on a different base model. In addition, \gls{HPS} is another useful metric that results from a pre-trained model on human annotated data, that contributes to an evaluation of competing methods based on alignment and aesthetics. Finally, note that the score for the 2D-Spatial category is based on UniDet~\cite{zhou2021simple}, as done in \gls{T2I}-CompBench~\cite{huang2023t2icompbench}.

\subsection{Details on our method \gls{MITUNE}}
Next, we provide additional details on \gls{MITUNE}, including fine-tuning prompts and training details.

\noindent \textbf{Fine-tuning prompts.} For the fine-tuning prompts set $\mathcal{P}$, we use text prompts defined in~\cite{huang2023t2icompbench}. We select prompts from each of the 6 available categories. For each fine-tuning prompt $\mbp^{(i)}$, we generate $M$ images using the pre-trained \gls{SD}-2.1-base model, compute their point-wise \gls{MI}, and select the top $k$. We remark that, albeit in a different context, this task resembles that of image retrieval, as discussed in~\cite{krojer2023HNitm}.
We also study the benefits of several rounds $R$ of model fine-tuning. Furthermore, we explore the impact of fine-tuning prompt variety, as well as different values for $M$ and $k$.
The default values are $M=50$, $k=1$, and $R=1$, which result in a fine-tuning set $\mathcal{S}$ containing 700 prompt-image pairs. Additional configurations and results are available in \Cref{app:full_results}.
\noindent \textbf{Fine-tuning Details.} With \gls{MITUNE}, we inject DoRA~\cite{liu2024dora} layers into the denoising \acrshort{UNET} network only, whereas other layers, and the \acrshort{CLIP} text encoder, are frozen.  DoRA layers' rank and scaling factor $\alpha$ are set to 32. Full setup details are available in \Cref{app:exp_protocol}. We experimented with different configurations, including LoRA layers~\cite{hu2021lora}, as well as fine tuning both the denoising network and the \acrshort{CLIP}-based text encoder, but measured no performance improvements, as shown in \Cref{app:full_results}.

\section{Discussion}


The self-supervised nature of our approach to fine-tuning, which solely relies on the \gls{T2I} model for the generation of a synthetic candidate set of prompt-image pairs, is limited by the capabilities of the pre-trained model. Indeed, an underlying assumption we make is to expect the pre-trained model to eventually produce images that are aligned to the prompt, as done in previous works~\cite{Samuel2023SeedSelect, karthik2023if}. Then, our information-theoretic alignment measure can be used to produce a useful fine-tuning set $\mathcal{S}$. However, in some cases (e.g., spatial or complex prompts) the pre-trained model can systematically fail in producing aligned samples. In this scenario, our method can be applied to existing \gls{T2I} models that have been fine-tuned with complementary techniques, and substantially boost alignment performance, as we demonstrate in our experiments, as shown in \Cref{tab:results_blip}.

In our work, we considered a large design space. In the context of fine-tuning approaches, we designed an alternative method to exploit pair-wise \gls{MI}. Indeed, it is simple to rewrite the loss from \Cref{eq:lsimple} to include a point-wise \gls{MI} loss term, and use a small fraction of the \gls{T2I} model training set to fine-tune it according to an ``embedded'' information-theoretic alignment measure. While this approach is in principle valid, we noticed that throughout the parameter optimization phase, \gls{MI} estimates are affected by high variance, due to the poor quality of the \textit{unconditional} denoising network of typical pre-trained \gls{T2I} models~\cite{lin2024diffusion}. Training the \gls{T2I} model from scratch according to \cite{lin2024diffusion} can mitigate this issue, but defeats the purpose of using a pre-trained model.

Alternatively, we designed a variant of our method that operates at inference time, in a similar vein to the work in~\cite{chefer2023attendandexcite}. To do that, we blend the works~\cite{franzese2024minde, kong2024interpretable}, to replace attention maps with pixel-level point-wise \gls{MI} maps. Conditional sampling can then be amended as in~\cite{chefer2023attendandexcite}: we can define a new inference-time loss term that we use to update $\mbz_t$ on-the-fly, using information maps instead of cross-attention maps to steer model alignment. This is another valid approach that however bears the same problems discussed above: information maps quality suffers from a poorly trained unconditional denoising network, leading to poor results. Since the work in \cite{kong2024interpretable} does not explicitly focus on model alignment, the authors build information maps that use the unconditional denoising network with an edited prompt, instead of a null token, which is not an option for our study.



\section{Conclusion}



\gls{T2I} alignment emerged as an important endeavor to steer image generation to follow the semantics and user intent expressed through a natural text prompt, as it can save considerable manual effort.

In this work, we presented a novel approach to measure alignment, that uses point-wise \gls{MI} between prompt-image pairs to quantify the amount of information ``flowing'' between natural text and images. We demonstrated, both qualitatively and quantitatively, that pair-wise \gls{MI} is coherent with existing alignment measures that either use external \gls{VQA} models or human preference.

Armed with an information-theoretic measure of alignment, we presented \gls{MITUNE}, a lightweight, self-supervised fine-tuning method that uses a pre-trained \gls{T2I} model such as \gls{SD} to estimate \gls{MI}, and generates a synthetic set of aligned prompt-image pairs, which is then used in a parameter-efficient fine-tuning stage, to align the \gls{T2I} model.
Our approach does not require human annotation, auxiliary \gls{VQA} models, nor costly inference-time techniques, and outperforms state-of-the-art approaches on most alignment categories examined in the literature.

\noindent \textbf{Limitations.} Accurate \gls{MI} estimation is a difficult task, especially for high-dimensional, real-world data. For our method, \gls{MI} estimation accuracy depends on the quality of the pre-trained denoising network of the base \gls{T2I} model which, in the case of \gls{SD}, is problematic for the unconditional version. As such, it is possible that \gls{MI}-based alignment can suffer. Furthermore, despite \gls{MI} appears to be more aligned to human preference than existing metrics, it is less interpretable when compared to methods that perform linguistic prompt analyses, or use auxiliary \gls{VQA} models for explainability.

Finally, our information-theoretic alignment measure can be applied to arbitrary distributions, beyond text and images. Further work is necessary to demonstrate its application to other pre-trained models that operate on other modalities, such as e.g., the audio domain.

\noindent \textbf{Broader impact.} A valid concern to point out regarding our method \gls{MITUNE} is that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate deepfakes for disinformation. Nevertheless, we believe that the positive impact of our work outweighs such problems.
