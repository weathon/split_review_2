\title{Provably Efficient Learning in Partially Observable Contextual Bandit}

\begin{abstract}


In this paper, we investigate transfer learning in partially observable contextual bandits, 
where agents have limited knowledge from other agents and partial information about hidden confounders.
We first convert the problem to identifying or partially identifying causal effects between actions and rewards through optimization problems.
To solve these optimization problems, we discretize the original functional constraints of unknown distributions into linear constraints, 
and sample compatible causal models via sequentially solving linear programmings to obtain causal bounds with the consideration of estimation error. 
Our sampling algorithms provide desirable convergence results for suitable sampling distributions. 
We then show how causal bounds can be applied to improving classical bandit algorithms and affect the regrets with respect to the size of action sets and function spaces.
Notably, in the task with function approximation which allows us to handle general context distributions,
our method improves the order dependence on function space size compared with previous literatures.
We formally prove that our causally enhanced algorithms outperform classical bandit algorithms and achieve orders of magnitude faster convergence rates. 
Finally, we perform simulations that demonstrate the efficiency of our strategy compared to the current state-of-the-art methods.
This research has the potential to enhance the performance of contextual bandit agents in real-world applications where data is scarce and costly to obtain.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Bandit learning problems involve an agent making a series of decisions with the aim of maximizing rewards or minimizing regrets, as described in \cite{banditBook}. 
Classical bandit algorithms typically do not consider any prior knowledge, 
which may result in substantial time and computational costs to learn a near-optimal policy. 
To address this issue, transfer learning (TL) techniques have been proposed to expedite the learning process of a target task by leveraging knowledge obtained from a related source task. 
These techniques often rely on exploiting domain expertise and transferring knowledge that remains invariant across the source and target domains, as discussed in \cite{TLsurvey}.

Transfer learning (TL) methods commonly assume that both the expert and learner agents have access to the same information, 
as noted in \cite{TLinCB,TCBforCrossDomain}. 
However, this assumption does not always hold true in practical scenarios. 
For instance, human drivers rely on past experiences to form a mental model of the world, 
which guides their actions along with other sensory inputs such as visual information. 
Such experiential information cannot be captured by sensors like cameras and lidar present in autonomous vehicles. 
Furthermore, autonomous vehicles themselves have their own observed contexts that are not available to human drivers. 
This leads to a transfer learning problem in partially observable contextual bandit problems (TLPOCB).

Drawing from the motivating example mentioned previously, 
we examine transfer learning problems in partially observable contextual bandits. 
In this setting, the expert has access to either complete or partial contextual information, 
which it passes on to the agent. 
However, the agent can only gather partial contextual information due to restricted transfer resulting from factors such as privileged information, privacy concerns, or physical limitations. 
Another scenario involves enhancing the performance of a partial model by discovering a new factor that can impact the performance of the current model, 
though the causal relationship of this factor is currently unknown. 
In such cases, we can estimate the frequency of the new factor and use our method to improve model performance. 
Typically, the frequency of the new factor is independent of pre-existing models.

Using expert data without comprehending the expert context can result in biased learned policies that require a considerable amount of new data samples to improve. 
Causal inference has been widely employed in addressing unobserved contextual information in bandit and transfer learning problems, 
as discussed in \cite{bandits_causal_approaches,boundingCE_continuous_IV,causalbandit,TLinCB,TLinRL,gxp}, 
where qualitative knowledge on causal relationships is frequently available in complex reinforcement learning (RL) settings. 
In \cite{causalbook}, a framework is established for inferring the effect of actions (target) from a combination of a causal model (to be defined) and diverse sources of data (source). 
Building upon this framework, we outline three transfer learning tasks in partially observable contextual bandits. 
Identifying causal effects in offline learning often necessitates instrumental variables (IVs) or proxies, which may not be practical, as noted in \cite{IAB,DeepProxy,gxp}. 
Therefore, we develop new causal bounds for general context and reward distributions, whether discrete or continuous, 
to assist learner agents in achieving faster learning rates with limited source requirements. 
These causal bounds enable the reduction of exploration variance in our bandit algorithms, 
thus accelerating learning rates.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows. 
Firstly, we introduce new tight causal bounds for partially observable transfer learning algorithms. 
To approximate these bounds, we develop sampling algorithms that sequentially solve refined linear programming problems to obtain valid samples. 
We demonstrate that under mild assumptions on sampling distribution, 
the ordered statistics generated by our algorithms can converge to the bounds obtained by discretization optimization problems in probability. 
Moreover, with the help of optimization procedure, the convergence results can be enhanced to almost surely convergence.
Additionally, our algorithms incorporate estimation error, which is often neglected in previous literature \cite{boundingCE_continuous_IV,CEbound,TLinRL}. 
In the Task 2 where the causal effect can be fully identified, we compute the sample size required to achieve $\epsilon$-identification \cite{Epsilon-Identifiability}.
Secondly, we provide theoretical evidence that our algorithms consistently outperform classical algorithms, as shown in \cref{tb: comparison}. 
Our theoretical results illustrate how our bounds impact the gap-dependent regrets and minimax regrets in terms of bandit quantities, such as the cardinality of action sets. 
Moreover, our transfer learning algorithms only require several causal bounds rather than the whole dataset, 
which may show advantages when transferring the whole dataset is costly.
Thirdly, we consider transfer learning with function approximation in Task 3 and develop a context-dependent method to handle the bounds and improve the learning rate. 
Our regret demonstrates an improvement in dependence on policy space $\Pi$ from $\sqrt{|\Pi|}$ to $\sqrt{\log |\Pi|}$, compared with \cite{boundingCE_continuous_IV}. 
Finally, we conduct various numerical experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of our methods.

\subsection{Related Work}
Our work is related to prior research on causal bounds. 
In \cite{causaloverview}, a general calculus known as do-calculus is developed for identifying causal effects using probabilistic tools. 
For non-identifiable causal problems, \citet{tianBound} derives a model-free causal bound. 
\citet{TLinMAB} formulates a linear programming approach to obtain a causal bound in a discrete setting, 
while \citet{scalableLPbound} makes this approach scalable. 
\citet{CE_nonbinary,unitSelectionCE} extend these methods to non-binary outcomes, 
and \citet{boundingCE_continuous_IV} employs instrumental variables to extend them to continuous outcomes. 
However, these LP-based methods may not be tight and rely on structural assumptions on hidden confounders. 
\citet{CEbound} is the first to consider partially observable back-door and front-door criteria and employs a non-linear optimization problem to compute causal bounds. 
Nonetheless, solving a non-linear optimization problem is computationally intensive and may get trapped in local optima. 
\citet{summaryCB} summarize these optimization-based methods and provides an automated approach to causal inference in discrete settings. 
\citet{MCMCbound} employ a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method to approximate causal bounds using observational and experimental data. 
However, this method is not directly applicable in our setting because the agent in our scenario has access to partial knowledge about latent confounders, 
whereas \citet{MCMCbound} make structural assumptions on latent confounders.
As a result, \citet{MCMCbound} only need sample a random vector from Dirichlet distributions
while we need to sample constrained table from distributions supported on more complex simplex.

Our work is also closely linked to transfer learning. 
\citet{TLinMAB,TLinMABwithFiniteModels} investigate transfer learning in multi-armed bandit problems. 
\citet{TLinCB} examine transfer learning in contextual bandits with covariate shift, 
where two tasks share the same reward function (causal effects) across two bandits. 
\citet{TCBforCrossDomain} apply transfer learning techniques in recommendation systems. 
\citet{TLinRL} extend the ideas in \cite{TLinMAB,boundingCE_continuous_IV} to reinforcement learning settings. 
A similar work is \cite{PObandit}, which uses a partially missing offline dataset to enhance the performance of online learning in linear bandits.
Though several papers \cite{POCB_linear,POCB_linear_TS} investigate partially observable contextual bandits,
we found few papers focusing on transfer learning in partially observable contextual bandits.
Partially Observable Markov decision process (POMDP), including general partially observable dynamical systems \cite{POMDPDynamicalSystems}, also shares the similarity with our setting. 
Researchers have developed various methods to address causal inference in POMDPs. 
For example, \citet{POMDP_IV} use instrumental variables to identify causal effects, 
while \citet{OEPOMDP,offlinePOMDPproxy} extend this approach to general proxy variables in offline policy evaluation. 
In online reinforcement learning, \citet{POMDPlinear,ProvablyEfficientCRL} use the backdoor criterion to explicitly adjust for confounding bias when confounders are fully observable. 
They also incorporate uncertainty from partially observable confounders into the Bellman equation and demonstrate provably optimal learning with linear function approximation in both fully and partially observable tasks.
However, due to the complexity of reinforcement learning, transfer learning in POMDPs with the general function approximation still remains unknown. 
In our task 3, we address the problem of partially observable contextual bandit with the general function approximation under realizability assumption, 
which shows the potential to generalize to POMDPs and other related settings.




\section{Preliminaries}



\emph{Causal models.}
In this section, we introduce the basic notations and definitions used throughout the paper. 
We use capital letters to denote variables ($X$) and small letters for their values ($x$). 
Let $\mathcal{X}$ stand for the domain of $X$, 
and $|\mathcal{X}| $ for its cardinality if $X$ is finite and discrete.
We use $F(x)$ to represent the cumulative distribution function for random variable $X$.
We will use structural causal models (SCMs) \cite{causalbook} as the basic semantical framework of our analysis. 
A SCM $\mathcal{M}$ consists of a set of observed (endogenous) variables and unobserved (exogenous) variables. 
The values of each endogenous variable are determined by a structural function taking as argument a combination of the other endogenous and exogenous variables. 
A causal diagram associated with the SCM $\mathcal{M}$ is a directed acyclic graph where nodes correspond to variables and edges represent causal relations 
(see the causal graph of TLPOCB in \cref{fig: causal model with unobserved U and observed W}).
\emph{Multi-armed bandit.}
We now define the MAB setting using causal language. 
An agent for a stochastic MAB is given a SCM $\mathcal{M}$ with a decision node $A$ representing the arm selection and an outcome variable $Y$ representing the reward. 
For arm $a\in\mathcal{A}$, its expected reward $\mu_a$ is thus the effect of the action $do(a)$, i.e., $\mu_a = \mean{Y |do(a)}$. 
Let $\mu^*=\max_{a\in \mathcal{A}} \mu_a $ and $a^*$ denote the optimal expected reward and the optimal arm, respectively. 
At each round $t = 1,2,\cdots,T$, the agent performs an action $do(A_t = a_t)$ and observes a reward $Y_t$. 
The objective of the agent is to minimize the cumulative regret, namely,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq: MAB regret}
  Reg(T) = T\mu^* - \sum_{t=1}^T \mean{Y_t}.
\end{equation}
\emph{Contextual bandit.}
We then describe the contextual bandit in causal setting. 
An agent for a contextual bandit is given a SCM $\mathcal{M}$ with an observed context variable $W$ besides the arm selection node $A$ and a reward variable $Y$. 
At each round $t = 1,2,\cdots,T$, the agent can observe a context $w_t$ and performs an action $do(A_t = a_t)$ based on contexts and historical information. 
For each arm $a\in\mathcal{A}$, its expected reward given the context $w$ is defined as $\mu_{a,w} = \mean{Y |do(a),w}$. 
Let $\mu^*_w$ denote the optimal expected reward with respect to the context $w$.
The objective of the agent is to minimize the cumulative regret, namely,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq: CB regret}
  Reg(T) =  \sum_{t=1}^T \mean{ \mu^*_{w_t} -  \mu_{a,w_t} |w_t  }.
\end{equation}
Here, the expectation is taken w.r.t the randomness of algorithms. 

However, the regret in such setting typically scales with $|\mathcal{W}|$ as it can be regarded as generic reinforcement learning problems. 
A practical way to avoid dependent on $|\mathcal{W}|$ is to use function approximation methods. 
We assume that the agent has access to a class of reward functions $\mathcal{F}\subset \mathcal{W}\times \mathcal{A} \to [0,1]$ (e.g., linear function classes) 
that characterizes the mean of the reward distribution for a given context-action pair.
\begin{myasp}[Realizability]
  \label{asp: realizability}
  There exists a function $f^*\in\mathcal{F}$ such that $f(w, a) = E[Y | w,do(a)]$.
\end{myasp}
This realizability assumption is standard in contextual bandit literature \cite{fasterCB,CBwithRegressionOracles,instanceCB_RL}.
Let $\pi_f (w) = \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}} f(w, a)$ denote the policy induced by the regression function $f \in \mathcal{F}$.
The set of all induced policies is denoted as $\Pi = \{\pi_f | f \in \mathcal{F}\}$, which forms the policy space.
The objective of the agent is to achieve low regret with respect to the optimal policy $\pi_{f^*}$,
and the cumulative regret over time horizon $T$ is defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq: CB regret with function approximation}
Reg(T) = \sum_{t=1}^T  \mean{ f^*(w_t, \pi_{f^*} (w_t)) - f^*(w_t, \pi_{f} (w_t)) | w_t }.
\end{equation}

The presence of unobserved confounder $U$ can make the transfer learning tasks challenging.
Next, we give an example to show that direct approaches can lead to a policy function that is far from the true optimal policy.

\textbf{Example.} 
Consider a 2-arm contextual bandit problem, where the action $A$, context $U,W$ are binary. 
At the beginning of each round, two binary contextual variables $U,W$ are sampled from two independent Bernoulli distribution,
$
\prob{U=1} = 0.9, \prob{W=1} = 0.5.
$
The context variables $U,W$ affect the reward $Y$ received during each round according to \cref{tb:Reward table}. 

The expert agent can observe the contextual variable $U,W$ and executes a context-aware optimal policy $\pi^*_{expert}: \mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{U} \to \mathcal{A}$.
As a result, the expert generates multiple data points to obtain an observational distribution
$\prob{A,W}$ as shown in \cref{tb:Expert probability table after transfer}. 
Now we compute the causal effect 
$$
\mean{Y|do(A=0),W} = 10\times 0.1 + 0.9\times 0.9 = 1.81, \forall W\in\{0,1\}
$$
and 
$$
\mean{Y|do(A=1),W} = 0\times 0.1 + 1\times 0.9 = 0.9, \forall W\in\{0,1\}.
$$
Therefore, the optimal policy for the agent is $\pi_{agent}^*(W)=0$ with probability $1$.
However, the policy differs a lot from $\pi^*_{expert}$ as $\pi^*_{expert}$ mainly select the arm $1$. 

Upon closer examination of the example, it becomes clear that the asymmetrical probability mass function of the hidden context variable $U$ leads to a significant difference between the optimal expert and agent policies. 
Specifically, for the context $U=1$ that occurs frequently, 
the rewards of the two arms are relatively similar, 
while for the rarely occurring context $U=0$, the rewards differ significantly between the two arms.
If the agent has knowledge of the distribution of $U$, 
it can infer that the observed rewards may be heavily influenced by the skewness from $F(u)$ and adjust its decision-making accordingly to make the correct choices.

\section{Transfer Learning via Causal Inference}















In this section, we connect causal analysis with transfer learning \cite{TLinMAB,TLinRL,TLinCB} in RL by discussing a transfer scenario between two different agents. 
We apply do-calculus to systematically estimate the expected reward of each arm for the target agent.

\subsection{Task 1: transfer from contextual bandit to MAB}
\label{subsec: TL in MAB}
We first consider an off-policy learning problem between an contextual bandit expert and a MAB agent. 
Contextual bandits \cite{CBwithPredictableRewards,contextualBanditRecommendation,linContextual} is a variation of MAB where the expert can observe extra contextual information $W$ associated with the reward signal $Y$. 
The expert follows certain policy and summarizes its experiences as the joint distribution $\hat{F}(a,y,w)$. 
However, there exists a latent confounder $U$ that affects the causal effects between actions and rewards, making the observed contextual bandit model incomplete.
The agent, who is observing the expert interacting with the environment, 
wants to be more efficient and reuse the observed $\hat{F}(a,y,w)$ with the help of extra prior knowledge about $U$, i.e., $\hat{F}(u)$,
to find the optimal policy faster.

\textbf{Causal bounds.}
This transfer scenario is depicted in \cref{fig: causal model with unobserved U and observed W}, 
where the actions $A$, outcomes $Y$, context $W$ and $U$, 
and causal structures used by the expert and the agent are identical.
In the first task, the MAB agent is unable to observe contexts, 
but we will also examine the case where the agent can partially observe contexts in the task 2 and 3. 
The missing contexts may be due to privacy protection or legal restrictions. 
As the new agent cannot observe $W$ or $U$, its objective is to minimize regret as defined in equation \eqref{eq: MAB regret}.

One can find the optimal action $do(A = a^*)$ by evaluating $\mean{Y|do(a)}$.
Therefore, the off-policy learning problem is equivalent to identifying the causal effect $\mean{Y|do(a)}$ given the observational distribution $ {F}(a,y,w)$ and prior knowledge $F(u)$. 
Although $U$ is unobserved during online learning, its distribution can be usually known regardless of the model itself (e.g., $U$ stands for gender, gene
type, blood type, or age). Alternatively, if costs permit, one can estimate $ {F}(u)$ by re-testing within a small sampled sub-population.
However, even though $W \cup U$ is assumed to be sufficient, 
the causal effects of the action $A$ on the reward $Y$ is non-identifiable from incomplete experiences $ {F}(a,y,w)$ and $ {F}(u)$,
because the back-door path $A \leftarrow  U \rightarrow  Y$ is still unblocked. 
This means that the causal effects of $A$ on $Y$ cannot be estimated without bias, regardless of sample size.

However, we can tight obtain bounds on the causal effects via an optimization problem when given a prior distribution $F(u)$ and $F(a,y,w)$. 
The optimization problem is as follows:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]}
\begin{array}{rcl}
& &\sup / \inf \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}[{Y|do(a)}] \\
&s.t. & F_{\mathcal{M}}(a,y,w) = F(a,y,w), \\
& & F_{\mathcal{M}}(u) = F(u). \\
\end{array}
\end{equation}
Here, the sup/inf is taken over all causal models $\mathcal{M}$, and $F_{\mathcal{M}}$ represents the distributions in the causal model $\mathcal{M}$.
Denote the solutions to \eqref{eq: optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} as $h(a)$ and $l(a)$, respectively. 
Since the optimization is performed over all compatible causal models, the resulting bounds are guaranteed to be tight.
It is worth noting that the sufficient and necessary condition for causal identification is that $h(a)$ is equal to $l(a)$. 

This idea is similar to that proposed in other works, such as \cite{causaloverview, MCMCbound, tianBound}.

In Theorem \ref{thm: causal bound for E[Y|do(a)]}, we provide a specific version of the optimization problem \eqref{eq: optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} to obtain bounds on the causal effects of actions on rewards. 
We consider the estimation error $\epsilon$ and distinguish between known and unknown variables by using $\hat{F}$ to represent the observed distributions and $F$ to represent the unknown variables.
\begin{mythm}
   \label{thm: causal bound for E[Y|do(a)]}
   Given a causal diagram $\mathcal{G}$ and a distribution compatible with $\mathcal{G}$, let $\{W,U\}$ be a set of variables satisfying
   the back-door criterion in $\mathcal{G}$ relative to an ordered pair $(A,Y)$, where $\{W,U\}$ is partially observable, i.e., only
   probabilities $\hat{F}(a, y, w)$ and $\hat{F}(u)$ with the maximum estimation error $\epsilon$, 
   the causal effects of $A$ on $Y$ are then bounded as follows:
   $$
   l(a_0) \leq \mean{Y|do(a_0)} \leq h(a_0),
   $$
   where $l(a_0)$ and $h(a_0)$ are solutions to the following functional optimization problem for any given $a_0$
    \begin{align*}
     & l(a_0) = \inf \int_{w\in\mathcal{W},u\in\mathcal{U}}\int_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} y dF(y|a_0,w,u)dF(w,u) \\
     & h(a_0) = \sup \int_{w\in\mathcal{W},u\in\mathcal{U}}\int_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} y dF(y|a_0,w,u)dF(w,u) \\
    s.t. & \int_{u\in\mathcal{U} }  dF(a,y,w,u) = F(a,y,w),  \forall (a,y,w) \in \mathcal{A}\times \mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{U}  \\
         & \int_{a\in\mathcal{A},y\in\mathcal{Y},w\in\mathcal{W} }  dF(a,y,w,u) = F(u), \forall u \in \mathcal{U}  \\
         & \int_{y\in\mathcal{Y} }  dF(a,y,w,u) = F(a,w,u), \forall (a,w,u) \in \mathcal{A}\times \mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{U}  \\   
         & \int_{a\in\mathcal{A} }  dF(a,w,u) du = F(w,u), \forall (w,u) \in \mathcal{W}\times \mathcal{U}  \\
         &  F(y|a,w,u)F(a,w,u) = F(a,y,w,u), \forall (a,y,w,u) \in \mathcal{A}\times \mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{U}  \\   
         & |F(a,y,w)-\hat{F}(a,y,w)|\leq \epsilon, \forall (a,y,w)\in \mathcal{A}\times \mathcal{Y}\times \mathcal{W}  \\
         & |F(u)-\hat{F}(u)|\leq \epsilon,  \forall u\in  \mathcal{U}.
      \end{align*}
   Here the inf/sup is taken with respect to all unknown cumulative distribution functions $F(a,y,w,u)$, $F(a,w,u)$, $F(y|a,w,u)$, $F(w,u)$, $F(a,y,w)$ and $F(u)$.
\end{mythm}
The functional optimization problem presented in Theorem \ref{thm: causal bound for E[Y|do(a)]} provides tight lower and upper bounds on the average causal effect $l(a_0)$ and $h(a_0)$, respectively, 
for any given $a_0\in\mathcal{A}$. 
Our results generalize those of \cite{CEbound} to random variables with estimation error, 
while \cite{CEbound} only considers the case of discrete random variables without estimation error. 
Moreover, this optimization problem can be applied to both parametric and non-parametric cases.
It is essential to consider constraints for all $a\in\mathcal{A}$, rather than just the given $a_0$ of interest, 
as only considering constraints for $a_0$ would result in non-tight bounds.
As an example, the optimal solutions generated by the non-linear optimization problem in \cite{CEbound} may not belong to any valid causal models, 
leading to non-tight bounds. 
Therefore, one need to consider all values of the given random variable $X$ to obtain tight bounds, 
even if only causal bounds under a specific intervention $do(X=x)$ are required.
The original optimization problem is challenging to solve due to its inefficiency and intractability.
Firstly, the functional constraints for unknown distributions make the optimization problem intractable.
Secondly, solving a non-linear optimization problem can often get trapped in local optima. 
In some cases, narrow causal bounds that do not include the true causal effects may lead to critical false decisions.
To overcome these challenges, it is necessary to approximate the problem to make it easier to solve and to obtain global solutions with high probability.
The optimization problem can be naturally simplified by probability mass functions for discrete random variables with bounded support. 
To extend this approach to general random variables, we only consider random variables with bounded support 
and discretize $\mathcal{A}$, $\mathcal{W}$, $\mathcal{U}$, and $\mathcal{Y}$ into $n_{\mathcal{A}}$, $n_{\mathcal{W}}$, $n_{\mathcal{U}}$, and $n_{\mathcal{Y}}$ disjoint blocks, respectively. 
For random variables that take in finite values, 
they have natural discretization.
We first define
$$
x_{ijkl} = \int_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k,u\in\mathcal{U}_l} dF(a,y,w,u)
$$
and  then integrate both sides of the first equality over $\mathcal{A}_i$, $\mathcal{Y}_j$, and $\mathcal{W}_k$ to obtain
$$
\sum_{l} x_{ijkl}   =\int_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k} dF(a,y,w).
$$
This allows us to reformulate the first equality in terms of integration instead of the original pointwise equality.

As the discretization becomes finer, 
i.e., $diam(\mathcal{A}_i)$, $diam(\mathcal{Y}_j)$, $diam(\mathcal{W}_k)$, and $diam(\mathcal{U}_l)$ approach zero for all $(i,j,k,l)$, 
the approximation error converges to zero for good distributions. 
For discrete and finite random variables, 
the number of constraints is finite so the approximation error can be exactly zero for natural discretization. 
The other equalities hold naturally if the first constraint is satisfied.
For the constraints
$$
\int_{a\in\mathcal{A},y\in\mathcal{Y},w\in\mathcal{W}} dF(a,y,w,u)= F(u)
$$ 
for all $u\in\mathcal{U}$, 
we integrate these constraints using the same approach as 
$$
\sum_{ijk} x_{ijkl} = \int_{u\in\mathcal{U}_l} dF(u), \forall l.
$$
Compared with the original functional constraints in distributions, the linear constraints for $x_{ijkl}$ are much easier to handle.
After discretization, the objective of the original optimization problem is 
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: object-average causal effect}
   \hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{M}}[{Y|do(a)}]  =  \sum_{j,k,l}   \frac{y_j x_{ijkl} \sum_{i',j'} x_{i'j'kl}      }{  \sum_{j'} x_{ij'kl}      },
\end{equation}
where $y_j$ is chosen to be $\frac{\int_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_j} y dy }{ \int_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_j}  dy }$,

If $\mathcal{Y}_j$ is an interval, then the above choice of $y_j$ is just the midpoint of $\mathcal{Y}_j$, 

The first transfer learning task requires obtaining causal bounds from the number of $n_{\mathcal{A}}$ optimization problems.
Since every optimization problem shares the same feasible region, it is efficient to solve all optimization problems in a unified way.
To obtain the global solutions, we use Monto-Carlo method to approximate the above discrete optimization problem. 
\textbf{Monte-Carlo algorithm.}
Monte Carlo (MC) algorithms are a key approach for sampling causal models subject to constraints. 
However, efficiently sampling probability tables with row and column constraints can be challenging.
After discretization, the problem of sampling $x_{ijkl}$ is equivalent to sampling from a simplex $\mathcal{D}$ defined by the linear constraints
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: linear equations for x_ijkl}
   \begin{aligned}
      &  \sum_l x_{ijkl}  = \int_{a \in \mathcal{A}_i ,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k} dF(a,y,w)   , \forall (i,j,k) \in [n_{\mathcal{A}}]\times [n_{\mathcal{Y}}]\times [n_{\mathcal{W}}]\\
      &  \sum_{ijk} x_{ijkl}  =  \int_{u\in\mathcal{U}_l} dF(u)    , \forall l\in [n_{\mathcal{U}}].
      \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Let 
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: theta_ijk}
   {\theta}_{ijk} = \int_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i ,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k} d{F}(a,y,w) . 
\end{equation}
Similarly, we can define $\theta_l$ and $\hat{\theta}_l$, so we rewrite the linear equations as 
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: linear constraints}
   \begin{aligned}
     & \sum_{l} x_{ijkl}  =  {\theta}_{ijk} , \forall i\in[n_{\mathcal{A}}],j \in [n_{\mathcal{Y}}], k\in [n_{\mathcal{W}}] \\
     & \sum_{ijk} x_{ijkl}  = {\theta}_{l} , \forall l \in [n_{\mathcal{U}}].
   \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The overall equalities provide $n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}} + n_{\mathcal{U}} - 1$ linearly independent constraints, 
as both types of equalities imply $\sum_{ijkl}x_{ijkl}=1$.
Since there are $n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}}n_{\mathcal{U}} $ unknown variables, 
we need to determine the value of $n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}}n_{\mathcal{U}} - n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}} - n_{\mathcal{U}} + 1$
unknowns.

Directly sampling $x_{ijkl}$ from distributions supported on $[0,1]$ and rejecting invalid samples that do not satisfy the inequality constraints can be sample-inefficient.
To address this issue, we can incorporate inequalities to shrink the support of each unknown variable. For instance, \citet{CEbound} proposed using the bounds
$$
x_{ijkl} \leq \min\{ \int_{a \in \mathcal{A}_i ,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k} dF(a,y,w)  , \int_{u\in\mathcal{U}_l} dF(u)  \}
$$
and 
$$
x_{ijkl} \geq \max\{0,  \int_{a \in \mathcal{A}_i ,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k} dF(a,y,w)   + \int_{u\in\mathcal{U}_l} dF(u)  -1\}
$$
in their optimization problem.
However, the support of each unknown $x_{ijkl}$ in \cite{CEbound}: $[\max\{0,\theta_{ijk}+\theta_l-1\},\min\{\theta_{ijk},\theta_l\}]$,
is not as tight as those found by solving the linear programming problem \eqref{eq: linear programming to find support}:
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: linear programming to find support}
   \begin{aligned}
      & \min/\max x_{ijkl} \\
      s.t. & \text{ linear constraints \eqref{eq: linear constraints} are satisfied}  \\ 
           & 0\leq x_{i'j'k'l'} \leq 1, \forall (i',j',k',l') .
   \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Clearly, the solutions generated by \cite{CEbound} may not satisfy all the constraints in \eqref{eq: linear equations for x_ijkl}, 
which further contributes to the lack of tightness in their approach. 
We provide evidence for this claim by reporting the proportion of valid samples obtained with different sample spaces in \cref{tb: valid sample proportion with different sample spaces} for the example discussed in \cref{sec: numerical}.
\begin{table}[hbtp]
   \centering
   \begin{tabular}{|c|c|}
   \hline
   sample space for $x_{ijkl}$           &  valid sample proportion \\ \hline
   $[0,1]$ &       $\approx 0$         \\ \hline
   $[\max\{0,\theta_{ijk}+\theta_l-1\},\min\{\theta_{ijk},\theta_l\}]$ &    $<10^{-4}$             \\ \hline
   support found by LP\eqref{eq: linear programming to find support} &        $0.3\%$         \\ \hline
   \cref{alg: MC-causal bound} & $100\%$           \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
   \caption{Valid sample proportion with different sample spaces for the given example in \cref{sec: numerical}.}
   \label{tb: valid sample proportion with different sample spaces}
\end{table}
We also report the simulation results in \cref{sec: numerical} for causal bounds, 
which evidently support our arguments.
While the individual bounds for $x_{ijkl}$ obtained by \eqref{eq: linear programming to find support} are tight, 
the sample efficiency is still unsatisfactory. 
To improve sample efficiency, we introduce the sampling algorithm based on sequential LP. 
Suppose that we find a linearly independent variable index set $S:=\{n_1,n_2,\cdots,n_{|S|}\}$ with cardinality $n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}}n_{\mathcal{U}} - n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}} - n_{\mathcal{U}} + 1$ of \eqref{eq: linear constraints}.
For the first variable $x_{n_1}$, 
we solve \eqref{eq: linear programming to find support} to find its support $[l_{n_1},h_{n_1}]$, 
and we sample a $\hat{x}_{n_1}$ from a user-given distribution truncated to $[l_{n_1},h_{n_1}]$. 
Then, for the $n_i$ step, the previous values of $x_{n_1},\cdots,x_{n_{i-1}}$ have been determined by sampling.
We add the constraints for $x_{n_j}$, $j=1,2,\cdots,i-1$, where it is equal to its corresponding value.
Together with new constraints in \eqref{eq: linear programming to find support}, we can find a support $[l_{n_i},h_{n_i}]$ 
and sample a valid value for $x_{n_i}$ by solving
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: sequential linear programming to find support}
   \begin{aligned}
      & \min/\max x_{n_i}  \\
      s.t. & \text{ linear constraints \eqref{eq: linear constraints} are satisfied}  \\ 
           & 0\leq x_{i'j'k'l'} \leq 1, \forall (i',j',k',l') \\
           & x_{n_j} = \hat{x}_{n_j},j=1,2,\cdots,i-1,
   \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{x}_{n_j}$ is a sampled value for $x_{n_j}$.
After $|S|$ steps, the remaining $x_{ijkl}$ with its index $(i,j,k,l) \notin S$ can be solved by linear equations \eqref{eq: linear constraints}.
These sequential LP steps ensure that we always find valid samples in the simplex defined by \eqref{eq: linear programming to find support}.

Sequentially solving LPs and sampling components of $\mathbf{x}$ induce a distribution supported on the simplex defined by $\eqref{eq: linear programming to find support}$.
Although this is the most computationally extensive step, it can significantly improve the sample efficiency. 
In comparison, the sample efficiency of directly sampling $x_{ijkl}$ from distributions supported on $[0,1]$ or using the bounds proposed in \cite{CEbound} is much lower, 
as shown in Table \ref{tb: valid sample proportion with different sample spaces}.
The remaining concern is the estimation error in RHS of \eqref{eq: linear equations for x_ijkl}.
Denote 
$$
\hat{\theta}_{ijk} = \int_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i ,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k} d\hat{F}(a,y,w).
$$
Since $\theta_{ijk}\in[0,1]$, the variance of one sample is at most $\frac{1}{4}$. 
If ${\theta}_{ijk}$ has $n$ i.i.d. samples, then its largest variance of $\hat{\theta}_{ijk}$ is $\frac{1}{4n}$.
We can simply set $\epsilon = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{n}}$ if $n$ is given.
We can sample $\theta_l$ and $\theta_{ijk}$ from distributions with known parameters $\hat{\theta}_l$ and $\hat{\theta}_{ijk}$: 
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: parameter theta}
   \begin{aligned}
     & \hat{\theta}_{ijk} = \int_{a\in\mathcal{A}_i ,y\in\mathcal{Y}_j,w\in\mathcal{W}_k} d\hat{F}(a,y,w),\forall i\in [n_{\mathcal{A}}],j \in [n_{\mathcal{Y}}], k\in [n_{\mathcal{W}}] \\
     & \hat{\theta}_{l}  = \int_{u\in\mathcal{U}_l} d\hat{F}(u) , \forall l \in [n_{\mathcal{U}}].
   \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For example, if one want to reflect the concentration property, 
then one can choose the truncate Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\hat{\theta}_x, \frac{1}{4n_x})$ for $x=ijk$ or $x=l$.
If one expects the worst cases, then one can choose the uniform distribution for $\theta_x$ with discrepancy parameter $\epsilon$.
 

\begin{algorithm}[h]
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
	\caption{MC for causal bound}
	\label{alg: MC-causal bound}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
      \Require cumulative distribution functions $\hat{F}(a,y,w)$ and $\hat{F}(u)$, discrepancy parameter $\epsilon$, sampling distribution $ {F}_s$, batch size $B$ 
      \State Discrete the variable domain $\mathcal{A},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{U}$
      \State Select a linearly independent variable index set $S$ with size $n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}}n_{\mathcal{U}} - n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}} - n_{\mathcal{U}} + 1$ for linear equations \eqref{eq: linear constraints}
      \State Compute each $\hat{\theta}_{ijk}$ and $\hat{\theta}_l$ according to \eqref{eq: parameter theta}
      \State Set a counter $cnt$ to $0$
      \While{ $cnt < B$}
      \State Sample ${\theta}_{x} $ from the uniform distribution supported on $[ \max\{\hat{\theta}_{x} - \epsilon,0\},\max\{ \hat{\theta}_{x} + \epsilon,1\}]$ for all $x=ijk$ or $x=l$
      \State Sequentially solve LP \eqref{eq: sequential linear programming to find support} to find support $[l_{ijkl},h_{ijkl}]$ for each $x_{ijkl}$ with $(i,j,k,l)\in S$ and sample a value from $F_s$ truncated to $[l_{ijkl},h_{ijkl}]$
      \State Solving remaining $x_{ijkl}$ by linear equations \eqref{eq: linear constraints} for all $(i,j,k,l)\notin S$
      \State Compute the causal effect $b_{cnt}(a)$ by \eqref{eq: object-average causal effect} for each $a$
      \State $cnt=cnt+1$ 
      \EndWhile
      \State For each $a$, sort $B$ valid causal bound and get $b_{(1)}(a),b_{(2)}(a),\cdots,b_{(B)}(a)$ 
      \Ensure $l(a) = b_{(1)}(a)$ and $h(a)= b_{(B)}(a)$ for $a\in\mathcal{A}$
   \end{algorithmic}  
\end{algorithm}
The optimization problem \eqref{eq: optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} after discretization is 
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: discret optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]}
   \begin{aligned} 
       &\max / \min \hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{M}}[{Y|do(a)}] \\
      s.t.  & \sum_{l} x_{ijkl}  =  {\theta}_{ijk} , \forall i\in[n_{\mathcal{A}}],j \in [n_{\mathcal{Y}}], k\in [n_{\mathcal{W}}] \\
         & \sum_{ijk} x_{ijkl}  = {\theta}_{l} , \forall l \in [n_{\mathcal{U}}].
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
If all referred random variables are discrete, the optimization \eqref{eq: discret optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} can be exactly the same as \eqref{eq: optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} for natural discretization.
For general random variables, it is still an open problem that whether the solution to \eqref{eq: discret optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} will converge to 
the solution to \eqref{eq: optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} as the discretization becomes finer.
However, we have the following probability convergence results for variables $ \mathbf{x} :=(x_{ijkl})$.
Denote the solutions to \eqref{eq: discret optimization problem for E[Y|do(a)]} as $\hat{l}(a)$ and $\hat{h}(a)$.
For simplicity, we denote the induced sampling measure for $ \mathbf{x}$ as $\mathbb{P}_s$.
The existence and uniqueness can be easily proved by Kolmogorov's extension theorem.

\begin{myprop}
   \label{prop: convergence in prabability}
   Assume that
   the sampling measure $\mathbb{P}_s$ satisfies $\forall \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{D}$, and $\forall \delta >0$,
   $$
   \mathbb{P}_s( \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\delta) \cap \mathcal{D} ) > 0,
   $$
   where $ \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\delta)$ is a ball centered at $\mathbf{x}$ with radius $\delta$.
   If the discrepancy parameter is set to $0$,
   then $b_{(1)}(a)$ converges to $\hat{l}(a)$ in probability
   and $b_{(B)}(a)$ converges to $\hat{h}(a)$ in probability for $B\to \infty$.
\end{myprop}
The condition $\mathbb{P}_s( \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\delta) \cap \mathcal{D} ) > 0$ means that 
the sampling distribution can cover all feasible models. 
This condition can be satisfied by various continuous distributions such as the uniform distribution, truncated Gaussian distribution, and others.

Similar to \cite{MCMCbound}, we can establish a concentration result for our sampling algorithm using Chernoff's bound.
However, this result is not as useful as \cref{prop: convergence in prabability}. 
Firstly, the concentration property relies on the distribution of causal bounds, 
which is often complex and difficult to know in practice. 
Secondly, people are usually more interested in convergence results than concentration results in approximation problems.
For example, if one has several $l(a)$ samples, it is more reasonable to use the minimum value among them rather than the average results.

Regarding other statistical properties, 
\citet{quantilePerporty} shows that if the distribution on causal bounds $F_b(x)$ is continuous and has a density $p_b(x)$, 
then the $q$-th quantile $b_{[Bq]}$ asymptotically follows the normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(F_b^{-1}(q), \frac{q(1-q)}{B[p_b( F_b^{-1}(q) )]^2})$.

To accelerate the convergence speed of \cref{alg: MC-causal bound}, 
we can incorporate an optimization procedure \textbf{OPT}(min/max, object, feasible domain $\mathcal{D}$, initial guess $\mathbf{x}_0$). 
The optimization procedure \textbf{OPT} takes as input the objective function to minimize/maximize, the feasible domain $\mathcal{D}$, and an initial guess $\mathbf{x}_0$.
The procedure \textbf{OPT} seeks to find local optima $\mathbf{x}_{loc} \in \mathcal{D}$ that optimizes the objective function locally. 
Specifically, in each iteration of \cref{alg: MC-causal bound}, we use the sampled value $\mathbf{x}$ as the initial guess $\mathbf{x}_0$ and feed it to \textbf{OPT}. 
The output of \textbf{OPT} is then used as the updated estimate of causal bounds.
 
By leveraging the optimization procedure, we can further enhance the result in \cref{prop: convergence in prabability} under mild assumptions on \textbf{OPT}.
\begin{myprop}
   \label{prop: a.s. convergence}
   Assume that
   the sampling measure $\mathbb{P}_s$ satisfies $\forall \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{D}$, and $\forall \delta >0$,
   $$
   \mathbb{P}_s( \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\delta) \cap \mathcal{D} ) > 0,
   $$
   where $ \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x},\delta)$ is a ball centered at $\mathbf{x}$ with radius $\delta$.
   Given a deterministic procedure \textbf{OPT} which satisfies for any local optima $\mathbf{x}_{loc}$, 
   there exists $\delta >0$ such that for any initial guess $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathcal{B}(\mathbf{x}_{loc},\delta) \cap \mathcal{D}$, 
   \textbf{OPT} can output $\mathbf{x}_{loc}$ as a result.
   If the discrepancy parameter is set to $0$,
   then $b_{(1)}(a)$ and $b_{(B)}(a)$ converge almost surely to $\hat{l}(a)$ and $\hat{h}(a)$ for $B\to \infty$, respectively.

\end{myprop}

This result suggests that the modified algorithm with \textbf{OPT} can converge to the optimal solution with higher rate, 
and the convergence speed can be accelerated compared to the original algorithm. 
Intuitively, the optimization procedure \textbf{OPT} makes the probability $F_b$ concentrate at local optima rather than spread over the whole feasible domain.
Additionally, optimization procedures can usually work effectively at local regions and guarantee local optimality for sufficiently close initial guesses.
Therefore, the assumption on \textbf{OPT} is not so strict.

\textbf{MAB.}
We now consider the first transfer learning task for $|\mathcal{A}|<\infty$. 
The algorithm proceeds as follows: 
first, we remove any arm $a$ for which $h(a)<\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}} l(i)$. 
Next, we truncate the upper confidence bound $U_a(t)$ for the remaining actions using their respective causal bounds. 
Specifically, we define the truncated upper confidence bound as $\hat{U}_a(t) = \min\{ U_a(t), h(a)\}$. 
The algorithm then chooses the action with the highest truncated UCB and update the average reward of the chosen action.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
	\caption{Transfer learning in multi-armed bandit}
	\label{alg: TL-MAB}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
      \Require time horizon $T$, causal bound $l(a)$ and $h(a)$
      \State Remove the arm $a$ for $h(a)<\max_{i\in\mathcal{A}} l(i)$ and denote the remaining arm set as $\mathcal{A}^*$
      \State Initialize reward vector $\hat{\mu}_a(1) $ and the number of pull $n_a(1)$ to zero, for all $a\in\mathcal{A}^*$
      \For{ round $t = 1,2,\cdots,T$ }
         \State Compute the upper confidence bound $U_a(t) = \min\{1, \hat{\mu}_a(t) + \sqrt{\frac{2\log T}{ n_a(t)}} \}$
         \State Truncate $U_a(t)$ to $\hat{U}_a(t) = \min\{ U_a(t), h(a)   \}$ for all $a\in\mathcal{A}^*$
         \State Choose the action 
         $$
         a_t = \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}^*} \hat{U}_a(t)
         $$
         \State Observe a reward $y_t$ 
         \State Update the empirical mean $\hat{\mu}_{a_t}(t+1) = \frac{\hat{\mu}_{a_t}n_{a_t}(t)  + y_t}{n_{a_t}(t) + 1}$ and the number of pulling $n_{a_t}(t+1) = n_{a_t}(t) +1$
         \State For $a\neq a_t$, update $\hat{\mu}_{a}(t+1) = \hat{\mu}_{a}(t)$ and $n_{a}(t+1) = n_{a}(t)$
      \EndFor
   \end{algorithmic}  
\end{algorithm}


\begin{mythm}
   \label{thm: number of pulling in MAB}
   Consider a $|\mathcal{A}|$-MAB problem with rewards bounded in $[0,1]$. 
   For each arm $a\in\mathcal{A}$, its expected reward $\mu_a$ is bounded by $[l(a), h(a)]$. 
   Then in the \cref{alg: TL-MAB}, the number of draws $\mean{N_a(T)}$ for any sub-optimal arm is upper bounded as:
   $$
   \mean{N_a(T)} \leq 
   \left\{
      \begin{aligned}
        &  0 ,   h(a) < \max_{i\in\mathcal{A}} l(i) \\
        &  \frac{\pi^2}{6} ,  \max_{i\in\mathcal{A}} l(i) \leq h(a) <  \mu^* \\
        &  \frac{\log T}{\Delta_a^2} ,    h(a) \geq \mu^*.
      \end{aligned}
   \right.
   $$
\end{mythm}

As a simple corollary, the minimax upper bound is $\mathcal{O}( \sqrt{ |\widetilde{\mathcal{A}^* }|T\log T  }  )$, 
 

\subsection{Task 2: transfer from contextual bandit to partially observable contextual bandit}

In the second task, 
we consider the transfer learning problem from contextual bandit to partially observable contextual bandit. 
The expert has access to the complete contexts $W$ and $U$, 
while the agent can only observe partial context $W$. 
The objective is to derive the causal effect $\mean{Y|do(a),w}$ using the knowledge $F(a,y,w,u)$.

\begin{myprop}
   \label{prop: casual identification in task 2}
In the task 2, the causal effect is fully identified and given by 
$$
\mean{Y|do(a),w}=\int_{u\in\mathcal{U}} \mean{Y|a,w,u  }dF(u).
$$
\end{myprop}

Since $W$ and $U$ block the back-door path from $A$ to $Y$, 
we can use the rules of do-calculus and condition on the value of $U$ to fully identify the causal effect $\mean{Y|do(a),w}$. 
This proposition justifies the use of standard off-policy learning techniques that assume source and target agents are identical.

Given that the expert provides $F(a,y,w,u)$, it is possible to compute $F(y|a,w,u)$ and $F(u)$ from observation data. 
However, in practical scenarios, estimating $F(y|a,w,u)$ and $F(u)$ can result in uncertainties. 
Recent research in causal inference, such as \cite{Epsilon-Identifiability,learningCEfromFiniteData}, 
has investigated the impact of estimation error from finite samples. 
In particular, \citet{Epsilon-Identifiability} introduced the concept of $\epsilon$-identification, 
which extends the original identification concept and accounts for the influence of estimation error from finite samples.
To address this, we establish an $\epsilon$-identification result using finite samples.
\begin{myprop}
   \label{prop: epsilon-indentifiction in task 2}
   Suppose $A$, $Y$, $W$, and $U$ are all discrete random variables with bounded support. 
   Assume that the samples are independently and identically distributed. 
   For given $a,w$ and each tuple $(y,u)$, the fraction of samples containing $(y,u)$ is at least $\kappa$, 
   where $\kappa$ is an absolute positive constant.
   Then, for a sample size of $n \geq \frac{ 2 |\mathcal{U}|^2|\mathcal{Y}|^2  }{\kappa \delta^2} \ln \left( \frac{ 2|\mathcal{U}|\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}} y   }{\epsilon}   \right)$, 
   one can achieve $\epsilon$-identification for $\mathbb{E}[Y|do(a),w]$, 
   meaning that $|\mathbb{E}[Y|do(a),w] - \hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y|do(a),w]| \leq \epsilon$ with probability at least $1-\delta$. 
   Here, $\hat{\mathbb{E}}[Y|do(a),w]$ is the sample estimate of the causal effect.
\end{myprop}


\subsection{Task 3: transfer from partially observable contextual bandit to partially observable contextual bandit}

We will now consider a more challenging scenario in which knowledge is transferred between two partially observable contextual bandit agents. 
In this task, the expert only provides partial model knowledge $\hat{F}(a,y,w)$ to the agent, 
while the agent can have prior information $\hat{F}(u)$. 
In this setting, the causal effect $\mean{y|do(a),w}$ is partially identified.

Similarly, we need to solve the following optimization problem:
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: optimization problem for E[Y|do(a),w]}
   \begin{array}{rcl}
      & &\sup / \inf \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}[{Y|do(a),w}] \\
      &s.t. &  F_{\mathcal{M}}(a,y,w) = F(a,y,w), \\
      &  &  F_{\mathcal{M}}(u) = F(u), \\
    \end{array}    
\end{equation}
and discrete it as \cref{subsec: TL in MAB} does.
The object is 
\begin{equation}
   \label{eq: object-average causal effect with w}
   \hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathcal{M}}[{Y|do(a),w}]  =  \sum_{j,k,l}   \frac{y_j \theta_l x_{ijkl}      }{  \sum_{j'} x_{ij'kl}      }.
\end{equation}
\Cref{alg: MC-causal bound with w} provides causal bounds for several discrete points, 
which can be generalized to the entire space $\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{W}$ through interpolation.
 

\begin{algorithm}[hbtp]
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
	\caption{MC for causal bound with $w$}
	\label{alg: MC-causal bound with w}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
      \Require cumulative distribution functions $\hat{F}(a,y,w)$ and $\hat{F}(u)$, discrepancy parameter $\epsilon$, sampling distribution $ {F}_s$, batch size $B$ 
      \State Discrete the variable domain $\mathcal{A},\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{W},\mathcal{U}$
      \State Select a linearly independent variable index set $S$ with size $n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}}n_{\mathcal{U}} - n_{\mathcal{A}} n_{\mathcal{Y}} n_{\mathcal{W}} - n_{\mathcal{U}}$ for linear equations \eqref{eq: linear constraints}
      \State Compute each $\hat{\theta}_{ijk}$ and $\hat{\theta}_l$ according to \eqref{eq: parameter theta}
      \State Set a counter $cnt$ to $0$
      \While{ $cnt < B$}
      \State Sample ${\theta}_{x} $ from the uniform distribution supported on $[ \max\{\hat{\theta}_{x} - \epsilon,0\},\max\{ \hat{\theta}_{x} + \epsilon,1\}]$ for all $x=ijk$ or $x=l$
      \State Sequentially solve LP \eqref{eq: sequential linear programming to find support} to find support $[l_{ijkl},h_{ijkl}]$ for each $x_{ijkl}$ with $(i,j,k,l)\in S$ and sample a value from $F_s$ supported on $[l_{ijkl},h_{ijkl}]$
      \State Solving remaining $x_{ijkl}$ by linear equations \eqref{eq: linear constraints} for all $(i,j,k,l)\notin S$
      \State Compute the causal effect $b_{cnt}(w,a)$ by \eqref{eq: object-average causal effect with w} for each $a$ and $w$
      \State $cnt=cnt+1$ 
      \EndWhile
      \State For each $w,a$, sort $B$ valid causal bound and get $b_{(1)}(w,a),b_{(2)}(w,a),\cdots,b_{(B)}(w,a)$ 
      \Ensure $l(w,a) = b_{(1)}(w,a)$ and $h(w,a)= b_{(B)}(w,a)$ for $(w,a)\in\mathcal{W}\times\mathcal{A}$
   \end{algorithmic}  
\end{algorithm}



\textbf{Contextual bandit.}
We will begin by discussing discrete contexts and then move on to more general function approximation problems. 
The objective is to minimize the regret as shown in \eqref{eq: CB regret}. 
Suppose that the true causal effect $\mean{Y|do(a),w}$ falls within the interval $[l(w,a), h(w,a)]$. 
We define the set $\mathcal{A}^*(x)$ as follows:
$$
\mathcal{A}^*(x) = \mathcal{A} - \{ a \in\mathcal{A} | h(x,a) \leq \max_{i \in \mathcal{A}} l(x,i) \}.
$$
This set eliminates suboptimal context-action pairs $(w,a)$ through causal bounds. 
Recall that the context-dependent optimal expected reward is denoted $\mu_w^* = \max_{a\in\mathcal{A}} \mean{Y|do(a),w}$.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
	\caption{Transfer learning in contextual bandit}
	\label{alg: TL-contextual bandit}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
      \Require time horizon $T$, causal bound $l(w,a)$ and $h(w,a)$
      \State Denote $\hat{\mu}_{w,a}(t)$ as the empirical mean of the arm $a$ when context $w$ occurs
      \State Denote $n_{w,a}(t)$ as the number of pulling the arm $a$ when context $w$ occurs
      \State Initialize $\mu_{w,a}(1)$ and $n_{w,a}(1) $ to zero with size $|\mathcal{A}| |\mathcal{W}|$
      \For{ round $t = 1,2,\cdots,T$ }
         \State Observe the context $w_t$ 
         \State Compute the upper confidence bound $U_{w_t,a}(t) = \min\{1, \hat{\mu}_{w_t,a}(t) + \sqrt{ \frac{ \log t}{  n_{w_t,a}(t) }  }    \}$
         \State Compute the best arm candidate set $\mathcal{A}^*(w_t)$
         \State Truncate $U_{w_t,a}(t)$ to $\hat{U}_{w_t,a}(t) = \min\{   U_{w_t,a}(t), h(w_t,a)\}$ for $a \in\mathcal{A}^*(w_t)$
         \State Choose the action  
         $$
         a_t = \argmax_{a \in \mathcal{A}^*(w_t)} \hat{U}_{w_t,a}(t)
         $$
         \State Observe a reward $y_t$
         \State Update the empirical mean $\hat{\mu}_{w_t,a}(t)$ and the number of pulling $n_{w_t,a}(t)$
      \EndFor
   \end{algorithmic}  
\end{algorithm}


\begin{mythm}
   \label{thm: number of pulling in CB}
   Consider a contextual bandit problem with $|\mathcal{A}|<\infty$ and $|\mathcal{W}|<\infty$. 
   For each arm $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and expected conditional reward $\mu_{w,a}$ bounded by $[l(w,a), h(w,a)]$. 
   For any given context $w\in\mathcal{W}$, suppose $w$ occurs for $T_w$ times.
   Then in the \cref{alg: TL-MAB}, the conditional number of draws $\mean{N_a(T_w)}$ for any sub-optimal arm is upper bounded as:
   $$
   \mean{N_a(T_w)} \leq 
   \left\{
      \begin{aligned}
        &  0 ,   h(w,a) < \max_{i\in\mathcal{A}} l(w,i) \\
        &  \frac{|\mathcal{A}|\pi^2  }{6} , \max_{i\in\mathcal{A}} l(w,i) \leq h(w,a) <  \mu^*_w \\
        &  \frac{8\log T_w}{\Delta_{w,a}^2}+ |\mathcal{A}|  ,   h(w,a) \geq \mu^*_w
      \end{aligned}
   \right.
   $$
\end{mythm}
In this scenario, the classical agent minimizes regrets for $|\mathcal{W}|$ independent bandit instances. 
The above theorem (\cref{thm: number of pulling in CB}) shows how causal bounds improve the performance of classical bandit algorithms by controlling the number of times each arm is pulled.
Note that \cref{thm: number of pulling in MAB} is a special case when $|W|=1$.

Compared with \cite{ImprovedInstanceRL},
authors provide gap-dependent regret bounds for tabular reinforcement learning
which removes state-action pairs that are not reached by an optimal policy, i.e., 
$$
\mathcal{O}(\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}} \sum_{a\in \widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(w)}  \frac{\log T}{\Delta_{w,a}}).
$$
This result shows similarity in our transfer learning setting as 
we can model each context as a state and apply the generic RL methods. 
However, our method still outperforms generic RL algorithms 
as the contexts can not be affected by the actions. 
Hence, the goal of RL algorithms for optimal state-action (context-action) pair is not realizable,
so the define the regret \eqref{eq: CB regret} with respect to a context-dependent optimal action.

\begin{mythm}
   \label{thm: regret upper bound of CB}
   Consider a contextual bandit problem with $|\mathcal{A}|<\infty$ and $|\mathcal{W}|<\infty$. 
   Denote 
   $$
   \widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(x) =  \mathcal{A} - \{  a \in\mathcal{A} | h(x,a) < \mu_w^*   \}.
   $$
   Then the regret of \cref{alg: TL-contextual bandit} satisfies
$$
 \limsup_{T\to\infty}  \frac{ \mean{ Reg(T)} }{ \sqrt{T\log T}  } \leq \sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}}  \sqrt{ 8  (|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(w)|-1) \prob{W=w} } .
$$
\end{mythm}

The result does not scale with $\sqrt{|\mathcal{A}^*(w)|}$ but with $\sqrt{|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(w)|}$. 
Although our \cref{alg: TL-contextual bandit} does not explicitly remove suboptimal arms in $\widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(w) -\mathcal{A}^*(w)$, 
the property of UCB can still control the number of such suboptimal arms due to truncation by causal upper bounds.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have
$$
\sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}} \sqrt{ 8 (|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(w)|-1) \prob{W=w} } \leq \sqrt{ 8 \sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}} (|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(w)|-1) }.
$$
This inequality shows clearly that any elimination of suboptimal arms will improve transfer learning algorithms.
It also implies that our regret result outperform that of \cite{ImprovedInstanceRL} in terms of minimax versions, 
as the minimax regret in \cite{ImprovedInstanceRL} scales with the right hand side.

Denote the contextual bandit instances with prior knowledge $l(w,a)$ and $h(w,a)$ as
 $$
 \mathfrak{M} = \{ \text{contextual bandit instances with } l(w,a) \leq \mu_{w,a} \leq h(w,a), \forall a\in\mathcal{A},\forall w\in\mathcal{W} \}.
 $$

\begin{mythm}
   \label{thm: regret lower bound of CB}
   Suppose $|\mathcal{A}|<\infty$ and $|\mathcal{W}|<\infty$. Then for any algorithm $\mathsf{A} $, there exists an absolute constant $c>0$ such that
$$
    \sup_{  \mathfrak{M}  }  \limsup_{T\to\infty}  \frac{  Reg(T) }{ \sqrt{T}  } \geq  \frac{1}{27} \sum_{w\in\mathcal{W}}  \sqrt{   (|\widetilde{\mathcal{A}^*}(w)|-1) \prob{W=w} }.
$$
\end{mythm}

The lower bound result indicates that our \cref{alg: MC-causal bound with w} is near-optimal up to logarithmic terms in $T$.



\textbf{Function approximation.}
Next, we consider continuous and general context distributions. 
Since contexts are infinite, it is impossible to learn an optimal policy, 
except when the reward has specific function structures, such as linear. 
Therefore, we consider the following function approximation setting under the realizability assumption. 
Once again, we assume that the true causal effect falls within $[l(w,a),h(w,a)]$.

We eliminates the functions that cannot be the true reward function using causal bound:
$$
\mathcal{F}^* = \{  f\in\mathcal{F} |  l(w,a) \leq f(w,a) \leq h(w,a)  \}
$$
and modify the best arm candidate set as following
$$
\mathcal{A}^*(x) = \{  a \in\mathcal{A} | a = \argmax_{i \in \mathcal{A}} f(x,i) \text{ for some $f$ in $\mathcal{F}^*$} \}.
$$
Our algorithm (\cref{alg: TL-function approximation}) is based on the inverse gap weighting technique (IGW) \cite{CBwithOracle,CBwithPredictableRewards,instanceCB_RL,fasterCB}. 
In previous literature concerning IGW, this strategy is employed using a fixed action set size $|\mathcal{A}|$, 
and the learning rate $\gamma$ is typically chosen to follow fixed non-adaptive schedule \cite{CBwithOracle,fasterCB}.
However, our algorithm differs from these existing approaches in three aspects.
Firstly, we only apply the IGW scheme to functions and actions that are not eliminated by causal bounds. 
Secondly, we select the learning rate $\gamma_t$ to be context-dependent, capturing the influence of causal bounds on the arm set. 
This adaptive approach enhances the efficiency of our algorithm in the online learning process, 
resulting in improved performance in the transfer learning task.
Thirdly, in comparison with \cite{instanceCB_RL}, our regret order in $T$ is $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T \log(\delta^{-1} \log T)})$ instead of $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{T \log(\delta^{-1} T^2)} \log T)$. 
By eliminating suboptimal functions using certain causal bounds rather than relying on data-driven upper confidence bounds on the policy space, 
we are able to save additional $\log T$ terms.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
	\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
	\caption{Transfer learning in function approximation}
	\label{alg: TL-function approximation}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
      \Require time horizon $T$, confidence parameter $\delta$, tuning parameters $\eta$, causal bound $l(w,a)$ and $h(w,a)$
      \State Eliminate function space $\mathcal{F}$ and obtain $\mathcal{F}^*$ via causal bound
      \State Set epoch schedule $\{\tau_m=2^m,\forall m \in \mathbb{N} \}$      
      \For{ epoch $m = 1,2,\cdots,\lceil \log_2 T \rceil$ }
            \State Compute the function 
            $$
            \hat{f}_m = \argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}^*} \sum_{t=1}^{\tau_{m-1}}( f(w_t,a_t) -y_t )^2 
            $$ 
            via the \textbf{Least Square Oracle}
            \For{ round $t = \tau_{m-1}+1,\cdots, \tau_{m}$ }
            \State Observe the context $w_t$ 
            \State Compute the best action candidate set $\mathcal{A}^*(w_t) $
            \State Compute $\gamma_t =  \sqrt{  \frac{\eta |\mathcal{A}^*(w_t)| \tau_{m-1}  }{   \log (   2\delta^{-1}|\mathcal{F}^*|\log T   )  }     }    $ (for the first epoch, $\gamma_1 = 1$)
            \State  Compute $\hat{f}_m(w_t,a)$ for each action $a\in\mathcal{A}^*(w_t)$ and the following probabilities
            $$
            p_t(a) = \left\{
            \begin{array}{lcl}
                \frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}^*(w_t)|+\gamma_t (\hat{f}_m (w_t, \hat{a}_t )- \hat{f}_m (w_t, a ) ) },  \text{ for all } a\in \mathcal{A}^*(w_t)-\{ \hat{a}_t \} \\
                0 ,  \text{ for all } a\in \mathcal{A} -\mathcal{A}^*(w_t),  \\
                1- \sum_{a\neq \hat{a}_t }  p_t(a), \text{ for } a = \hat{a}_t ,\\
            \end{array}
            \right.
            $$
            where $\hat{a}_t =\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}} \hat{f}_m(w_t,a)   $.
            \State Sample $a_t \sim p_t(\cdot)$ and take the action $a_t$
            \State Observe a reward $y_t$
            \EndFor
         \EndFor
   \end{algorithmic}  
\end{algorithm}

In \cref{alg: TL-function approximation}, one needs to compute $\mathcal{F}^*$ and $\mathcal{A}^*(w)$. 
A naive way costs $\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{F}|)$ time complexity,
which becomes inefficient for large $|\mathcal{F}|$ and infeasible for infinite $|\mathcal{F}|$.
Actually, we can implicitly compute $\mathcal{F}^*$ by clipping, i.e., using
$$
\min\{\max\{\hat{f}_m(w,a),l(w,a)\}, h(w,a)\}
$$
as the estimator at the epoch $m$.
As $\hat{f}_m$ gets closer to the true reward function $f^*$, 
which is within the causal bounds, 
the causal bounds gradually lose their constraint effect. 
For computing $\mathcal{A}^*(w)$, we refer readers to the section 4 of \cite{instanceCB_RL}, 
where a systematic method for computing $\mathcal{A}^*(w)$ within a given accuracy is provided.

Another option to implement \cref{alg: TL-function approximation} is to compute $\mathbb{E}_W[|\mathcal{A}^*(W)|]$ using expert knowledge $F(a,y,w)$. 
We can set $\gamma_t = \sqrt{ \frac{\eta \mathbb{E}_W[|\mathcal{A}^*(W)|] \tau_{m-1} }{ \log ( 2\delta^{-1}|\mathcal{F}^*|\log T ) } }$, so that $\gamma_t$ remains constant within an epoch. 
Our proof still holds for this option, and the regret order is the same as in \cref{thm: upper bound for TL with function approximation}.
Intuitively, $|\mathcal{A}(w_t)|$ is a sample from an induced distribution with a mean of $\mathbb{E}_W[|\mathcal{A}^*(W)|]$, 
so on average, the regrets of both options are of the same order.
\begin{mythm}
   \label{thm: upper bound for TL with function approximation}
   Consider a contextual bandit problem with $|\mathcal{A}|<\infty$ and $|\mathcal{F}|<\infty$. 
   Suppose that the realizability \cref{asp: realizability} holds.
   Then with probability at least $1-\delta$, the expected regret $\mean{Reg(T)}$ of \cref{alg: TL-function approximation} is upper bounded by 
  $$
    \mathcal{O} \left(\sqrt{ \mathbb{E}_W[ \mathcal{A}^*(W) ] T  \log (\delta^{-1}|\mathcal{F}^*|\log T  ) } \right).
  $$
\end{mythm}
As pointed out by \cite{instanceCB_RL}, the gap-dependent regret of general contextual bandits is not possible, so we only consider minimax regrets. 
Previous works such as \cite{boundingCE_continuous_IV} and \cite{TLinRL} have also explored transfer learning in general contextual bandits, 
but the method in \cite{boundingCE_continuous_IV} relies on instrumental variables, 
and their regrets scale with $\sqrt{|\Pi|}$ instead of the more desirable $\sqrt{\log|\Pi|}$.
However, finding instrumental variables is still an open problem in academia. 
Additionally, the authors of \cite{boundingCE_continuous_IV} treat each basis policy as an independent arm in bandit problems, 
which is often invalid as similar policies can provide information to each other.
For example, exploring the optimal policy can also provide information about the second optimal policy, 
since these two policies typically yield similar actions and only differ in a small amount of contexts.
This insight helps explain why their regret depends on $\sqrt{|\Pi|}$ (as $ |\Pi| = |\mathcal{F}|$) rather than more desirable $\sqrt{\log|\Pi|}$.


We also prove that our upper bound in \cref{thm: upper bound for TL with function approximation} matches the lower bound in transfer learning.
Denote the contextual bandit instances with prior knowledge $l(w,a)$ and $h(w,a)$ as
 $$
 \mathfrak{M} = \{ \text{contextual bandit instances with } l(w,a) \leq f^*(w,a) \leq h(w,a),  \forall (a,w)\in\mathcal{A} \times\mathcal{W} \}.
 $$

\begin{mythm}
   \label{thm: lower bound for TL with function approximation}
   Consider a contextual bandit problem with $|\mathcal{A}|<\infty$ and $|\mathcal{F}|<\infty$. 
   Assume that the agent has access to the function space $\mathcal{F}$ and the realizability assumption holds.
   Then for any algorithm $\mathsf{A} $, we have
   $$
     \sup_{  \mathfrak{M}  }  \limsup_{T\to\infty}  \frac{  Reg(T) }{ \sqrt{T}  } \geq  \sqrt{\mathbb{E}_W [ | {\mathcal{A}^*}(W)|  ] \log |\mathcal{F}^*| }.
   $$
\end{mythm}


\textbf{Infinite function classes.}
It is worth noting that \cref{alg: TL-function approximation} and \cref{thm: upper bound for TL with function approximation} can be easily
extended to handle infinite $\mathcal{F}$ using standard learning-theoretic tools such as metric entropy. 
Suppose $\mathcal{F}$ is equipped with a maximum norm $\norm{\cdot}$. 
We can consider an $\epsilon$-covering $\mathcal{F}^*_{\epsilon}$ of $\mathcal{F}^*$ under maximum norm. 
Since $|\mathcal{F}^*_{\epsilon}| $ is finite, we can directly replace $\mathcal{F}^*$ with $\mathcal{F}^*_{\epsilon} $ 
and do not change any algorithmic procedure. 
Thanks to the property of $\epsilon$-covering,
there exists a function $f_{\epsilon}^*\in \mathcal{F}^*_{\epsilon}$ such that 
$
\norm{ f_{\epsilon}^* - f^*  } \leq \epsilon.
$
Hence, the regret can be bounded by 
$$
Reg(T) \leq 8 \sqrt{ \mathbb{E}_W[ \mathcal{A}^*(W) ] T  \log (2\delta^{-1}|\mathcal{F}^*_{\epsilon}|\log T  ) } + \epsilon T.
$$
By replacing the dependence on $\log |\mathcal{F}^*|$ in the algorithm's parameters with $ \log|\mathcal{F}^*_{\epsilon}|$ and setting $\epsilon=\frac{1}{T}$, 
we obtain a similar result as \cref{thm: upper bound for TL with function approximation} up to an additive constant of $1$.
\begin{mydef} [\cite{minimax}]
Let $(\mathcal{F},\norm{\cdot})$ be a normed space. The set $\{f_1,\cdots,f_N\}$ is an $\epsilon$-covering of $\mathcal{F}$ if 
$ \forall f \in \mathcal{F}$, there exists $i\in[N]$ such that $\norm{f-f_i}\leq \epsilon$.
The covering number $N(\mathcal{F},\norm{\cdot},\epsilon )$ is defined as the minimum cardinality $N$ of the covering set over all $\epsilon$-coverings of $\mathcal{F}$.
\end{mydef}

It clear that $Reg(T)$ scales with $\sqrt{ \log N(\mathcal{F}^*,\norm{\cdot},\epsilon )  }$. 
Note that $N(\mathcal{F}^*,\norm{\cdot},\epsilon ) \leq N(\mathcal{F},\norm{\cdot},\epsilon ) $ as $\mathcal{F}^*\subset \mathcal{F}$.
The covering number shows clearly how extra causal bounds help improve the algorithm performance by shrinking the search space.
Let $m = \inf_{w,a} l(w,a)$ and $M=\sup_{w,a} h(w,a)$. 
These bounds chip away the surface of the unit sphere and scoop out the concentric sphere of radius $m$. 
Therefore, the transfer learning algorithm only needs to search within a spherical shell with a thickness of at most $ M-m$.



\textbf{Discussion}
\textbf{Infinite function classes.}
Note that \cref{alg: TL-function approximation} and \cref{thm: upper bound for TL with function approximation} can trivially
extend to infinite $\mathcal{F}$, with the dependence on $\log |\mathcal{F}^*|$ in the algorithm's parameters and the regret bounds
replaced by standard learning-theoretic complexity measures such as metric entropy. 
Suppose $\mathcal{F}$ is equipped with a maximum norm $\norm{\cdot}$. 
Actually, the following argument holds for general distance metric.

$

$$

\textbf{Implementation details.}
We need to compute $\mathcal{F}^*$ and $\mathcal{A}(w)$. 
A naive way costs $\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{F}|)$ time complexity,
but becomes inefficient for large $|\mathcal{F}|$ and infeasible for infinite $|\mathcal{F}|$.
Actually, we can implicitly compute $\mathcal{F}^*$ by clipping, i.e., using
$$
\min\{\max\{\hat{f}_m(w,a),l(w,a)\}, h(w,a)\}
$$
as the estimator at the epoch $m$.
Intuitively, the causal bounds will gradually lose its constraint effect as $\hat{f}_m$ will gradually get get close to the true reward function $f^*$ which is within these bounds. 
For $\mathcal{A}(w)$, we refer readers to the section 4 in \cite{instanceCB_RL}. 
\citep{instanceCB_RL} provides a systematic method to computing $\mathcal{A}(w)$ within given accuracy. 

Another option to implement \cref{alg: TL-function approximation} is to compute $\mathbb{E}_W[|\mathcal{A}(W)|]$ using expert knowledge $F(a,y,w)$. 
We can choose $\gamma_t =  \sqrt{  \frac{\eta  \mathbb{E}_W[|\mathcal{A}(W)|]  \tau_{m-1}  }{   \log (   2\delta^{-1}|\mathcal{F}^*|\log T   )  }     }    $
so $\gamma_t$ will not change in one epoch. 
Our proof still holds for this option and the regret order is the same as \cref{thm: upper bound for TL with function approximation}.
Intuitively, $|\mathcal{A}(w_t)|$ is a sample from an induced distribution with mean $\mathbb{E}_W[|\mathcal{A}(W)|] $, 
so on average, the regrets of two options are of the same order.

\textbf{Causal bounds.}
To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed \cref{alg: MC-causal bound}, we compare it with the method proposed by \citet{CEbound} when all variables are binary.
Specifically, we randomly generate distributions $\prob{a,y,w}$, as shown in Table \ref{tb: comparison with causal bounds in partially observable problems}, and set $\prob{U=1} = 0.1$. 
\begin{table}[h]
    \centering

\textbf{Transfer learning in MAB.}
We compare our proposed transfer learning algorithm (\cref{alg: TL-MAB}) with standard multi-armed bandit (MAB) algorithms that do not have access to causal bounds. 
We also include the counterparts that incorporate a naive transfer procedure (without distinguishing the do-distribution), 
which we refer to as UCB-.

We present simulation results for $5$-armed Bernoulli bandits with probability $0.1,0.2,0.3,0.6,0.8$.
Simulations are partitioned into rounds of $T = 10^5$ trials averaged over $50$ repetitions. 
For each task, we collect $1000$ samples generated by a source agent and compute the empirical joint distribution.
The estimated causal bounds without the knowledge of $F(u)$ (CUCB in \cref{fig: comparison with classical and causally enhanced algorithms} \cite{TLinMAB}) 
are $h(a) = 0.9,0.35,0.92,0.96,0.92$, and $l(a) = 0,0.08,0.1,0.3,0.4$.
The estimated causal bounds with the knowledge of $F(u)$ are $h(a) = 0.2,0.25,0.77,0.7,0.9$, and $l(a) = 0.01,0.08,0.19,0.38,0.71$.


The regret curves are presented in \cref{fig: comparison with classical and causally enhanced algorithms}. 
We observe that the regret of UCB- sharply grows linearly and becomes sublinear after a certain turning point. 
This negative transfer result indicates that the agent spends many trials to overcome the influence of wrong knowledge. 
In contrast, our proposed algorithm achieves orders of magnitude faster convergence rates, 
as the causal bounds effectively eliminate suboptimal arms.
These results corroborate with our findings and demonstrate that prior experiences can be transferred to improve the performance of the target agent, even when identifiability does not hold.

Moreover, we find that a tighter causal bound can further improve the performance of classical algorithms. 
Even when the number of observations is not statistically significant, our approach can still rival standard methods, 
i.e., no negative transfer occurs. 
Overall, our experiments provide strong evidence that our proposed transfer learning algorithm can significantly improve the performance of contextual bandit agents.

\textbf{Transfer learning in contextual bandits.}
We randomly generate a function space $\mathcal{F}=\{(w-w_0)^\top (a-a_0) \}$ with a size of $50$ by sampling parameters $w_0,a_0$ in $\mathbb{R}^d$ from $\mathcal{N}(0,0.1)$, where $d=10$.
We then randomly choose a function as the true reward function $f^*$ from the first 5 functions, 
and generate the reward as
$$
Y= f^*(W,A) + \mathcal{N}(0,0.1),
$$
where the context $W$ is drawn i.d.d. from standard normal distributions and $A$ is the selected action. 
The whole action set $\mathcal{A}$ is randomly initialized from $[-1,1]^d$ with a size of $10$.
We repeat each instance $50$ times to obtain a smooth regret curve.

We compare the performance of our algorithm with FALCON \cite{fasterCB}, a well-known version of IGW.
From the numerical results, we observe that our algorithm outperforms FALCON significantly, even without eliminating infeasible functions. 
The average size of $\mathcal{A}(w)$ is $3.254$ in our randomly selected instances, 
indicating that eliminating action size can significantly improve performance.
Additionally, our algorithm is particularly effective for homogeneous functions, as these functions usually attain the maximum at the same points. 
In this case, adaptively eliminating suboptimal actions is a very effective way to reduce regrets.
