\title{Kolmogorov–Arnold Graph Neural Networks}

\begin{abstract}
Graph neural networks (GNNs) excel in learning from network-like data but often
lack interpretability, making their application challenging in domains requiring
transparent decision-making. We propose the Graph Kolmogorov–Arnold Network
(GKAN), a novel GNN model leveraging spline-based activation functions on edges
to enhance both accuracy and interpretability. Our experiments on five benchmark
datasets
demonstrate that GKAN outperforms state-of-the-art GNN models in node
classification, link prediction, and graph classification tasks. In addition to
the improved accuracy, GKAN's design inherently provides clear insights into the
model’s decision-making process, eliminating the need for post-hoc
explainability techniques. This paper discusses the methodology, performance,
and interpretability of GKAN, highlighting its potential for applications in
domains where interpretability is crucial.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\begin{IEEEkeywords}
deep learning, graph neural networks, interpretability
\end{IEEEkeywords}
Neural networks and deep learning have clearly revolutionized countless fields,
encompassing the domains of image, text, audio, and medical data. The basic
building block of neural network models is the multilayer perceptron
(MLP)~\cite{haykin1998neural}. Very recently, a promising alternative to MLPs
was proposed, namely the Kolmogorov--Arnold Network (KAN)~\cite{liu2024kan}.
MLPs are proved to be universal function
approximators~\cite{hornik1989multilayer}, whereas KANs are inspired by the
Kolmogorov--Arnold representation theorem~\cite{Kolmogorov1956,Kolmogorov1957},
which states that a smooth multivariate function can be written as the finite
sum of the composition of univariate functions; this has a similar structure to
a two-layer neural network.

The idea of Liu et al.~\cite{liu2024kan} is to generalize this two-level
structure to more levels.  This extension allows the definition of a novel
framework that proved increased accuracy and interpretability with respect to
standard MLPs. Indeed, lack of interpretability in neural networks is a major
issue, which limits its usage in domains in which interpretable results are
crucial (e.g., medical and financial scenarios). To cope with this flaw,
research focuses on the development of explainability techniques that try to
unveil what was learned by those models in terms of salient
features~\cite{burkart2021survey,saleem2022explaining}. In the domain of neural
networks, models specialized in learning from graph-structure data have emerged.
Graph neural networks (GNNs)~\cite{scarselli2008graph} are able to leverage the
connectivity structure of network-like data, learning representative
topology-aware embeddings. In literature, we find different architectures
addressing different aspects and characteristics of graph data. Graph
convolutional networks (GCNs)~\cite{semikipf2017} extend the concept of
convolution to graph structures, aggregating information from each node's
neighbors using spectral graph convolutions.
GraphSAGE~\cite{hamilton2017inductive} is another GNN model that generates node
embeddings by sampling and aggregating information from neighbor nodes. It is
based on an inductive learning procedure that allows embedding generation also
for previously unseen nodes. Graph attention networks
(GATs)~\cite{velickovic2018} incorporate attention mechanisms into GNNs,
allowing the model to focus on the most salient parts of a node’s neighborhood.
An additional GNN model is offered by GINs (graph isomorphism
networks)~\cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/XuHLJ19}. Such models are designed to have an
effective discriminative ability, theoretically proven to be as powerful as the
Weisfeiler--Lehman graph isomorphism test~\cite{weisfeiler1968reduction}. GNNs
have also been extended to relational structures, with the introduction of
relational graph convolutional networks (RGCNs). They are designed to handle
graphs with multiple types of edges and nodes by incorporating relations
explicitly into the model. Furthermore, variational graph autoencoders
(VGAE)~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/KipfW16a} aim to encode graph data into a latent
space and then reconstruct the graph from the generated embeddings.

Similarly to classic multilayer perceptron models, GNNs are not immune to the
interpretability curse; to shed light on their predictions, several
explainability methodologies have been developed. Such methods explain GNN
predictions in terms of importance subgraphs built of salient nodes, edges, node
features, connected subgraphs, or a combination of those elements. The
pioneering work in eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) for GNNs is
GNNExplainer~\cite{ying2019gnnexplainer}, which determined explanations by
generating important subgraphs using a mask on the adjacency matrix able to
maximize the mutual information between the prediction and the distribution of
the possible explanation subgraphs. GraphSVX~\cite{duval2021graphsvx} in another
method, which determines explanation in terms of important nodes and node
features relying on the theoretical background behind Shapley
values~\cite{shapley1953value}. It uses a decomposition technique relying on a
surrogate linear model for approximating Shapley values. A methodology targeting
edges as a means for determining explanation subgraphs is
EdgeSHAPer~\cite{mastropietro2022edgeshaper}. It employs Monte Carlo sampling to
approximate Shapley values determining salient edges forming relevant subgraphs
driving predictions. One additional XAI tool is SubgraphX. Also exploiting
Shapley value approximation, it looks for explanations only in terms of
connected subgraphs by using a Monte Carlo Tree Search approach. Given the
underlying mechanisms and the different ideas on which each explainer is based,
rationalizing a model's predictions may lead to different explanations, given
the intrinsic methodological biases different explainers have, which may lead to
counterintuitive outcomes~\cite{rudin2019stop}.

Motivated by the need for inherently interpretable GNN models, we extend the
Kolmogorov–Arnold representation theorem to GNNs, introducing the Graph
Kolmogorov–Arnold Network (GKAN). Our main contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Novel GNN Architecture:} We propose GKAN, a novel GNN model
      that employs spline-based activation functions on graph edges. This design
      enhances the model's flexibility and interpretability while retaining the
      efficiency of message-passing mechanisms in GNNs.
    \item \textbf{Enhanced Interpretability:} GKAN provides inherent
      interpretability by design, eliminating the need for external
      explainability techniques. This feature is crucial for applications in
      domains requiring transparent decision-making processes.
    \item \textbf{Performance Improvement:} We demonstrate that GKAN outperforms
      state-of-the-art GNN models in node classification, link prediction, and
      graph classification tasks on benchmark datasets (Cora, PubMed, CiteSeer,
      MUTAG, and PROTEINS).
\end{itemize}

In the following sections, we detail the GKAN architecture and its components,
present experimental results to validate our approach, and discuss the
interpretability of GKAN compared to existing methods. Our findings suggest that
GKAN represents a significant advancement in the development of accurate and
interpretable GNNs, with potential applications in various fields where
interpretability is fundamental. \footnote{We will make the code available upon publication.}

and operation of our proposed \GKAN model. We begin by revisiting the key
elements of Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs), upon which \GKAN is built.
\subsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold Networks}
The core idea of \GKAN lies behind KANs. Kolmogorov--Arnold theorem states that
a multivariate continuous function in a bounded domain can be rewritten using a
finite composition of continuous functions on one single variable and the
addition operation. Given $\mathbf{x}$ a vector of dimension $n$, $f$ a function
such that $f:\left[0,1\right]^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, it is thus possible to
write:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:2-layer-kan}
    f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{q=1}^{2n+1}\Phi_q\left(\sum_{p=1}^{n}\phi_{q,p}(x_p)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{q,p}:\left[0,1\right] \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ and $\Phi_q :
\mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2024kan} extended
Equation~\eqref{eq:2-layer-kan}, representing a two-layer KAN with $2n+1$ terms
in the hidden layer, to larger depths and widths, parametrizing each
one-dimensional function as a B-spline curve. This kind of neural network has an
activation function on the edges instead of nodes; the latter simply perform a
summation. From the implementation side, KANs activation functions $\phi(x)$ are
built as a sum of a basis function $b(x)$ and the spline function such that:

\begin{equation}
    \phi(x)=w_b(x)+w_s\,\mathrm{spline}(x).
\end{equation}
The original KAN model uses 
\begin{equation}
    b(x)=\mathrm{silu}(x)=\frac{x}{(1+e)},\,\mathrm{spline}(x) = \sum_{i}c_iB_i(x).
\end{equation}
At initialization, all activation functions are such that $w_s=1$ and
$\mathrm{spline}(x) \approx 0$ and $w_b$ are initialized using Xavier
initialization. The simplified interpretable KAN is the one at the basis of our
\GKAN.

To maximize KANs' interpretability, some simplifications are needed. The
interpretable KAN is trained with sparsity regularization techniques and
pruning. They use the L1 norm of the activation functions with entropy
regularization to favor sparsity. Once the network has been trained with a
sparsification penalty to reduce the number of activation functions, pruning
occurs at the node level. Reducing the number of active neurons renders
interpretation of the network more straightforward. To prune nodes, two scores
are defined, namely the incoming and the outgoing scores. If both scores are
above a certain threshold ($10^{-2}$ in the default implementation), a node is
considered important and, thus, preserved; otherwise, it is pruned. An
additional adaptation made by Liu et al. consists of symbolification. In the
simplified KAN, if an activation function is suspected to be a symbolic
function, such activation is set to be that symbolic function with shift and
scaling of input and output learned with grid search; for more details we refer
the reader to the original KAN paper~\cite{liu2024kan}.

\subsection{Kolmogorov--Arnold Graph Neural Networks (GKAN)}
Building upon the strengths of KANs, we introduce GKAN (Graph Kolmogorov-Arnold
Network), a novel GNN architecture (Figure~\ref{img:gkan}) designed for
processing graph-structured data. GKAN leverages the flexibility, accuracy, and
interpretability of KANs while retaining the efficient message passing
mechanisms of GNNs.

\subsubsection{GKAN Architecture}
GKAN employs learnable spline-based activation functions on the edges of the
graph, allowing for flexible nonlinear transformations of node features based on
their connections. The architecture is composed of multiple layers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{KAN-based convolutional layers:} Each layer efficiently
    handles the propagation and aggregation of messages between nodes by
    applying a KAN-based transformation to the features of each node, taking
    into account information from its neighbors.
    \item \textbf{KAN-based linear layers:} A final linear layer performs a
    linear transformation on the aggregated node features, producing the final
    output of the network (e.g., node labels for classification).
\end{itemize}

The Xavier uniform initialization, also known as Glorot initialization, is a
widely used method for initializing the weights of neural networks. This
technique aims to maintain the variance of the gradients approximately the same
across all layers, thereby mitigating the vanishing and exploding gradient
problems. The weights are initialized by sampling from a uniform distribution in
the range $[-r,r]$, with $r=\sqrt{\frac{6}{n_{in} + n_{out}}}$, where \(n_{in}\)
and \(n_{out}\) denote the number of input and output units in the weight
tensor, respectively \cite{glorot2010understanding}. As suggested in the KAN
paper, also in \GKAN the basis function weights are initialized using the Xavier
initialization, facilitating effective training and optimization.

\subsubsection{Mathematical Formulation of GKAN}
In the next part of this section we will go through the basic mathematical
formulation of the constituent steps of \GKAN.

\textbf{Message Passing and Aggregation}: Each node \( i \) in the graph has an
initial feature vector \( \mathbf{x}_i \). For each layer \( l \) in the GKAN,
the node representations are updated through message passing and aggregation.
The message from node \( j \) to node \( i \) at layer \( l \) is denoted as \(
\mathbf{m}_{j \to i}^{(l)} \).

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Message Passing}:
	\[
	\mathbf{m}_{j \to i}^{(l)} = \mathrm{spline}(\mathbf{x}_j^{(l-1)})
	\]
	where \( \mathrm{spline}(\cdot) \) denotes the spline-based activation
	function applied to the features of node \( j \).

	\item \textbf{Aggregation}: The aggregated message for node \( i \) is
	obtained by summing or averaging the messages from its neighbors \(
	\mathcal{N}(i) \):
	\[
	\mathbf{a}_i^{(l)} = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{m}_{j \to i}^{(l)}
	\]
	or
	\[
	\mathbf{a}_i^{(l)} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(i)|} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \mathbf{m}_{j \to i}^{(l)}
	\]
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Node Update}: The node representation is then updated using the
aggregated message and a spline-based transformation:
\[
\mathbf{x}_i^{(l)} = \mathrm{spline}(\mathbf{a}_i^{(l)})
\]

\textbf{Spline-Based Activation Function}: The spline-based activation function
\( \varphi \) used in GKAN is defined as:
\[
\varphi(x) = w_b b(x) + w_s \mathrm{spline}(x)
\]
where \( b(x) \) is a basis function (e.g., SiLU), and \( \mathrm{spline}(x) \)
is a B-spline function:
\[
\mathrm{spline}(x) = \sum_i c_i B_i(x)
\]
with \( B_i(x) \) being the basis splines.

\textbf{GKAN Layer}: Each GKAN layer combines these steps, resulting in the
following layer-wise update rule for node \( i \):
\[
\mathbf{x}_i^{(l)} = \varphi \left( \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \varphi(\mathbf{x}_j^{(l-1)}) \right)
\]

\textbf{Output Layer}: After passing through multiple GKAN layers, the final
node representations are transformed using a KAN-based linear layer for tasks
like classification or regression:
\[
\mathbf{z}_i = \mathrm{KANLinear}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(L)})
\]
where \( L \) is the number of layers and \( \mathrm{KANLinear} \) applies a
final spline-based transformation.

\subsubsection{Overall Model}
The overall model can be summarized as:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initialize node features \( \mathbf{x}_i \).
    \item For each GKAN layer \( l = 1, \ldots, L \):
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute messages \( \mathbf{m}_{j \to i}^{(l)} \) for each edge.
        \item Aggregate messages \( \mathbf{a}_i^{(l)} \) for each node.
        \item Update node features \( \mathbf{x}_i^{(l)} \) using spline-based
        activation functions.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Apply a KAN-based linear layer to obtain final node representations.
\end{enumerate}

This section provided a comprehensive overview of the GKAN architecture and its
core components. The next sections will delve into the experimental results and
demonstrate the effectiveness and interpretability of this new approach to GNNs.

\subsection{\GKAN Performances}
We compare the performances of our proposed model against established GNN
architectures, namely GCN, GAT, and GraphSAGE. \GKAN was able to outperform all
the mentioned GNN models in all tasks with all datasets, with the single
exception of the link prediction with PubMed, in which GCN performed better.

Each method is unique, and the hyperparameters chosen for training are as
crucial as the implementation itself. To ensure a fair comparison, each model
has been trained following the guidelines and hyperparameters provided by their
authors. For training \GKAN, we conducted hyperparameter fine-tuning on the Cora
dataset; we then use these hyperparameters to train \GKAN also on the other two
citation datasets, PubMed and CiteSeer. This hyperparameter tuning strategy was
also reported in previous work~\cite{semikipf2017}. Similarly, in the compared
methods, we use their optimal hyperparameters suggested for the Cora dataset to
train, evaluate, and compare on PubMed and CiteSeer.

Hyperparameter fine-tuning was conducted using a grid search over a broad range
of potential values: learning rate \([0.01, 0.001, 0.005]\), hidden channels 
\([8, 16, 32, 64]\), dropout rate \([0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.7]\), number of layers 
\([1, 2, 3, 4]\), spline grid size \([2, 3, 4, 8, 10, 20, 30]\), splines degree 
\([1, 2, 4, 8, 10, 15]\), aggregation function \([add, mean]\), 
and L2-regularization \([0.0001, 0.00001]\).
The optimal hyperparameters that we found for GKAN in the node classification
task are as follows: a dropout rate of 0.7, a learning rate of 0.001, 2 layers,
32 hidden channels, a weight decay of 1e-4, the \textit{mean} function for
aggregating the messsages, a spline grid size of 3, and a spline degree of~2.
For link prediction, the dropout rate is set to 0.2 and the weight decay to
1e-5. For the graph classification task, the best hyperparameters found (on the
MUTAG dataset) are: dropout rate of 0, learning rate of 0.001, 3 layers, 32
hidden channels, a weight decay of 1e-4, and the \textit{add} aggregation
function. The results summarized in Table~\ref{tab:results-experiments} are
obtained by averaging over 100 runs the test accuray (or AUC-ROC for link
prediction) of the models that achieved the highest validation accuracy during
training over each run.

The models were all trained for up to 300 epochs, utilizing early stopping with a window size of 10.
Training a single epoch of \GKAN takes significantly longer compared to other
GNN models (Table \ref{tab:epoch_times}) due to its design and operational
complexity. \GKAN employs learnable spline-based activation functions, which
require additional computational resources. This results in more precise and
interpretable transformations but increases computational complexity. The
sophisticated message passing and aggregation process, involving spline
transformations, adds further computational cost. Thus, even though \GKAN offers
improved interpretability and accuracy, these benefits come with the trade-off
of increased training time.

The added value of \GKAN lies not only in its higher accuracy and effectiveness
but also in its inherent interpretability Figure \ref{fig:int_at_hops}. Built upon the simplified and
interpretable KAN, GKAN allows for understanding its predictions without relying
on external explainers, which can sometimes provide biased or approximated
explanations \cite{rudin2019stop}. As a representative example, we present an
interpretation of a node's prediction by GKAN in a node classification problem,
and compare it with an explanation generated by GNNExplainer on a GCN prediction
for the same node (Figure \ref{fig:int_vs_xai}).
It could be said that in GKAN, the values (which have been normalized
for comparison purposes) truly represent the message of the network, 
meaning the values used to arrive at the prediction. In contrast, 
in GNNExplainer, they are importance scores calculated post hoc on 
a subgraph that maximizes mutual information and do not necessarily 
reflect what the network has learned.

Spline-based activation functions, which are central to KAN and consequently
GKAN, are inherently more interpretable compared to traditional neural network
activation functions. Traditional GNNs use fixed, nonlinear activation functions
like ReLU or Sigmoid, which can make it challenging to understand the decision
boundaries or the transformations applied to the input features. These
activations are often considered as ``black boxes'' because they do not provide
a straightforward way to interpret how input features are transformed and
combined to make predictions.

In contrast, spline functions are defined as piecewise polynomials which can be
easily visualized and understood. The smooth and continuous nature of splines
allows for a clear representation of how inputs are mapped to outputs. By
examining the spline functions, one can see exactly how each input feature
contributes to the final prediction. This transparency makes it possible to
trace the influence of individual features and understand the model's
decision-making process.

In traditional GNNs, post-hoc explainability techniques like GNNExplainer
attempt to infer the importance of nodes and edges after the model has made its
predictions. These solutions can have methodological bias resulting from the different strategies employed to determine the explanations (e.g., mutual
information maximization, Shapley value estimation, etc.) and may not accurately
represent the underlying decision process of the model. On the other hand,
GKAN's intrinsic interpretability provides a more reliable and accurate
understanding of the model's behavior, as it directly reflects the structure and
parameters used during training.

In summary, its inherent interpretability makes GKAN particularly valuable in
applications where understanding the decision-making process is crucial, such as
in biomedical and financial domains.

\section{Conclusions}



In this paper, we propose GKAN, a novel GNN architecture inspired by the
Kolmogorov–Arnold theorem and based on the recently introduced KAN model. The
added value of GKAN is two-fold. First, it is more accurate than established
state-of-the-art GNNs in node and graph classification and link prediction
tasks. Second, thanks to the usage of splines and the simple aggregation
(addition or average) functions, GKAN models are inherently interpretable. As we
showed in our experiments, the message carried by the graph edges represents,
indeed, in a clear and direct way, what the neural network used to deliver its
predictions. GKAN extends the opportunity offered by KAN to render neural
network models interpretable also to GNNs, opening up the possibility of using
GNNs in scenarios in which interpretability is a strong requirement, as in
biomedical and life sciences applications.

\subsection{Limitations and Interpretability}
While GKAN exhibits promising results, it faces certain 
limitations that requires further investigation:
\begin{itemize}

	\item \textbf{Memory Usage:} Scaling the number of splines or 
	increasing their degree leads to a significant increase 
	in memory consumption. This can pose a challenge when 
	working with large graphs or when higher-degree splines 
	are required to capture complex relationships.
	\item \textbf{Lack of Edge Features:} The current implementation 
	of GKAN does not utilize edge features. Many real-world graph 
	datasets contain valuable information about edges, and 
	incorporating this information could potentially enhance 
	model performance.
	\item \textbf{Interpretability:} : We want to make clear that we do not claim
	that GKAN is interpretable, but that it is more interpretable
	than other models. For instance, if the neural network is deep
	(large number of layers) then some of the information is lost.
	The added value is that the intepretation is given by the model
	itself and not by a surrogate explainability model.
\end{itemize}

We are actively exploring strategies to address these limitations and improve GKAN's efficiency, adaptability, and interpretability.

\subsection{Future Work}

To further improve and expand the applicability of GKAN, we propose the
following directions for future research:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Framework optimization}: Optimize the computational efficiency
		of GKAN. This includes reducing memory usage and improving the speed of
		training and inference. Techniques such as more efficient spline
		implementations, approximation methods, and parallelization strategies
		could be explored.
    \item \textbf{Incorporating edge features}: Extend GKAN to handle edge
		features. This would involve developing new methods to integrate edge
		features into the spline-based message passing and aggregation process,
		thereby leveraging additional information that could enhance the model’s
		predictive power.
    \item \textbf{Real-world applications}: Apply GKAN to real-life problems
		where its increased performance and inherent interpretability can bring
		significant improvements. Potential application domains include
		biomedical research, where understanding the model's decision-making
		process is crucial, and financial analytics, where interpretability can
		aid in regulatory compliance and trust.
    \item \textbf{Scalability studies}: Investigate the scalability of GKAN to
		very large graphs. This includes exploring distributed training
		approaches and evaluating the model’s performance on large-scale
		datasets.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid models}: Explore hybrid models that combine GKAN with
		 other advanced GNN architectures, such as attention mechanisms or
		 variational techniques, to further boost performance while maintaining
		 interpretability.
\end{itemize}

By addressing these limitations and exploring these future research directions,
GKAN can be further developed into a robust and versatile tool for a wide range
of graph-based machine learning tasks, combining high accuracy with clear
interpretability.
