\title{Students Rather Than Experts: A New AI for Education Pipeline to Model More Human-like and Personalised Early Adolescences}

\begin{abstract}

The capabilities of large language models (LLMs) have been applied in expert systems across various domains, providing new opportunities for AI in Education (AI4Education). Educational interactions involve a cyclical exchange between teachers and students. Current research predominantly focuses on using LLMs to simulate teachers, leveraging their expertise to enhance student learning outcomes. However, the simulation of students, which could improve teachers' instructional skills, has received insufficient attention due to the challenges of modeling and evaluating virtual students. This research poses the question: ``\textit{Can LLMs be utilized to develop virtual student agents that mimic human-like performance and individual variability?}" Unlike expert systems focusing on knowledge delivery, virtual students must replicate learning difficulties, emotional responses, and linguistic uncertainties. These traits present significant challenges in both modeling and evaluation. To address these issues, this study focuses on language learning as a context for modeling virtual student agents. We propose a novel AI4Education framework, termed \textbf{SOE} (\textbf{S}cene - \textbf{O}bject - \textbf{E}valuation), to systematically construct \textbf{LVSA} (\textbf{L}LM-based \textbf{V}irtual \textbf{S}tudent \textbf{A}gents). By curating a dataset of personalized teacher-student interactions with various personality traits, question types, and learning stages, and fine-tuning LLMs using LoRA, we conduct multi-dimensional evaluation experiments. Specifically, we: (1) develop a theoretical framework for generating LVSA; (2) integrate human subjective evaluation metrics into GPT-4 assessments, demonstrating a strong correlation between human evaluators and GPT-4 in judging LVSA authenticity; and (3) validate that LLMs can generate human-like, personalized virtual student agents in educational contexts, laying a foundation for future applications in pre-service teacher training and multi-agent simulation environments.
More related resources will be released at:  \href{https://marsgemini.io/SOE-LVSA/}{https://marsgemini.io/SOE-LVSA/}.

\end{abstract}

In recent years, large language models (LLMs) have achieved breakthroughs in natural language processing (NLP) and knowledge generation, enabling advancements in AI-driven educational applications (\cite{ge2024openagi,liu2024make,hong2024knowledge,latif2023knowledge,chen2024learning,jury2024evaluating}). While LLMs show significant potential as virtual tutors in generating high-quality content and personalized recommendations, their application in pre-service teacher training remains underexplored (\cite{lee23generative,wang2024survey}). Traditional pre-service training faces constraints like limited teaching environments and insufficient practical experience (\cite{loewenberg2009work,zeichner2012turn,ward2018teaching,sparks2011simulations}).
Virtual students offer cost-effective, flexible practice opportunities (\cite{dotger2015exploring,dotger2010medicine,shaughnessy2018uncovering}), allowing teachers to practice techniques in controlled environments (\cite{kervin2006classsim,lugrin2019towards}). However, existing models are often pre-scripted, resulting in predictable behaviors that fail to capture the complexity of human emotional responses and cognitive variability, limiting their effectiveness in dynamic settings (\cite{badiee2015design}).
This gap in realistic virtual student modeling raises a critical question: ``\textit{Can LLMs create human-like, personalized virtual student agents for teacher training?}" Unlike expert systems focused on knowledge accuracy, virtual student agents must replicate a broader range of human behaviors, including cognitive challenges, emotional reactions, and language production issues like hesitations and self-corrections (\cite{christensen2011simschool,delamarre2021interactive,shernoff2018early,kelleci2021using}). For early adolescents, cognitive load can lead to linguistic struggles and emotional states like frustration or self-doubt. Thus, LLM-based virtual students must demonstrate both knowledge and authentic social interactions, presenting unique modeling and evaluation challenges, especially due to the subjectivity in assessing behavior realism.
This study addresses these challenges by modeling virtual student agents in the context of language learning. We evaluate the capabilities of four foundational models to simulate student-like behaviors in language tasks. Next, we propose a comprehensive framework for generating \textbf{LLM-based virtual student agents (LVSA)}, facilitating personalized teacher-student interaction datasets. Using LoRA fine-tuning, we adapt the LLMs to embody specific traits, enhancing pre-service teacher training with realistic, personalized interactions. Finally, multi-dimensional evaluations are conducted to assess the feasibility and effectiveness of generating human-like virtual agents.
This research introduces the \textbf{``Scene - Object - Evaluation" (SOE)} framework, a novel AI4Education pipeline for constructing LVSA (see Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). By addressing modeling and evaluation complexities, the SOE framework provides a robust foundation for applications in pre-service teacher training, multi-agent simulations, and educational AI systems. The main contributions are: (1) \textbf{Theoretical framework for LVSA}: A comprehensive framework for constructing virtual student agents with scientific rigor and feasibility. (2) \textbf{Subjective evaluation metrics integration}: Incorporating human subjective metrics into GPT-4’s evaluation pipeline, aligning with human assessments of virtual student authenticity. (3) \textbf{LVSA validation}: Extensive multi-dimensional experiments confirming the feasibility of creating human-like, personalized LVSA for educational AI.



\section{Related Work}


\textbf{Virtual student development and related technologies:}
The development of virtual students for pre-service teacher training has evolved through three approaches: role-playing, programmatically pre-set models, and LLM-based virtual students. Role-playing offers realism but lacks scalability and flexibility due to resource constraints (\cite{kersh1963classroom,colwell2013simulating,frederick2010teacher,shapira2015simulation,dalgarno2016practising}). Programmatically pre-set models provide predictable, repeatable responses but lack adaptability to real-world classroom dynamics (\cite{christensen2011simschool,delamarre2021interactive,shernoff2018early,kelleci2021using}). Recently, LLM-based virtual students, such as those based on GPT-4, introduced greater flexibility in natural language interactions (\cite{achiam2023gpt,li2024leveraging,zhang2024simulating,lee23generative,markel2023gpteach}). However, these models still struggle to replicate the complex cognitive and emotional behaviors of real students.
This work addresses the gap by moving beyond basic prompt engineering to develop LVSA with human-like behaviors, cognitive challenges, and emotional responses. These virtual students facilitate authentic, dynamic, and adaptive interactions, enhancing the effectiveness of pre-service teacher training.

\textbf{LLMs’ main capabilities to support AI4Education:}
Recent advancements in LLMs have enhanced capabilities in natural language processing, including understanding, generation, reasoning, transfer learning, and multimodal processing (\cite{brown2020language, achiam2023gpt, guo2023evaluating, li2024pre, huang2024c, ahuja2023mega, kafle2017visual}). Models like GPT-4, LLaMA, and PaLM have demonstrated remarkable performance, often surpassing human levels in various benchmarks (\cite{touvron2023llama, chowdhery2023palm, wang2019superglue, clark2018think}). However, while these models excel in language tasks, they face challenges in replicating the nuanced cognitive and emotional behaviors typical of real students, as current applications mainly focus on knowledge delivery (\cite{achiam2023gpt,li2024leveraging,zhang2024simulating,lee23generative,markel2023gpteach}). This study aims to leverage these capabilities to create personalized LVSA that simulate realistic learning challenges and emotional responses, enhancing the authenticity of pre-service teacher training.
\textbf{Representative AI4Education applications:}
Educational technology companies and universities have developed LLM-based products to enhance student learning, such as Duolingo Max, Khan Academy’s Khanmigo, and TAL's MathGPT (\cite{Duolingo, Khan, mathGPT, squirrelai, chen2021evaluating}). Numerous benchmark datasets and evaluation tools, including C-Eval, SciQAG, and Mukea, assess LLMs' effectiveness in education (\cite{huang2024c, wan2024sciqag, ding2022mukea}). Despite these advancements, most research prioritizes student learning over enhancing teachers' pedagogical skills (\cite{lee23generative,wang2024survey}). LLM applications focus on automating educational tasks, leaving a gap in teacher development support (\cite{garcia2024review,yue2024mathvc,mcnichols2023automated,sahai2023improving,han2023exploring}). This study introduces a novel approach by leveraging virtual students to enhance teachers' skills through realistic, interactive environments, thereby preparing them better for actual classrooms.



\section{Scene: Basic Chinese Understanding Ability in Education}

\label{sec:scene}

This study explores the capabilities of LLMs in simulating realistic student behavior, focusing on early adolescents (ages 10-15) as the target group. Early adolescence is a critical developmental phase involving the transition from concrete to abstract thinking (\cite{hall1905adolescence, piaget1952origins, elkind1981children}), which presents distinct cognitive and linguistic challenges (\cite{caskey2014developmental}). Students at this stage often exhibit uncertainty in language expression, relying heavily on intuition and concrete experiences (\cite{nippold1993developmental}). Modeling this group provides valuable insight into the limits of LLMs in complex educational scenarios.
Language education, particularly in junior high school Chinese education, emphasizes expression, emotional experience, and value formation, aligning with LLMs' strengths in natural language processing (\cite{mcnaught2012literacies, liu2007teaching, Williams2016}). Therefore, a critical question arises: \textit{Do foundation models possess basic comprehension abilities for middle school language subjects?} Addressing this question is essential for determining whether LLMs can accurately replicate the learning behaviors typical of early adolescents in real classroom settings. Therefore, this section will assess the foundation models' basic Chinese comprehension abilities to evaluate their potential.

\subsection{Basic Chinese Understanding Ability Evaluation Dataset}
\label{subsec:BCUAE}
\textbf{Data source:} The dataset used in this section, named the \textbf{Basic Chinese Understanding Ability Evaluation Dataset}, is sourced from the National Smart Education Platform\footnote{\href{https://basic.smartedu.cn/}{National Smart Education Platform}}, a resource developed by the Chinese Ministry of Education. This dataset focuses on two essential skills: text comprehension and memorization, critical for assessing junior high school Chinese language proficiency. Comprehension tasks evaluate understanding of text structure, sentence organization, detail inference, and emotional tone, while memorization tasks assess the model's ability to recall and reproduce content. The dataset is regularly updated to align with national curriculum standards.
\textbf{Dataset construction:} As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (a), the construction process involved three steps: (1) Data preparation—organizing Chinese exercises into units and converting them to PDF format for model input; (2) Prompt design—using structured prompts to guide model tasks; (3) Expert revision—junior high school teachers reviewed and refined model outputs for accuracy. Ultimately, 613 comprehension items and 438 memorization tasks were collected, forming a solid basis for future evaluation and model improvement.
\subsection{Foundation Models}
\begin{wrapfigure}{ht!}{0.5\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{image/BCUAE-result.png}
\caption{Accuracy of evaluation for basic Chinese understanding ability.}

This section evaluates the performance of four foundation models in junior high school Chinese tasks, focusing on text comprehension and memorization. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (b) illustrates the detailed steps. The selected models—InternVL (\cite{chen2024internvl}), LLaVa (\cite{li2024llava}), MiniCPM (\cite{hu2024minicpm}), and Qwen (\cite{bai2023qwen})—each demonstrate distinct strengths. InternVL integrates visual and linguistic information, making it suitable for cross-modal reasoning. LLaVa balances efficiency with strong language understanding, ideal for medium-scale tasks. MiniCPM is optimized for Chinese tasks, excelling in comprehension and recitation. Qwen performs well in both visual-linguistic and text-based contexts, offering flexibility for multimodal applications.
Comparing these models provides insights into their ability to handle Chinese language content, especially within junior high school education. Including multimodal models like InternVL and Qwen prepares for future applications involving visual content recognition, laying a foundation for advanced teaching environments where virtual students can interact with teacher-presented materials.

\subsection{Results and Analysis}
The evaluation of four LLMs on junior high school Chinese language tasks, specifically text comprehension and memorization, revealed notable performance differences (see Figure~\ref{fig:BCUAE-result}). InternVL and MiniCPM performed best, with accuracies of 0.736 in comprehension and 0.758 and 0.664 in memorization, respectively, indicating strong proficiency in handling nuanced texts. Conversely, Qwen and LLaVa showed weaker performance, with comprehension accuracies of 0.584 and 0.491, and memorization accuracies of 0.614 and 0.397.
Overall, InternVL achieved the highest average accuracy (0.747), followed by MiniCPM (0.700), while Qwen and LLaVa averaged 0.599 and 0.444, respectively. The lower performance of Qwen and LLaVa likely reflects their design focus on multimodal tasks, limiting their effectiveness in Chinese language processing. These findings suggest InternVL and MiniCPM are more suitable for junior high school Chinese tasks, while Qwen and LLaVa may have broader applications in multimodal educational contexts.


\section{Object: Virtual Student Construction}

\label{sec:object}
\subsection{Theoretical Framework}
\textbf{Conceptual theory:}
Early adolescence (ages 10-15) is marked by significant physiological, cognitive, social-emotional, and moral-spiritual changes, which are crucial for modeling virtual student agents (\cite{hall1905adolescence}). Physiologically, rapid prefrontal cortex development and imbalance between cognitive regulation and emotional responses result in intense emotions and inconsistent behaviors (\cite{blakemore2006development, giedd2004structural}). Cognitively, adolescents transition from concrete to abstract reasoning, influenced by emotions, leading to repetitive and uncertain language (\cite{piaget1952origins, elkind1981children, nippold1993developmental}). Socially, heightened self-awareness and sensitivity to peer feedback impact engagement (\cite{raphael2012academic}), while moral development involves internalizing societal norms, often resulting in simplified moral judgments (\cite{kellough2008teaching, kohlberg1987psychology}).

\textbf{Operational theory:}
To construct LVSA, it is essential to generate dialogues that are operational and predictable, enabling realistic simulations of personalized student performance. These dimensions form the foundation for generating diverse student performance, ensuring virtual students mirror real classroom scenarios.
The design incorporates various question-answer types to simulate cognitive diversity (\cite{chin2006classroom}), the Big Five Personality Traits to capture individual differences (\cite{costa1992neo}), and learning stages to reflect different cognitive abilities (\cite{hu2024teaching}). Response styles represent individual language differences (\cite{pennebaker1999linguistic}), while the generation source combines techniques like Few-shot In-Context Learning (Few-shot ICL) (\cite{brown2020language}) and Chain of Thought (CoT) (\cite{wei2022chain}) with fine-tuning on classroom data (\cite{hu2021lora}) to maintain response authenticity. Together, these dimensions ensure that LVSA effectively replicate the complex, varied performance observed in real classrooms.


\subsection{Fine-Tuning Dataset}

\textbf{Data source:} The fine-tuning dataset is aligned with the Basic Chinese Understanding Ability Evaluation Dataset described in Section~\ref{subsec:BCUAE} and incorporates data from real classroom video recordings, textbook content, and teacher-prepared lesson plans. These diverse and credible sources ensure that LLMs generate responses that accurately reflect authentic classroom interactions and align with real-world instructional goals.

\textbf{Dataset construction:} 
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (c), the process involves multiple key stages to ensure realistic and personalized dialogues. These stages include data preparation, prompt design, expert revision, large-scale dialogue generation based on the Big Five personality traits, and the creation of fine-tuning datasets. Incorporating Few-shot ICL significantly enhanced the model's ability to simulate distinct student personalities.
Data preparation involved selecting representative Chinese texts and transcribing classroom videos for authentic teacher-student dialogues, providing essential material for fine-tuning. Prompt design was informed by real classroom interactions to align with Big Five traits using GPT-4, enhancing virtual student personalization. Expert revision ensured alignment before large-scale dialogue generation, resulting in a dataset that reflects diverse personality-based interactions. Finally, five distinct instruction fine-tuning datasets were created (see Figure~\ref{fig:fine-tuning-data}), each corresponding to a specific personality trait to ensure contextual appropriateness and individuality in student responses.

\textbf{Dataset analysis:} 
After generating fine-tuning student-teacher dialogues based on the Big Five personality traits, word cloud visualizations were used to analyze students' expression styles, such as lexical richness.
The word cloud for \textbf{HE} (Figure ~\ref{fig:wordcloud-en} (a)) highlights dominant words like ``we", ``feel", and ``stories," indicating frequent use of self-referential and positive language, with a strong inclination toward interaction and narration. This suggests highly extraverted students actively engage in discussions, driven by social needs.
For \textbf{HN} (Figure~\ref{fig:wordcloud-en} (b)), words such as``I", ``um", ``just like", and ``if" highlight hesitancy, anxiety, and uncertainty. These students exhibit emotional fluctuation, often marked by nervousness and frequent backtracking.
The word cloud for \textbf{LO} (Figure~\ref{fig:wordcloud-en} (c)) predominantly features words like ``possibly", ``we", ``because", and ``questions," indicating conservative and restrained language, relying on established knowledge structures with less inclination toward complex reasoning.
In \textbf{HA} (Figure~\ref{fig:wordcloud-en} (d)), words like ``we",  ``feel", ``oneself", ``together", and ``mother" emphasize cooperation, care, and empathy, suggesting warmth and an ability to connect with others.
For \textbf{LC} (Figure~\ref{fig:wordcloud-en} (e)), frequent words include ``possibly", ``should", and ``exactly," indicating a lack of precision, disorganized expression, and vague language, reflecting lower conscientiousness.

These findings align with existing research, showing that language style reflects cognitive abilities and personality traits (\cite{pennebaker1999linguistic}). This analysis underscores linguistic differences across various personalities and validates the uniqueness and effectiveness of the fine-tuning dataset used in constructing LVSA.

\subsection{Fine-tuning the foundation models}
To accurately capture the linguistic traits of students with different personalities, the model was fine-tuned using a high-performance setup with eight A6000 GPUs, ensuring efficiency and stability for large datasets (see Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (d)). This approach effectively personalizes the model to reflect specific personality traits.
 A comparison of pre- and post-fine-tuning results reveals significant improvements in linguistic capabilities, with fine-tuned models producing LVSA responses that better align with targeted language styles and traits, demonstrating enhanced structure and coherence. A comprehensive evaluation of these improvements is discussed in subsequent sections.



\section{Evaluation: Analysis of object capabilities in scenes}

\label{sec:evaluation}

In this section, we comprehensively evaluate the LVSA by constructing a subjective evaluation dataset. The dataset construction process is depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (e), comprising inference dataset creation, fine-tuned inference, direct inference, and evaluation data reconstruction. The evaluation process is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (f), consisting of human evaluation (Section~\ref{subsec:human turing}), human-GPT-4 comparison evaluation (Section~\ref{subsec:human-gpt4}), and large-scale GPT-4 evaluation (Section~\ref{subsec:gpt4}).

\subsection{Subjective Evaluation Dataset}
The comprehensive evaluation of LLM-generated virtual students was conducted through a four-step process (Figure~\ref{fig:overview} (e)): inference dataset creation, fine-tuned inference, direct inference, and evaluation data reconstruction. This dataset supports both human evaluation and automated assessment by GPT-4 to thoroughly examine virtual student performance.
\textbf{(1) Inference dataset construction}: GPT-4 generated teacher questions tailored to student personality traits using prompt engineering techniques from the fine-tuning process. The dataset included two fields: system (teaching scenario and student traits) and query (teacher questions in various contexts).
\textbf{(2) Fine-tuned inference}: The fine-tuned models generated student responses for five distinct personality traits—HE, LO, HN, HA, and LC—resulting in personalized outputs.
\textbf{(3) Direct inference}: Direct inference was conducted using non-fine-tuned models to assess their baseline capabilities, highlighting the improvements in personalization and realism after fine-tuning.
\textbf{(4) Subjective evaluation dataset reconstruction}: A total of 12,312 responses were generated across four foundation models and five personality types, covering different learning stages and question types before and after fine-tuning. The dataset was reconstructed for human and GPT-4 evaluations.
i) For human-subject evaluation, 80 responses were randomly selected—40 from fine-tuned models, 40 from direct inference—along with 35 real student responses as a control group, totaling 115 samples. Five were used as fatigue test items to ensure participant alertness.
 

\subsection{Human Turing Test}
\label{subsec:human turing}
\textbf{Setup:}
Inspired by the classic Turing test, this study aimed to determine whether LVSA could emulate real students' language expressions and cognitive abilities such that human evaluators could not distinguish between them. Participants, acting as ``judges," used their teaching experience to assess whether the generated dialogues resembled those of real students.
The experimental procedure consisted of three steps: questionnaire completion, think-aloud protocol, and semi-structured interviews.The subsequent semi-structured interviews further explored evaluation criteria, which were coded to develop a scientific framework for optimizing future virtual students.

\textbf{Are the LVSA easily recognizable by humans?}
Fleiss's Kappa was used to evaluate participants' ability to distinguish between virtual and real students, yielding a value of 0. The average recognition probability for fine-tuned LVSA exceeded 0.9 across multiple personality traits, demonstrating their close resemblance to real students and difficulty in differentiation (see Figure~\ref{fig:radar-he}). In some cases, virtual students with traits such as HN, LC, and LO even surpassed real students in recognition rates, highlighting LVSA's potential to effectively emulate human-like language in educational contexts.

To enhance GPT-4's accuracy in evaluating LVSA responses, evaluation criteria were systematically extracted from human evaluators' interviews and integrated into GPT-4 as prompts. Two trained coders used ATLAS.ti software for a two-level coding process, resulting in 4 primary and 15 secondary codes covering emotional integration, cognitive level, psychological state, and verbal expression. These coded dimensions were used to design prompts for GPT-4 using a chain-of-thought (CoT) approach, enabling step-by-step evaluations closely aligned with human judgments. This rigorous approach positions GPT-4 as a reliable tool for large-scale virtual student assessments.

\textbf{Evaluation results for different LVSA types:}
This section presents an in-depth analysis of virtual students with different personality traits across four foundational models, evaluating both pre- and post-fine-tuning results. The primary objective was to determine whether LLMs could effectively construct personalized, human-like virtual students. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:result-diff-student-type}, the average evaluation score for the five personality types significantly increased from 36.76\% to 72.51\% post-fine-tuning, highlighting LLMs' capability in generating realistic and personalized behaviors.

InternVL and Qwen demonstrated relatively strong pre-fine-tuning performance, with scores of 54.20\% and 40.55\%, respectively, while LLaVa showed the weakest performance at 15.22\%, likely due to its English-language foundation limiting compatibility with Chinese-language tasks. However, after fine-tuning, all models showed substantial improvements, with average scores rising to 72.51\%. Notably, LLaVa's accuracy increased to 67.26\%, demonstrating effective adaptation to Chinese contexts. InternVL and Qwen achieved post-fine-tuning accuracy rates of 78.24\% and 74.19\%, indicating strong adaptability and language generation capabilities. Paired t-tests confirmed the statistical significance of these improvements, with p-values well below 0.05 for all models.

An analysis of different personality types revealed that, except for students with LC, all types showed significant gains post-fine-tuning. Virtual students with HA exhibited the most notable improvements, with p-values below 0.001, indicating strong statistical significance. For instance, the score for HN students increased from 44.98\% to 91.00\%, HA from 29.29\% to 75.61\%, and HE from 26.86\% to 62.97\%.

Overall, the findings suggest that while certain personality types, such as LC, may need further optimization, fine-tuned LLMs effectively captured the linguistic characteristics of LVSA with different personalities. These improvements brought virtual students' performance closer to that of real students, providing substantial evidence for LLMs' potential in constructing personalized, human-like virtual students at scale. With further enhancement, these virtual students could play a crucial role in personalized education, pre-service teacher training, and educational experimentation, achieving effective student simulation.

\textbf{Evaluation results for different learning stages:}
This section further analyzes the models' performance across different learning stages. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:results-diff-stage}, InternVL and Qwen performed particularly well after fine-tuning, achieving scores of 78.82\% and 74.82\%, respectively. On average, the performance of all four models improved by 36.03\%, with paired t-tests showing p-values below 0.001, indicating highly statistically significant improvements. These results suggest that fine-tuning based on learning stages is more straightforward compared to virtual student personality traits due to the structured and hierarchical nature of learning stage data. Each stage has clearly defined teaching content and cognitive benchmarks, allowing the models to recognize and adapt to these relationships effectively during fine-tuning.
In contrast, modeling student personalities is more complex, as personality traits are fluid and context-dependent, lacking explicit hierarchical structures, making the fine-tuning process more challenging.

Experimental results also show significant improvements in virtual students' performance across all learning stages, with evaluation scores increasing by 31.96\%, 35.53\%, 37.98\%, 35.84\%, 38.68\%, and 35.99\% for pre-lesson introduction, new lesson instruction, knowledge consolidation, class exercises, and lesson summary, respectively. These findings suggest that LVSA effectively adapts to different learning stages, offering comprehensive support for pre-service teacher training. Virtual students enable teachers to practice and refine instructional strategies across all teaching phases, enhancing skill development throughout the entire instructional process.

\textbf{Evaluation results for different question types:}
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:results-diff-question-type}, we analyzed the performance of virtual students on different question types before and after fine-tuning to assess their ability to handle both closed and open questions effectively. The paired t-test P-values for closed, open, and overall questions were 0.015, 0.006, and 0.009, respectively—all below the 0.05 threshold—indicating statistically significant improvements. Specifically, fine-tuning led to an average improvement of 31.59\% for closed questions and 40.49\% for open questions, suggesting a greater impact on open-ended tasks due to their higher complexity and lower initial performance.

From a model perspective, InternVL and Qwen performed best post-fine-tuning, with evaluation scores of 78.81\% and 74.82\%, respectively, reflecting improvements of 24.30\% and 33.77\%. LLaVa achieved statistically significant improvement across all question types (P-value of 0.041), whereas InternVL, Qwen, and MiniCPM showed non-significant differences (P-values of 0.117, 0.117, and 0.068, respectively), highlighting challenges in achieving consistent improvement across models. LLaVa's significant gains across both question types and previous evaluations of student types and learning stages indicate superior generalization.

The differences in model performance between closed and open questions are likely due to inherent variations in complexity. Closed questions require specific factual recall, benefiting from structured dataset pretraining, while open questions involve creative reasoning, posing greater challenges. These findings suggest that fine-tuning enhances adaptability and response quality, particularly for tasks requiring sophisticated reasoning and creativity.
\subsection{Bad Case Analysis}

The experimental results indicate that fine-tuned virtual students demonstrated strong performance across various educational scenarios, effectively simulating personalized, human-like behaviors. However, some noteworthy anomalies were identified, revealing limitations of LLMs in role-playing contexts and suggesting areas for future research. 
 Expressions and behaviors typical of low-conscientiousness students are underrepresented in the original training data, making it difficult for models to learn these traits accurately. This scarcity leads to a higher likelihood of ``hallucination," where generated responses lack semantic coherence. Furthermore, LLMs are generally designed to avoid promoting negative traits associated with antisocial performance, further complicating the modeling of this personality type.

\textbf{Inconsistent fine-tuning effects across question types:} Although all four models improved in handling both closed and open-ended questions, none achieved statistically significant improvements across both types. Closed-ended questions are more structured, making them easier for models to manage, whereas open-ended questions require deeper reasoning and creative thinking, resulting in greater variability in performance.
 This disparity is primarily due to differences between pre-training and fine-tuning data domains, particularly cross-language issues. Given that LLaVa predominantly relies on English pre-training data, its adaptability and generalization to Chinese contexts are constrained.



\section{Conclusion}



This study introduces the ``Scenario-Object-Evaluation" (SOE) pipeline for building virtual student agents using LLMs to explore whether these models can effectively construct human-like, emotionally resonant agents. Designed for pre-service teacher training, this approach addresses the limitations of current simulation platforms that often produce rigid interactions. The multi-dimensional evaluation assessed virtual students across personality traits, question types, and learning stages. Comparative experiments with human evaluators showed that LLM-generated dialogues closely resembled real student behavior, indicating LLMs' significant potential in enhancing pre-service teacher training by simulating realistic student roles.
Future work will integrate multimodal tasks, such as images and videos, to improve virtual students' adaptability to complex teaching scenarios. Additionally, during experiments, virtual students occasionally generated irrelevant responses due to the ``hallucination" issue (\cite{pu2023summarization}). Future research will optimize the model’s generation mechanism to ensure errors remain contextually relevant and aligned with teaching logic. Moreover, while the study preliminarily examined the performance of low-conscientiousness students, further improvements are needed. Future work will introduce more data and refine fine-tuning strategies to enhance the accuracy and consistency of low-conscientiousness student simulations.
