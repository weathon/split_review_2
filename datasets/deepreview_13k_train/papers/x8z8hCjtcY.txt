\title{Elephant in the Room: Unveiling the Pitfalls of Human Proxies in Alignment}

\begin{abstract}
The demand for regulating the behavior of large language models (LLMs) has ignited research on alignment algorithms, the essence of which is to align LLMs' generations with human preferences. Due to infeasibility of humans directly participating in the training or generation of LLMs, existing alignment algorithms choose to align with human preferences carried by proxies, i.e., preference data or reward models. However, whether these human proxies faithfully represent human preferences remain under-explored. We categorize human proxies into two levels based on the degree to which they directly embody human preferences: Level-1 Proxy (preference data) and Level-2 Proxy (reward models). We empirically examine the faithfulness of both levels of proxies and its impacts on alignment performance.
We notice that current algorithms tend to overlook the faithfulness of these proxies in reflecting human preferences; many works even directly use reward models as their automatic evaluators without any correlation verification. Current literature of alignment overly focuses on optimizing algorithms, rendering the faithfulness of human proxies an "elephant in the room"—something extremely important yet largely overlooked. According to experimental results, we unveil potential risks of using inferior ``human proxies'', aiming to arouse attention to this huge ``elephant'' in alignment research. We summarize existing pitfalls from different angles and provide a re-labeled preference dataset and insights about reward model usage to facilitate the healthy development of alignment\footnote{This work contains examples that potentially implicate stereotypes, associations, and other harms that could be offensive to individuals in certain social groups.}.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated formidable capabilities across diverse applications, necessitating their alignment (Ouyang et al., 2022) with human values and preferences for responsible use. Since it is infeasible for humans to directly participate in the training or inference of LLMs, existing alignment methods turn to indirect alignment with various human proxies, such as preference datasets (Bai et al., 2022; Köpf et al., 2023) or reward models. For example, the pioneering alignment method, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022), uses a reinforcement learning algorithm where the reward model provides preference signals (Schulman et al., 2017) for the alignment of the target LLM. Following RLHF, various novel alignment methods (Yuan et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2023) have emerged, aiming to address the inherent limitations of RLHF, e.g., instability, difficulty in convergence, and sensitivity to hyperparameters (Wolf et al., 2023; Casper et al., 2023), while achieving comparable alignment performance. For almost all the alignment algorithms, the consensus that “human proxies faithfully reflect human preferences” is taken for granted. Otherwise, aligning with something that are unrelated or even opposite to real human preferences may seem too scary. Since participants (reward models for providing alignment signals) are also evaluators (reward models for evaluating alignment performance)2, even if there is indeed a misalignment, this secret seems to be deeply buried in this closed loop. The reliability of human proxies are gradually becoming an “elephant in the room”.

In this work, we try to make this “elephant” visible by unveiling pitfalls and risks from different angles through empirical analysis. According to the direct degree to which different proxies reflect human preferences, we first categorize them into two levels: Level-1 Proxy (preference data) and Level-2 Proxy (reward models trained with Level-1 Proxy). Level-1 Proxy, preference data, is normally expected to be the most direct carrier of genuine human preferences, which is usually used in the following two ways: (1) directly used to train LLMs to fit human preferences through alignment algorithms like DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023); (2) used to train Level-2 Proxy, i.e., reward models, which are further used to guide the alignment of LLMs using alignment algorithms like PPO (Schulman et al., 2017). As for reward models, the Level-2 Proxy, there are generally two uses in alignment: (1) providing alignment signals for alignment optimization (Ouyang et al., 2022; Yuan et al., 2023; Song et al., 2024); (2) serving as an automatic evaluator for alignment performance (Song et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2023). Due to the heavy reliance of alignment on various human proxies, realizing and studying the reliability of them is important. As shown by the illustrative example in Figure 1 in the widely-used HH-RLHF preference dataset, the Chosen Reponse for LLMs to align with expresses severe toxicity by suggesting the user to “get a new mom”. In considering how this preference data might guide LLMs to align with distorted values, we observe flaws that trigger doubts about the reliability of these human proxies: Do these so-called human proxies indeed faithfully reflect genuine human values? If not, what impacts they may have on alignment performance?  

To answer these questions, we first study the quality of the widely-used preference dataset, HHRLHF (Bai et al., 2022). We re-label the original dataset and present our cleaned version, CHH-RLHF, as a more reliable testbed for alignment research. Using CHH-RLHF, we compute the correlation between human evaluation and reward model evaluation, which unveils the pitfalls of using inferior reward models for evaluation of alignment performance. We also examine the accuracy of various reward models used in previous alignment research, uncovering potential risks of misalignment. Moreover, we conduct systematic experiments on various alignment algorithms to study the impacts of Level-1 and Level-2 Proxy with different quality on alignment performance. This work aims to ring the bell for researchers to attach importance to verification of human proxies before using them whether for alignment optimization or evaluation. Our main contributions are summarized below:  

• We first categorize different human proxies into two levels: Level-1 Proxy and Level-2 Proxy. We then analyze the HH-RLHF preference dataset and present our cleaned version, CHH-RLHF, to facilitate reliable alignment research in the future3.   
• Using CHH-RLHF, we conduct empirical analysis to systematically unveil pitfalls and potential risks of using unverified human proxies for alignment optimization and evaluation. Specially, we examine the accuracy of various reward models and the correlation between human evaluation and reward model evaluation. We also conduct extensive experiments to study the impacts of 2 levels of human proxies with different quality on the alignment performance.   
• Through systematic analysis and experiments of 2 levels of human proxies from different angles, we aim to ring the bell for researchers to pay attention to this “huge elephant” in the alignment research.

\section{2 TAXONOMY OF HUMAN PROXIES  }


As illustrated in Figure 2, we categorize human proxies into two levels according to the direct level to which they reflect human preferences.  



\section{2.1 LEVEL-1 PROXY: PREFERENCE DATA  }


Preference data is the most direct information carrier of human preferences, which usually takes the form of pairwise data with labels annotated by humans: “chosen” for the human preferred response, which “rejected” for the dispreferred one. Preference data is expected to faithfully reflect human preferences, which is usually used to train LLMs to generate responses aligned with the chosen responses. Since preference data is directly annotated by humans, making it the most direct information carrier of human preferences, we thus define it as the Level-1 Proxy. In alignment research, preference data is usually used for two purposes: (1) used to train LLMs to align with human preferences contained in the preference data using algorithms like DPO; (2) used to train reward models. However, pitfalls in preference data may hinder the achievement of both purposes.  



\section{2.2 LEVEL-2 PROXY: REWARD MODEL  }


Reward models used in alignment are models that are typically trained with the Level-1 Proxy (preference data) to automatically score any given responses: generate higher scores for humanpreferred responses and lower scores for human-dispreferred ones. Reward models are expected to infer human intentions and preferences from Level-1 Proxy, not directly labeled or guided by humans, which is thus defined as Level-2 Proxy. In alignment research, reward models are usually utilized for two purposes: (1) providing signals to LLMs for alignment optimization; (2) providing scores to LLMs’ generations as an evaluation metric for the alignment performance. However, pitfalls of reward models may pose great obstacles to achieving these two purposes.

3.2 EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS  

3.2.1 PREFERENCE DATA ANALYSIS AND CLEANING  

We thoroughly analyze and meticulously re-label the HH-RLHF dataset and present our cleaned version, CHH-RLHF, to facilitate reliable alignment research. For both the training and test (Harmlessbase, Helpfulbase, Helpfulonline, Helpfulrejection, $\mathrm{\breve{Test}}_{\mathrm{mixed}}{}^{5}$ ) sets of the HH-RLHF dataset, we ask four human annotators to select the superior response from two responses, disregarding the original “chosen" and “rejected" labels provided in the dataset. According to the re-labeling results, we categorize the whole dataset into six types: “Same", “Opposite", “Toxic", “Vague", “Repetitive", and “Empty". The proportions of the six data types within the dataset are depicted in Figure 3. As shown in Figure 3, there are only $67.8\%$ and $51.\bar{5}\%$ of data respectively in train and test sets are not affected by these pitfalls. We curate our cleaned dataset, CHH-RLHF, using the re-label results. We keep “Same" data, correct the labels of “Opposite" data, and fliter out “Toxic" “Vague", “Repetitive", and “Empty" data. Due to limited space, we only present analysis charts for HH-RLHF Train set and Helpful-Online test set.

Background Despite satisfactory effectiveness, RLHF requires high training costs. As a solution, SFT-based alignment has received increasing attention. A representative method is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023), which optimizes the loss below without an explicit reward model:  

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{w}\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{l}\mid x)}\right)\right],
$$  

where $\delta$ is the sigmoid function and $\beta$ is a hyper-parameter. DPO establishes connections between reward function and policy πθ (LLMs) and obtains the ground-truth reward r∗(x, y) = β log ππref ((yy||xx)) $\beta\log Z(x)$ where $Z(x)$ is the partition function and $\pi^{*}(y\mid x)$ is the optimal policy. Minimizing Eq.(1) is equivalent to optimizing an implicit Bradley-Terry Preference Model (Bradley $\&$ Terry, 1952), $\begin{array}{r}{p^{*}(y_{w}\,\succ\,y_{l})\,=\,\frac{\exp\left(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\right)}{\exp\left(r^{*}(x,y_{l})\right)+\exp\left(r^{*}(x,y_{w})\right)}}\end{array}$ . Circumventing the reward model, DPO only requires loading two models, $(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y|\boldsymbol{x})$ and $\pi_{r}(y|x))$ , enhancing training efficiency and stability. In this case, human preference is represented as an implicit reward, $\begin{array}{r}{r(x,y)\propto\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)}}\end{array}$ , directly reflected in training data, which might be more sensitive to data quality.

\section{4 PITFALLS OF LEVEL-2 PROXY  }


4.1 UNQUALIFIED AUTOMATIC EVALUATOR  

4.1.1 CORRELATION EVALUATION  

We analyze the correlation between human evaluation and reward model evaluation to assess whether reward models can serve as effective automatic evaluators for alignment performance. The results are depicted in Figure 4. The left part of Figure 4 illustrates the correlation between human evaluation and the automatic evaluation by GPT-J (Wang & Komatsuzaki, 2021). It is evident that there is poor consistency between GPT-J’s automatic evaluation and human assessment, indicating that GPT-J is not qualified to act as a human proxy for automatic evaluation of alignment performance. This finding also casts doubt on the effectiveness of alignment methods that rely on GPT-J or other reward models with similar inconsistencies for their evaluation. The right part of Figure 4 presents the correlation between human evaluation and automatic evaluation by Starling-34B, one of the best-performing open-source reward models. The automatic evaluations by Starling-34B show reasonable consistency with human assessments, suggesting it is a more reliable option compared to GPT-J 7.  

4.1.2 INVENTORY OF PITFALLS  

As shown by the illustrative example in Table 3, we summarize pitfalls of using reward models as automatic evaluators in current alignment research as following.  

• Inverted Scores: As shown in Table 3, we can clearly see that the reward model gives a lower score to the better response, but a higher score to the worse response: the response generated using DPO is reasonable and helpful by trying to persuade the user not to drive after drinking, but it only receives the reward score 0.50, which is much lower than that of the helpless response “I’m sorry, I don’t understand.” Scores given by such a reward model should not be used as an evaluation metric for alignment performance.

• Negligible Score Gap: We notice that the reward model would give similar scores to responses with significant differences in quality.   
• High Score for Harmful Response: We find that some severely toxic and harmful responses can also receive high scores. Some responses incite users to choose angles to stab others to cause more fatal knife wounds and to sexually harass women shockingly received scores above 0.5.   
• Score for Empty Responses: We notice that some reward models give higher scores to empty responses than some reasonable responses.   
• High Scores for Helpless Responses: We find that some reward models, e.g., Pythia-6.9B used in PRO, tend to give much higher scores to helpless responses like “I’m sorry, I don’t understand”, but gives much lower scores to more helpful responses that give specific suggestions to users. We speculate that using inferior reward models rewarding helpless responses is one of the reasons that LLMs are becoming less helpful after alignment.  

4.2 INACCURATE REWARD SIGNAL  

4.2.1 EVALUATION OF REWARD MODEL ACCURACY  

Evaluation Metric We use accuracy as the metric to evaluate the performance of reward models, which is measured as the rate at which chosen responses receive higher scores than rejected responses:  

$$
\mathsf{A c c u r a c y}_{\mathrm{RM}}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}_{\mathrm{Score}_{i}(\mathrm{chosen})>\mathrm{Score}_{i}(\mathrm{rejected})}}{\mathrm{N}}\times100,
$$  

where N is the number of data samples, Scorei(chosen) and Scorei(rejected) are the scores given by the reward model for the chosen response and rejected response of the i-th sample, respectively.

Empirical Analysis on PRO Compared to PPO, PRO moves the use of the reward model to the earlier stage by first scoring good and bad responses and then using these scores to guide the model’s alignment with the SFT method. \section{5 DISCUSSION  }




\section{5.1 STUDIES RELATED TO PROXIES  }


Research on the reliability of human proxies is limited. (Wang et al., 2024) mainly analyzes the impact of preference data quality on reward model performance, and also proposes to use contrastive learning to enhance the abilities of reward models. Nevertheless, this work also fails to analyze the impact of preference data or reward model on the alignment performance, which is a key gap we aim to fill in this work: validating the reliability of human proxies is vital for alignment development. REWARDBENCH (Lambert et al., 2024) benchmarks existing open-source reward models. However, they only establish a leaderboard for reward models, failing to unveil the relationship between the alignment performance and reward models quality. In this situation, even with a leaderboard clearly reflecting the performance of various reward models, it remains uncertain which reward model should be chosen to achieve optimal alignment performance and reliable alignment evaluation.  



\section{5.2 GOODHART’S LAW  }


“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Charles Goodhart  

Although our experiments have demonstrated that using a better reward model in existing alignment algorithms can improve alignment performance, this finding is constrained by a limitation known as Goodhart’s Law (Hennessy & Goodhart, 2023; Manheim & Garrabrant, 2019). Originally articulated by economist Charles Goodhart in the context of economic policy (Goodhart, 1984), this principle posits that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Applying Goodhart’s Law, we recognize that over-optimizing the reward model—an imperfect proxy of human intentions—may impair the effectiveness of the policy model. While this phenomenon is frequently discussed in the field of reinforcement learning, it has been somewhat overlooked in alignment research. We aim to highlight the limitations of reward model optimization and call for future research to adopt a balanced approach, carefully navigating the risks of both under-optimization and over-optimization.  



\section{6 CONCLUSION  }


This paper highlights the overlooked yet critical role of various human proxies in alignment research. We curate a cleaned dataset, CHH-RLHF, derived from a widely-used preference dataset, HH-RLHF, to facilitate reliable alignment research. We categorize human proxies into two levels (Level-1 Proxy and Level-2 Proxy), and conduct empirical analysis from different angles to systematically unveil potential pitfalls and risks in current alignment research. We call on alignment researchers to recalibrate their future research objectives by not only focusing on alignment algorithms but also considering other influential elements such as reliability of various human proxies.
