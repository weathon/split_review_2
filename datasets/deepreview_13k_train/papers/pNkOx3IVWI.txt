\title{UltraFeedback: Boosting Language Models with High-quality Feedback}

\begin{abstract}
Learning from human feedback has become a pivot technique in aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences. 
However, acquiring vast and premium human feedback is bottlenecked by time, labor, and human capability, resulting in small sizes or limited topics of current datasets. This further hinders feedback learning as well as alignment research within the open-source community.
To address this issue, we explore how to go beyond human feedback and collect high-quality \textit{AI feedback} automatically for a scalable alternative. Specifically, we identify \textbf{scale and diversity} as the key factors for feedback data to take effect. Accordingly, we first broaden instructions and responses in both amount and breadth to encompass a wider range of user-assistant interactions. Then, we meticulously apply a series of techniques to mitigate annotation biases for more reliable AI feedback.
We finally present \textsc{UltraFeedback}, a large-scale, high-quality, and diversified AI feedback dataset, which contains over 1 million GPT-4 feedback for 250k user-assistant conversations from various aspects. Built upon \textsc{UltraFeedback}, we align a LLaMA-based model by best-of-$n$ sampling and reinforcement learning, demonstrating its exceptional performance on chat benchmarks.
Our work validates the effectiveness of scaled AI feedback data in constructing strong open-source chat language models, serving as a solid foundation for future feedback learning research. 


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Large language models (LLMs)~\citep{openai2022chatgpt, openai2023gpt4} have demonstrated proficiency in generating fluent text as well as solving various language-oriented tasks.
Trained on massive corpora through likelihood maximization techniques, these LLMs have equipped the ability to execute diverse tasks in response to user directives~\citep{ouyang2022training, wei2022finetuned, sanh2022multitask}.
Unfortunately, relying solely on imitation learning during training leads to well-known issues - LLMs may generate convincing but incorrect or unsafe content that deviates from human preferences~\citep{stiennon2020learning, perez2022red}.
To further align LLMs with human preferences, learning from human feedback~\citep{ouyang2022training, askell2021general, bai2022training, touvron2023llama} has been introduced and widely adopted by leading corporations. 
Over a period, feedback learning has been widely applied to closed-source models but scarcely used in open-source models.


\looseness=-1
Many factors hinder the implementation of feedback learning in the open-source community, but the first and primary issue is \textit{data}. 
Preference data, which rates and compares different responses given the same prompt, is central to feedback learning. When scaled sufficiently, preference data reflects the intrinsic values of the annotators.
Such annotators are often assumed, by default, to be human beings who can provide the most flexible and accurate supervision signals, yet the data they generate is severely bounded by factors like financial resources, time, and knowledge.
As a result, existing preference datasets are either small in scale~\citep{wu2023fine} or limited on specific tasks~\citep{stiennon2020learning, nakano2021webgpt}. To this end, more efficient and principled methods to scale preference data are on the horizon.


This study aims to scale feedback data in an efficient manner.
Specifically, we explore \textit{AI feedback}~\citep{bai2022constitutional, lee2023rlaif}, which substitutes human annotators with advanced LLMs. 
Compared with human feedback, AI feedback is more \textit{scalable}, which means (1) it is easier to collect and expand with lower cost; (2) its quality improves as the LLM annotators become more capable. 
In previous research, it is shown that advanced AI systems are capable of conducting chatbot evaluations~\citep{dubois2023alpacafarm, zheng2023judging}, giving textual critiques~\citep{selfee2023, wang2023shepherd}, or assisting human annotators~\citep{saunders2022self}. However, open-source LLMs have not yet benefited from AI feedback through the lens of feedback learning.

This paper establishes a comprehensive AI feedback collection pipeline. Besides scalability, we prioritize \textbf{diversity} of both instructions and responses for holistic language model alignment. Particularly, we compile a diverse array of over $60,000$ instructions and $17$ models from multiple sources to produce comparative conversations in broad topics and quality. 
Then, we adopt a bunch of techniques to alleviate annotation biases and improve feedback quality to the greatest extent. These include (1) decomposing annotation documents into four different aspects, namely instruction-following, truthfulness, honesty, and helpfulness, to reduce ambiguity; (2) providing objective grading criteria and reference responses for score calibration; (3) asking GPT-4 for detailed textual critique before scores as chain-of-thought~\citep{wei2022chain} rationales.
Comprehending all above, we finally build \textsc{UltraFeedback}, a million-scale AI feedback dataset for aligning open-source LLMs. %To the best of our knowledge, \textsc{UltraFeedback}

We comprehensively validate the advantage of AI feedback in boosting open-source models with \textsc{UltraFeedback}. By fine-tuning a LLaMA2-13B model~\citep{touvron2023llama}, we build a state-of-the-art reward model UltraRM, which significantly outperforms existing open-source reward models. Based on UltraRM, we enhance a powerful open-source model UltraLM~\citep{ding2023enhancing, touvron2023llama1} with best-of-$n$ sampling and PPO. Experiments show that both strategies improve the model dramatically.
Moreover, we fine-tune a critique model that could criticize and judge model responses. We also conduct a detailed analysis of the consistency and inconsistency between AI and human feedback.

To summarize, our contributions are three-fold:
(1) To the best of our knowledge, we for the first time demonstrate the beneficial effect of scaled AI feedback on open-source chat LLMs.
(2) We establish a systematic and sizable pipeline to collect high-quality and diversified AI feedback.
(3) We release a suite of resources for feedback learning research, including a dataset, reward model, and critique model.

\section{\textsc{UltraFeedback}

\subsection{Overview}
Inspired by the data engineering principles in supervised fine-tuning \citep{ding2023enhancing,vicuna2023,xu2023wizardlm}, we identify scalability and diversity as pivot factors of the overall generalizability of preference data.
We argue that existing preference data suffer from satisfying either one of the two factors. To be specific, human feedback collection usually relies on human annotators to compare a pair of completions ~\cite{stiennon2020learning, nakano2021webgpt, ouyang2022training, bai2022training}. 
Thus, the data is hard to scale up due to time and budget constraints, especially for open-source researchers. On the other hand, existing AI feedback approaches~\cite{bai2022constitutional, lee2023rlaif} reduce human involvement and enjoy scalability via capable LLMs, but they are limited to specific domains~\cite{bai2022constitutional, lee2023rlaif} or forms~\cite{selfee2023} and hence lack the necessary diversity to boost LM performance under broader contexts.

To this end, we take into account scalability and diversity in all three stages of the preference data collection process: collecting instructions, sampling completions, and annotating comparison pairs. The overview of the data collection pipeline is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ultraf}. Firstly, we collect a large-scale and diversified instruction set to enhance LLMs' capabilities from four aspects:
(1) \textit{Follow Instructions}: LLMs should respond to humans without deviating from the requirements.
(2) \textit{Helpful and Informative}: LLMs should provide useful and correct answers to address the given problems. % Answers should also detail the relevant information or rationale for humans to fully understand, unless a concise answer is explicitly required.
(3) \textit{Truthful}: LLMs' output should be grounded in the instructions and real-world knowledge, and avoid introducing any self-contradiction. %The outputs should also be logically self-consistent.
(4) \textit{Honesty}: LLMs should know what they (don't) know and express uncertainty towards the given problem. %so that humans can prepare for the potential errors in advance rather than blind faith.
For the second stage, to avoid the sameness of comparison responses, we build a pool of distinct models at different capability levels to sample completions. 
Finally, to overcome the issues concerning scalability~\cite{nakano2021webgpt, stiennon2020learning} and quality~\cite{pmlr-v162-ethayarajh22a}, we seek scalable AI feedback from GPT-4, and explore several techniques to improve the reliability. 
Next, we will introduce our data construction pipeline in detail.



\subsection{Instruction Collection}
\label{sec:ultra_construct}

We select instructions that target four distinct but all-important abilities of language models, namely instruction-following, truthfulness, honesty, and helpfulness. 
Specifically, we include all instructions from TruthfulQA~\citep{lin2022truthfulqa} and FalseQA~\citep{hu2023falseqa} training set for truthfulness. For instruction-following and helpfulness, we randomly sample 10k instructions from Evol-Instruct~\citep{xu2023wizardlm} and UltraChat~\citep{ding2023enhancing} respectively, and sample 20k from ShareGPT~\citep{vicuna2023}. 
We finally include FLAN~\citep{longpre2023flan} to improve LLMs' helpfulness in various NLP tasks due to the task diversity within FLAN.
We adopt a stratified sampling strategy following \citep{mukherjee2023orca}, randomly picking 3k instructions from the ``CoT'' subset and sampling 10 instructions per task for the other three subsets, while excluding those with overly long instructions. 
In particular, honesty will be assessed by TruthfulQA and FLAN as they both contain reference answers, based on which it is easier for the annotator to judge if the uncertainty expressed in LLMs' responses calibrates with the accuracy.

Finally, we obtain $63,967$ instructions of various types from the six publicly available high-quality datasets. 


\subsection{Completion Sampling}
\looseness=-1
To guarantee that the collected responses are dissimilar and well-distributed, we include different models to generate completions for each instruction.
To alleviate the potential spurious correlation between text styles and response quality within the dataset, we introduce intervention by selecting not only different series of models at different levels, but also models with different model sizes, architectures, and training data within the same model series. 
This strategy enables one type of text style to present responses of different quality levels, namely the response of one series of models may be better or worse than another depending on model sizes, thus avoiding the establishment of spurious correlations. 
Specifically, we set up a pool of 17 models: (1) For commercial models, we choose GPT-4, \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo} (ChatGPT), and Bard~\footnote{\url{https://bard.google.1/13B-v1.2/70B-v1.1 \citep{xu2023wizardlm}, Vicuna-33B-v1.3 \citep{vicuna2023}, LLaMA2-7B/13B/70B-Chat \citep{touvron2023llama}, and Alpaca-7B \citep{taori2023stanford}; (3) For Non-LLaMA series, we choose MPT-30B-Chat \citep{MosaicML2023Introducing}, Falcon-40B-Instruct \citep{falcon40b}, StarChat \citep{Tunstall2023starchat-alpha}, and Pythia-12B \citep{Biderman2023pythia}.
We randomly sample four different models from the pool to complete each instruction. %\lifan{should we elaborate more about why this intervention could prevent RM from learning shortcuts?}
\looseness=-1
To further improve diversity in model responses, we elicit distinct model behaviors by adding different principles before completing each instruction.
Following \citet{Sun2023PrincipleDrivenSO} and \citet{mukherjee2023orca}, we first hand-craft one principle for each aspect and then automize the procedure by invoking GPT-4 to curate another ten based on the human-written example. %Particularly, for Honesty, the above 11 prompts guide LMs to generally express uncertainty using weakeners like "probably", and we supplement one extra prompt to force LMs to express uncertainty in percentages.
According to dataset characteristics, each data source is assigned with different principle prompts. 
We randomly sample a corresponding principle for each completion and add it to the system prompt to induce model behaviors. 



\subsection{AI Feedback Annotation}
After generating $255,864$ model completions based on the $63,967$ instructions, we employ GPT-4 to provide two types of feedback for each completion: (1) scalar scores that indicate the fine-grained quality regarding multiple aspects, and (2) textual critique that gives detailed guidance on how to improve the completion. These lead to over 1 million feedback data in total.

\textbf{Preference Annotation.}
Regarding the potential subjectivity and randomness of GPT-4 annotation, we apply four techniques to improve the annotation quality: (1) \textbf{Decomposition}. To reduce ambiguity and the difficulty of annotation, we decompose the overall quality assessment into four fine-grained assessments, namely instruction-following, truthfulness, honesty, and helpfulness. (2) \textbf{Standard}. For each aspect, we provide GPT-4 with detailed documentation of scores from 1 to 5 for reference, thus avoiding variable and subjective standards. %\lifan{more details?}
(3) \textbf{Reference}. To prevent inconsistency ratings across different runs, we wrap one instruction and all its completions into the prompt and ask GPT-4 to score four completions simultaneously to reduce randomness.
(4) \textbf{Rationale}. Besides scoring each response, GPT-4 is required to generate a rationale on how the response should be scored according to the documentation.
Combining all the techniques, we finally have four fine-grained scalar scores and rationales for each response. 

\textbf{Critique Generation.}
Besides scalar reward, we also seek textual critique from GPT-4. We prompt GPT-4 to act as a tutor and provide detailed suggestions specified for each completion to help models improve rather than propose answers directly.
Different from the above comparison-oriented annotations, critique prompts are generated separately from an overall perspective for each completion.
 
\subsection{Dataset Statistics}

We compare \textsc{UltraFeedback} with current open-source datasets in Table~\ref{tab:stat_critique_dataset}. \textsc{UltraFeedback} stands out to be the largest one among all preference and critique datasets, which is at least twice as large as other datasets. Also, its completions and critiques are the longest. 
Moreover, we highlight that \textsc{UltraFeedback} is \textbf{the only dataset that provides both scalar preferences and textual feedback}, enabling it to serve as a preference and critique dataset simultaneously. Overall, \textsc{UltraFeedback} outperforms previous datasets in both scale and diversity, and we also validate its high quality by experiment in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\subsection{\textsc{UltraFeedback}-Powered Models}
\label{sec:models}
Based on \textsc{UltraFeedback}, we develop UltraRM, an advanced open-source reward model that provides preferences for AI responses given user instructions. Additionally, we train a critique model UltraCM from the textual feedback in \textsc{UltraFeedback}. UltraCM could interact with human and AI assistants more flexibly in text. 

\looseness=-1
\textbf{UltraRM.} 
For reward modeling, we train UltraRM based on LLaMA2-13B~\citep{touvron2023llama}. Specifically, we train three versions of UltraRM.
We mix several open-source datasets with \textsc{UltraFeedback} to train UltraRM. The open-source datasets include Stanford SHP \cite{pmlr-v162-ethayarajh22a}, OpenAI Summarization~\citep{stiennon2020learning}, and Anthropic Helpful~\citep{bai2022training}. 
To validate the quality of UltraFeedback, we also train one model with \textit{merely} the fine-grained scores of this dataset, i.e. averaging the preference scores in each aspect to get a final reward score.
Further, to compare the effectiveness of the fine-grained scores and overall scores, we replace the fine-grained scores in UltraRM with the assessment ratings in critique generation, while remaining the open-source datasets.

We keep the training strategy, including loss objective and training hyperparameters, exactly the same as \citet{touvron2023llama}. 


\looseness=-1
\textbf{UltraCM.} 
We also train a critique model stemming from \textsc{UltraFeedback} to boost future research in learning from textual feedback \citep{wang2023mint}. 
UltraCM has the same initialization as UltraRM but is trained solely on \textsc{UltraFeedback}   critique data, i.e. $255,864$ textual feedback in total. 
Given a response, we fine-tune the model to give a corresponding critique that judges the response, figures out flaws, and provides suggestions for improvement.

\textbf{Setup.} To evaluate how UltraRM aligns with human preference, we conduct experiments on four \textbf{human annotated} preference datasets, OpenAI WebGPT~\citep{nakano2021webgpt}, OpenAI Summarization~\citep{stiennon2020learning}, Anthropic HH-RLHF~\citep{bai2022training}, and Standford SHP. %Specifically, we train two versions of reward models, where UltraRM-UF is merely fine-tuned on \textsc{UltraFeedback} and UltraRM is fine-tuned on a mixture of \textsc{UltraFeedback} and an equal-size sample from three open-source datasets (except OpenAI WebGPT). 
On each dataset, we calculate the rewards of two responses for one prompt and predict which one is more preferred. We compare our UltraRM-UF, UltraRM-Overall, and UltraRM with open-source baselines, including Moss~\citep{zheng2023secrets}, Ziya~\citep{Fengshenbang-LM}, OASST~\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/OpenAssistant/reward-model-deberta-v3-large-v2}}, 
and SteamSHP \cite{pmlr-v162-ethayarajh22a}. We also report the results in LLaMA2~\citep{touvron2023llama}, although their reward models are not released. 

\looseness=-1
\textbf{Results.}  The preference prediction accuracy results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:reward}. As we can see, the UltraRM series outperforms baseline reward models except for the closed LLaMA2 reward model (much larger) by a large margin, indicating that UltraRM series are the \textbf{best open-source reward models}. Notably, our reward model can still surpass all other baselines even without mixing open-source datasets. These results reveal that, \textsc{UltraFeedback} is highly consistent with human preference, and its high quality as well as diversity enable strong out-of-distribution generalization. On average, the model trained with only \textsc{UltraFeedback} outperforms open-source baseline models by over $6.3$ percent in accuracy, while mixing open-source datasets with overall scores and fine-grained scores of \textsc{UltraFeedback} achieves $3.1$ and $4.2$ percent more improvement respectively.

We highlight that the OpenAI WebGPT dataset has no training and test splits, and neither most baselines nor we train reward models on this dataset\footnote{The OASST and LLaMA2 Helpfulness reward model used WebGPT dataset for training. To prevent data leakage, we do not report their performance on WebGPT.}, making it a fair benchmark to evaluate the generalization ability of reward models. Obviously, UltraRM series are significantly better, reaching $2.6\%$ absolute points improvement over baselines. Another intriguing finding is that adding open-source datasets has a minor effect on the WebGPT dataset, which again proves the transferability advantage of \textsc{UltraFeedback}.
On another benchmark Stanford SHP, UltraRM also achieves remarkable performance.

A noteworthy finding is that, despite exhibiting comparably on the other three datasets, the reward model trained with overall scores discernably lags behind the other two variants on WebGPT. There can be two potential explanations for this observation. 
First, fine-grained annotation, which scores model outputs from different aspects respectively, provides a more precise assessment for each completion than aggregating evaluation into an overall number.
Second, in the overall quality annotation process, each sample is sent to GPT-4 separately whereas, in fine-grained rating, all four completions are scored at the same time, which may provide GPT-4 with cross-references and prevent it from applying inconsistent standards, reducing the impact of randomness. These superiorities demonstrate the high quality of our fine-grained preference data, and we advocate future work to adopt the fine-grained annotation schema and rate multiple completions at one time.

\subsection{Best-of-$n$ Experiments}
\label{sec:bon}
\begin{figure}[thb]
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{figures/bon.pdf}
  \end{center}
  \caption{Win rate against \texttt{text-davinci-003} on AlpacaEval benchmark. We sample $n$ responses and choose the one with the highest reward.}
  \label{fig:bon}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Setup.} To verify that UltraRM could serve as a good indicator of response quality, we conduct best-of-$n$ experiments. On the AlpacaEval benchmark, we randomly sample $16$ completions from the original UltraLM-13B and calculate their corresponding rewards. We then select the best-of-$\{1,2,4,8,16\}$ completions as the final response. The sampling parameters are set to $\text{temperature}=1$ and top-$p=1$.

\textbf{Results.} We present results in Figure~\ref{fig:bon}. Apparently, we can see the win rate on AlpacaEval increases proportionally with rewards. This validates that our UltraRM gives rigorous rewards that reflect the overall response quality. Notably, the best-of-$n$ sampling strategy is surprisingly effective. The initial UltraLM-13B model achieves a $76.53\%$ win rate for a single sampling, and a simple best-of-$2$ sample increases the win rate to $84.64\%$. With more samples, we can get even more high-quality responses, and the final best-of-$16$ win rate hits $91.54\%$. The best-of-$n$ sampling is universally applicable across models and tasks, which enhances models without training.


\subsection{PPO Experiments}
\label{sec:ppo}

\textbf{Setup.} Given the state-of-the-art UltraRM, we aim to push the upper bound of open-source chat language models with RLAIF. Specifically, we perform PPO over UltraLM-13B~\citep{ding2023enhancing} to get its PPO version, UltraLM-13B-PPO. We tune UltraLM for 80 iterations on the \textsc{UltraFeedback} prompts. In each iteration, we collect 512 samples and update the policy model with a mini-batch size of 64. The learning rate is fixed at 1e-6.

\textbf{Baselines.} We compare UltraLM-13B-PPO with leading open-source models and proprietary models, including LLaMA2-Chat~\citep{touvron2023llama}, Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023}, WizardLM~\citep{xu2023wizardlm},
OpenChat~\citep{wang2023openchat}, and ChatGPT~\citep{openai2022chatgpt}.

\textbf{Benchmarks.} We conduct experiments on three public benchmarks, namely AlpacaEval~\citep{alpaca_eval}, Evol-Instruct~\citep{xu2023wizardlm}, and UltraChat~\citep{ding2023enhancing}. On each benchmark, we ask GPT-4 to judge which response is better given the same instruction.
AlpacaEval adopts \texttt{text-davinci-003} as the competitor model, while we compete with \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo} on Evol-Instruct and UltraChat.
To avoid position bias, we randomly switch the comparing responses. For all models, we use the same decoding parameter with $\text{temperature}=0.7$ and top-$p=1$.

\textbf{Results.} We report experiment results in Table~\ref{tab:exp_res}. We take the official results on the AlpacaEval leaderboard for baseline models and conduct evaluations by ourselves for other results. Overall, our UltraLM-13B-PPO achieves the highest average win rate on the three benchmarks, outperforming all other open-source models. Among LLaMA-based models, UltraLM-13B-PPO overtakes other models by at least $3.6$ percent on average. Even when compared with the much larger LLaMA2-70B-Chat model, our model still holds the advantage, illustrating the huge benefit of RLAIF alignment.
Our model also reaches the highest win rate on two of the benchmarks, Evol-Instruct and UltraChat, against the more powerful \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo}.
It is worth noting that, compared with the original UltraLM-13B, the PPO process benefits the model greatly, leading to a $16.8$ percent enhancement.%On the Evol-Instruct and UltraChat test set, we also evaluate the relative scores compared with \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo}. As presented in Table~\ref{tab:evol}, UltraLM-13B-PPO still gets the highest score, outperforming all strong baselines.

\subsection{Reliability of AI Evaluation}
\label{sec:human_eval}
We first supplement another AI evaluation using Claude-3 Sonnet \cite{claude3} to investigate the agreement among different series of AI models. The prompts are the same as the GPT-4 evaluation.
Then, we compare both of our AI evaluation results with human annotations to examine if AI evaluations reliably correlate with humans. Particularly, we use the majority votes of the three annotators and filter out samples with all different votes. 

We present GPT-4, Claude-3, and human evaluation results on the remaining 266 pairs in Table~\ref{tab:app_human}. Overall, Claude-3 shares the same trend as GPT-4 and further increases our models' win rates.
Human evaluations are mostly consistent with GPT-4, giving a 64.3\% against 67.3\% average winning rate. We notice that human labelers tend to assign more ties than GPT-4, leading to slightly lower winning rates.

For fine-grained analysis, we categorize the evaluated samples into reasoning, writing, and QA tasks. The categorical comparison results are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:human_cate}. It is shown that human evaluations are mostly consistent with GPT-4, where they both prefer our models on writing and QA tasks. On reasoning tasks including coding, math, and logic, human and GPT-4 judgments diverge on ties and losses, where GPT-4 gives fewer ties but more losses. To delve into the discrepancy deeper, we ask another expert labeler to determine the ground truth answer for each question. In this sense, a model wins when it gives the correct answer while the other does not, and vice versa. The two models tie when they both successfully or unsuccessfully answer the question. The final win/tie/lose rate comes at $42.1 \%/ 26.3\% / 31.6\%$ and closely matches human evaluations. 
The GPT-4 judge, in this case, potentially underestimated our model's reasoning performance and still has space to improve.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Feedback Learning for LLMs.}
Incorporating human feedback with imitation learning or reinforcement learning~\citep{schulman2017proximal, rafailov2023direct} has been the mainstream approach to align LLMs with human preferences in leading cooperations~\citep{stiennon2020learning, ouyang2022training, bai2022training, glaese2022improving, openai2022chatgpt, openai2023gpt4, touvron2023llama1}. 
However, human feedback relies on human capabilities, which makes it hard to scale up and apply to superhuman tasks. Accordingly, some researchers proposed \textit{scalable oversight}, which aims to supervise potent AI models by models themselves~\citep{Irving2018AISV, Leike2018ScalableAA, Christiano2018SupervisingSL}. Empirically for LLMs, \citet{bai2022constitutional} first presented Constitutional AI to let LLMs refine their responses given a set of regulations. \citet{lee2023rlaif} and \citet{Burns2023WeaktoStrongGE} further validated that learning from AI feedback could surpass human feedback on some specific tasks. More broadly, our work verified that scaled AI feedback could enhance the general ability of open-source chat models.
\paragraph{Data for LLM Alignment.}
The importance of data scalability and quality has been widely recognized in the literature on instruction tuning (also known as SFT). Early works collected various NLP tasks or real user conversations to conduct instruction tuning and observed that LLMs could generalize well across different tasks~\citep{wei2022finetuned,sanh2022multitask,wang2022super,ouyang2022training}. After the release of ChatGPT, most recent research on SFT emphasized the importance of data construction and reached conclusions that scalability, diversity, as well as quality, are vital for the final performance~\citep{ding2023enhancing, taori2023stanford, vicuna2023, xu2023wizardlm}. However, when it goes to the feedback learning stage, the importance of data engineering has not been well illustrated. Among current preference datasets, some of them focus on specific tasks (e.g. summarization~\citep{stiennon2020learning}, search-based question answering~\citep{nakano2021webgpt}, safety-oriented scenarios~\citep{ji2023beavertails}, and math problems~\citep{lightman2023let}), thus cannot boost general chat models. Some datasets are small in scale~\citep{wu2023fine, wang2023shepherd} or provide only community votes as coarse-grained preferences~\citep{pmlr-v162-ethayarajh22a, askell2021general}. Therefore, a large general-purpose preference dataset with diverse instructions and fine-grained annotations in the open-source community is urgently in need, which motivates us to construct \textsc{UltraFeedback}.


\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we proposed to enhance open-source LLMs with scaled AI feedback. Through meticulous designation, we constructed \textsc{UltraFeedback}, a large-scale and diverse AI feedback dataset. With the data, we embarked on a thorough exploration of AI feedback's multifaceted utilities, including modeling human preferences, improving chat language models, and training critique models.
Our analysis further delved deep into human agreement and model capability evaluations, revealing some nuanced insights.
We believe that AI feedback would become a scalable and reliable source for future AI oversight. We hope our work could serve as an early exploration and data support in this area, facilitating researchers in the open-source community. 
In future work, we will continue exploring diverse, high-quality, and scalable preference data construction, expanding AI feedback in multi-turn dialogues, complex reasoning, coding, and safety scenarios.
