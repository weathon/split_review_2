\title{Role of Momentum in Smoothing Objective Function and Generalizability of Deep Neural Networks}

\begin{abstract}
For nonconvex objective functions, including deep neural networks, stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with momentum has fast convergence and excellent generalizability, but a theoretical explanation for this is lacking. In contrast to previous studies that defined the stochastic noise that occurs during optimization as the variance of the stochastic gradient, we define it as the gap between the search direction of the optimizer and the steepest descent direction and show that its level dominates generalizability of the model. We also show that the stochastic noise in SGD with momentum smoothes the objective function, the degree of which is determined by the learning rate, the batch size, the momentum factor, the variance of the stochastic gradient, and the upper bound of the gradient norm. By numerically deriving the stochastic noise level in SGD and SGD with momentum, we provide theoretical findings that help explain the training dynamics of SGD with momentum, which were not explained by previous studies on convergence and stability. We also provide experimental results supporting our assertion that model generalizability depends on the stochastic noise level.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\subsection{Background}\label{sec:1.1}
First-order optimizers that use mini-batch stochastic gradients, such as stochastic gradient descent (SGD) \cite{Robbins1951Ast}, SGD with momentum \cite{Polyak1964Som, Rumelhart1986Lea}, and adaptive methods \cite{Duchi2010Ada, Kingma2015Ame}, are the most commonly used methods for solving empirical risk minimization problems that appear in machine learning. These methods have been well studied for their convergence \cite{Bottou2018Opt, Jinghu2021Clo, Xian2019Ont, Benjamin2020Con, Iiduka2022App, Nicolas2021Sto, Kevin2020Rob, Zaheer2018Ada, Dongruo2020Ont, Fangyu2019ASu} and stability \cite{Moritz2016Tra, Fen2019Con, Jun2016Gen, Wen2018Gen}, and it has been shown that tuning the hyperparameters such as the learning rate, batch size, and momentum factor is essential for successful training. This paper focuses on the SGD with momentum method and provides new insights into its convergence and stability and the role of the momentum factor.

For nonconvex objective functions, including deep neural networks (DNNs), SGD with momentum experimentally converges faster than SGD without momentum (simply "SGD" hereafter) and has better generalizability, but theoretical explanations for these characteristics have not yet been provided. Although convergence analyses of SGD with momentum for nonconvex functions have been provided \cite{Gitman2019Und, Liu2020AnI, Defazio2020Mom}, none of them explain why convergence is faster than with SGD. The generalizability of SGD with momentum has been well studied, and various experimental findings have been reported. While it has been suggested that momentum plays a role in reducing stochastic noise \cite{Defazio2020Mom, Cutkosky2020Mom}, stochastic noise has been shown to increase generalizability \cite{Li2019Tow, Wen2020AnE, HaoChen2021Sha}, and it has been claimed that stochastic noise can help an algorithm escape from local solutions with poor generalizability \cite{Rong2015Esc, Chi2017How, Hadi2018Esc, Harsh2021Esc, Robert2018AnA}. Furthermore, several studies \cite{Shallue2019Mea, Jelassi2022Tow, Kunstner2023Noi} have shown that the gap in convergence speed and generalizability between SGD and SGD with momentum is more pronounced for large batches. There is an inconsistency in that adding momentum should reduce stochastic noise, but because momentum has excellent generalizability, it should have sufficiently large noise, and this contradiction makes it difficult to understand the effect of momentum in DNNs.

The geometry of loss landscapes, in particular the relationship between the flatness of the minima and generalization, has been extensively studied from both theoretical and empirical perspectives \cite{Hochreiter1997Fla, Shirish2017OnL, Dziugaite2017Com, Jiang2020Fan, Foret2021Sha}. In general, the local optimal solution with flatter neighborhoods is considered to have better generalizability than that with steeper neighborhoods. One previous study \cite{Robert2018AnA} suggested that the function is smoothed by stochastic noise in the optimizer, and in particular, stochastic noise in SGD has been shown to implicitly smooth the objective function \cite{Sato2023Usi}.

This paper focuses on the smoothing of the objective function by stochastic noise in the optimizer and the relationship between the degree of smoothing and the generalizability of the model. When considering stochastic noise in optimizers, most previous studies \cite{Zhang2020Why, Zhou2020Tow, Kunstner2023Noi} defined stochastic noise as the difference between the mini-batch stochastic gradient and the full gradient, which we call ``gradient noise.'' In contrast, we define stochastic noise as the difference between the search direction of the optimizer and the steepest descent direction, which we call ``search direction noise.'' Search direction noise can be viewed as an extension of gradient noise; in particular, the gradient noise and search direction noise in SGD are consistent.

The simplest method for adding a momentum term to SGD is the stochastic heavy ball (SHB) method (Algorithm \ref{alg:shb}) \cite{Polyak1964Som}. Although it has been widely used in experiments, it is lacking in theoretical analysis. In contrast, the normalized-SHB (NSHB) method (Algorithm \ref{alg:qhm} with $\nu =1$) \cite{Gupal1972Ast} has been well analyzed theoretically for convergence and stability but has rarely been used in experiments. Note that the algorithm referred to as ``SGD with momentum (SGDM)'' in many previous studies is actually NSHB, while that provided by PyTorch \cite{Paszke2019Pyt} is SHB. Many variants of the momentum method have been proposed, including Nesterov's accelerated gradient method \cite{Nesterov1983Ame, Nesterov2004Int, Nesterov2013Gra, Ilya2013Ont}, synthesized Nesterov variants \cite{Lessard2016Ana}, Triple Momentum \cite{Scoy2018The}, Robust Momentum \cite{Cyrus2018ARo}, PID control-based methods \cite{An2018API}, accelerated SGD \cite{Jain2018Acc, Kidambi2018Ont, Varre2022Acc, Li2024Ris}, and quasi-hyperbolic momentum (QHM, Algorithm \ref{alg:qhm}) \cite{Ma2019Qua}. This paper focuses on SHB and QHM, which covers many momentum methods, especially NSHB, but does not cover SHB.

\textbf{Motivation.}
Our main goal in this paper is to resolve the contradiction described in Section \ref{sec:1.1} that exists between momentum and stochastic noise and to clarify the role of momentum in DNN training. It was recently found that the stochastic noise in SGD implicitly smoothes the objective function and that the noise level is determined by the learning rate, batch size, and variance of the stochastic gradient \cite{Sato2023Usi}. We extend this analysis to SGD with momentum, and by focusing on the stochastic noise between the search direction and the steepest descent direction, we attempt to reveal how momentum is involved in the smoothing of the objective function.

\subsection{Contributions}\label{sec:1.3}
\textbf{SGD with momentum's smoothing property (Section \ref{sec:4.1}).} We show that SGD with momentum's search direction noise has a smoothing effect on the objective function, the degree of which is determined by the momentum factor $\beta$, the variance of the stochastic gradient $C_{\text{opt}}^2$, and the upper bound of the gradient norm $K_{\text{opt}}^2$, in addition to learning rate $\eta$ and batch size $b$:
\begin{align}\label{eq:0}
\delta^{\text{\rm{SGD}}} = \eta \sqrt{\frac{C_{\text{\rm{SGD}}}^2}{b}}, \ 
\delta^{\text{\rm{SHB}}} = \eta \sqrt{\left(1 + \hat{\beta} \right)\frac{C_{\text{SHB}}^2}{b} + \hat{\beta} K_{\text{SHB}}^2}, \ 
\delta^{\text{\rm{NSHB}}} = \eta \sqrt{\frac{1}{1-\beta} \frac{C_{\text{\rm{NSHB}}}^2}{b}},
\end{align}
where $\hat{\beta} := \frac{\beta(\beta^2 - \beta + 1)}{(1-\beta)^2}$. We call this the "degree of smoothing." The larger the degree, the smoother the function and the greater the difference from the original function, and the smaller the degree, the smaller the difference between the smoothed function and the original function and the less smoothed the function. We also show that these are determined by the product of the learning rate and the search direction noise level. Therefore, these hyperparameters are interrelated through the degree of smoothing. Equation (\ref{eq:0}) shows that a large learning rate and/or small batch size, and/or a large momentum factor, smooths the function even more. Although their results were derived from a different perspective, Smith et al. obtained similar results \cite{Samuel2018Don}. Furthermore, the results of several experimental studies suggest that these hyperparameters are interrelated \cite{Kidambi2018Ont, Leclerc2020The, Kunstner2023Noi, Fu2023Whe}. Our results provide theoretical support for these previous findings and new insights into the role of hyperparameters in DNN training.

\textbf{Estimation of critical batch size and variance of stochastic gradient (Section \ref{sec:4}).} To estimate the variance of stochastic gradient $C_{\text{opt}}^2$ contained in the degree of smoothing as in (\ref{eq:0}), we consider a critical batch size that is defined by a global minimizer of the stochastic gradient computation cost. We show the existence of a critical batch size in the training of a DNN with SGD and SGD with momentum and provide a formula for estimating the size. We also estimate the variance of the stochastic gradient for an optimizer from the experimentally estimated critical batch size and show that SGD with momentum, especially SHB, has a smaller variance than SGD. This is the first paper to provide a formula for estimating the critical batch size for SGD and SGD with momentum, and, to the best of our knowledge, the first attempt to estimate the variance of stochastic gradients.

\textbf{Why and when momentum improves generalizability (Section \ref{sec:5}).} Using the estimated variance of the stochastic gradient, we numerically derived the degree of smoothing introduced by search direction noise (see Figure \ref{fig:01} (Left)). Figure \ref{fig:01} shows that SHB always has a greater degree of smoothing than SGD. Of particular note is that the degree of smoothing depends on the batch size, so that as the batch size increases, the degree of smoothing for SGD and NSHB approaches zero, whereas that for SHB does not decrease thanks to a momentum factor close to 1. Figure \ref{fig:01} also shows that the degree of smoothing introduced by search direction noise is closely related to the generalizability of the model. We observed that an appropriate degree of smoothing, neither too large nor too small, leads to high generalizability. Therefore, the theoretical reason for the phenomenon observed experimentally in some previous studies \cite{Kunstner2023Noi, Shallue2019Mea, Jelassi2022Tow} that the generalization performance of SHB compared with that of SGD does not deteriorate with an increase in the batch size is that SHB is able to maintain a reasonably large degree of smoothing when the batch is large. Conversely, when the batch is small, the degree of smoothing of SHB is too large, and generalization performance is not excellent. Therefore, the role of the momentum factor in SHB is maintaining a high degree of smoothing even when the batch is large. Furthermore, since an appropriate degree of smoothing leads to high generalizability, we can say that our results are useful for selecting appropriate hyperparameters.

\textbf{Resolving the contradiction that exists between momentum and stochastic noise (Section \ref{sec:5}).} In summary, adding momentum reduces the gradient noise. Conversely, adding momentum increases search direction noise, which contributes to smoothing of the objective function. Furthermore, the degree of smoothing can be expressed in terms of hyperparameters including the momentum factor. Since the degree of smoothing is well correlated with the generalizability of the model, the stochastic noise that contributes to the generalizability of the model is search direction noise, not gradient noise. The degree of smoothing of SHB leads to high generalizability because the momentum factor enables it to maintain an appropriate value even when the batch is large. Therefore, the arguments ``adding momentum should reduce stochastic noise'' and ``stochastic noise leads to generalizability'' do not conflict, and the contradiction is resolved, namely, ``adding momentum reduce gradient noise'' and ``search direction noise leads to generalizability.''

\textbf{Limitation:} The only limitation of our study is that we have only been able to provide experimental results for a limited number of models and data sets—results for similar experiments with a larger number of models and data sets should be conducted to more rigorously test our theory.



\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Notation, definitions, and assumptions}
Let $\mathbb{N}$ be the set of non-negative integers. For $m \in \mathbb{N} \backslash \{0\}$, define $[m] := \{1, 2, \ldots, m\}$. $\mathbb{R}^d$ is a $d$-dimensional Euclidean space with inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$, which induces the norm $\| \cdot \|$. $B(\bm{\hat{x}}; r)$ is the Euclidian closed ball of radius $r$ centered at $\bm{\hat{x}}$, i.e., $B(\bm{\hat{x}}; r) := \left\{ \bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d \colon \| \bm{x} - \bm{\hat{x}} \| \leq r \right\}$. The DNN is parametrized with parameter $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, which is optimized by minimizing empirical loss function $f(\bm{x}) := \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in [n]} f_i(\bm{x})$, where $f_i(\bm{x})$ is a loss function for $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and sample $\bm{z}_i (i \in [n])$. Let $\xi$ be a random variable %supported on $\Xi$
that does not depend on $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and $\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[X]$ means the expectation with respect to $\xi$ of a random variable $X$. $\xi_{t,i}$ is a random variable generated from the $i$-th sampling at time $t$, and $\bm{\xi}_t := (\xi_{t,1}, \xi_{t,2}, \ldots, \xi_{t,b})$ is independent of $(\bm{x}_k)_{k=0}^{t} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$, where $b$ $(\leq n)$ is the batch size. From the independence of $\bm{\xi}_0, \bm{\xi}_1, \ldots,$ we can define the total expectation $\mathbb{E}$ by $\mathbb{E} = \mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_0}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_1}\cdots\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_t}$. Let $\mathsf{G}_{\bm{\xi}_t}(\bm{x})$ be the stochastic gradient of $f(\cdot)$ at $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$. $\mathcal{S}_t$ is the mini-batch of $b$ samples $\bm{z}_i$ at time $t$, and $\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}_t}(\bm{x}_t)$ is the mini-batch stochastic gradient of $f(\bm{x}_t)$ for $\mathcal{S}_t$; i.e., $\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}_t}(\bm{x}_t) := \frac{1}{b}\sum_{i \in [b]} \mathsf{G}_{\xi_{t, i}} (\bm{x}_t)$.

In general, smoothing of a function is achieved by convolving the function with a random variable that follows a normal or uniform distribution:
\begin{dfn}
[Smoothed function] \label{dfn:fhat} Given an $L_f$-Lipschitz function $f$, define $\hat{f}_\delta$ to be the function obtained by smoothing $f$ as
$\hat{f}_\delta(\bm{x}) := \mathbb{E}_{\bm{u} \sim B(\bm{0}; 1)} \left[f(\bm{x} - \delta \bm{u}) \right],$
where $\delta \in \mathbb{R}$ represents the degree of smoothing and $\bm{u}$ is a random variable distributed uniformly over $B(\bm{0}; 1)$.
\end{dfn}

The following lemma represents an important property of smoothed function $\hat{f}_{\delta}$. This is general and has already been reported by \cite{Elad2016OnG}. The proof of Lemma \ref{lem:d.1}.
\begin{lem}\label{lem:d.2}
Let $\hat{f}_\delta$ be the smoothed version of $f$; then, for all $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$,
$|\hat{f}_\delta (\bm{x}) - f(\bm{x})|
\leq |\delta| L_f.$
\end{lem}
Considering that a local optimal solution with a flatter landscape in the neighborhood yields better generalizability, we can say that the degree of smoothing $\delta$ must be sufficiently large. However, Lemma \ref{lem:d.2} implies that the greater the $\delta$, the greater the gap between original function $f(\bm{x})$ and smoothed function $\hat{f}_{\delta}$. %\textcolor{red}{meaning that the information from the original function may not be properly carried over, resulting in meaningless smoothing.} 
Therefore, if the degree of smoothing is constant throughout the training, we can say that its level must be neither too large nor too small.

\begin{assum}
(A1) $f_i \colon \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\ (i \in [n])$ is continuously differentiable and a $L_f$-Lipschitz function; i.e., for all $\bm{x}, \bm{y} \in \mathbb{R^d}$, $| f(\bm{x}) - f(\bm{y}) | \leq L_f \| \bm{x} - \bm{y} \|$.
(A2) $(\bm{x}_t)_{t \in \mathbb{N}} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ is a sequence generated by an optimizer. 
(i) For each iteration $t$, \ 
$\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_t}\left[ \mathsf{G}_{\bm{\xi}_t}(\bm{x}_t) \right]
= \nabla f(\bm{x}_t).$
(ii) There exists a non-negative constant $C_{\text{\rm{opt}}}^2$ for an optimizer such that
$\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\xi}_t}\left[ \| \mathsf{G}_{\bm{\xi}_t}(\bm{x}_t) - \nabla f(\bm{x}_t) \|^2 \right]
\leq C_{\text{\rm{opt}}}^2$.
(A3) For each iteration $t$, the optimizer samples a mini-batch $\mathcal{S}_t \subset \mathcal{S}$ and estimates the full gradient $\nabla f$ as 
$\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}_t} (\bm{x}_t)
:= \frac{1}{b} \sum_{i\in [b]} \mathsf{G}_{\xi_{t,i}}(\bm{x}_t)
= \frac{1}{b} \sum_{\{i\colon \bm{z}_i \in \mathcal{S}_t\}} \nabla f_i(\bm{x}_t)$.
(A4) There exists a positive constant $K_{\text{\rm{opt}}}$ for an optimizer, for all $t \in \mathbb{N}$,
$\mathbb{E} \left[ \| \nabla f (\bm{x}_t) \|^2 \right] \leq K_{\text{\rm{opt}}}^2$.
\end{assum}

The variance of the stochastic gradient and the upper bound of the gradient are often assumed to be constant for any optimizer, but we define it for each optimizer.

\subsection{Algorithms}
We consider two algorithms that are a type of SGD with momentum.

\label{alg:shb}

\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Quasi-Hyperbolic Momentum (QHM)}
\label{alg:qhm}
\begin{algorithmic}

\FOR{$t=0$ to $T-1$}
\STATE $\bm{d}_{t} := (1-\nu \beta)\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}_t} (\bm{x}_t) + \nu \beta \bm{d}_{t-1}$

\section{SGD with Momentum's Stochastic Noise and Smoothing}

\label{sec:4.1}
Kleinberg et al. suggested that stochastic noise in SGD may smooth the objective function \cite{Robert2018AnA}. Sato and Iiduka supported this theoretically and showed that the degree of smoothing is determined by hyperparameters \cite{Sato2023Usi}. In this section, we extend this discussion to SHB and QHM.

At time $t$, let $\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}$ be the difference between the search direction of the gradient descent and the search direction of SHB, and let $\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{QHM}}}$ be the difference between the search direction of the gradient descent and the search direction of QHM: 
\begin{align*}
\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}} := \bm{m}_t - \nabla f(\bm{x}_t) \text{\ \ and \ }
\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{QHM}}} := \bm{d}_t - \nabla f(\bm{x}_t).
\end{align*}
Then, the following theorem holds:
\begin{thm}\label{prop:4.1}
Suppose that Assumptions (A2)(ii), (A3), and (A4) hold, then, for all $t \in \mathbb{N}$,
\begin{align*}
&\mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}} \right\| \right] 
\leq \sqrt{\frac{C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}{b} + \frac{\beta(\beta^2 - \beta + 1)}{(1-\beta)^2} \left( \frac{C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}{b} + K_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2\right)}, \ \ 
\mathbb{E} \left[ \left\| \bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{QHM}}} \right\| \right] 
\leq \sqrt{\frac{1}{1-\nu\beta} \frac{C_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^2}{b}}.
\end{align*}
\end{thm}
Hence, search direction noise $\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}$ can be expressed as
\begin{align*}
&\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}} = \sqrt{\left(1 + \frac{\beta(\beta^2 - \beta + 1)}{(1-\beta)^2} \right) \frac{C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}{b} + \frac{\beta(\beta^2 - \beta + 1)}{(1-\beta)^2} K_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2} \bm{u}_t =: \psi^{\text{SHB}} \bm{u}_t,
\end{align*}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}[0pt]{0.6\textwidth}%
\vspace*{-12pt}
\centering%
\includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{omega_combined-crop.pdf}%
\caption{Histogram of sampled search direction noise at 10k steps for SHB and NSHB for training ResNet18 on CIFAR100 dataset.}
\label{fig:omega}
\end{wrapfigure}%

where $\bm{u}_t \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0}; \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}I_d \right)$. It has been observed that the gradient noise $\nabla f_{\mathcal{S}_t}(\bm{x}_t) - \nabla f(\bm{x}_t)$ follows a normal distribution in CNN-based image classification models \cite{Zhang2020Why}. We confirmed experimentally that the search direction noise follows a normal distribution as well (see Figure \ref{fig:omega}). In addition, let $\bm{y}_t$ be the parameter updated by the gradient descent and $\bm{x}_{t+1}$ be the parameter updated by SHB at time $t$; i.e., 
\vspace*{-16pt}

\begin{align*}
\bm{y}_t &:= \bm{x}_t - \eta \nabla f(\bm{x}_t), \ \ \ 
\bm{x}_{t+1} := \bm{x}_t - \eta \bm{m}_t
=\bm{x}_t - \eta (\nabla f(\bm{x}_t) + \bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}).
\end{align*}
Then, according to Definition \ref{dfn:fhat} and Assumption (A1), we have
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}} \left[ \bm{y}_{t+1} \right] 
&= \mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}} \left[ \bm{y}_t \right] - \eta \nabla \mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}} \left[ f \left(\bm{y}_t - \eta \bm{\omega}_t^{\text{SHB}}\right)\right] \label{eq:11} \\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}} \left[ \bm{y}_t \right] - \eta \nabla \mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}_t \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0}; \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}I_d \right)} \left[ f(\bm{y}_t - \psi^{\text{SHB}} \bm{u}_t) \right] \nonumber\\
&\approx \mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}} \left[ \bm{y}_t \right] - \eta \nabla \mathbb{E}_{\bm{u}_t \sim B(\bm{0};1)} \left[ f(\bm{y}_t - \psi^{\text{SHB}} \bm{u}_t) \right] \nonumber\\
&= \mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}} \left[ \bm{y}_t \right] - \eta \nabla \hat{f}_{\eta \psi^{\text{SHB}}} (\bm{y}_t), \nonumber
\end{align}
where we have used the fact that the standard normal distribution in high dimensions $d$ is close to a uniform distribution on a sphere with radius $\sqrt{d}$ \cite[Section 3.3.3]{Vershynin2018Hig}.2}.) This shows that the function $\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\omega}_t^{\text{\rm{SHB}}}} \left[ f \left(\bm{y}_t - \eta \bm{\omega}_t^{\text{SHB}}\right)\right]$ is a smoothed version of $f$ with noise level $\eta \psi^{\text{SHB}}$. Furthermore, optimizing function $f$ with SHB is approximately equivalent to optimizing function $\hat{f}_{\eta \psi^{\text{SHB}}}$ with gradient descent. Therefore, we can say that the degree of smoothing due to search direction noise in SHB is determined by $\delta^{\text{SHB}} = \eta \psi^{\text{SHB}}$, i.e., learning rate $\eta$, batch size $b$, momentum factor $\beta$, the variance of stochastic gradient $C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2$, and the upper bound of full gradient $K_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}$ for SHB. The same argument holds for QHM. The degree of smoothing for each optimizer can be expressed as
\begin{align}
&\delta^{\text{\rm{SGD}}} = \eta \sqrt{\frac{C_{\text{\rm{SGD}}}^2}{b}}, \label{eq:sgd}\\
&\delta^{\text{\rm{SHB}}} = \eta \sqrt{\left(1 + \frac{\beta(\beta^2 - \beta + 1)}{(1-\beta)^2} \right) \frac{C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}{b} + \frac{\beta(\beta^2 - \beta + 1)}{(1-\beta)^2} K_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}, \label{eq:shb} \\
&\delta^{\text{\rm{QHM}}} = \eta \sqrt{\frac{1}{1-\nu\beta} \frac{C_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^2}{b}}, \label{eq:nshb}
\end{align}
and if $\nu =1$ in $\delta^{\text{\rm{QHM}}}$, we obtain $\delta^{\text{\rm{NSHB}}}$. Note that $\delta^\text{SGD}$ is the result derived by a previous study \cite{Sato2023Usi}. Since $\delta^{\text{SHB}}$ and $\delta^{\text{NSHB}}$ coincide with $\delta^{\text{SGD}}$ when $ \beta= 0$ and $\nu = 0$, respectively, our results are an extension of their result. Since the terms $\frac{\beta(\beta^2-\beta+1)}{(1-\beta^2)}$ and $\frac{1}{1-\nu\beta}$ are monotone increasing for momentum factor $\beta$ or $\nu\beta$, we can say that a larger momentum factor leads to a greater degree of smoothing. In addition to the momentum factor, Equations (\ref{eq:shb}) and (\ref{eq:nshb}) show that hyperparameters such as the learning rate and batch size also contribute to smoothing. Therefore, the learning rate, the batch size, and the momentum factor are interrelated, and they should be selected such that the degree of smoothing is appropriate. This finding is helpful in selecting appropriate hyperparameters. For example, from Lemma \ref{lem:d.2}, if a large learning rate is used, a small momentum should obviously be used in order to obtain the appropriate degree of smoothing, i.e., one that is neither too large nor too small. Leclerc and Madry observed this phenomenon experimentally \cite[Figure 4]{Leclerc2020The}. 

Then, what are the magnitudes of the degree of smoothing $\delta^{\text{SGD}}$, $\delta^{\text{SHB}}$, and $\delta^{\text{QHM}}$, respectively? Since these include hyperparameters $\eta$, $b$, and $\beta$ as well as the unknowns $C_{\text{opt}}^2$ and $K_{\text{SHB}}^2$, it is necessary to estimate them in order to reveal the magnitude of the degree of smoothing. To estimate them, we provide some results in Section \ref{sec:4}.



\section{Theoretical Analysis of SHB and QHM}

We first use convergence analysis of SHB and QHM to clarify the relationship between batch size and the number of steps required for training. We then provide an equation for estimating the critical batch size and the variance of the stochastic gradient for an optimizer. To analyze SHB and QHM, we further assume that, 
\begin{assum}\label{assum:3.1}%
For all $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, there exists a positive real number $D(\bm{x})$ such that, for all $t \in \mathbb{N}$,
\begin{align*}%
\| \bm{x}_t - \bm{x} \| \leq D(\bm{x}).%
\end{align*}%
\end{assum}%
Assumption \ref{assum:3.1} has been used to provide upper bounds on the performance measures when analyzing both convex and nonconvex optimization of DNNs \cite{Kingma2015Ame, Reddi2018Ont, Zhuang2020Ada}. An example satisfying this assumption \ref{assum:3.1} is the boundedness condition of $(\bm{x}_t)$; i.e., there exists $D_1 > 0$ such that, for all $t \in \mathbb{N}, \| \bm{x}_t \| \leq D_1$. Then, we have that, for all $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and all $t \in \mathbb{N}$, $\| \bm{x}_t - \bm{x} \| \leq \| \bm{x}_t \| + \| \bm{x} \| \leq D_1 + \| \boldsymbol{x} \| =:D(\bm{x})$, which implies that Assumption \ref{assum:3.1} holds.

\subsection{Convergence analysis of SHB and QHM}

\begin{thm}\label{thm:shb}
Suppose that Assumptions (A1)$-$(A4) and \ref{assum:3.1} hold and consider the sequence $(\bm{x}_t)_{t \in \mathbb{N}}$ generated by SHB. Then, for all $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and all $T \geq 1$, the following holds:
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}\left[ \langle \bm{x}_t - \bm{x}, \nabla f(\bm{x}_t) \rangle \right] 
&\leq
\frac{\| \bm{x}_0 - \bm{x} \|^2}{2\eta T} + \frac{\beta D(\bm{x})}{1-\beta}\sqrt{\frac{C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}{b} + K_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}\\
&\quad + \frac{\eta \left( \beta^2 - \beta + 1 \right)}{2\beta (1-\beta)^2}\left( \frac{C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}{b} + K_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2 \right).
\end{align*}
\end{thm}
5}).
\begin{thm}\label{thm:qhm}
Suppose that Assumptions (A1)$-$(A4) and \ref{assum:3.1} hold and consider the sequence $(\bm{x}_t)_{t \in \mathbb{N}}$ generated by QHM. Then, for all $\bm{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and all $T \geq 1$, the following holds:
\begin{align*}
\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \mathbb{E}\left[ \langle \bm{x}_t - \bm{x}, \nabla f(\bm{x}_t) \rangle \right] 
&\leq
\frac{\| \bm{x}_0 - \bm{x} \|^2}{2\eta (1-\nu \beta) T} + \frac{\nu \beta D(\bm{x})}{1-\nu \beta}\sqrt{ \frac{C_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^2}{b} + K_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^2} \\
&\quad + \frac{\eta}{2(1-\nu \beta)}\left( \frac{C_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^2}{b} + K_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^2 \right).
\end{align*}
\end{thm}
Convergence analysis for NSHB is performed using Theorem \ref{thm:qhm} with $\nu = 1$.

 It implies that, if the upper bound of the inner product $\langle \bm{x}_t - \bm{x}, \nabla f(\bm{x}_t) \rangle$ becomes small, $\bm{x}_t$ comes to approximate a local minimizer of $f$ and that, if the upper bound is negative, $\bm{x}_t$ is simply a local minimizer of $f$. Therefore, Theorems \ref{thm:shb} and \ref{thm:qhm} can be used to evaluate the inner products of unknown positivity.

\subsection{Estimation of critical batch size}
We first define the stochastic first-order oracle (SFO) complexity, which is the stochastic gradient computation cost. If an optimizer uses batch size $b$ for training a DNN, the optimizer computes $b$ stochastic gradients per step. If $T$ is the number of steps needed to train the DNN, the optimizer has a stochastic gradient computation cost of $Tb$, which is the SFO complexity. We would like to minimize SFO complexity in order to minimize the computational cost. Previous studies \cite{Shallue2019Mea, Ma2018The, Sam2018AnE} have shown experimentally that the number of steps $T$ required to train a DNN is halved when batch size $b$ is doubled, but this phenomenon is not observed beyond critical batch size $b^\star$. Therefore, the critical batch size is defined as the batch size that minimizes the SFO complexity for training, which is why it is desirable for the optimizer to use the critical batch size that is the global minimizer of the SFO complexity $Tb$. Zhang et al. suggested that the critical batch size depends on the optimizer \cite{Zhang2019Wch}, and Iiduka and Sato theoretically proved its existence and provided a formula for estimating its lower bound from the hyperparameters \cite{Iiduka2022Cri, Sato2023Exi}.

Letting $\epsilon > 0$ and using Theorems \ref{thm:sgd}, \ref{thm:shb}, and \ref{thm:qhm}, we take $T_{\text{opt}}$ satisfying $\frac{1}{T_{\text{opt}}}\sum_{t=0}^{T_{\text{opt}}-1} \mathbb{E}\left[ \langle \bm{x}_t - \bm{x}, \nabla f(\bm{x}_t) \rangle \right] \leq \epsilon^2$ to be the number of steps required for training each optimizer. Thus, $\epsilon^2$ is a threshold and a stopping condition for training. Critical batch size $b_{\text{opt}}^\star$ is defined as $b_{\text{opt}}^\star := {\operatorname{argmin}}_{b \in [n]} T_{\text{opt}} b$. From Theorems \ref{thm:sgd}, \ref{thm:shb}, and \ref{thm:qhm}, we can derive the following proposition, which gives a lower bound on critical batch size $b_{\text{\rm{opt}}}^\star$. The proof of Proposition \ref{prop:3.

\begin{prop}\label{prop:3.1}
Suppose that Assumptions (A1)$-$(A4) and \ref{assum:3.1} hold and consider SGD, SHB, and QHM. Let $\epsilon > 0$. Then, the following hold:
\begin{align*}
&b_{\text{\rm{SGD}}}^\star > \frac{\eta C_{\text{\rm{SGD}}}^2}{\epsilon^2}, \ \ 
b_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^\star > \frac{\eta (\beta^2 - \beta + 1)C_{\text{\rm{SHB}}}^2}{\beta(1-\beta)^2\epsilon^2}, \ \ 
b_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^\star > \frac{\eta C_{\text{\rm{QHM}}}^2}{(1-\nu \beta)\epsilon^2}.
\end{align*}
\end{prop}
Proposition \ref{prop:3.1} implies that the lower bound on the critical batch size of SHB is determined by learning rate $\eta$, the variance of the stochastic gradient $C_{\text{SHB}}^2$, momentum factor $\beta$, and threshold $\epsilon$. It has been shown experimentally that there is a relationship between critical batch size and $\epsilon$, with more severe conditions increasing the critical batch size; see, for example, \cite{Zhang2019Wch}. Our Proposition \ref{prop:3.1} theoretically supports their experimental results. It also provides a formula for estimating the lower bound for the critical batch size. In practice, however, estimating the critical batch size completely in advance is impossible because it involves an unknown, $C_{\text{\rm{opt}}}^2$. Nevertheless, this is an important proposition because it connects theory and experiment, and we can use it to back-calculate the variance of stochastic gradient $C_{\text{\rm{opt}}}^2$ (see Section \ref{sec:3.3}).

\textbf{Why and when momentum improves generalizability.} We measured the test accuracy for 11 batch sizes for 200 epochs for training ResNet18 for SGD, SHB, and NSHB on the CIFAR100 dataset. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:04}, the generalizability of SGD and NSHB deteriorated as the batch size was increased, whereas that of SHB remained stable. If the degree of smoothing is not sufficient, the optimizer will fall into a sharp local optimal solution, and generalizability will be compromised. Therefore, the reason SHB outperforms SGD and NSHB when the batch is large is that the degree of smoothing of SGD and NSHB approaches zero, whereas SHB has a reasonably large degree of smoothing even for large batches. This is also why the gap in generalizability between SGD and SHB is more pronounced for large batches as observed in several previous studies \cite{Shallue2019Mea, Jelassi2022Tow, Kunstner2023Noi}.

Figure \ref{fig:04} also shows that SHB has stable generalizability for all batch sizes, but accuracy never exceeds 70\%, which is highest accuracy of SGD and NSHB. This can also be explained by the greater or lesser degree of smoothing shown in Figure \ref{fig:delta}: SHB does not decrease in degree of smoothing with increasing batch size but always has a greater degree of smoothing than SGD and NSHB. From Lemma \ref{lem:d.2}, a too large degree of smoothing leads to too large deviations from the original function. Therefore, the reason that the test accuracy of SHB never exceeds 70\% is that the degree of smoothing for SHB is always slightly greater than the appropriate value. Then, we can say that the degree of smoothing from $b=2^3$ to $b=2^8$, where SGD and NSHB achieve high test accuracies, is an appropriate value for training on the ResNet18 on CIFAR100 dataset.

There is an impressive correlation between the degree of smoothing and model generalizability, which is why we can say that the degree of smoothing introduced by the optimizer's search direction noise dominates model training and generalizability.

\textbf{Resolving the contradiction between momentum and stochastic noise.} Figure \ref{fig:delta} shows that SHB always has a greater degree of smoothing than SGD. Therefore, for SHB, which is often used experimentally, adding momentum increases the search direction noise. We can thus say that adding momentum reduces the variance of the stochastic gradient (see Section \ref{sec:3.3}) and conversely increases the degree of smoothing introduced by the search direction noise. This is why the arguments that ``adding momentum should reduce stochastic noise'' and that ``stochastic noise leads to generalizability'' do not conflict, which resolves the contradiction.

Figure \ref{fig:delta} also shows that NSHB has the same degree of smoothing as SGD. Thus, for NSHB, which is rarely used experimentally, adding momentum does not contribute to an increase in the degree of smoothing. In fact, the performances of SGD and NSHB are very similar (see Figures \ref{fig:02} and \ref{fig:04}). This not only demonstrates that the degree of smoothing is a hidden factor governing the training of the model but also that the reason NSHB is not as good and not used experimentally as often as SHB is that the degree of smoothing does not differ from that of SGD despite the addition of momentum.



\section{Conclusion}

Our investigation of the smoothing properties of SGD with momentum resolved the contradiction between momentum and stochastic noise, namely that adding momentum reduces gradient noise and conversely increases search direction noise, which contributes to smoothing of the objective function. It also showed that the degree of smoothing is determined by the hyperparameters such as the momentum factor. Through convergence analysis and discussion of critical batch size estimation, we derived the degree of smoothing numerically and found an impressive correlation between the degree of smoothing and model generalizability. Specifically, too large or too small a degree of smoothing leads to poor generalizability, whereas a moderate one leads to high generalizability. From this perspective, we showed that SHB and NSHB are completely different, that NSHB has almost no experimental value, and that the momentum factor in SHB maintains a high degree of smoothing even when the batch is large. The relationship between the degree of smoothing and model generalizability is, so to speak, a hidden factor in DNN training, and it helps in selecting the optimal hyperparameters and understanding the training dynamics of a DNN.
