\title{Improved Convergence Rate for Diffusion Probabilistic Models}

\begin{abstract}
Score-based diffusion models have achieved remarkable empirical performance in the field of machine learning and artificial intelligence for their ability to generate high-quality new data instances from complex distributions. 
Improving our understanding of diffusion models, including mainly convergence analysis for such models, has attracted a lot of interests.
Despite a lot of theoretical attempts, there still exists significant gap between theory and practice.
Towards to close this gap, we establish an iteration complexity at the order of $d^{1/3}\varepsilon^{-2/3}$, which is better than $d^{5/12}\varepsilon^{-1}$, the best known complexity achieved before our work.
This convergence analysis is based on a randomized midpoint method, which is first proposed for log-concave sampling \citep{shen2019randomized}, and then extended to diffusion models by \citet{Gupta2024Faster}. 
Our theory accommodates $\varepsilon$-accurate score estimates, and does not require log-concavity on the target distribution.
Moreover, the algorithm can also be parallelized to run in only $O(\log^2(d/\varepsilon))$ parallel rounds in a similar way to prior works.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Score-based diffusion models are a class of generative models that have gained prominence in the field of machine learning and artificial intelligence for their ability to generate high-quality new data instances
from complex distributions \citep{Dhariwal2021Diffusion,Ho2020Denoising,SohlDickstein2015Deep,Song2019Generative,song2021denoising}. These models operate by gradually transforming noise into samples from the target distribution through a denoising process guided by pretrained neural networks that approximate the score functions. In practice, score-based diffusion models have demonstrated remarkable performance in generating realistic and diverse content across various domains
\citep{Croitoru2023Diffusion,Ramesh2022Hierarchical,Rombach2021HighResolution,Saharia2022Photorealistic}, achieving state-of-the-art performance in generative AI.

The development of score-based diffusion models is closely related to the theory of stochastic processes. At a high level, we consider a forward process:
\begin{align*}
    X_0 \overset{{\rm add~noise}}{\rightarrow} X_1 \overset{{\rm add~noise}}{\rightarrow} \cdots \overset{{\rm add~noise}}{\rightarrow} X_T,
\end{align*}
which draws a sample from the target data distribution (i.e., $X_0 \sim p_{\mathsf{data}}$), and then progressively diffuses it to Gaussian noise over time. The key step of the diffusion model is to construct a reverse process:
\begin{align*}
    Y_T \overset{{\rm denoise}}{\rightarrow} Y_{T-1} \overset{{\rm denoise}}{\rightarrow} \cdots \overset{{\rm denoise}}{\rightarrow} Y_0,
\end{align*}
satisfying $Y_t\overset{d}{\approx} X_t$ for all $t$, which starts with pure Gaussian noise (i.e., $Y_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I_d)$) and gradually converts it back to a new sample $Y_0$ sharing a similar distribution to $X_0$.
Evidently, the most crucial step of the diffusion model lies in effective design of the reverse process. 
To accomplish this goal, $Y_{t-1}$ in each step is typically obtained from $Y_t$ with the aid of score functions ($s_t^{\star} = \nabla \log p_{X_t}$), 
which are pre-trained by means of score matching techniques 
(e.g., \citet{Hyvarinen2005Estimation,Ho2020Denoising,Hyvrinen2007Some,Vincent2011AConnection,Song2019Generative,Pang2020Efficient}).

Due to the impressive empirical success, in the past few years, there have been a lot of works exploring the convergence of diffusion models \citep{Bortoli2022Convergence,Gao2023Wasserstein,Lee2022Convergence1,Lee2022Convergence2,Chen2022Sampling,Benton2023Nearly,Chen2023Improved,Li2023Towards,huang2024reverse,li2024sharp,Gupta2024Faster,Chen2023The,Li2024Accelerating,li2024adapting}.
They typically treat the score matching stage as a blackbox and study how the sampling steps $T$ and the score estimation error will affect the sampling accuracy. 
Given the observation that there still exists a huge gap between prior theories and practice, this paper is devoted to improving the convergence theory for diffusion models.
\subsection{Motivation}

Following the most existing works, let's focus on the total variation distance between distributions of the target data and generated samples.
Let $d$ be the dimension, and $\varepsilon$ denote the accuracy which means the total variation is smaller than $\varepsilon$.
For the general target distribution, \citet{Benton2023Nearly} provides first linear $d$-dependency rate with an iteration complexity $d/\varepsilon^2$.
Later on,~\citet{li2024sharp} achieves an improved complexity scaling as $d/\varepsilon$, while with a burn-in cost $d^2$.
More recently, a same complexity $d/\varepsilon$ is shown in~\citet{li2024d} with no burn-in cost, which, to our best knowledge, provides the state-of-the-art convergence rate.

However, such kind of result is still far away from practical observations.
For example, for two typical image datasets, CIFAR-10 with $d = 32\times 32\times 3=3072$ and ImageNet $256\times256$ with $d = 256 \times  256 \times  3=196608$, it is sufficient to use about $50$ steps to generate good samples \citep{song2021denoising,Nichol2021Improved}, which is much smaller than the theoretical requirement.
To close this gap, people try to derive faster convergence rate through exploiting extra data distribution properties, such as low-dimensional structure \citep{li2024adapting} and smoothness of score functions \citep{Gupta2024Faster,Chen2023The,huang2024reverse}. 
Following this line of work, the scope of this paper lies in establishing some convergence theory for diffusion models towards aligning more closely with practical requirements.
\subsection{Our Contributions}

Focusing on the diffusion models with $L$-smooth score functions, this paper develops an improved iteration complexity as following (up to some logarithmic factors):
\begin{align*}
Ld^{1/3}\varepsilon^{-2/3},
\end{align*}
which is inspired by the randomized mid-point design~\citep{shen2019randomized,Gupta2024Faster}.
In what follows, let us make a brief comparison between our results and the state-of-the-art results about the convergence rate of score-based generative models. 
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Comparison under smoothness condition.} 
Similarly, \citet{Chen2023The,huang2024reverse} also studied the convergence theory by assuming smooth score functions.
Recently, the iteration complexity is improved by~\citet{Gupta2024Faster} in this setting, which, to ensure $\varepsilon$-accuracy, scales as $\tilde{O}(L^{5/3}d^{5/12}\varepsilon^{-1})$.
This means that our convergence rate improves prior works by a factor of $L^{2/3}d^{1/12}\varepsilon^{-1/3}$.

\item \emph{Comparison under general condition.} 
Without the smoothness condition on score functions,~\citet{Benton2023Nearly} proved an iteration complexity at the order of $d\varepsilon^{-2}$, which first exhibits linear dependency in the data dimension $d$.
This bound is improved to $\tilde{O}(d/\varepsilon)$ by~\citet{li2024sharp,li2024d}. 
In comparison, to achieve $\varepsilon$-accuracy, our theory improves these works provided that the Lipschitz conditions of score functions satisfy
\begin{align*}
L < d^{2/3}\varepsilon^{-1/3}.
\end{align*}
Hopefully, such relations can be satisfied in most real cases, for example, considering ImageNet $256\times256$, this requirement becomes $L < 3381$ for $\varepsilon \approx 1$ and $L < 15694$ for $\varepsilon \approx 0.01$.

\end{itemize}


\section{Preliminary}


In this section, we introduce some basics and notations of score-based generative models. 
The diffusion generative model typically encompasses two processes: a forward process and a reverse process, as described below.

\paragraph{Forward process.}
Starting from $X_0$ drawn from some target distribution $p_{\mathsf{data}}$, the forward process evolves as follows:
\begin{align}
X_t =\sqrt{\alpha_t}X_{t-1} +
\sqrt{1-\alpha_t} W_t, \quad 1 \le t \le T,
\end{align}
where $0 < \alpha_t < 1$, and $\{W_t\}_{1\le t\le T}$ is a sequence of independent noise vectors drawn from $W_t \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_d)$. 
In addition, let's define
\begin{align}
\overline{\alpha}_t:=\prod_{k=1}^t\alpha_k,\quad 1\le t\le T,
\end{align}
which will be helpful in our following analysis.
The diffusion models draw a lot of motivations from the stochastic differential equations.
Hence, let's also introduce the continuous-time limit of the forward diffusion process, which can be modeled as
\begin{align}
\mathrm{d}X_{\tau} = -\frac{1}{2(1-\tau)}X_{\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau + \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\tau}}\mathrm{d}B_{\tau},\quad\text{for }0\le \tau < 1,
\end{align}
where $B_{\tau}$ denotes some Brownian motion.
\paragraph{Reverse process.}
The core of diffusion models lies in constructing a reverse-time process with nearly identical marginals as the forward process, namely, $Y_{\tau} \overset{d}{\approx}X_{\tau}$.
Fortunately, it is shown that there exists some probability flow ODE
\begin{align}\label{eq:ode-0}
\mathrm{d}Y_{\tau} = -\frac{1}{2(1-\tau)}\big(Y_{\tau} + \nabla \log p_{X_{\tau}}(Y)\big) \mathrm{d} \tau,
\end{align}
which can achieve our goal, such that if we start from $Y_{\tau_0} \sim p_{X_{\tau_0}}$ for any $0\le \tau_0 < 1$, it can be ensured that $Y_{\tau} \overset{d}{\approx}X_{\tau}$ for all $0\le \tau < 1$.
Then the design of reverse process is equivalent to discretize the above ODE process~\eqref{eq:ode-0}, as well as estimate $\nabla \log p_{X_{\tau}}$, which is also called score function.
\paragraph{Score functions.}
The score functions are crucial for score-based generative modeling, and the definition of score function is as follows.

\begin{definition}\label{def-score} The score function, denoted by $s^{\star}_t : \mathbb{R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d (1 \le t \le T)$, is defined as
\begin{align}
s_{t}^{\star}(x) &:= \nabla \log p_{X_t}(x) = -\frac{1}{1-\overline{\alpha}_t}\int_{x_0} p_{X_0 \mymid X_t}(x_0 \mymid x)(x - \sqrt{1-\overline{\alpha}_t}x_0)\mathrm{d}x_0, \quad 1 \le t \le T.
\end{align}
\end{definition}

Following the same way as prior analysis, we assume access to faithful estimates of the score functions $s_{t}^{\star}$ across all intermediate steps $t$ as following.
\begin{assumption}\label{assu:score-error}
We assume access to an estimate $s_t(\cdot)$ for each $s^{\star}_t(\cdot)$ with the averaged $\ell_2$ score estimation error as
\begin{align} \label{eq:score-error}
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}^2 = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t = 1}^T \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q_t}\big[\|s_{t}(x) - s_{t}^{\star}(x)\|_2^2\big]
=: \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t = 1}^T \varepsilon_t^2.
\end{align}
\end{assumption}

In addition, we mainly focus on the case with smooth score functions in this paper, which is stated below.
\begin{assumption}\label{assu:score-lipschitz}
Assume that $s_t^{\star}(x)$ and $s_t(x)$ are Lipschitz for all $t$ such that
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\|s_{t}^{\star}(x_1) - s_{t}^{\star}(x_2)\|_2 &\le L\|x_1 - x_2\|_2; \\
\|s_{t}(x_1) - s_{t}(x_2)\|_2 &\le L\|x_1 - x_2\|_2.
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
\end{assumption}

\noindent \textbf{Target data distribution.}
Following prior works, we also need the following assumption on the target data distribution $p_{\mathsf{data}}$ to establish our theory.
\begin{assumption}\label{assu:distribution}
We assume that the target distribution $p_{\mathsf{data}}$ has bounded second-order moment in the sense that
\begin{align}
R := \mathbb{E}[\|X_0\|_2^2] < T^{c_R}
\end{align}
for arbitrarily large constant $c_R > 0$.
\end{assumption}
Here, this assumption requires the second-order moment of $p_{\mathsf{data}}$ to be exceedingly large (given that the exponent $c_R$ can be arbitrarily large), which is a mild assumption.



\section{Main results}


In this section, we first introduce a new score-based sampler with randomized midpoints, which is modified from~\citet{Gupta2024Faster} for ease of analysis.
Then, we prove a faster convergence rate for this sampler based on a new analysis framework, which might be useful for improving convergence guarantees for other variations of samplers.
Finally, we also provide a parallel sampling strategy for our sampler following the similar idea from prior works~\citep{Gupta2024Faster,chen2024accelerating}.
\subsection{Algorithm}

This part is devoted to explaining the details of our score-based sampler with randomized midpoints. 
Before proceeding, let's first specify the choice of learning rates to be used in our sampler.

\paragraph{Randomized schedule.}
Similar to prior works, we adopt the following randomized learning rate schedule
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:learning-rate}
\begin{align}
\overline{\alpha}_{t} \sim \mathsf{Unif}(\widehat{\alpha}_{t}, \widehat{\alpha}_{t-1}),
\quad\text{for }t = 1, \ldots, T+1,
\end{align}
where $\mathsf{Unif}$ denotes the uniform distribution, and 
\begin{align}
\widehat{\alpha}_{T+1} = \frac{1}{T^{c_0}},
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\widehat{\alpha}_{t-1} = \widehat{\alpha}_{t} + \frac{c_1\widehat{\alpha}_{t}(1-\widehat{\alpha}_{t})\log T}{T}
\end{align}
for some sufficiently large constants $c_0, c_1 > 0$ obeying $c_1/c_0$ sufficiently large.

Moreover, for ease of presentation, we let $K = c_2L\log T$ for some constant $c_2 > 0$ such that $\frac{c_2}{c_1}$ sufficiently large, and $N = \frac{2T}{K}$.
Then define for $n = 0, \ldots, N$, and $k = 0, \ldots, K-1$,
\begin{align}
\widehat{\tau}_{k, n} := 1-\widehat{\alpha}_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-n}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\tau_{k, n} := 1-\overline{\alpha}_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-n+1},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
which satisfy the following lemma.

\begin{lemma} \label{lem:learning}
Our choice of learning schedules~\eqref{eq:learning-rate} satisfies
\begin{align}
1-\tau_{0, 0} \le \widehat{\alpha}_{T} \le \frac{2}{T^{c_0}},
\qquad
\tau_{K, 0} \le 1-\widehat{\alpha}_{1} \le \frac{1}{T^{c_0}},
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,n}}{\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1}(1-\widehat{\tau}_{k, n-1})} = \frac{c_1\log T}{T}.
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
\paragraph{Sampling procedure.}
With the learning schedule in hand, we are now ready to introduce the sampling procedure.
The algorithm is actually a discretization of the probability ODE flow incorporated with some stochastic noise,
which proceeds as follows.
We start from $Y_{0} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$,
and then for $k = 0, \ldots, K-1$, we keep updating $Y_k$ through the formula:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:sampler}
\begin{align}
Y_{k+1} &= \sqrt{\frac{1-\tau_{k+1, 0}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}Y_{k, N} + \sqrt{\frac{\tau_{k+1, 0}-\tau_{k, N}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}Z_k,
\end{align}
where $Z_k \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and for $n = 1, \ldots, N$, we compute
\begin{align}
\frac{Y_{k, n}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} &= \frac{Y_{k}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}} 
+ \frac{s_{T-\frac{kN}{2}+1}(Y_{k})}{2(1-\tau_{k,0})^{3/2}}(\tau_{k, 0} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,0})
+ \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{s_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-i+1}(Y_{k,i})}{2(1-\tau_{k,i})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i}) \notag\\
&\qquad\qquad+ \frac{s_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-n+2}(Y_{k,n-1})}{2(1-\tau_{k,n-1})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \tau_{k, n}). 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Here, we let $Y_{k, 0} = Y_{k}$.
Notice that for each step to calculate $Y_{k,n}$, only one additional score evaluation for $s_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-n+2}(Y_{k,n-1})$ is needed.
This implies that the total iteration complexity is $KN = 2T$.
\subsection{Convergence analysis}

We are now positioned to present the convergence guarantees --- measured by the total variation distance between the forward and the reverse processes --- for the proposed sampler \eqref{eq:sampler}. The proof is postponed to Section \ref{sec:analysis}.
\begin{theorem} \label{thm:main}
Suppose that Assumptions \ref{assu:score-error}, \ref{assu:score-lipschitz}, and \ref{assu:distribution} hold true.  
Then the sampling process \eqref{eq:sampler} with the learning rate schedule \eqref{eq:learning-rate} satisfies
\begin{align}\label{eq:TV-main}
\mathsf{TV}\left(p_{\mathsf{data}}, p_{Y_{K}}\right) \le \frac{CL^{3/2}d^{1/2}\log^{2} T}{T^{3/2}} + C\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\log^{1/2} T
\end{align}
for some constant $C > 0$ large enough.
\end{theorem}

We now take a moment to discuss the main implications of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
\paragraph{Iteration complexity.}
Let's focus on the first term in \eqref{eq:TV-main}, which corresponds to discretization error, and ignore the score estimation error.
To ensure $\mathsf{TV}\left(p_{\mathsf{data}}, p_{Y_{K}}\right) \le \varepsilon$, it is sufficient to choose
\begin{align*}
T \gtrsim \frac{Ld^{1/3}\log^{4/3} T}{\varepsilon^{2/3}}.
\end{align*}
As far as we know, the existing iteration complexity guarantees (with sub-linear dependency on $d$) are $\widetilde{O}(L^{5/3}d^{5/12}\varepsilon^{-1})$ achieved in \citet{Gupta2024Faster} and $\widetilde{O}(L^{2}d^{1/2}\varepsilon^{-1})$ derived in \citet{Chen2023The,huang2024reverse}.
Our theory improves the prior results with at least $\widetilde{O}(L^{2/3}d^{1/12}\varepsilon^{-1/3})$.

\paragraph{Dependency of score estimation error.}
Turning attention to the second term, the sampling accuracy scales as $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\log^{1/2} T$, suggesting that our sampler is stable to imperfect score estimation.
In comparison, the existing results (with sub-linear dependency on $d$) scale as $L^{1/2}d^{1/12}\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\log T$ and $L^{1/2}\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\log T$ in \citet{Gupta2024Faster} and \citet{Chen2023The,huang2024reverse}, respectively.
Our theory exhibits a better dependency on score estimation error, which is similar to the results in~\citet{Chen2022Sampling} and~\citet{Benton2023Nearly}.

\paragraph{Lipschitz condition of score functions.}
It is noteworthy that our better iteration complexity holds under the condition of Lipschitz continuity for score functions.
Without this condition, the convergence rates become $\widetilde{O}(d\varepsilon^{-2})$ and $\widetilde{O}(d\varepsilon^{-1} + d^2)$, which are achieved in~\citet{Benton2023Nearly} and~\citet{li2024sharp}, respectively.
Very recently, $\widetilde{O}(d\varepsilon^{-1})$ rate is shown in~\citet{li2024d}, which provides the state-of-the-art convergence rate.
Our theory is better than such result when the Lipschitz constant for the score functions satisfies $L < d^{2/3}\varepsilon^{-1/3}$,
and it is still unknown how to achieve sub-linear dependency on $d$ in the general case.
\subsection{Parallel sampling}

In this section, we provide a way to do parallel sampling for our proposed sampler that needs the similar number of parallel rounds as prior works~\citep{Gupta2024Faster}, while the number of parallel processors here is significantly reduced.
In the following, we first introduce the parallel sampling procedure, and then present the theoretical guarantee.

\paragraph{Details of parallel sampling procedure.}
In the parallel sampling, all procedures are the same as the above sampler~\eqref{eq:sampler}, except that we will update $Y_{k,n}$ for $n=1,\cdots, N$ at the same time.
Specifically, we first initialize
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:sampler-parallel}
\begin{align}
\frac{Y_{0, k, n}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} = \frac{Y_k}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}}, \quad n=1,\cdots,N.
\end{align}
Then we update all $Y_{k,n}$ for $M$ rounds simultaneously:
at the $m$-th round, we update $Y_{m, k, n}$ for $n = 1, \ldots, N$ in the following way:
\begin{align}
\frac{Y_{m, k, n}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} &= \frac{Y_k}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}} 
+ \frac{s_{T-\frac{kN}{2}+1}(Y_{k})}{2(1-\tau_{k,0})^{3/2}}(\tau_{k, 0} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,0})
+ \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{s_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-i+1}(Y_{m-1, k,i})}{2(1-\tau_{k,i})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i}) \notag\\
&\qquad\qquad+ \frac{s_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-n+2}(Y_{m-1,k,n-1})}{2(1-\tau_{k,n-1})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \tau_{k, n}).
\end{align}
Finally, we compute
\begin{align}
Y_{k+1} &= \sqrt{\frac{1-\tau_{k+1, 0}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}Y_{M, k, N} + \sqrt{\frac{\tau_{k+1, 0}-\tau_{k, N}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}Z_k,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
and we complete the update from $Y_k$ to $Y_{k+1}$ in the parallel way.
In this parallel setting, the total number of parallel rounds to yield sample $Y_K$ is $MK$, and $N$ parallel processors are needed.

\paragraph{Theoretical guarantee of parallel sampling.}
The convergence guarantees for the above parallel sampling is as follows, whose proof can be found in Section~\ref{sec:proof-thm-parallel}:
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:parallel}
With the same setting as Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, it is sufficient to choose
\begin{align}
N \gtrsim \frac{d^{1/3}\log^{1/3} T}{\varepsilon^{2/3}},
\qquad
MK \gtrsim L\log^2 T,
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}^2 \lesssim \varepsilon^2\log^{-1} T
\end{align}
to achieve $\mathsf{TV}\left(p_{\mathsf{data}}, p_{Y_{K}}\right) \lesssim \varepsilon$ for the sampler~\eqref{eq:sampler-parallel}.
\end{theorem}

Finally, let us briefly compare our theory with the prior works.
This theorem states that the parallel sampler achieves $\varepsilon$-accuracy with respect to total variation distance through using $O(\log^2(Ld/\varepsilon))$ parallel rounds, which is similar to \citet{Gupta2024Faster,chen2024accelerating}.
In addition, we only need $\widetilde{O}(d^{1/3}\varepsilon^{-2/3})$ parallel processors, which improves their results significantly.



\section{Analysis}

\label{sec:analysis}

This section is devoted to establishing Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.
Before proceeding, we find it helpful to introduce the following notations.
Without abuse of notations, we use $X_{\tau}$ to denote the continuous process as follows:
\begin{align}\label{eq:def-Xtau}
X_{\tau} \overset{\mathrm{d}}{=} \sqrt{1-\tau}X_0 + \sqrt{\tau}Z,\text{ with }Z\sim \mathcal{N}(0, I),\text{ for }0\le\tau\le1,
\end{align}
and the corresponding score function is
\begin{align*}
s_{\tau}^{\star}(x) &:= \nabla \log p_{X_{\tau}}(x) = -\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{x_0} p_{X_0 \mymid X_\tau}(x_0 \mymid x)(x - \sqrt{1-\tau}x_0)\mathrm{d}x_0. 
\end{align*}
In addition, we rewrite the sampling process with the continuous index as following:
\begin{align*}
\frac{Y_{\tau_{k, n}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} &= \frac{Y_{k}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}} 
+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,0}}(Y_{k})}{2(1-\tau_{k})^{3/2}}(\tau_{k, 0} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,0})
+ \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{s_{\tau_{k,i}}(Y_{\tau_{k,i}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,i})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i}) \\
&\qquad\qquad+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,n-1}}(Y_{\tau_{k,n-1}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,n-1})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \tau_{k, n}),
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
Y_{k+1} &= \sqrt{\frac{1-\tau_{k+1, 0}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}Y_{\tau_{k, N}} + \sqrt{\frac{\tau_{k+1, 0}-\tau_{k, N}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}Z_k,
\end{align*}
where $Y_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and $Z_k \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
Here, $Y_k$ corresponds to the $Y_{k}$ in the discrete case.
In the following, it is enough to consider $T > c_3L\log T$ for some constant $c_3 > 0$ large enough;
otherwise, the desired bound holds trivially.
The proof can be divided into three steps.

\subsection{Step 1: introduce the auxiliary sequence}

Let $\Phi_{\tau_1 \to \tau_2}(x) := x_{\tau_2}\mymid_{x_{\tau_1} = x}$ defined through the following ODE
\begin{align} \label{eq:ODE}
\mathrm{d} \frac{x_{\tau}}{\sqrt{1-\tau}} = -\frac{s_{\tau}^{\star}(x_{\tau})}{2(1-\tau)^{3/2}} \mathrm{d} \tau.
\end{align}
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:ODE}
It can be shown that
\begin{align}\label{eq:lem-ODE}
\Phi_{\tau_1 \to \tau_2}(X_{\tau_1}) \overset{\mathrm{d}}{=} X_{\tau_2}.
\end{align}
Moreover, assume that $\widehat{X}_0 \overset{\mathrm{d}}{=} X_{\tau_{0, 0}}$.
Then we have for $0 \le k < K$,
\begin{align}\label{eq:lem-distribution}
\widehat{X}_{k+1} = \sqrt{\frac{1-\tau_{k+1, 0}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}\Phi_{\tau_{k, 0} \to \tau_{k, N}}(\widehat{X}_k) + \sqrt{\frac{\tau_{k+1, 0}-\tau_{k, N}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}Z_k \overset{\mathrm{d}}{=} X_{\tau_{k+1, 0}},
\end{align}
where $Z_k \overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
\end{lemma}

Given the definition of $\widehat{X}_k$, we make the following decomposition:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:error-decomposition}
\begin{align}
\mathsf{TV}\big(p_{\mathsf{data}}, p_{Y_{K}}\big)
\le \mathsf{TV}\big(p_{\mathsf{data}}, p_{\widehat{X}_{K}}\big) + \mathsf{TV}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{K}}, p_{Y_{K}}\big),
\end{align}
and
\begin{align}
\mathsf{TV}^{2}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{K}}, p_{Y_{K}}\big) 
& \overset{\text{}}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{K}}\Vert p_{Y_{K}}\big)\overset{\text{}}{\leq}\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{0},\ldots,\widehat{X}_{K}}\Vert p_{Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{K}}\big)\nonumber \\
 & \overset{\text{}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{0}}\Vert p_{Y_{0}}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}\sim p_{\widehat{X}_{k}}}\left[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\widehat{X}_{k}}\left(\,\cdot\mymid x_{k}\right)\,\Vert\,p_{Y_{k+1}|Y_{k}}\left(\,\cdot\mymid x_{k}\right)\big)\right],
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where we make use of Pinsker's inequality and the data-processing inequality.
In the next step, we will bound the divergence between $p_{\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\widehat{X}_{k}}$ and $p_{Y_{k+1}|Y_{k}}$, and the remaining terms can be controlled easily, which is left to the final step.

\subsection{Step 2: control the divergence between $p_{\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\widehat{X}_{k}}$ and $p_{Y_{k+1}|Y_{k}}$}

Based on the update rule for $Y_{\tau_{k, n}}$, we let
\begin{align*}
\frac{y_{\tau_{k, n}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} &= \frac{y_{\tau_{k, 0}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}} 
+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,0}}(y_{\tau_{k, 0}})}{2(1-\tau_{k})^{3/2}}(\tau_{k, 0} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,0})
+ \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{s_{\tau_{k,i}}(y_{\tau_{k,i}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,i})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i}) \\
&\qquad\qquad+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,n-1}}(y_{\tau_{k,n-1}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,n-1})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \tau_{k, n}),
\end{align*}
for $n = 1, \ldots, N$.
Then notice that $\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\widehat{X}_{k}$ and $Y_{k+1}|Y_{k}$ are both normal distributions with the same variance $\frac{\tau_{k+1,0}-\tau_{k,N}}{1-\tau_{k,N}}$ and different means $\sqrt{\frac{1-\tau_{k+1, 0}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}\Phi_{\tau_{k, 0} \to \tau_{k, N}}(\widehat{X}_k)$ and $\sqrt{\frac{1-\tau_{k+1, 0}}{1-\tau_{k, N}}}y_{\tau_{k, N}}$, respectively.
This tells us that
\begin{align}\label{eq:KL-condition}
\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\widehat{X}_{k}}\left(\,\cdot\mymid x_{k}\right)\,\Vert\,p_{Y_{k+1}|Y_{k}}\left(\,\cdot\mymid x_{k}\right)\big)
&= \frac{1-\tau_{k+1, 0}}{2(\tau_{k+1, 0}-\tau_{k, N})}\|y_{\tau_{k, N}} - x_{\tau_{k, N}}\|_2^2,
\end{align}
when we set $x_{\tau_{k, 0}}, y_{\tau_{k, 0}} = x_k$, and $x_{\tau_{k, N}}$ is defined as $x_{\tau_{k, N}}=\Phi_{\tau_{k, 0} \to \tau_{k, n}}({x}_{k})$.

Towards controlling the difference between $y_{\tau_{k, N}}$ and $x_{\tau_{k, N}}$, let's consider the ODE trajectory beginning from $x_{\tau_{k, 0}} = x_k$, and define
\begin{align}\label{eq:def-ytaukN}
\xi_{k, n}(x_k) &:= \frac{x_{\tau_{k, n}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} - \frac{x_{\tau_{k, 0}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}}
- \frac{s_{\tau_{k,0}}(x_{\tau_{k, 0}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,0})^{3/2}}(\tau_{k, 0} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,0}) \nonumber\\
&\qquad\qquad- \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{s_{\tau_{k,i}}(x_{\tau_{k,i}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,i})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i})
- \frac{s_{\tau_{k,n-1}}(x_{\tau_{k,n-1}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,n-1})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \tau_{k, n}),
\end{align}
which denotes the discretization and estimation error of the ODE process~\eqref{eq:ODE}, and is controlled in the following lemma.
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:discrete}
For any $k$ and $n$, it can be seen that with probability at least $1 - T^{-100}$,
\begin{align}
\mathbb{E}_{x_k \sim p_{\widehat{X}_{k}}}\big[\|\xi_{k, n}(x_k)\|_2^2\big] \lesssim \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \frac{L^3d\log^4 T}{(1-\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1})^2T^3}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i})
+ \frac{N\log^2 T}{T^2}\sum_{i = 0}^{n-1} \widehat{\tau}_{k,i}(1-\widehat{\tau}_{k,i})^{-1}\varepsilon_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-i+1}^2,
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-i+1}^2$ is defined in~\eqref{eq:score-error}.
\end{lemma}

With the above relation, we can bound the divergence as following.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:KL}
According to Lemma~\ref{lem:discrete}, it can be shown that
\begin{align} \label{eq:KL}
\mathbb{E}_{x_{k}\sim p_{\widehat{X}_{k}}}\left[\mathsf{KL}\left(p_{\widehat{X}_{k+1}|\widehat{X}_{k}}\left(\,\cdot\mymid x_{k}\right)\,\Vert\,p_{Y_{k+1}|Y_{k}}\left(\,\cdot\mymid x_{k}\right)\right)\right]
\lesssim \frac{L^3d\log^4 T}{T^3}
+ \frac{\log T}{T}\sum_{i = 0}^{N-1} \varepsilon_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-i+1}^2,
\end{align}
where $\varepsilon_{T-\frac{kN}{2}-i+1}^2$ is defined in~\eqref{eq:score-error}.
\end{lemma}
\subsection{Step 3: putting everything together}

\begin{lemma} 
\label{lem:endpoint}
Under our choice of learning schedule~\eqref{eq:learning-rate}, we have
\begin{align} \label{eq:endpoint}
\mathsf{TV}\big(p_{\mathsf{data}}, p_{\widehat{X}_{K}}\big) \le \frac{1}{T^{10}}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{\widehat{X}_{0}}\Vert p_{Y_{0}}\big) \le \frac{1}{T^{10}}.
\end{align}
\end{lemma}
Inserting~\eqref{eq:endpoint} and~\eqref{eq:KL} into~\eqref{eq:error-decomposition} leads to
\begin{align*}
\mathsf{TV}\left(p_{\mathsf{data}}, p_{Y_{K}}\right) \lesssim \frac{1}{T^{10}} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{T^{10}} + \frac{L^3d\log^4 T}{T^3} + \varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}^2\log T} \lesssim \frac{L^{3/2}d^{1/2}\log^{2} T}{T^{3/2}} + \varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\log^{1/2} T,
\end{align*}
and we conclude the proof here.



\section{Analysis for parallelization (Theorem~\ref{thm:parallel}

\label{sec:proof-thm-parallel}

By comparing the update rules for $Y_{k, n}$ and $Y_{m, k, n}$,
it is natural to control the difference of the following two sequences:
\begin{align*}
\frac{y_{\tau_{k, n}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} &= \frac{y_{\tau_{k, 0}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}} 
+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,0}}(y_{\tau_{k, 0}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,0})^{3/2}}(\tau_{k, 0} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,0})
+ \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{s_{\tau_{k,i}}(y_{\tau_{k,i}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,i})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i}) \\
&\qquad\qquad+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,n-1}}(y_{\tau_{k,n-1}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,n-1})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \tau_{k, n}),
\end{align*}
and
\begin{align*}
\frac{y_{m, \tau_{k, n}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} &= \frac{y_{\tau_{k, 0}}}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,0}}} 
+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,0}}(y_{\tau_{k, 0}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,0})^{3/2}}(\tau_{k, 0} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,0})
+ \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{s_{\tau_{k,i}}(y_{m-1,\tau_{k,i}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,i})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,i-1} - \widehat{\tau}_{k,i}) \\
&\qquad\qquad+ \frac{s_{\tau_{k,n-1}}(y_{m-1,\tau_{k,n-1}})}{2(1-\tau_{k,n-1})^{3/2}}(\widehat{\tau}_{k,n-1} - \tau_{k, n}).
\end{align*}
The above update rules, together with the Lipschitz condition of score estimates, lead to
\begin{align*}
\frac{\big\|y_{m, \tau_{k, n}} - y_{\tau_{k, n}}\big\|_2}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} 
&\lesssim \frac{L\log T}{T} \sum_{i = 1}^{n-1} \frac{\big\|y_{m-1, \tau_{k,i}} - y_{\tau_{k,i}}\big\|_2}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k,i}}}.
\end{align*}
Applying the above relation recursively gives
\begin{align*}
\max_n\frac{\big\|y_{M, \tau_{k, n}} - y_{\tau_{k, n}}\big\|_2}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} 
\le \bigg(\frac{NL\log T}{T}\bigg)^M\max_n\frac{\big\|y_{0, \tau_{k, n}} - y_{\tau_{k, n}}\big\|_2}{\sqrt{1-\tau_{k, n}}} 
\le \frac{1}{\mathsf{poly}(T)},
\end{align*}
provided that $M \gtrsim \log T$ and $T \gtrsim NL\log T$.
Thus as long as $N \gtrsim \frac{d^{1/3}\log^{1/3} T}{\varepsilon^{2/3}}$, which guarantees that $T \gtrsim \frac{Ld^{1/3}\log^{4/3} T}{\varepsilon^{2/3}}$,
we can get the desired result immediately through just inserting the above error boound into Lemma~\ref{lem:KL}.
We omit the details here due to the similarity.



\section{Discussion}


In this paper, we establish a faster convergence rate for diffusion probabilistic models, which scales as $Ld^{1/3}\varepsilon^{-2/3}$, assuming smooth score functions. Our theory achieves at least $L^{2/3}d^{1/12}\varepsilon^{-1/3}$ improvement over prior works~\citep{Gupta2024Faster,Chen2023The,huang2024reverse}, and thereby aligns more closely with practical requirements.
Recent researches~\citep{Benton2023Nearly,li2024sharp} have shown an $O(d\varepsilon^{-1})$ dependence for diffusion models without the assumption of smoothness. However, it remains unclear whether any algorithm can achieve a sub-linear dependence on $d$ in the absence of smoothness. We leave this as an open question for future research. Furthermore, estimating the Lipschitz constant in real-world cases would be highly beneficial, potentially broadening the applicability of our results.
