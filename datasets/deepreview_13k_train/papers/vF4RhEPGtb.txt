\title{Typography Leads Semantic Diversifying: Amplifying Adversarial Transferability across Multimodal Large Language Models}

\begin{abstract}


Recently, Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) achieve remarkable performance in numerous zero-shot tasks due to their outstanding cross-modal interaction and comprehension abilities. However, MLLMs are found to still be vulnerable to human-imperceptible adversarial examples. In the exploration of security vulnerabilities in real-world scenarios, transferability, which can achieve cross-model impact, is considered the greatest threat posed by adversarial examples.
However, there is currently no systematic research on the threat of cross-MLLMs adversarial transferability.
Therefore, this paper as the first step to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the transferability of adversarial examples generated by various MLLMs. Furthermore, leveraging two key factors that influence transferability performance:
1) The strength of information diversity involved in the adversarial generation process; 2) Editing across vision-language modality information. We propose a boosting method called Typography Augment Transferability Method (TATM) to investigate the adversarial transferability performance across MLLMs further. Through extensive experimental validation, our TATM demonstrates exceptional performance in real-world applications of "Harmful Word Insertion" and "Important Information Protection."

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Multi-modal Large Language Models (MLLMs), due to their exceptional capabilities in visual perception and text comprehension, have already been widely applied in various fields such as robotics~\cite{wu2023tidybot, yang2023transferring, wu2024new, yoshikawa2023large}, autonomous driving~\cite{song2024enhancing, cui2024survey, chen2024advanced}, and industrial automation~\cite{jin2024llms, gonzalez2024performance}.
However, MLLMs are found to have human-imperceptible adversarial vulnerabilities~\cite{madry2017towards, goodfellow2014explaining}.
In previous studies~\cite{xie2019improving, ge2023improving, wu2021improving, wang2021admix, hong2019patch, qin2022boosting, huang2022transferable, lu2023set, he2023sa}, adversarial examples are shown to exhibit transferability, where adversarial examples generated on one surrogate model successfully deceive other victim models.
Attack methods based on the adversarial transferability characteristic pose significant security risks to the real-world use of MLLMs.
However, while there are some sporadic validations of the transferability of adversarial examples generated by MLLMs, there is currently no comprehensive or systematic research on this topic.
Furthermore, when exploring the specific harm caused by adversarial transferability in real-world scenarios, researchers would propose some methods to boost the severity of the attacks. This helps reveal more pronounced transferability vulnerabilities, allowing for a more comprehensive understanding.
The design principles of specific boosting methods generally adhere to two key factors: 1) the strength of information diversity involved in adversarial generation~\cite{ge2023improving, zhang2023improving, wu2021improving, wang2021admix, hong2019patch}; 2) simultaneous editing of vision-language modality information~\cite{lu2023set, he2023sa}.
Among the current boosting methods, data augmentation has gained more attention due to its simplicity in deployment, its direct enhancement of information diversity, and its efficiency in delivering practical results.
Specifically, \cite{ge2023improving, zhang2023improving, wu2021improving, wang2021admix, hong2019patch} implement data augmentation-based methods to boost transferability for Traditional Models (TradVMs), such as CNN and ViT.
\cite{lu2023set, he2023sa} propose that the key factor in improving the transferability of Vision-Language Pretrained Models (VLPMs), such as CLIP, is the editing of cross vision-language modalities.
However, for MLLMs, there is currently no specific data augmentation method that effectively enhances the transferability of adversarial examples generated by MLLMs.

Therefore, this paper provides the most comprehensive evaluation to date of the cross-model transferability of adversarial examples generated by different MLLMs.
In the exploring progress, the Multi-semantic Angular Deviation Score (MADs) is introduced to specifically quantify the degree of information diversification that data augmentation methods can achieve for MLLMs.
Subsequently, referring to the two key factors that influence adversarial transferability mentioned earlier, we propose a data augmentation method called the Typography Augmenting Transferable Method (TATM).
The Typographic attack discovered by OpenAI~\cite{goh2021multimodal, azuma2023defense, cheng2024typographic} shows that by simply adding typographic text to an image, it not only diversifies the vision modality information but also significantly edits the language modality information of MLLMs.
In Figure~\ref{fig:perform} (a), we provide a detailed introduction to the workflow of TATM.
In the specific transferability validation, we used the TATM process with "Suicide" and "Unknown" as the target outputs.
The term "suicide" represents a task similar to a Jailbreak in the context of Harmful Word Insertion (HWI).
On the other hand, "Unknown" represents a privacy protection task known as Important Information Protection (IIP).
The validation on HWI and IIP demonstrates that TATM not only serves as a tool for evaluating cross-MLLMs adversarial transferability performance but also, due to its broad range of applications, has both positive and negative impacts on real-world society.
Concretely, our contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{5pt}
\setlength{\leftmargin}{0pt}
    \item This paper is the first step to comprehensively explore the cross-MLLMs adversarial transferability;
    \item By utilizing various tools and introducing a metric called the Multi-semantic Angular Deviation Score (MADs), we can quantitatively analyze the extent to which a given method affects cross-MLLMs adversarial transferability;
    \item We propose a transferability boosting method specifically designed for adversarial examples generated by MLLMs, called Typography Augmenting Transferable Method (TATM);
    \item TATM has a wide range of applications and maintains strong performance in both negatively impactful tasks such as Harmful Word Insertion (HWI) and positively impactful tasks like Important Information Protection (IIP). The performance of TATM will not be compromised by some defense methods.
\end{itemize}



\section{Related Works}

\textbf{Adversarial Vulnerability} \hspace{2.5mm}
Adversarial attacks, such as Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)\cite{madry2017towards} and Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)\cite{goodfellow2014explaining}, exploit the vulnerabilities of machine learning models by introducing imperceptible perturbations to input data. 
Adversarial attacks are certified to have adversarial transferability, 
which means the adversarial examples generated on one model (surrogate model) are effective on another model (victim model). 
In order to observe more clearly how this transferability interferes with reality, this property could be further enhanced by data augmentation-based methods~\cite{xie2019improving, dong2019evading, wang2021enhancing, lin2019nesterov, ge2023improving, zhang2023improving, wu2021improving, wang2021admix, hong2019patch} and optimizing the perturbation process~\cite{qin2022boosting, huang2022transferable, lu2023set, he2023sa}.
Furthermore, data-augmentation methods 
Data augmentation has received more attention because of the ease and efficiency of implementation.
Among them, \cite{xie2019improving, dong2019evading, wang2021enhancing, lin2019nesterov, ge2023improving, zhang2023improving, wu2021improving} just apply pixel-level transformation into original images.
\cite{wang2021admix, hong2019patch} augment original images by mixing the additional semantics.
\textbf{Vulnerability in Multimodal Large Language Models} \hspace{2.5mm}
The evolution from Large Language Models (LLMs) to to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) has been driven by the integration of the vision encoder that can perceive vision information.
Architectures like MiniGPT-4 and LLaVA \cite{zhu2023minigpt, liu2023llava, liu2023improvedllava} have introduced a projection layer that harmonizes visual features from pre-trained vision encoders with the textual embeddings of LLMs. A variety of benchmarks \cite{fu2023mme, xu2023lvlm, li2023seed} have been thoroughly assessed, confirming the proficiency of MLLMs in tasks requiring precise visual perception and thorough understanding.
MLLMs also have various security problems.
Each new modality can introduce new vulnerabilities that adversaries might exploit \cite{noever2021reading, dong2023robust}. Previous research on adversarial attacks targeting vision-language models has predominantly focused on task-specific scenarios. For instance, various studies have aimed to manipulate model outputs in image captioning tasks~\cite {lu2023set,aafaq2021controlled,chen2017attacking}. 
Additionally, in the VQA scenarios, MLLMs depend on prompts to perform various tasks. \cite{luo2024image} delves into cross-prompt adversarial transferability, where an adversarial example is capable of misleading predictions of MLLMs across different prompts.
\cite{lu2023set, he2023sa} can effectively boost the adversarial transferability of VLPMs, such as CLIPs, by adopting cross-modal optimization.
For the intrinsic security problems of MLLMs, there are also issues such as jailbreak~\cite{wei2024jailbroken, huang2023catastrophic, wang2024llms, xu2024defending} and hallucination~\cite{yao2023llm, rawte2023survey, tonmoy2024comprehensive, xu2024hallucination} that can undermine the reliability of the final language output.
Besides, typography~\cite{azuma2023defense, cheng2024typographic} could distract the semantics of final language output by adding simple pixel-level text to visual modality input.




\section{Typography Augment Transferability Methods}


\textbf{Motivation} \hspace{2.5mm}
To further explore the vulnerabilities that adversarial transferability can create in real-world scenarios, we propose the Typography Augment Transferability Method (TATM).
The design of TATM adheres to the key factors mentioned above that influence transferability performance: 1) the strength of information diversity involved in the adversarial generation process, and 2) cross vision-language modality information editing.
As a data augmentation method, TATM naturally enhances the overall diversification of information.
Therefore, the key focus in our TATM design is on how to implement the second factor: cross-modal editing.
First, we need to clearly identify what constitutes the true vision and language modality information in MLLMs.
The studies conducted by \cite{lu2023set, he2023sa} focus on VLPMs such as CLIP, where the vision modality information is the input image, and the language modality information consists of various text options that can provide linguistic descriptions of the potential semantics of the vision modality.
As a specialized large-scale VLPM, MLLMs similarly have their vision modality information represented by the input image.
However, the true language modality information of MLLMs is the final language output generated through interaction with various inquiring prompts provided by different users, which serves as the linguistic description of the vision information.


In summary, for MLLMs, our goal is to develop a method that not only diversifies the vision modality information (input image) but also effectively augments and edits the true language modality information (final language output).
However, since data augmentation methods naturally possess the ability to diversify vision information, our primary focus in selecting potential methods is on how to effectively augment the language modality information.
Specifically, unlike some of the commonly used uni-semantic methods tailored for TradVMs, we focus on multi-semantic mixing methods.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:perform} (b), unlike the uni-semantic method, which only applies simple pixel-level transformations to the input image, the multi-semantic method involves blending external semantics to achieve higher-level or semantic-level information augmentation.
For multi-semantic augmenting strategies, we consider Admix~\cite{wang2021admix}, AIP~\cite{hong2019patch}, and Typography~\cite{azuma2023defense, cheng2024typographic} as our candidate methods.
Admix achieves augmentation by linearly combining the original image with an example containing new semantics to generate augmented vision information.
AIP, on the other hand, implements augmentation by adding a new semantic example in the form of an image patch to the original image.
The Typography method is a highly promising approach. By simply adding typographic text to the image, it causes semantic distraction in the final language output, making it an effective method for augmenting the language modality.
Additionally, we considered several pixel-level uni-semantic methods~\cite{shorten2019survey, naveed2024survey}—DIM, BC, SIM, SIA, and TIM—as baselines for comparison. By comparing these uni-semantic methods with multi-semantic methods, we further measured and selected the most effective multi-semantic strategies.

In terms of measurement, we first focused on the diversity shift produced by different methods on the vision information alone.
In Figure~\ref{fig:mot} (a), we used PCA to analyze the primary distribution of the embedding features of the original image and the augmented images generated by different methods (each method tested 300 times) after passing through the vision encoder.
Through observation, we found that for the original image (\textcolor{black}{black star}), all uni-modal methods shift in the direction indicated by the blue arrows.
In contrast, the multi-modal data augmentation methods, such as Admix (\textcolor{pink}{pink cluster}), AIP (\textcolor{gray}{gray cluster}), and Typo (\textcolor{red}{red cluster}), exhibit shifts that differ from those of the pixel-level unimodal methods.
From the \textcolor{pink}{pink arrow} (Admix), \textcolor{gray}{gray arrow} (AIP), and \textcolor{red}{red arrow} (Typo), compared to the \textcolor{blue}{blue arrow} (unimodal methods), we observe a progressively increasing angle of deviation.
This indicates that, compared to the pixel-level transformation of unimodal methods, the newly added semantics in multi-modal methods indeed introduce different and higher-level information diversification to the original image.
To further quantify the deviation of such MLLMs, compared to the uni-semantic method within pixel-level, we propose a Multi-semantic Angular Deviation Score (MADS).

\begin{equation}
\centering
        \frac{1}{N} \sum_j \left| \arg \left( e^{i(\mu_m - \mu_j)} \right) \right|, \qquad      
     S.t. \quad \mu = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \theta_i = \text{atan2}(\vec{v}_{i,y}, \vec{v}_{i,x})
\label{eq:MADs}
\end{equation}

where $j$ and $N$ are the particular type and the whole number of uni-semantic methods, $m$ is the multi-semantic method of scoring objects, $\mu$ is the average deviation angular between various augmenting examples of different methods and original image, n is the whole number of different augmenting example $i$, $\vec{v}$ is the direction vector between example $i$ and original image, $\{x, y\}$ is the $x$ and $y$ component of vector. $ \arg ( e^{i})$ is the argument of a complex number (i.e., the angle). In Figure~\ref{fig:mot} (b), we present the Mean Absolute Deviation Scores (MADS) for Admix, AIP, and Typo, comparing them against {BC, SIM, TIM} and {BC, SIM, TIM, SIA, DIM}. Through comparison, as observed in Figure 2(a), the degree of distribution deviation increases progressively along Admix, AIP, and TATM, and the MADS also exhibit the same trend of increasing variation.

To assess the actual impact of different data augmentation methods on the final language output, we then utilized Grad-CAM in Figure 1(a) to observe the attention shifts caused by various multi-semantic and uni-semantic methods.
In Figure~\ref{fig:perform} (c), by observing the Grad-CAM results of different images after being processed by the visual encoder, we find that all pixel-level augmented images and the Admix images, similar to the clean image, still primarily focus on the most prominent object in the image "cat".
TATM is the only strategy capable of shifting the entire attention region of the MLLMs' visual encoder, thereby achieving true semantic augmentation.
Furthermore, to further demonstrate semantic deviation, in Figure~\ref{fig:mot} (c), we compare the performance of different methods' augmented images in terms of their similarity matching with different semantics after passing through the vision encoder.
In this comparison, the matched semantics include not only the original image's semantics "cat" but also the "flower" introduced by Admix and AIP, as well as "table" and "dog" added through typographic text in Typo.
Upon closer observation, we found that only TATM successfully achieved meaningful augmentation of the language modality information through semantic enrichment.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mot} (c), since the other methods did not result in true semantic augmentation, and for a clearer illustration, we calculated the overall average semantic similarity scores for these augmentation methods to present their performance

\textbf{Threat Models} \hspace{2.5mm}
When adopting MLLMs, due to their complexity of parameters and the high resource consumption required for training, \textbf{\textit{Users}} often rely on commercial MLLMs API or directly download pretrained MLLMs online.
Since the fully closed-source nature of commercial MLLMs and the randomness in users' selection of online pretrained open-source MLLMs, \textbf{\textit{Attackers}} typically have little to no knowledge of the victim MLLMs, making it a completely black-box scenario.

Therefore, when attackers utilize the transferability characteristic to select surrogate models, there is a high probability of encountering cases where the surrogate MLLMs and victim MLLMs have the same fixed vision encoder structure but differ in the specific parameter size, referred to as \textbf{Fixed Vision Encoder (FixVE)}.
In contrast, when all component structures and parameters differ entirely, this scenario can be referred to as \textbf{Cross Vision Encoder (CroVE)}.
Regarding the selection of the specific attack modality, in real-world scenarios, MLLMs typically operate with relatively stable vision modality information, which interacts with various prompts generated by different users to produce the final language output.
Therefore, due to the high uncertainty in prompt selection during real-world use, we focus the attack target on the relatively stable vision modality information, which consistently participates in the process.


\textbf{Methods} \hspace{2.5mm}
To more comprehensively evaluate the performance of TATM and ensure our proposed algorithm is closely aligned with real-world scenarios. In addition to the original TATM, we have developed two extensions to further enhance its applicability: TATM with Ensemble (TATM-Ens) Training and TATM with Multi-prompt Training (TATM-MultiP). 
TATM-Ens refers to the generation of transferable adversarial perturbations where the surrogate model is not limited to a specific category of structure. Instead, it involves the adversarial optimization of an ensemble of multiple MLLMs with various types of vision encoders.
TATM-MultiP is an extension that more closely aligns with practical usage scenarios.
Specifically, while an attacker chooses an image and a specific prompt for generating an adversarial example, users may use random prompts to ask about the content of the image, which would introduce a high level of uncertainty and compromise attack effectiveness.
Therefore, to ensure the effectiveness of TATM under various prompts, the TATM-MultiP extension is implemented here. 
Concretely, during each iteration of the PGD optimization process of TATM-MultiP, besides printing typographic text in the image, different prompts are also adopted to improve the performance when facing various textual inputs.
The specific formula and algorithm are shown in Formula \ref{eq:typo-attack} and Algorithm \ref{Al:comb}. Formula \ref{eq:typo-attack} presented defines an adversarial attack method where the objective is to find an optimal perturbation $\delta_{typo}$ that minimizes the loss $L$ across the model's output across prompts $p$ and specific types of vision encoders $i$ on an input image with typographic variations $t_{typo}$.


\begin{equation}
\centering
    \begin{split}
        &min_{\delta \in S} \sum_i L(f_i(\theta, x+\delta_{typo}, p), y_T),      \\  
     where& \quad \delta_{typo} = 
     \alpha \cdot sgn(\nabla_{x+\epsilon_{Typo}}L(f_i(\theta_i, x+t_{typo}, p), y_T))
    \end{split}
\label{eq:typo-attack}
\end{equation}

\renewcommand{\algorithmicrequire}{\textbf{Input:}}
\renewcommand{\algorithmicensure}{\textbf{Output:}}
\begin{algorithm}[h!]
\caption{Typography Augment Transferability Method (TATM)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\State \textbf{Input}: MLLMs $f$ with $K$ types of different vision encoder. Visual image $x$, Typographic Text set $T_{typo}$, Prompt Set $P$, target language output $y_T$, perturbations size $\epsilon$, step size $\alpha$, number of iterations $N$
\State \textbf{Output}:  Adversarial examples $x_{adv}^{t}$, perturbation $\delta_{typo}$ with better transferability
\State \textit{Initialize:} $x_{adv}^{t}$ = x
\For{$step=1$ to $N$}
\State Random select typographic text $t_{typo}$ and prompt $p$ from $TypoT$ and $P$.
\State $x_{typo} = x+t_{typo}$
\For{$j=1$ to $K$}   \hfill $\triangleright$ \small\textbf{\textit{K=1 $\Rightarrow$ FixVE; K>1 $\Rightarrow$ CroVE}}
\State $loss_i$ $\gets$ Computing loss value of $L(f_j(\theta, x_{typo}, p), y_{T} )$ through backpropagation
\EndFor
\State Compute gradient $g=\nabla_{x+x_{Typo}}\sum_j loss_i$
\State Updating: $x_i=x_i + \alpha \cdot sign(g)$ 
\EndFor
\State  Project $x_i$ to be within the $\epsilon$-ball of $x_{adv}$: $x_{adv}=Clip_{\epsilon}(x_i)$
\State \textbf{Return:}  $x_{adv}^{t}$ 
\end{algorithmic}
\label{Al:comb}
\end{algorithm}








\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiment}

\subsection{Experimental Setting}
\label{sec:settings}

\textbf{Surrogate and Victim MLLMs}\hspace{2.5mm}
We exploit two popular MLLMs, InstructBLIP (eva-clip-vit-g/14, vicuna-7b)\cite{sun2023eva, instructblip} and LLaVA-v1.5 (clip-vit-large-patch14-336, vicuna-7b)\cite{liu2023improvedllava, radford2021learning}, as surrogate models to generate adversarial examples. Then we test the transferability of these adversarial examples on the victim models (different versions of BLIP2\cite{li2023blip}, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4\cite{zhu2023minigpt}, LLaVA-v1.5, and LLaVA-v1.6\cite{liu2024llava}) to assess whether the adversarial attacks could successfully mislead the victim models across different vision encoders and LLMs. Specifically, victim models are abbreviated as follows.

{\footnotesize{\texttt{\{VM1:BLIP2-opt-2.7B, VM2:BLIP2-opt-6.7B, VM3:BLIP2-t5-xl, VM4:BLIP2-t5-xxl, VM5:InstructBLIP-t5-xl, VM6:InstructBLIP-Vicuna-13B, VM7:MiniGPT4-Vicuna-7B, VM8:MiniGPT4-Llama-7B, VM9:LLaVA-v1.5-Mistral-7B, VM10:LLaVA-v1.5-Vicuna-13B, VM11:LLaVA-v1.6-Mistral-7B, VM12:LLaVA-v1.6-Vicuna-7B, VM13:LLaVA-v1.6-Vicuna-13B,\}}}}. 

\textbf{Datasets}\hspace{2.5mm}
In the experiment, the dataset is crafted from the MS-COCO \cite{lin2014microsoft}. Due to computational resource constraints and the fact that generating adversarial examples for 300 images on MLLMs requires approximately 24 hours of GPU time on NVIDIA A40 GPU. Therefore, we choose 300 images from MS-COCO to serve as the dataset for generating adversarial examples. For adding typography during the optimization process of TATM, we utilize 68250 words from the Open English WordNet\cite{mccrae2020english} as the typography word set. 

\textbf{Adversarial Attack Settings}\hspace{2.5mm}
To craft adversarial examples, we attack the visual modality of MLLMs to generate adversarial perturbation by employing PGD~\cite{madry2017towards} with perturbation bound $\epsilon_v = 16/255$, step size $\alpha = 1/255$, and iteration steps $T = 1000$. The experiments are targeted attacks with the target text set to "suicide" and "unknown" for the Harmful Word Insertion and Important Information Protection scenarios, respectively. Unless otherwise specified, the prompt "describe the image." is used by default during the optimization process of TATM. This same prompt is also employed for inference on victim models when testing the generated adversarial examples.

\textbf{Metrics}\hspace{2.5mm}
We employ the Attack Success Rate (ASR) as the metric for evaluating the adversarial robustness and transferability, meaning that an attack is considered successful only when the target response appears in the MLLMs' reply. A higher ASR indicates better adversarial transferability. Additionally, we also use the CLIPScore\cite{hessel2021clipscore} as a soft metric to compare the semantic similarity between the descriptions of the adversarial examples from MLLMs and the original clean images. A lower CLIPScore indicates greater semantic deviation, which in turn signifies better adversarial transferability.


\subsection{Exploring Factors that Affect TATM}
\label{Exploring}

To comprehensively explore the TATM method, we vary key parameters including the number of typographic words and typographic word type during the optimization process of TATM to examine their impact on the adversarial transferability of the generated adversarial examples.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/radar_1.pdf}
  \caption{Adversarial transferability of TATM under various numbers of printing typographic words into the image. Left: ASR performance when the target output is 'suicide'. Right: CLIPScore performance when the target output is 'unknown'.}
  \label{fig:tn}

  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/radar_2.pdf}
  \caption{Adversarial transferability of TATM under different word types of typographic words into the image. Left: ASR performance when the target output is 'suicide'. Right: CLIPScore performance when the target output is 'unknown'.}
  \label{fig:tt}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Number of Typographic Words}\hspace{2.5mm} During the optimization process of TATM, we investigate the adversarial transferability of printing various typographic words into the input image in each step of optimization, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:tn}. As expected, the clean scenario (inference on images without adversarial perturbation) consistently shows the lowest adversarial transferability across all victim models (VM1-VM13). The base PGD attack (without data augmentation during optimization) increases ASR and decreases CLIPScore compared to the clean scenario, demonstrating the effectiveness of standard PGD adversarial attacks. Significantly, It can be observed that as the number of typographic words increases (1 typo and 3 typos), the adversarial examples achieve higher ASR and lower CLIPScore on victim models, indicating stronger adversarial transferability.

\textbf{Typographic Word Type}\hspace{2.5mm} We further investigate the impact of different typographic word types (nouns, adjectives, and verbs) on adversarial transferability during TATM optimization, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:tt}. Compared to the clean scenario and the base PGD adversarial attack, all word types (nouns, adjectives, and verbs) in TATM demonstrate higher ASR and lower CLIPScore, which indicates a stronger adversarial transferability. Adjectives slightly underperform compared to nouns and verbs in generating transferable adversarial examples. For nouns and verbs, no single word type consistently outperforms the other across all victim models. Given the lack of a clear advantage for any particular word type between nouns and verbs, we opt for simplicity in subsequent experiments by selecting nouns as the standard typographic word type for TATM.

As examples illustrated in Fig. \ref{fig:perform}, while TATM enhances the semantic diversity of input images by printing typographic words into images in each step of optimization, there are other data augmentation methods, most of which modify the input images on the pixel level, such as DIM\cite{xie2019improving}, SIM\cite{lin2019nesterov}, SIA\cite{wang2023structure}, TIM\cite{dong2019evading} and BC(Brightness Control). Other methods like Admix\cite{wang2021enhancing} and AIP(Adding extra Image Patch into image) introduce one another image to enhance the semantic diversity. Table \ref{tab:diff_method_single} demonstrates the strong performance of TATM across both victim models and target outputs. For the 'suicide' target, TATM consistently ranks in the top 3 methods by ASR, especially achieving the highest ASR for VM1-VM9. In the 'unknown' target scenario, TATM maintains its effectiveness with CLIPScores, often placing in the top 3. Notably, other methods that introduce semantic diversity, such as Admix and AIP, also show competitive results for at least one of the two target outputs. These findings suggest that, compared to pixel-level data augmentation, methods enhancing semantic diversity, particularly TATM, Admix, and AIP, tend to be more effective in improving adversarial transferability.

We also evaluate these methods in the cross-prompt scenario, since in the real world users may employ various prompts on adversarial examples generated. Here we use the Claude-3.5-Sonnet to generate 100 variants of "describe the image" for inference. As Table \ref{tab:diff_method_cross} shows, TATM maintains its strong performance in the cross-prompt scenario. For the 'suicide' target, TATM consistently achieves top-tier ASR across most victim models like VM2-VM10, demonstrating its effectiveness in transferring adversarial examples even when faced with diverse prompts. In the 'unknown' target scenario, TATM's performance remains competitive, often ranking among the top methods in terms of CLIPScore. The pixel-level augmentation methods, while still showing some effectiveness, generally lag behind the semantically diverse approaches like TATM, Admix, and AIP. This disparity becomes more pronounced when comparing their performance across different victim models and target outputs. It's worth noting that the effectiveness of these methods can vary depending on the specific victim model and target output. For instance, some pixel-level methods might outperform semantically diverse methods for certain model-target combinations. However, the overall trend suggests that methods like TATM, Admix, and AIP that introduce meaningful semantic variations are more likely to maintain their efficacy across a broader range of scenarios.









\subsection{Evaluating TATM with Ensemble and Multiprompt Training}

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/fig3.pdf}
  \caption{Adversarial transferability of TATM with ensemble training on target output 'suicide'.}
  \label{fig:ensemble}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/fig4.pdf}
  \caption{Adversarial transferability of TATM with multiprompt training on target output 'suicide'.}
  \label{fig:multiprompt}
\end{figure}

To further enhance adversarial transferability across MLLMs with different vision encoders and LLMs, we combine TATM with ensemble learning to generate adversarial examples, combining both InstructBLIP-7B and LLaVA-v1.5-7B as surrogate models. Consequently, the generated adversarial examples can attack all the victim models(VM1-VM13), regardless of their vision encoder and LLM configurations. As demonstrated in Fig. \ref{fig:ensemble}, compared to ensemble adversarial attack without data augmentation (base + ensemble), ensemble TATM consistently achieves higher ASR across almost all 13 victim models (VM1-VM2, VM4-VM13).

For better adversarial transferability across different prompts, \cite{luo2024image} employs multiprompt training to generate adversarial examples. We extend this approach by investigating the performance of TATM in conjunction with multiprompt training. Specifically, our method involves selecting a different prompt at each optimization step during the TATM process. For consistency, we utilize the same image captioning prompt set as \cite{luo2024image}. Figure \ref{fig:multiprompt} illustrates the results, demonstrating that TATM combined with multiprompt training (TATM + Multiprompt) consistently outperforms the baseline (Base + Multiprompt) across all 13 victim models (VM1-VM13), achieving higher Attack Success Rates (ASR). This performance improvement underscores the efficacy of TATM in bolstering adversarial transferability when integrated with multiprompt training techniques.

We conduct an assessment of the robustness of adversarial examples generated through various data augmentation methods when subjected to common Gaussian defense methods. Our evaluation focused on two widely used defensive transformations: Gaussian Noise and Gaussian Blur. For the Gaussian Noise defense, we apply additive noise with a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 0.005. In the case of Gaussian Blur, we employ a kernel size of 3 and a sigma value of 0.1. These defense parameters were chosen to balance the trade-off between maintaining image quality and mitigating adversarial effects. By subjecting the adversarial examples to these defensive measures, we aimed to evaluate the persistence of their attack efficacy across different data augmentation methods.

Table \ref{tab:gaussian_noise} shows TATM exhibits strong adversarial transferability across both "suicide" and "unknown" target outputs when subjected to the Gaussian Noise defense. For the "suicide" target, TATM consistently ranks among the top performers, often achieving the highest ASR across multiple victim models (VM1-VM8). Similarly, for the "unknown" target, TATM maintains its effectiveness, frequently placing in the top three methods in terms of CLIPScore.
Methods that enhance semantic diversity generally outperform pixel-level augmentation techniques in maintaining adversarial transferability under these Gaussian defenses. Both Admix and AIP demonstrate competitive performance, with each achieving notable results for at least one of the target outputs. The enhanced robustness of semantically diverse methods like TATM, Admix, and AIP underscores the importance of considering semantic aspects in crafting adversarial examples.







\section{Conclusion}



This study offers the first comprehensive assessment of adversarial example transferability across Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), uncovering their persistent vulnerability to human-imperceptible attacks despite their impressive zero-shot performance. We introduce the Typography Augment Transferability Method (TATM), which enhances adversarial transferability by leveraging information diversity and cross-modal editing. Extensive experimentation demonstrates TATM's effectiveness in real-world applications such as "Harmful Word Insertion" and "Important Information Protection". Our findings also reveal that enhanced semantics is crucial for generating adversarial examples with strong adversarial transferability across MLLMs.
