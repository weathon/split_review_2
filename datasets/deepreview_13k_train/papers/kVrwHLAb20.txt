\title{Ward: Provable RAG Dataset Inference via LLM Watermarks}

\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) improves LLMs by enabling them to incorporate external data during generation. 
This raises concerns for data owners regarding unauthorized use of their content in RAG systems. 
Despite its importance, the challenge of detecting such unauthorized usage remains underexplored, with existing datasets and methodologies from adjacent fields being ill-suited for its study. 
In this work, we take several steps to bridge this gap. First, we formalize this problem as (black-box) RAG Dataset Inference (\emph{RAG-DI}). 
To facilitate research on this challenge, we further introduce a novel dataset specifically designed for benchmarking RAG-DI methods under realistic conditions, and propose a set of baseline approaches. 
Building on this foundation, we introduce \textsc{Ward}, a RAG-DI method based on LLM watermarks that enables data owners to obtain rigorous statistical guarantees regarding the usage of their dataset in a RAG system. 
In our experimental evaluation, we show that \textsc{Ward} consistently outperforms all baselines across many challenging settings, achieving higher accuracy, \mbox{superior query} efficiency and robustness. 
Our work provides a foundation for future studies of RAG-DI and highlights LLM watermarks as a promising approach to this problem.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

 
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a popular approach to mitigate limitations of large language models (LLMs), such as hallucinations, the high cost of adapting to new knowledge via fine-tuning, and the inability to back up claims by sources \citep{LewisPPPKGKLYR020}. 
By integrating retrieval, LLMs gain in-context access to large corpora of high-quality, up-to-date data, enabling them to generate more accurate and source-supported responses.
To maintain relevance, RAG providers must continuously update their corpus with new data.
However, this raises concerns regarding the unauthorized usage of documents, particularly when publicly available documents are used without the owner's permission \citep{nyt, WeiWJ24}.  
There is currently no way to conclusively prove such unauthorized usage by a RAG system, and enforce an opt-out by the  owner.

\paragraph{RAG Dataset Inference (\problem{})}
We formalize the corresponding problem as \emph{RAG Dataset Inference} (\emph{\problem{}}), 
where a data owner aims to detect unauthorized inclusion of their dataset within a RAG corpus via black-box queries, illustrated in~\cref{fig:accept}.
We aim to, for the first time, comprehensively study this problem. 
We first observe that existing datasets, used in adjacent works on RAG privacy, are fundamentally unsuitable for \problem{}.
First, the samples in these datasets may have been used in contemporary LLM training, complicating realistic evaluations, where RAG corpora consist of new data.
Second, these datasets do not model \emph{fact redundancy}, a key property of real-world RAG corpora, where multiple documents share similar content, either due to scraping data from various sources (e.g., news~\citep{ragsurvey}), or as an artifact of chunking.
Another challenge in studying \problem{} stems from the lack of baselines applicable in a realistic black-box setting.

\paragraph{Foundations for \problem{}}
In this work, we take multiple steps to bridge these gaps: First, we introduce \newdata{}, a new dataset specifically designed for \problem{} evaluation under realistic conditions.
\newdata{} contains fictional articles that are by design not part of any LLM training data, and can enable evaluations under fact redundancy, enabling accurate assessment of \problem{} methods.
Second, we adapt prior work on RAG Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs)~\citep{sib, ibm} to the \problem{} problem, and propose a simple baseline~\facts{}.
In our evaluation on \newdata{}, we find that (i) despite its extreme simplicity, \facts{} outperforms other baselines in settings with no fact redundancy, further underscoring the drawbacks of existing datasets, and (ii) when fact redundancy is present, no baseline achieves satisfactory performance.
This highlights the need for novel approaches capable of reliably identifying unauthorized usage of documents in RAG corpora.

\paragraph{LLM Watermarks as a reliable \problem{} method}
To this end, we introduce \ours{}, a \problem{} method that protects the data owner's dataset by imprinting LLM watermarks~\citep{kgw,stanford}. 
As \cref{fig:accept} illustrates, given a limited number of black-box queries $q_i$ to the retrieval-augmented LLM, the data owner can detect even small traces of the watermark across responses $r_i$, and for the first time obtain rigorous statistical guarantees regarding the usage of their dataset in the RAG corpus, enabling them to effectively audit the RAG provider.
In our experiments on \newdata{}, we show that \ours{} consistently outperforms all baselines across a variety of challenging settings, showing high rate of true positives (often 100\%), with no false accusations.
Further, due to its robustness across settings, and the fact that it engages only in natural-looking interactions with the retrieval-augmented LLM, \ours{} retains its performance even under attempts by the RAG provider to prevent unintended uses of the system.
These results underscore the effectiveness of \ours{} in enforcing data usage policies and protecting data owners' rights in the context of RAG systems.

\paragraph{Main contributions} We make the following key contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formalize a previously unexplored problem, RAG Dataset Inference (\problem{}), where a data owner aims to detect unauthorized usage of their dataset in a RAG system (\cref{sec:setting}).
    \item We facilitate research on this problem by (i) proposing a new dataset \newdata{}, specifically designed for benchmarking \problem{} methods under realistic conditions, and (ii) introducing an initial set of baseline methods (\cref{ssec:setting:data} and \cref{ssec:setting:baselines}).

\end{itemize}  


\section{Background}


 
\paragraph{Retrieval augmented generation (RAG)} 
RAG is a popular way to enhance LLM capabilities: For a given user query $q$, the $k \in \mathbb{N}$ most relevant documents $D_q = \mathcal{R}(q, D) \subseteq D$ are retrieved from a corpus $D$, using a retrieval method $\mathcal{R}$ \citep{ragsurvey}.
While instantiations differ, the query $q$ is generally combined with the result $D_q$, and fed into an LLM to generate a more factual and relevant response $r = \llm(q, D_q)$. Expanding $D$ further enables access to new information without having to rely on costly retraining~\citep{LewisPPPKGKLYR020}.
RAG is especially suitable for domains where new information is generated often, such as news articles or software documentation.
In this work we use $\mathcal{M}^\star$ to denote a \emph{RAG system}, i.e., a retrieval-augmented LLM $\mathcal{M}$.

\paragraph{LLM watermarking}
LLM watermarks enable model owners to provably and reliably track text generated by their LLM. 
In this work, we focus on the prominent \emph{red-green watermarks} \citep{kgw}.
During text generation, at each step $t$, the token vocabulary $V$ is split into two parts, $\gamma |V|$ \emph{green} (encouraged) tokens, and $(1-\gamma) |V|$ \emph{red} (discouraged) tokens, for some $\gamma \in [0,1]$.
Given \emph{context width} $h$, the split is commonly a function of tokens at positions ${t-h}, \dots, t$, as well as a secret salt.
To add the watermark, the logits of green tokens are increased by $\delta \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, boosting their probability of being sampled. 
The watermark is detected using a statistical test, based on the expectation that non-watermarked text of length $T$ has $\gamma T$ green tokens.
Namely, we use the z-score
\begin{equation} 
    z = \frac{|s|_g - \gamma T}{\sqrt{\gamma (1-\gamma) T}},
    \label{eq:zscore}
\end{equation}
where $|s|_g$ is the number of green tokens in a given text $s$, and $T=|s|$.
From here, we derive the $p$-value $p = 1 - \Phi(z)$, where $\Phi$ is the CDF of the standard normal distribution, and consider the text watermarked if $p<\alpha$ for some threshold $\alpha$.
While not perfectly robust, these watermarks generally persist under moderate text transformations, such as paraphrasing or segment omission~\citep{markmy,kgw2,radioactivity}, making them suitable for our setting, where our goal will be to propagate the watermark signal through the RAG pipeline, as we will describe in~\cref{sec:method}.

 
\paragraph{RAG membership inference attacks}
While RAG is a relatively novel concept, recent work already studies membership inference attacks (MIAs) in this setting,
 proposing two methods that we denote \sib{}~\citep{sib} and \ibm{}~\citep{ibm}.
A MIA's goal is to output  $\mathrm{mi}(d, \ragsys)=1$ if a document $d$ is part of the retrieval corpus $D$ of a RAG system $\ragsys$, or $\mathrm{mi}(d, \ragsys)=0$ otherwise, based only on queries to $\ragsys$.
To this end, \sib{} queries $\ragsys$ with $q$, a prefix of $d$, to obtain the response $r = \llm(q, D_q)$.
Then, it computes two scores: the cosine similarity between the embeddings of $d$ and $r$, and the perplexity of $r$, outputting $\mathrm{mi}(d, \ragsys)=1$ if the similarity is above a threshold $\theta_\text{similarity}$, and the perplexity is below a threshold $\theta_\text{perplexity}$, both trainable parameters. 
Note that this requires gray-box access to $\ragsys$ for perplexity computation. 
The other method, \ibm{}, directly prompts $\ragsys$ to answer if $d$ is in the context. 
If it replies positively, they set $\mathrm{mi}(d, \llm)=1$.
In~\cref{sec:setting}, we will introduce the black-box RAG dataset inference setting, and adapt both baselines to it.
Notably, while dataset inference was studied alongside MIA for training data (see~\cref{sec:related_work}), no prior work studies it for RAG.


\section{RAG Dataset Inference}

We formalize the problem of \emph{{RAG Dataset Inference}} (\emph{\problem{}}), and present our contributions aimed at facilitating studies of this problem. 
In~\cref{ssec:setting:data}, we make the case that existing datasets commonly used for adjacent tasks (e.g., RAG MIA) are fundamentally unsuitable for \problem{}, and propose a new dataset in an attempt to address these shortcomings. 
In~\cref{ssec:setting:baselines}, we establish a set of baselines for \problem{}, by adapting RAG MIA work introduced in~\cref{sec:background}, and proposing a simple baseline, \facts{}.

\paragraph{The \problem{} problem}
The key entities in \problem{} are the \emph{data owner}, who aims to protect their $n$-document dataset $\ownerdata$ from unauthorized usage in a RAG corpus, and the \emph{RAG provider}, who exposes black-box access to their retrieval-augmented LLM $\ragsys$, which uses a corpus $D$.
The data owner's goal is to determine if $\ownerdata \subseteq D$, i.e., whether their data was secretly included in the corpus. 
To this end, they may proactively modify $\ownerdata$ before publishing it, and can query $\ragsys$ in a black-box way, aiming to minimize the number of such queries.
Crucially, the data owner makes a single dataset-level decision, as opposed to document-level decisions of MIAs.
Formally, a \problem{} method $di$ should output $\mathrm{di}(\ownerdata, \ragsys)=1$ if $\ownerdata \subseteq D$ (\incase{} case) and $0$ otherwise (\outcase{} case).

\subsection{A dataset suitable for \problem} \label{ssec:setting:data}

To enable suitable evaluation of \problem{} methods, we require a dataset of documents with the following properties.
First, we aim to match a key use-case of RAG (as described in~\cref{sec:background}) where up-to-date knowledge is added to $D$ instead of costly repeated fine-tuning of $\llm$.
To model this, our documents should provably not be part of the training data of $\llm$, i.e., of current open/closed LLMs, as those will be used to instantiate $\llm$ when studying \problem{}.
Second, to model the practical case where knowledge is redundant and spread across multiple sources (e.g., news articles, a common motivating example for RAG~\citep{ragsurvey}), the dataset should contain documents with overlapping topics and information (\emph{fact redundancy}).
As we will empirically demonstrate in~\cref{sec:evaluation}, while fact redundancy is more realistic, it makes \problem{} significantly harder.

\paragraph{Current state}
As there is no prior work on \problem{}, we turn to related work on RAG privacy~\citep{ibm,sib,goodbad}, including MIAs introduced in~\cref{sec:background}.
We observe that evaluations in these works rely primarily on \emph{EnronEmails}~\citep{enron} and \emph{HealthcareMagic} datasets~\citep{meddialog,healthcaremagic}, which is motivated by the presence of PII in their samples.  
However, it can not be ruled out that these datasets were used to train contemporary LLMs, as e.g., \emph{EnronEmails} has been publicly available since 2004. 
More importantly, fact redundancy is by design not satisfied in either of these cases.
These shortcomings motivate us to construct a new dataset, \newdata{} (\emph{Fact-Redundant Article Dataset}), tailored to \problem{}.
 
\paragraph{The \newdata{} dataset}
\newdata{} consists of a number of \emph{groups}.
Each group contains articles that share a topic and a significant amount of information, but are independently written by a different (LLM) author. 
As our data source we use \repliqa{}~\citep{repliqa}, that contains articles about fictional entities and events, which by design ensures that this knowledge was not present in any LLM training data.
\repliqa{} is released gradually---to create \newdata{}, we use split $0$ as the only one available at the time of writing, but plan to expand this to future splits.
 
Each \repliqa{} article is a \emph{source} for one of our groups---we use the pipeline illustrated in~\cref{fig:pipeline}.
First, we prompt \gptBig{} to distill the information content of the article into 5 self-contained \emph{key facts}, crucial to understanding the article, and 10 self-contained \emph{additional facts}, that are present in the article but not essential to its key message (see~\cref{app:prompts:data}).
Next, to create a group, we sample an author model from the set $\mathcal{A}= \{\gptBig{}, \sonnet{}, \llamaBig{}, \qwen{}\}$ of state-of-the-art LLMs.
The selected model writes a $500$--$1000$ word article that must include all $5$ key facts, $2$ randomly sampled additional facts, and is encouraged to invent additional quotes, hypotheses, or personal opinions, as long as they do not contradict any present fact.
The diversity of authors, sampled facts, and (desirable) hallucinated content results in a varied set of fictional articles grounded in the same core knowledge, which satisfies our earlier requirements.
While this pipeline can be extended to larger/more groups, more authors, and different fact combinations, we limit \newdata{} to $3591$ groups ($3391$ for testing, and $200$ for training) of $4$ articles each, one per author from $\mathcal{A}$.

\paragraph{Use in \problem{} evaluation}
In our experiments in \cref{sec:evaluation}, we use \newdata{} to create two evaluation settings: \easy{} and \hard{}, illustrated in~\cref{fig:pipeline} (bottom).
The \easy{} setting follows the traditional setup described above where facts are uniquely represented, by always using at most $1$ article per \newdata{} group. 
The \hard{} setting introduces fact redundancy by always including all $4$ articles from a group. 
As we demonstrate shortly, the \easy{} setting is solvable by a simple baseline, while the \hard{} setting, as the realistic one and our main focus, is a significant challenge for all baseline methods.

\subsection{RAG-DI Baselines} \label{ssec:setting:baselines}
We proceed to establish an initial set of baselines for the \problem{} problem. For this we adapt existing RAG MIAs, \ibm{} and \sib{}, introduced in \cref{sec:background}, and additionally propose a simple baseline, \facts{}.

\paragraph{Adapting RAG MIAs}
By design, the existing RAG MIAs make document-level decisions, i.e., they decide if a \emph{single document} is in the RAG corpus ($\mathrm{mi}(d, \ragsys)=1$) or not ($\mathrm{mi}(d, \ragsys)=0$).
We extend this to dataset-level decisions of \problem{} in an (empirically) optimal way.
First, we apply a given method to each $d \in \ownerdata$ to obtain $n$ binary decisions, and set $s(\ownerdata, \ragsys) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{d \in \ownerdata} \mathrm{mi}(d, \ragsys)$. 
We then instantiate several \incase{} and \outcase{} cases of \problem{} using the training set, and compute the corresponding $s_{\text{in}}$ and $s_{\text{out}}$.
We finally make a dataset-level statement $\mathrm{di}(\ownerdata, \ragsys) = 1$ if $s(\ownerdata, \ragsys) > \frac{1}{2} (s_{\text{in}} + s_{\text{out}})$ and $\mathrm{di}(\ownerdata, \ragsys) = 0$ otherwise.
During this process, for \sib{}, we also grid search over its trainable parameters $\theta_{\text{similarity}}$ and $\theta_{\text{perplexity}}$ (as in~\citet{sib}), choosing values that maximize $s_{\text{in}} - s_{\text{out}}$.
We note that \sib{} is proposed as a gray-box method; we adapt it to our black-box setting by using an auxiliary language model to estimate the perplexity of responses.

\paragraph{The \facts{} baseline}
To substantiate our point about the effect of fact multiplicity on the hardness of \problem{}, we introduce a simple baseline, \facts{}.
As the above RAG MIAs, \facts{} is a document-level method.
Given a document $d$, \facts{} prompts an auxiliary LLM to generate a single question that is only answerable by reading $d$. 
Then, $\ragsys$ is prompted with that question, and if it deems it unanswerable, we set $\mathrm{mi}(d, \ragsys) = 0$, and set it to $1$ otherwise.
We aggregate such document-level decisions into a corpus-level decision by fitting a threshold on the training set, as described above. 
In~\cref{sec:evaluation}, we will demonstrate that this simple method is sufficient to solve the \easy{} setting more reliably than other baselines, yet is, due to fact multiplicity, extremely unreliable in the \hard{} setting.


\section{LLM Watermarking as an Effective \problem{}


Before describing our proposed \problem{} method based on LLM watermarks, we first outline three key design requirements that a desirable \problem{} method should fulfill.
We require the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Monotonicity}. With more queries to the retrieval-augmented LLM $\ragsys{}$, the accuracy of the method's predictions should consistently improve, preferably at a high rate. 
    \item \textit{Guarantees}. The method should be able to provide a statistical guarantee for its decision, with exceedingly rare and well-controlled Type 1 errors, as falsely accusing RAG providers is highly undesirable in practice, and undermines the trust in the method.
    \item \textit{Robustness}. The method should maintain high accuracy under diverse evaluation settings, including attempts by the RAG provider to actively conceal unauthorized data usage.
\end{enumerate}
As we demonstrate in~\cref{sec:evaluation}, all \problem{} baselines introduced in~\cref{ssec:setting:baselines} violate \emph{all} above requirements to some extent. 
To address this, we propose \ours{} (\emph{Watermarking for \problem{}}), a proactive \problem{} method that is based on LLM watermarks, and discuss why it is likely to fulfill all stated desiderata.

\paragraph{\ours{}: \problem{} via LLM watermarking}
We assume the data owner has protected each $d_i \in \ownerdata$ by embedding an LLM watermark either via a human-in-the-loop procedure or (as in this work) by rephrasing each document with a watermarked LM.
While, in principle, any LLM watermark can be applied, we focus on popular red-green watermarks (see~\cref{sec:background}).
In~\cref{ssec:eval:quality}, we confirm that this results in quality texts, faithful to the original ones.
To audit the RAG provider's corpus, for each $d_i \in \ownerdata$, \ours{} generates an open-ended content-related question $q_i$, and queries $\ragsys{}$.
If $d_i \in D$ (\incase{} case), we expect the retrieval method $\mathcal{R}$ to introduce watermarked content from $d_i$ into the LLM context.
As noted in~\cref{sec:background} and validated in \cref{sec:evaluation}, the robustness of watermarks to text transformations is then sufficient to propagate the traces of the signal to $r_i = \ragsys{}(q_i)$, the final response of the LLM.

 
\paragraph{Boosting a weak signal}
Requiring that each $r_i$ is flagged as watermarked, i.e., has watermark detector p-value $p < \alpha$, would be a strong assumption, as the watermark signal is likely to degrade throughout the RAG pipeline.
However, this is not necessary for \ours{} to be effective.
Instead, following~\citet{radioactivity}, after $n$ queries we compute a \emph{joint} p-value $R = \{r_1, \ldots, r_n\}$, directly corresponding to the null hypothesis \emph{``the data owner's dataset $\ownerdata$ is not in the RAG corpus $D$''}.

This joint p-value can satisfy $p < \alpha$, i.e., reject the null hypothesis, even when individual $r_i$ carry only weak watermark signal, that would individually not reject it. 
To illustrate this, given a desired p-value threshold $\alpha$, \cref{eq:zscore} implies that the required ratio of green tokens in $R$ is at least 
\begin{equation} 
    \gamma' \geq \Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha) \cdot \sqrt{\gamma (1-\gamma) / |R|^{\oplus}} + \gamma, \label{eq:scaling} 
\end{equation}
\input{figures/scaling_tex}

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution CDF, and $|R|^{\oplus}$ denotes the joint total length of the responses in $R$. 
This lower bound decreases quickly as $|R|^{\oplus}$ increases.
For example, in~\cref{fig:scaling} we plot the lower bound as a function of $n$, assuming $\forall i\colon~|r_i|=400$ tokens, \mbox{$\alpha \approx 3 \cdot 10^{-5}$} (z-score of at least $4$), and $\gamma=0.25$, as in our experiments.
We see that for $n \geq 100$, if propagation of the watermark through the RAG pipeline increases the ratio of green tokens by only $1\%$, this is already detectable with high confidence.
This property makes \ours{} viable, satisfying the \emph{Guarantees} requirement, unlike any baseline. 
It also contributes to \emph{Monotonicity}: assuming each ${d}_i$ has a green token ratio of $\gamma''$, which propagated through $\ragsys{}$ reduces to $\gamma' \in (\gamma, \gamma'')$, the p-value for \incase{} cases strictly decreases for more queries.
In~\cref{ssec:eval:desiderata} we experimentally confirm that \ours{} scales consistently and efficiently in the number of queries. 

\paragraph{Test validity and signal leakage}

Another requirement for \emph{Monotonicity} is that the p-value for \outcase{} cases is well-calibrated, ensuring exceedingly rare false positives.
As pointed out in prior work~\citep{kgw,radioactivity}, to ensure independence between tokens scored by the watermark detector, it is necessary to ignore duplicate $h$-grams across $R$.
While this reduces the number of useful tokens per query, our results in~\cref{sec:evaluation} show that it does not affect \ours{} performance.
 
For our statistical test to be valid, we must ensure that the watermark signal can only originate from the use of $\ownerdata$ in $D$, i.e., that it is not otherwise \emph{leaked} to the responses $r_i$.
In particular, we observed that watermarked queries, or queries based on watermarked versions of $d_i$, drastically increase false positives.
To confirm that \ours{} does not suffer from this issue, in~\cref{ssec:eval:desiderata}, we verify that the p-values in \outcase{} cases are distributed in $[0,1]$ roughly as expected with no noticeable decrease in $|R|$.

\paragraph{Scheme choice}  
The key parameter of red-green schemes is $h$, the context width. 
In common applications of LLM watermarks, a low $h$ is not recommended, as it makes the watermark easy to steal via repeated queries~\citep{kgw2,ws}. 
In the context of \problem{}, this is less of a concern, as the data owner never exposes unconstrained query access to watermarked content, as is the case when watermarking LLMs.
Thus, as low $h$ benefits watermark propagation through RAG, we use $h=2$.
This contributes to high \emph{Robustness} of \ours{}, empirically validated in~\cref{ssec:eval:desiderata}.
We discuss other scheme parameters and present ablation studies over each in \cref{ssec:eval:ablations}.

\paragraph{Practicality}
Another aspect making \ours{} robust is that it is inconspicuous, as it operates on natural-looking documents, queries, and responses (confirmed in~\cref{ssec:eval:add_considerations}), through legitimate use of the RAG system---this makes potential attempts by the RAG provider to thwart malicious interactions less effective. 
This differs from baselines such as \ibm{}, which directly reveal the intention to leak the information about $D$.
As a final advantage, we note that, in contrast to all baselines, \ours{} does not require any training or adaptation to \problem{}, as LLM watermarks naturally apply to this task.


\section{Experimental Evaluation}

 
We evaluate the \problem{} baselines (\cref{ssec:setting:baselines}) and \ours{} (\cref{sec:method}) on the \newdata{} dataset (\cref{ssec:setting:data}).
\cref{ssec:eval:main_results} presents our main experiment.
In~\cref{ssec:eval:desiderata} we focus on desiderata from \cref{sec:method}, showing that only \ours{} does not violate them. 
In \cref{ssec:eval:add_considerations} we validate several key assumptions, and in~\cref{ssec:eval:ablations} present additional ablations.
 
\paragraph{Setup} 
Our experimental setup follows \cref{sec:setting}: we use \newdata{} to define two evaluation settings, and in both evaluate \incase{} and \outcase{} cases, i.e., where the data owner's data \emph{is} (resp. \emph{is not}) contained in $D$.
We use $|\ownerdata|=200$, and $|D|=800$ for \newdatae{}, and $|D|=3000$ for \newdatah{}, following from our sampling procedure described in~\cref{app:expdetails}.
We note that \ours{} by design only depends on $|\ownerdata{}|$, but not $|D|$.
We use several LLMs as $\mathcal{M}$: \gptSmall, \haiku, and \llamaMid, and vary the system prompt: we use a short \emph{naive} prompt (\naive{}) with basic RAG instructions, and a longer \emph{defense} (\defended{}) prompt, which models a RAG provider that elaborately instructs the model not to answer any questions about or repeat sections from retrieved documents.
Each experiment is run with $5$ random seeds.
To ensure a controlled setting, in our main experiments, we assume a perfect retrieval system that always retrieves documents from the same group.
In \cref{ssec:eval:add_considerations}, we show that \ours{} works equally well on a practical end-to-end RAG implementation. 
If not specified otherwise (see \cref{ssec:eval:ablations}), the RAG uses $k=3$ shots.
For baselines that utilize auxiliary LMs we use \llamaSmall{}, and to compute the cosine similarity in \sib{} we use paragraph-level \sentenceTf{} with BERTScore aggregation~\citep{bertscore}.
For \ours{}, we use \textit{PositionPRF}~\citep{kgw2}, $h=2$, and $\delta=3.5$, ablating these choices in \cref{sssec:eval:ablation_parameters}. 
We list all prompts used by the RAG provider or the methods, and examples of \newdata{} samples and watermarked documents, in \cref{app:prompts}.

We present our main results in \cref{fig:main_exp}, where we evaluate all \problem{} baselines and \ours{} across several settings, models, prompts, and random seeds.
We make several key observations.

First, all baselines perform somewhat well in the \easy{} setting (no fact redundancy).
However, our extremely simple \facts{} baseline obtains perfect results, outperforming both \ibm{} and \sib{}.
This emphasizes that traditional non-redundant datasets (see~\cref{ssec:setting:data}) fail to capture the complexity of \problem{}, and can provide an incomplete view of the capabilities of \problem{} methods. 
We also observe that already in the \easy{} setting, straightforward system prompt defenses significantly impact both \ibm{} and \sib{}, leading to a noticeable increase in false negatives. 
We further investigate defenses in \cref{ssec:eval:desiderata}.

Moving to the \hard{} setting, all baselines fail to perform consistently well, inducing both false positives and negatives.
This can be directly attributed to the shortcomings of all baselines in handling fact redundancy: \facts{} directly relies on facts, while \sib{} and \ibm{} rely on semantic similarity directly influenced by factual content.
Notably, only \ours{} achieves 100\% accuracy across all settings, models, and system prompts. 
We find that, despite the retrieval of documents with partially overlapping facts, watermarking, unlike our baselines, provides a reliable signal for dataset inference.  
Our results both back up our claims regarding the importance of fact-redundancy for realistic \problem{} evaluation and highlight the potential of watermarking as a promising approach.

As stated in \cref{sec:method}, \problem{} methods should steadily improve with more queries, for us corresponding to $|\ownerdata{}|$ (see \cref{ssec:eval:ablations} for a generalization).
We evaluate this by modifying our above setup to use $|\ownerdata{}| \in \{20, 40, \ldots, 200\}$ in \newdatah{}, presenting the results of \ours{} and \sib{}, as an illustrative example, in \cref{fig:consistency}.
\ours{} improves consistently with $|\ownerdata{}|$, reaching perfect accuracy across all settings for at most $80$ documents.
In contrast, \sib{}, besides never reaching full accuracy, exhibits strongly varying accuracy over $|\ownerdata{}|$, often decreasing despite using more queries. 
This is a consequence of the need to adapt the MIA baselines to the \problem{} setting---we find similar behavior across all baselines, and present an additional study of their decision thresholds in \cref{app:more_results:baselines}.

\paragraph{Guarantees}
In contrast to \emph{all} baselines, \ours{} inherits the guarantees of the watermarking scheme, and can directly compute a p-value for each decision. 
In~\cref{fig:nul_scaling}, we see that p-values rapidly decrease with more queries for \incase{}, but stay consistently close to the expected value for \outcase{}. 
Further, in \cref{tab:p_vals}, we show the \emph{maximum} p-values for \incase{} cases and \emph{minimum} p-values for \outcase{} cases in our main experiment, i.e., closest to a false negative/positive.
We see that all p-values are orders of magnitude from our decision boundary of $\approx 3e^{-5}$ (z-score of $4$), highlighting robustness to Type 1 errors and the fact that LLM watermark signals persist through a RAG pipeline.

\paragraph{Robustness}
Finally, we evaluate the methods' robustness to different settings. 
In particular, we further examine the \defended{} system prompt setting, shown in~\cref{fig:main_exp} to often prevent all baselines from obtaining any useful estimates. 
Notably, both \ibm{} and \sib{} rely on the model's \emph{willingness} to leak information about its context, either explicitly or by regurgitating similar content. 
To validate the effect of \defended{}, we show the longest token-string overlap between the response of $\ragsys{}$ and the \sib{} target document (\cref{fig:maxrep}), observing strictly less overlap with \defended{}, inducing false negatives.
Importantly, this does not prevent watermarks from propagating through the RAG pipeline, resulting in a reliable signal for \ours{}.

We additionally investigate a defense based on \textsc{MemFree} decoding~\citep{memfree}.
Namely, we adapt the decoding of $\ragsys{}$ to strictly prevent $n$-gram overlap with the retrieved documents (using $n=10$ as in~\citet{memfree}), modeling an even stronger attempt by the provider to protect the RAG corpus.
Our results (\cref{app:more_results:memfree}), show that even in this setting, \ours{} achieves full accuracy.

Lastly, we provide ablations for important hyperparameters of \ours{} and the \problem{} setup. 

\paragraph{Watermark parameters} 
\label{sssec:eval:ablation_parameters}

As introduced in \cref{sec:background}, red-green watermarks have $2$ key parameters: the strength $\delta$ and the context size $h$. 
We ablate over their impact on \ours{} using \llamaMid{} as $\llm$ with the \defended{} sysprompt. 
The results for $\delta$ are shown in \cref{fig:watermark_params} (Left).
We observe a \emph{sweet spot} of $\delta \in [2.5,4.5]$ with high accuracy (not the case for $\delta < 2.5$) and the minimal impact on the quality of watermarked documents (not the case for $\delta > 4.5$). 
While accuracy with $200$ queries is $100\%$ in~\cref{fig:watermark_params}, our additional plot in~\cref{app:more_results:method} clearly shows that $\delta>4.5$ negatively affects the query efficiency, on top of text utility.
This range of $\delta \in [2.5,4.5]$ directly aligns with recommendations in prior work~\citep{kgw2}, and supports our choice of $\delta=3.5$ for the main experiments. 

Regarding $h$, as noted in~\cref{sec:method} and prior work, larger values are expected to degrade robustness.
We compare our choice of $h=2$ with $h=4$, noticing a slight decrease in accuracy ($100\%$ on \easy{}, but $80\%$ on \hard{}), confirmed by our plots of query efficiency---full results are deferred to~\cref{app:more_results:method}.

\paragraph{\problem{} parameters}
The \problem{} setting has a wide range of parameters that we so far have not explored. 
First, we ablate over $k$, the number of retrieved documents---our results in~\cref{app:more_results:setting} show that \ours{} performs well independent of this parameter.
Also, in~\cref{app:more_results:setting}, we study the case where only a subset of $\ownerdata{}$ is included in $D$, relaxing the assumptions of \problem{}. While this naturally weakens the watermark signal, our results imply that \ours{} shows some robustness to this setting.

Finally, while we generally assume a single query per document $d_i$, we briefly study the potential of \ours{} to increase its query efficiency by extracting more value from each $d_i$. 
In particular, we introduce a parameter $qpd$ (\emph{queries per document}, $qpd=1$ in the main experiments) and modify the query generation to generate more questions for each $d_i$.
The main results for $qpd \in \{1,2,4,5,10,20,50\}$ are shown in~\cref{fig:watermark_params} (Right), where we plot the average p-value over the total number of \emph{queries} in each case. 
As expected, for high $qpd$, additional queries related to the same $d_i$ bring only marginal value to our decision---this is also confirmed by our plot in~\cref{app:more_results:qpd}, which shows how the effective number of tokens scored by the watermark detector increases slower for high $qpd$, as we encounter many repeated n-grams. 
However, for lower values such as $qpd=4$, reducing the required $|\ownerdata|$ $4$ times, we observe strong results, suggesting a promising avenue for future work.


\section{Related Work}


Closest related works to ours are \citet{sib} and \citet{ibm}, which propose membership inference (MI) for RAG that we adapt to \problem{} in~\cref{sec:setting}, and works that highlight the risk of MI in the related paradigm of in-context learning such as \citep{flocks}. 
Others study broader privacy and security aspects of RAG, such as poisoning to jailbreak the model or exfiltrate data~\citep{poisonedrag,badrag,phantom,goodbad}, concerns similar to \problem{}.

\paragraph{Passive MI/DI}
The problems of membership inference (MI)~\citep{mia,lira} or dataset inference (DI)~\citep{dia, diassl} have been long studied on training data, as opposed to RAG corpora as in this work.
Recent attempts to adapt these methods to LLMs~\citep{llmia1,llmia2,llmia,lldia} cite the challenge of rigorous evaluation, and primarily focus on graybox settings, citing the difficulty of inference attacks in the blackbox setting~\citep{labelonly}, which is what we consider in \problem{}.
Another perspective on the problem of tracing data usage in model training is given by recent works on LLM data contamination~\citep{dekonick,contam}, none of which consider RAG.
 
\paragraph{Proactive MI/DI/model protection}
Another approach to MI/DI is (as in this work) \emph{proactive}, e.g., by watermarking the data~\citep{entruth,domainwm,radioimg}. 
However, not many works study this in the context of LLMs, and only the ones cited above consider RAG.
\citet{activellm} focus on LLMs in a graybox setting, inserting random sequences or applying unicode substitution to trace the data through training.
\citet{radioactivity} find that LLM watermarks propagate through fine-tuning, but in the blackbox setting, \ownerdata{} has to make up 10\% of the fine-tuning corpus for detection.
As confirmed above, \problem{} relaxes this requirement, as RAG is unaffected by $|D|$, and degrades the signal much less than fine-tuning.
Another orthogonal area is watermarking of \emph{models} against model stealing, often via backdoors~\citep{ip1,ip3}.

\paragraph{LLM Watermarking}
Finally, we note that many works study red-green LLM watermarks~\citep{kgw,kgw2,gptwm,semstamp}, but also other approaches such as sampling modification~\citep{stanford,orzamir}, model-based watermarking~\citep{private}, or watermarking in weights~\citep{learnability}.
We note that \ours{} could be combined with many of these, and leave this interesting direction to follow-up work. 
   


\section{Conclusion}

We studied the problem of black-box RAG Dataset Inference (\problem{}), where the goal is to detect unauthorized usage of a dataset in a RAG system.
We formalized the problem, proposed a dataset and a set of baselines, and presented \ours{}, a method based on LLM watermarks, which provides rigorous statistical guarantees.
Our evaluation showed that \ours{} outperforms the baselines, achieving perfect accuracy and high query efficiency and robustness.
This establishes \ours{} as a practical tool that can be directly applied to protect the rights of data owners in current RAG systems.
We hope our work provides a valuable foundation for future work on \problem{}---interesting directions include combining \ours{} with other LLM watermarks, or designing watermarks specifically tailored to \problem{}.
