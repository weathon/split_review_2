\title{Enhancing Near OOD Detection in Prompt Learning: Maximum Gains, Minimal Costs}

\begin{abstract}
Prompt learning has shown to be an efficient and effective fine-tuning method for vision-language models like CLIP. While numerous studies have focused on the generalisation of these models in few-shot classification, their capability in near out-of-distribution (OOD) detection has been overlooked. A few recent works have highlighted the promising performance of prompt learning in far OOD detection. However, the more challenging task of few-shot near OOD detection has not yet been addressed. In this study, we investigate the near OOD detection capabilities of prompt learning models and observe that commonly used OOD scores have limited performance in near OOD detection. To enhance the performance, we propose a fast and simple post-hoc method that complements existing logit-based scores, improving near OOD detection AUROC by up to 11.67\% with minimal computational cost. Our method can be easily applied to any prompt learning model without change in architecture or re-training the models. Comprehensive empirical evaluations across 13 datasets and 8 models demonstrate the effectiveness and adaptability of our method.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Pre-trained vision-language models such as ALIGN~\cite{jia2021scaling} and CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} have shown outstanding visual-text understanding by learning to align image features and textual features of a large-scale image-text dataset via contrastive learning. Consequently, CLIP naturally excels at zero-shot image classification, utilising a class name as natural language text instead of arbitrarily numbered category. For instance, cosine similarity between encoded image feature of an image and encoded text feature of ``a photo of a [CLASS].'' with a class name as ``[CLASS]'' can perform zero-shot classification without requiring any additional classifier. Logits with different class names can then be transformed to probabilities by using the softmax function.

Despite the powerful classification capability of CLIP, a study~\cite{zhou2022learning} has shown that the CLIP's classification accuracy is sensitive to wording of the prompt. For instance, using ``a photo of [CLASS].'' instead of ``a photo of a [CLASS].'' results in more than 5\% decrease in accuracy in Caltech101~\cite{li2004learning} where the only difference in the prompt is a single character ``a''. 
Inspired by these observations,
CoOp~\cite{zhou2022learning} was proposed to optimise the prompt in word embedding space instead of handcrafting the prompt, which has been shown to be an effective method with superior performance with little training cost.

As CoOp learns a single global prompt with few-shot training samples,
one notable drawback of CoOp is that the learned prompt is not generalisable to unseen classes. 
Subsequently, significant research efforts have been made to improve accuracy on samples from unseen classes by generalising the learned prompt~\cite{zhou2022conditional,yao2023visual,zhu2023prompt,khattak2023maple,khattak2023self}. In safety-critical applications, beyond achieving high accuracy, it is crucial for a model to distinguish between samples from unseen classes and those from seen classes. This capability is an equally important research question that has not been adequately explored.

Recently, a few studies have shown promising improvement in out-of-distribution (OOD) detection of CoOp~\cite{bai2023id,miyai2023locoop}. Nevertheless, a few gaps still exist. Firstly, these methods require to train a separate model, which is computationally costly and not applicable to various existing prompt learning models. Secondly, they were only compared with the early works of prompt learning models such as CoOp and CoCoOp~\cite{zhou2022conditional} without being compared with more recent models such as MaPLe~\cite{khattak2023maple} and PromptSRC~\cite{khattak2023self}. More importantly, no study has yet addressed near OOD detection of prompt learning models, which is a more challenging task in which in-distribution (ID) and OOD samples share the same image domain without any overlapping label classes~\cite{ren2021simple,fort2021exploring,yang2021generalized,yang2022openood,zhang2023openood}. 

To address this gap, we propose a post-hoc framework applicable to any prompt learning models improving near OOD detection performance. %We first identify that the fine-tuned prompt learning models tend to make overconfident predictions for near OOD samples due to their fine-tuned prompt. Then, we introduce a simple and effective method to calibrate predictions without requiring any access to OOD samples, architecture change, re-training models, or optimisation.  Our contributions are:
The framework involves using new OOD score functions that complement existing logit-based scores. Our method is simple and effective that does not require any OOD samples, architecture change, or re-training models (see Talbe~\ref{table:auroc improvement}). Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item  We analyse limitation of the existing OOD scores when applied to prompt learning models and introduce a new concept of Empty-Class score that complements existing logit-based scores.
    \item We propose a simple post-hoc framework that can be applied to the existing score functions to improve near OOD detection performance without affecting its classification accuracy.
    \item We extensively validate our method with 13 datasets and 8 prompt learning models, improving near OOD AUROC up to 11.67\%.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Background}

\subsection{Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP)} Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training (CLIP)~\cite{radford2021learning}, a pre-trained vision-language model learned to align 400 million image-text pairs, is renowned for its powerful zero-shot image classification performance. It measures cosine similarity between image feature of an unseen image and text feature of a text prompt formatted as \texttt{``a photo of a [CLASS]''} where \texttt{[CLASS]} is the name of a class in a label space of interest. Formally, given an image $I\in \mathbb{R}^{H\times W \times 3}$ with $H$ being the height and $W$ being the width and a text prompt $T=\texttt{``a photo of a [CLASS]''}$, a classification logit is computed by $\langle\text{Enc}_{\text{I}}(I),\text{Enc}_{\text{T}}(T)\rangle$ where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is cosine similarity, $\text{Enc}_{\text{I}}(\cdot)$ is an image encoder, and $\text{Enc}_{\text{T}}(\cdot)$ is a text encoder. The image encoder can be either ResNet~\cite{he2016deep} or Vision Transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021an}, and the text encoder is Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention}. For the brevity of notation, we omit the notations of the encoders for the remainder of the paper.

\subsection{Context Optimization (CoOp)} Context Optimization (CoOp)~\cite{zhou2022learning} is a pioneering work that applied prevalent prompt learning technique from the natural language processing (NLP) literature to CLIP. It addresses the issue of CLIP's classification being sensitive to the prompt's prefix  (e.g., a large performance gap between when using \texttt{``a photo of a [CLASS]''} and \texttt{``a [CLASS]''}) by optimising the prefix with few-shot samples. CoOp learns $M$ continuous context vectors within word-embedding space $V=\{V_1, V_2, \cdots,V_M\}$ where $V_i \in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the $i^{th}$ vector with $D$ being the word-embedding dimension which is 512 for CLIP. The learnable prompt is formalised as $P=\{V_1, V_2, \cdots, V_M, C\}$ where $C\in \mathbb{R}^D$ is the word-embedding of a class name appended to the context vectors. The classification logit of $i^{th}$ class is then computed by $\langle I, P_i\rangle$ where $P_i$ is the learnable prompt with the $i^{th}$ class name. The probability is estimated by the Softmax function as $p(y=i\vert I,P_i)=\frac{\exp{(\langle I, P_i\rangle/\tau)}}{\sum_{k=1}^K{\exp{(\langle I,P_k\rangle/\tau)}}}$
where $K$ is the total number of classes and $\tau$ is the temperature scale. Cross-entropy loss is then minimised to learn the context vectors. Note that the only learnable parameters that are common among different prompt learning models are the $M$ context vectors.

\subsection{Near Out-Of-Distribution Detection} OOD detections are largely categorised as far OOD detection and near OOD detection based on the distribution shift between an ID test dataset and an OOD dataset along with difficulty of detection~\cite{ren2021simple,fort2021exploring,yang2021generalized,yang2022openood,zhang2023openood}. Far OOD datasets have covariate shift in images (i.e., OOD samples are from different domains), and near OOD datasets which are more challenging to detect involve semantic shift (i.e., OOD samples are drawn from unseen classes). Near OOD detection with CLIP requires computing a score for each sample to determine whether a given image is from in-distribution (ID) or from OOD, which can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    g\left(I;\{P_i\}_{i=1}^K,\alpha\right)=\begin{cases}
    1 & S\left(I;\{P_i\}_{i=1}^K\right) \geq \alpha \\ %\left(I,\{P_i\}_{i=1}^K\right)
    0 & S\left(I;\{P_i\}_{i=1}^K\right)< \alpha
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $g(\cdot)$ is a OOD detector, $\alpha$ is the threshold, and $S(\cdot)$ is a score function. By convention, the ground truth label is 1 for ID samples and 0 for OOD samples. We leverage two logit-based score functions of Energy score~\cite{liu2020energy} and MaxLogit score~\cite{hendrycks2022scaling} that are widely used and directly applicable to fine-tuned prompt learning models~\cite{liu2020energy,hendrycks2022scaling,yang2021generalized,sun2022out,yang2022openood,zhang2023openood,han2022adbench,sun2021react}:
\begin{align}
    &\textbf{Energy Score}\quad S_{Energy}=\tau \log{\sum_{i=1}^K{\exp{(\langle I, P_i\rangle/\tau)}}} \\
    &\textbf{MaxLogit Score}\quad S_{MaxLogit}=\max\limits_i \langle I, P_i\rangle
\end{align}
Energy score can be viewed as an approximation of MaxLogit score when $\tau=1$ as $\max\limits_i \langle I, P_i\rangle\leq \log{\sum_{i=1}^K{\exp{(\langle I, P_i\rangle)}}} \leq \max\limits_i \langle I, P_i\rangle +\log{K}$. 



\section{Method}

In this work, we focus on a near OOD detection problem for prompt learning models of CLIP, which is to detect whether a given image $I_{test}$ is from the ID test dataset $\mathcal{D}^{ID}_{test}$ of $(I^{ID}_{test},y^{ID})$ pairs or a near OOD dataset $\mathcal{D}^{nearOOD}_{test}$ of $(I^{nearOOD}_{test},y^{nearOOD})$ pairs where $y^{ID}\in\{1,\cdots,K\}$ is the ID label with $K$ classes and $y^{nearOOD}\in\{1,\cdots,L\}$ is the OOD with $L$ classes. The ID dataset and the near OOD dataset contain the same types of images (i.e., no covariate shift in images) but have no overlapping classes (i.e., $y^{ID}\cap y^{nearOOD}=\varnothing$). Without loss of generality, we assume that the context vectors $V$ have already been fine-tuned using a prompt learning model with a few-shot ID training dataset.

Energy and MaxLogit scores are two widely-used scores in the OOD detection literature~\cite{liu2020energy,hendrycks2022scaling,yang2021generalized,sun2022out,yang2022openood,zhang2023openood,han2022adbench,sun2021react}. These scores measure a model's confidence in its predictions,  based on the assumption that ID samples typically have higher scores than OOD samples. 
Despite their simplicity and renowned effectiveness in far OOD detection, we find that they are insufficient for near OOD detection, leading to suboptimal performance. This is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:maxlogit density}, where a significant overlap between ID and near OOD scores can be observed.
Our objective is to develop a post-hoc method that improves MaxLogit and Energy scores to enhance near OOD detection for any CLIP-based prompt learning model with minimal learning cost.

We first introduce the concept of \textbf{Empty-Class Score} $S_{EC}=\langle I,V \rangle$, which represents the cosine similarity between the image and the context vectors \emph{without} any class name. This score reflects how well the image aligns with generic, non-class-specific features, indicating the likelihood that the image does not belong to any particular ID class. In practice, Empty-Class Score can be easily obtained by adding an additional label class with no class name (i.e., extending $K$ classes to $K+1$ classes).

We argue that Empty-Class score is a valuable complement to MaxLogit or Energy score. 
Ideally, if a model is confident that the image belongs to a specific ID class, its Energy or MaxLogit score should be large while its Empty-Class score should be small. 
Conversely, if the model is uncertain about an image belonging to any ID class, its Energy or MaxLogit score should be small while its Empty-Class score should be large. This contrast between Energy or MaxLogit score and Empty-Class score can be viewed as an alternative score, which we name \emph{Relative MaxLogit Score} and \emph{Relative Energy Score} defined as:
\begin{align}
    &\textbf{Relative Energy Score}\quad S_{R\text{-}Energy}=S_{Energy}-S_{EC} \\
    &\textbf{Relative MaxLogit Score}\quad S_{R\text{-}MaxLogit}=S_{MaxLogit}-S_{EC}
\end{align}

We illustrate the effectiveness of the proposed scores in Figure~\ref{fig:maxlogit 2d} and Figure~\ref{fig:relative maxlogit without beta} where MaxLogit and Relative MaxLogit scores are plotted at y-axis and Empty-Class scores are plotted at x-axis. Geometrically, subtracting Empty-Class score from MaxLogit score is the same as applying vertical shearing transformation~\cite{lax2007linear} to the samples, which essentially reduces the overlapping area highlighted by shaded boxes. With the original MaxLogit score, the near OOD AUROC is 0.785 which is increased to 0.879 with Relative MaxLogit score. 

In spite of the illustrated large improvement in AUROC, we observe that the performance is still suboptimal. In Figure~\ref{fig:relative maxlogit with beta}, we introduce a coefficient $\beta$ termed \emph{margin scale} that controls the amount of reduction of Empty-Class score from MaxLogit or Energy score. By enlarging the reduction by 2.2 times, the near OOD AUROC is improved from 0.879 to 0.942. With the margin scale, the relative scores can be rewritten as:
\begin{align}
    &\textbf{Relative Energy Score}\quad S_{R\text{-}Energy}=S_{Energy}-\beta \cdot S_{EC} \\
    &\textbf{Relative MaxLogit Score}\quad S_{R\text{-}MaxLogit}=S_{MaxLogit}-\beta \cdot S_{EC}.
\end{align}

A naive way to find the optimal margin scale is to find a scalar that maximises the near OOD AUROC. This, however, requires to have ground-truth near OOD samples, which is not available in most practical scenarios. Thus, we propose to estimate the margin scale by only using \emph{ID training samples}. Initially, MaxLogit score and Empty-Class score in Figure~\ref{fig:maxlogit 2d} exhibit positive correlation. When the near OOD detection AUROC is maximised as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:relative maxlogit with beta}, the correlation is minimised. Thus, we formulate this problem as finding the margin scale that minimises the correlation between MaxLogit or Energy score and Empty-Class score.






\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma:bivariate}
    Given $N$ scalar observations $\{\hat{x}_i\}_{i=1}^N$ and $\{\hat{y}_i\}_{i=1}^N$, we define two variables $x=\hat{x}$ and $y=\hat{y}-\beta \cdot \hat{x}$. The scale parameter $\beta$ that zeros out the covariance of two variables (i.e., the off-diagonals of a covariance matrix) which is approximated by maximum likelihood estimation is:
    \begin{equation}
        \beta =\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N{(\hat{x}_i-\mu_{\hat{x}})(\hat{y}_i-\mu_{\hat{y}})}}{\sum_{i=1}^N{(\hat{x}_i-\mu_{\hat{x}})^2}}
        \label{eq:scale}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mu_{\hat{x}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\hat{x}_i$ and $\mu_{\hat{y}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\hat{y}_i$.
\end{lemma}
We propose to estimate the margin scale by fitting a bivariate normal distribution with  maximum likelihood estimation. This scale estimation needs to be conducted only once after training finishes.
Figure~\ref{fig:scale} shows the approximation of the margin scale in red dotted lines. Even with a small number of ID training samples, we find that the estimation well approximate the margin scale close to the optimal point. Overall, the benefits of our approach are: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Adding an additional class of Empty-Class is cost-effective that requires no additional forward pass.
    \item The approach can be applied to any prompt learning model as long as it learns the context vectors.
    \item The post-hoc process does not require any training, being applicable to fine-tuned prompt learning models and fast in terms of computation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Vision-Language Models} Vision-language models have significantly advanced in recent years, bridging the gap between visual and textual data. Early approaches, such as image captioning models~\cite{karpathy2015deep,wang2016image,you2016image}, typically used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to extract visual features and recurrent neural networks (RNNs) to generate descriptive text. The advent of transformers~\cite{vaswani2017attention} handling long-range dependencies more effectively and contrastive learning~\cite{oord2018representation} revolutionised this field. Notably, ALIGN~\cite{jia2021scaling}, CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning}, and LiT~\cite{zhai2022lit} leveraged a contrastive learning framework that aligns image and text embeddings in a multimodal space, allowing for zero-shot learning capabilities and impressive generalisation to unseen tasks and datasets. In this work, we leverage the powerful vision-language model CLIP and extend its near OOD capability.

\paragraph{CLIP-based Prompt Learning} Despite the remarkable zero-shot performance of CLIP, CLIP shows inherently unstable classification accuracy that varies by wording of prompt. To mitigate this issue, CoOp~\cite{zhou2022learning} was proposed to optimise a prompt in word embedding space, leveraging prompt learning from the NLP literature. CoCoOp~\cite{zhou2022conditional} identified that CoOp has limited generalisation and proposed to condition image features to the learnable prompt. Subsequently, many studies have proposed different techniques to improve the generalisation~\cite{yao2023visual,zhu2023prompt,khattak2023maple,khattak2023self}. While its generalisation has been largely improved, its OOD detection has been overlooked. LoCoOp~\cite{miyai2023locoop} proposed a OOD regularisation to improve OOD detection performance. Nevertheless,  no study has addressed near OOD detection of prompt learning models.

\paragraph{OOD Detection} 
An early work of \citet{hendrycks2017baseline} utilised the maximum softmax probability (MSP) as a score to identify OOD samples. Another notable work is Out-of-DIstribution detector for Neural networks (ODIN)~\cite{liang2018enhancing} which extends MSP by introducing temperature scaling and input pre-processing to enhance separation of the scores from ID samples and OOD samples. Similar to ODIN, Mahalanobis~\cite{lee2018simple} score also uses input pre-processing in addition to measuring distance in feature space. Delving into a more challenging task of near OOD detection, several studies analysed benchmarks of pre-trained networks in near OOD detection~\cite{yang2023full,yang2022openood,zhang2023openood,fort2021exploring}, and different training methods and score functions were proposed for near OOD detection~\cite{ren2021simple,winkens2020contrastive}. Although significant advancements have been made in OOD detection for classifier-based neural networks, there is still room for improvement in detecting near OOD detection in vision-language models like CLIP, particularly in prompt learning models.

\subsection{Experimental Settings}
\paragraph{Datasets} Following previous works of CLIP-based prompt learning models~\cite{zhou2022conditional,khattak2023maple,yao2023visual,zhu2023prompt,khattak2023maple,khattak2023self,miyai2023locoop}, we use 11 publicly available datasets of ImageNet~\cite{deng2009imagenet}, Caltech101~\cite{li2004learning}, OxfordPets~\cite{parkhi2012cats}, StanfordCars~\cite{kraus2320133d}, Flowers102~\cite{nilsback2008automated}, Food101~\cite{bossard2014food}, , FGVCAircraft~\cite{maji2013fine}, SUN397~\cite{xiao2010sun}, DTD~\cite{climpoi2014describing}, EuroSAT~\cite{helber2019eurosat}, and UCF101~\cite{soomro2012ucf101}. A common task of these works involves training models on half of the label classes (e.g., base classes) and evaluating them on the other half classes (e.g., new classes) to measure base-to-new generalisation. We reframe this task as a near OOD detection problem. Specifically, the models trained on base classes are tested with a dataset where half of the samples belong to the base classes (ID) and the other half to new classes (near OOD). In addition, we include CIFAR10~\cite{krizhevsky2009learning} and CIFAR100~\cite{krizhevsky2009learning}, which are near OOD detection benchmarks~\cite{ren2021simple,fort2021exploring,yang2021generalized,yang2022openood,zhang2023openood}. \paragraph{Models} We use 8 prompt learning models: CoOp~\cite{zhou2022learning}, CoCoOp~\cite{zhou2022conditional}, IVLP~\cite{khattak2023maple}, KgCoOp~\cite{yao2023visual}, ProGrad~\cite{zhu2023prompt}, MaPLe~\cite{khattak2023maple}, PromptSRC~\cite{khattak2023self}, and LoCoOp~\cite{miyai2023locoop}, all of which uses ViT-B/16~\cite{dosovitskiy2021an} for the visual encoder and Transformer~\cite{vaswani2017attention} for the text encoder. For each model, we use MaxLogit score, Energy score, and MCM~\cite{ming2022delving} score as baselines. MCM is the SOTA score for CLIP. As our focus is on the post-hoc method directly applicable to fine-tuned prompt learning models without requiring near OOD samples, we exclude other scoring methods that require OOD samples, modifications of training procedures, or architecture changes in models.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Comparison with MCM} As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}, the proposed relative scores outperform MCM in AUROC and FPR95 averaged across 13 datasets. We notice that MCM outperforms these scores in some datasets. We analyse this difference from the perspective of dataset distance. As MCM is the SOTA OOD detection score for far OOD detection, MCM is expected to outperform as the dataset distance between an ID test dataset and a near OOD dataset increases. We validate this assumption by measuring the dataset distance by Optimal Transport Dataset Distance (OTDD)~\cite{alvarez2020geometric} which is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset distance}. 

For each of MCM and relative scores, we find a dataset in which each score outperforms and measure the dataset distance. While the relative scores outperform across a wide range of distance, it excels when the distance decreases. MCM, on the other hand, tends to outperform as the distance increases. Nevertheless, the relative scores outperform in the majority of cases, resulting in superior average performance.

\paragraph{Application to MCM} A straightforward extension of our method is the application of post hoc process to the MCM score (i.e., $S_{MCM}-\beta \cdot S_{EC}$). The differences in AUROC and FPR95 with the original and relative MCM are presented in Table~\ref{table:mcm auroc} and Table~\ref{table:mcm fpr}. 
While it improves performance with MCM on most datasets, some datasets exhibit a decline in performance. This decline is due to the lack of a positive correlation between MCM and the Empty-Class score, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration}. Figure~\ref{fig:maxlogit mcm} further demonstrates the differing relationships between the score and the Empty-Class score. This discrepancy primarily arises from the softmax normalisation, where MCM only considers the relative magnitude of logits between classes, allowing non-maximum logits to unexpectedly change the relationship.

\paragraph{Application to Far OOD Detection} Another extension of our method is far OOD detection. Following the OOD literature of CLIP~\cite{ming2022delving,miyai2023locoop,wang2023clipn,jiang2024negative}, we use ImageNet as an ID dataset and iNaturalist~\cite{van2018the}, SUN~\cite{xiao2010sun}, Places~\cite{zhou2018places}, and Texture~\cite{climpoi2014describing} as far OOD datasets. We leverage the fine-tuned models in our experiments and show AUROC results in Table~\ref{table:ood auroc} and FPR95 results in Table~\ref{table:ood fpr}. The post hoc framework is effective for both MaxLogit score and energy score, improving AUROC and FPR95 in 126 out of 128 evaluations where the relative scores outperform MCM in a half of evaluations. This aligns with the near OOD results, being effective across various models and OOD datasets. The limited performance also aligns with our observation in Figure~\ref{fig:dataset distance} that MCM is more likely to outperform when the dataset distance increases.



\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we address few-shot near OOD detection of CLIP-based prompt learning models. %By analysing that the existing prompt learning models are prone to overconfident predictions for near OOD samples, 
To enhance existing logit-based scores, we propose a simple and fast post-hoc method applicable to any proompt learning model. Without changing training procedures of the existing models nor compromising classification accuracy, our method effectively enhances near OOD AUROC and lowers FPR95 for 8 recent prompt learning models across 13 real-world datasets. One limitation is that its effectiveness decreases for far OOD detection. Nonetheless, our method is more effective in a more challenging task of near OOD detection.
