\title{Learning Differential Pyramid Representation for Tone Mapping}

\begin{abstract}
\label{abs}
Previous tone mapping methods mainly focus on how to enhance tones in low-resolution images and recover details using the high-frequent components extracted from the input image. These methods typically rely on traditional feature pyramids to artificially extract high-frequency components, such as Laplacian and Gaussian pyramids with handcrafted kernels. However, traditional handcrafted features struggle to effectively capture the high-frequency components in HDR images, resulting in excessive smoothing and loss of detail in the output image. To mitigate the above issue, we introduce a learnable Differential Pyramid Representation Network (DPRNet). Based on the learnable differential pyramid, our DPRNet can capture detailed textures and structures, which is crucial for high-quality tone mapping recovery. In addition, to achieve global consistency and local contrast harmonization, we design a global tone perception module and a local tone tuning module that ensure the consistency of global tuning and the accuracy of local tuning, respectively. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, improving PSNR by 2.58 dB in the HDR+ dataset and 3.31 dB in the HDRI Haven dataset respectively compared with the second-best method. Notably, our method exhibits the best generalization ability in the non-homologous image and video tone mapping operation. \textit{We provide an anonymous online demo at \href{https://xxxxxx2024.io/DPRNet/}{https://xxxxxx2024.io/DPRNet/}.}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\label{intro}
High dynamic range (HDR) imaging has revolutionized the way we capture and represent real-world scenes, preserving rich luminance details across varied intensity ranges. However, the challenge of effectively rendering these HDR images on conventional low dynamic range (LDR) display devices remains due to inherent hardware constraints. In this context, tone mapping (TM) algorithms emerge as a critical bridge, narrowing the dynamic range disparity between HDR content and LDR screens.

Since performing tone mapping operations at low resolutions can effectively reduce computational costs, previous methods \cite{Yang_SepLUT_2022, Zeng_lut_2020, Zhang_CLUT} typically process tone mapping on a multiple-downsampled low-resolution version of the input image and then recover the image resolution by interpolation or other upsampling. 
However, manipulating tone mapping on low-resolution images inevitably damages image details and quality.
Lacking fusion of high-frequency information leads to the output image looking blurry or distorted.
To mitigate this issue, some methods \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023, Yang_Cheng_2023} have introduced reversible pyramid decomposition \cite{Burt_Adelson_1983} to extract multi-scale high-frequency components and fuse them with the low-resolution tone mapped image to preserve and recover high-frequency details. 
However, extracting high-frequency components from HDR images remains challenging due to their extensive dynamic range and complex texture structure. The strong contrast and rich lighting variations of HDR images make it difficult for traditional handcrafted features to effectively capture these details \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023, Yang_SepLUT_2022}. 
Therefore, tone mapping methods based on the pyramid decomposition, including Laplacian \cite{paris2011local, liang2014improved, sumiya2021gaussian} and Gaussian pyramids \cite{krawczyk2005perceptual, eilertsen2017comparative, eilertsen2015real}, often lead to over-smoothing and detail loss in the output images. 
Fig.~\ref{examples}(a) and Fig.~\ref{lp_ldp} show that the Laplacian pyramid (LP) employed by existing methods is still difficult to effectively extract high frequency from HDR images, leading to severe loss of detail, especially in regions with strong contrast and lighting variations.

Additionally, we observe that customized software and mobile devices, such as Photoshop, often include both global and local tone mapping operations \cite{ashbrook2014photomatix, adobe2000adobe}. However, existing learning-based methods \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023, Zeng_lut_2020, kim2020global, Yang_SepLUT_2022} typically perform either global or local tone mapping, depending on their operational patterns. 
Although these methods effectively control and adjust the overall tones of the image, they struggle to maintain consistency while refining details. Visualization results in Fig.~\ref{examples}(a) show that global tone mapping methods like CLUT and SepLUT fail to enhance local regions adequately. Therefore, an ideal tone mapping method should integrate global and local TMO into a unified framework.
To address these problems, we introduce a learnable Differential Pyramid Representation Network (DPRNet). We find that features within the differential pyramid remain stable under scale transformations, rotations, and lighting variations, and features constructed through filtering and differencing operations effectively capture image edges, corners, and textures. 
Therefore, we propose the learnable differential pyramid module to effectively extract high-frequency components.
Compared to traditional pyramid decompositions with predefined parameters, the LDP can more flexibly and effectively capture complex details in HDR images, an effective solution to the detail loss issue in tone mapping results. To achieve global consistency and local contrast harmonization, we embed global tone perception and local tone tuning modules within the DPRNet. These modules aim to ensure perceptual consistency and finely tune the local tones of each patch. In addition, we gradually refine the high-frequency components with iterative detail enhancement modules. 

Extensive experiments demonstrate that our DPRNet has superior tone mapping capabilities and can generate more accurate tone mapping results. As shown in Fig.~\ref{examples}, one can observe that our results can effectively preserve details with better visualization, which proves the effectiveness of our method. Our contributions are summarized as follows: 
\begin{itemize}
\item[$\bullet$] We present an innovative end-to-end network, the Differential Pyramid Representation Network (DPRNet), capable of detail recovery and the simultaneous manipulation of global and local tone mappings.
\item[$\bullet$] We introduce the Learnable Differential Pyramid module for adaptive high-frequency extraction, coupled with an Iterative Detail Enhancement module to progressively restore image resolution and detail.
\item[$\bullet$] Our proposed DPRNet outperforms previous models on multiple metrics on different test sets, improving PSNR by \textbf{2.58 dB} in the HDR+ and \textbf{3.31 dB} in the HDRI Haven dataset respectively compared with the second-best method. The tone mapping results generated by DPRNet look visually pleasing and DPRNet has the best generalization ability in the non-homologous dataset.
\end{itemize}





\section{Related works}

\label{Rework}
\subsection{Learning-based tone mapping methods}
Recent advancements in tone mapping have leveraged deep learning techniques to address the nonlinear mapping from HDR to LDR images. Hou et al. \cite{hou2017deep} applied CNNs to tone mapping tasks, establishing a foundation for subsequent research. He et al. \cite{He_CSRnet_2020} developed a conditional sequential retouching network for effective image retouching. Similarly, Cao et al. \cite{Cao_2020}, Rana et al. \cite{Rana_DEEPTMO_2020}, and Panetta et al. \cite{Panetta_2021} explored generative adversarial networks (GANs) for pixel-precise mapping. Despite these efforts, issues such as halo effects, noise, and local area processing remain challenging. To combat this, Hu et al. \cite{Hu_2022} combined tone mapping with denoising, incorporating a discrete cosine transform module for noise removal and improved image quality. Zhang et al. \cite{zhang2019deep} manipulated tone in the HSV color space, which significantly reduced halos and preserved detail. Although these learning-based methods have achieved remarkable results, most of them are either global or local mappings, struggling to achieve satisfactory tone mapping results.

\subsection{3D LUT-based enhancement methods}
In pursuit of computational efficiency and performance, emerging tone mapping methods employed 3D LUT to render image brightness and color. Zeng et al. \cite{Zeng_lut_2020} developed an adaptive 3D LUTs prediction network that merges multiple foundational 3D LUTs. Zhang et al. \cite{Zhang_CLUT} presented a compressed representation of 3D LUTs, reducing parameters. Wang et al. \cite{wang2021real} studied pixel-level fusion based on 3D LUTs, extending the method to a spatially aware variant. Yang et al. \cite{yang2024taming} proposed effective ICELUT for extremely efficient edge inference. Yang et al. \cite{yang2022adaint} introduced the AdaInt mechanism, which adaptively learns non-uniform sampling intervals in the 3D color space for more flexible sampling point allocation. Furthermore, Yang et al. \cite{Yang_SepLUT_2022} proposed SepLUT, addressing the shortcomings of 1D LUTs in color component interaction while alleviating the memory consumption issues of 3D LUTs. Despite these efforts, these methods necessitate an initial down-sampling step to reduce the network computation, and in the case of 4K images, even need 16 times down-sampling. As a result, this leads to loss of image details and degradation of image quality. Zhang et al. \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023} attempted to use the Laplacian pyramid to compensate for the loss of high-frequency detail, but effectively capturing the high-frequency component of HDR images remains a challenge.


\section{Methods}

\label{method}
\subsection{Motivation}
Existing works have mainly focused on how to enhance tones in multi-downsampled low-resolution input images, and very little work has been dedicated to focusing on the high-frequency details hidden in HDR images. 
Although the pyramid decomposition-based paradigm has been widely used in various image processing tasks \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023, Liang_lptn_2021, yang2023two}. 
However, traditional feature pyramids \cite{Burt_Adelson_1983} using handcrafted kernels often struggle to effectively capture high-frequency detail in HDR images, limited by complex lighting environments. This can result in over-smoothing or loss of detail in the output image.
\textit{Therefore, how to effectively and adaptively extract the high-frequency components from the input HDR image is a critical step in tone mapping.} 
On the other hand, global tone mapping \cite{He_CSRnet_2020, Zeng_lut_2020} usually ensures overall consistency in luminance, while local tone mapping \cite{Boschetti_localhe_2010, liang2024low} preserves fine detail and improves local contrast in challenging lighting conditions. 
However, a single tone mapping method makes it difficult to maintain consistency while ensuring local contrast, and few works have successfully integrated global and local tone mapping strategies into a unified learning framework.
\textit{Therefore, how to achieve global consistency and local contrast harmonization in a single network is extremely challenging.}

\subsection{Framework Overview}
The pipeline of the proposed DPRNet is shown in Fig. \ref{model_arch}. 
Our framework is composed of four components: learnable differential pyramid (LDP), iterative detail enhancement (IDE), global tone perception (GTP), and local tone tuning (LTT).
Given an HDR image $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$, we aim to generate an LDR image $\mathbf{T}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W \times 3}$. 
To prevent loss of details of TM results, we first propose Learnable Differential Pyramids for extracting multi-scale high-frequency components from HDR images and manipulate tone mapping in downsampled low-resolution images $\mathbf{L}_{3}$ to optimize the computational costs.
Subsequently, we employ the GTP to manipulate the global tone on downsampled images.
To refine the local tone, we built the LTT module to refine the brightness and contrast of different regions in a patch manner.
The combination of GTP and LTT allows our framework to obtain harmonious image tones while adaptively enhancing local contrast.
Finally, we utilize IDE and extracted high-frequency components to progressively refine image details. 

\subsection{Learnable Differential Pyramid}
Compared with regular images, HDR images have a wider luminance range, more complex high-frequency details, and higher color accuracy and consistency. The traditional pyramid decomposition makes it difficult to effectively extract the high-frequency components, resulting in downsampled images that are unable to effectively recover the high-frequency details. 
Therefore, we introduce a Learnable Differential Pyramid module, inspired by scale-invariant feature transform \cite{lowe1999object, wang2012improved}, for adaptively extracting high-frequency information from HDR images.
The differential pyramid exhibits two key properties \cite{wang2012improved, lowe1999object} that make it suitable for tone mapping tasks. 
1) The features within the differential pyramid remain stable under scale transformations, rotations, and illumination variations, enabling effective tone mapping across varying conditions. 
2) The differential pyramid, constructed via filtering and difference operations, efficiently captures image edges, corners, and textures, which are crucial for preserving details. 

In detail, for the input image $\mathbf{X}$, we first apply a $3\times3$ convolution to obtain the initial feature map $\mathbf{F}_0$. Subsequently, we generate a lower-resolution feature map $\mathbf{F}_1$ through four consecutive convolutions and a maximum pooling operation. We then compute the difference between four convolutions to obtain three sets of differential features, which are concatenated together and fed into a residual network to generate the first high-frequency feature $\mathbf{H}_0$. This process iterates, with differential feature maps generated through successive convolutions and pooling, which are concatenated and fed into the residual network to predict high-frequency components. Through $2$ iterations, we obtain the complete differential pyramid $\mathbf{X}_{HF} = [H_0, H_1, H_2]$ that contains multi-scale high-frequency features adaptively learned from the input HDR image, tapering resolutions from $H \times W$ to $\frac{H}{4} \times \frac{W}{4}$.

\subsection{Global and Local Tone Mapping}

Global tone mapping typically involves adjusting global brightness and contrast across the entire image, and it is generally treated as a pixel-independent operation within the image signal processor pipeline. This operation can be efficiently approximated using a multi-layer perceptron \cite{taud2018multilayer, He_CSRnet_2020}.
As illustrated in Fig. \ref{model_arch}, we begin by obtaining the lowest-resolution low-frequency component $\mathbf{L}_3$ of size $\frac{H}{8} \times \frac{W}{8}$ through successive Gaussian downsampling operations. The component captures global illumination information with low-resolution inputs, reducing computational costs while facilitating efficient global tone adjustment. 
Next, the $\mathbf{L}_3$ component is input to the global tone perception module, which globally modulates the tones. The GTP module is composed of three full convolutions, conditional network \cite{He_CSRnet_2020}, and ReLU activation functions. Specifically, conditional network consists of three convolutions, ReLU activation, and an average pooling. The output of this network is a conditioning vector that modulates the intermediate features from the base network. This conditioning vector is used to globally adjust the tones of the features, generating the lowest-resolution global mapping image $\mathbf{L}^G_3$.

Although global tone mapping yields visually consistent, it lacks modification of local details as well as contrast. Especially in areas with highlights or shadows, the single global mapping can result in some parts of the image appearing as halo artifacts or dead white/black, which does not render light and shadow details well.
For this reason, we introduce a local tone tuning module, which enhances details in localized areas for better visual effects and details.
The LTT includes a lightweight CNN network and several 3D LUTs.
The CNN network is utilized to analyze the image context and predict the weights for multiple 3D LUTs. 
The CNN network consists of three convolutional layers with LeakyReLU and InstanceNorm, an average pooling layer, and a reshape operation.
Multiple 3D LUTs are responsible for the local tone mapping of each patch for local detail refinement.

Specifically, we first interpolate $\mathbf{L}^G_3$ to the fixed-resolution version (e.g., $80 \times 60$) and convert the feature maps into a compact vector representation $\mathbf{E} \in N^2 \times d$ via CNN network.
The $E$ represents the local attributes of the $N^2$ patches of the input image and $d$ indicates the number of sampling points. That is, we divide the image into $N^2$ patches.\
Then, we feed the $1 \times d$ local feature vectors of each patch into the 3D LUT prediction network, where the 3D LUT parameters are predicted by two fully connected layers. For the whole image, we obtain $N^2$ 3D LUT parameters for adjusting the tone of each patch.
Following the completion of 3D LUT prediction for each region, due to the limited number of sampling points, we implement LUT mapping through trilinear interpolation. To address the edge discontinuity problem caused by chunking, we obtain the mapping values at the boundary by bilinear interpolation of the LUT mappings of the adjacent regions. Finally, we obtain the low-resolution mapped image $\mathbf{T}_3$ through the LTT.

\subsection{Refinement on High-Frequency Component}
To achieve faithful reconstruction in tone mapping manipulation, we propose an iterative mask strategy to refine HF components $\mathbf{X}_{HF} = [H_0, H_1, H_{2}]$. Specifically, we first up-sample the low-frequency mapped image $\mathbf{T}_{3}$ and concatenate it with HF component $\mathbf{H}_{2}$, then fed it into a residual network to predict the mask $\mathbf{M}_{2}$. This mask allows pixel-by-pixel refinement of the HF component, which is subsequently added to the up-sampling $\mathbf{T}_{3}$ to generate the reconstructed result of the current layer $\mathbf{T}_{2}$. The operations at the $n_{th}$ layer can be formulated as:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}_{n-1} = \mathrm{Res}(\mathrm{Concat}(\mathrm{Up}(\mathbf{T}_n), \mathbf{H}_{n-1}))
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{T}_{n-1} = \mathrm{Up}(T_n) + (\mathbf{H}_{n-1} \times \mathbf{M}_{n-1}),
\end{equation}
where $\textrm{Res}$ and $\textrm{Up}(\cdot)$ denote the residual network, up-sampling operation, respectively. This iterative process continues, and the mapped image $\mathbf{T}_{n-1}$ is fed to the next layer to further refine the HF component $\mathbf{H}_{n-1}$, and obtain LDR images $\mathbf{T}_{n-2}$. Through $n-1$ iterations, we progressively refine the high-frequency components, ultimately obtaining a high-quality LDR image $\mathbf{T}$. The residual network consists of a $3 \times 3$ convolution, a ReLU activation function, three residual blocks, and a cascade of $3 \times 3$ convolution. 

\subsection{Loss Functions}
To maintain the accuracy of the reconstructed image, we jointly optimize the reconstruction with pixel-wise $L_{\textrm{Re}}$ and structural similarity $L_{\text{\textrm{ssim}}}$, high-frequency $L_{\mathrm{HF}}$, and perceptual loss $L_{\mathrm{p}}$. To summarize, the complete objective of our proposed model is combined as follows:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{total}} = \alpha \cdot L_{\mathrm{Re}} + \beta \cdot L_{\mathrm{ssim}} + \gamma \cdot L_{\mathrm{HF}}  + \eta \cdot L_{\mathrm{p}},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$, and $\eta$ are the corresponding weight coefficients.

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.} We evaluate model performance in five dimensions: pixel accuracy, structural integrity, mapping quality, perceptual quality, and color fidelity. We report traditional PSNR and SSIM metrics on the RGB channel to evaluate the reconstruction accuracy. We also employ TMQI \cite{Yeganeh_Wang_2013} and LPIPS \cite{zhang2018unreasonable} to evaluate image mapping quality and perceptual quality respectively. 
In addition, we use the CIELAB color space \cite{zhang1996spatial} ($\Delta E$) to evaluate the difference in color dimensions of the reconstructed images.

\textbf{Implementation details.} We implement our model with Pytorch on the RTX 3090 GPU platform. We have set the batch size to 1 (original 4K resolution) or 4 (480P). For optimization, we use AdamW optimizer \cite{Xiao_2021} with $\beta_{1}=0.9$, $\beta_{2}=0.99$. The learning rate is initialized to $1\times 10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Comparison with State-of-the-Arts} 
\textbf{Quantitative Comparison.} We quantitatively compare the proposed method with state-of-the-art tone mapping methods in Tab. \ref{hdrplus_tab}, Tab. \ref{hdrHaven_tab}, and Tab. \ref{tmqi}, in HDRNet \cite{Gharbi_hdrnet_2017}, UPE \cite{Wang_UPE_2019}, DeepLPF \cite{moran2020deeplpf}, CSRNet \cite{He_CSRnet_2020}, LUT \cite{Zeng_lut_2020}, CLUT \cite{Rana_DEEPTMO_2020}, sLUT \cite{wang2021real}, LPTN \cite{Liang_lptn_2021}, Sep-LUT \cite{Yang_SepLUT_2022}, LLF-LUT \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023}, and CoTF \cite{li2024cotf}. 
Note that the input images considered in our evaluation are 16-bit uncompressed images in the CIE-XYZ color space, while the target images are 8-bit compressed images in the sRGB color space. 
Among these methods, DeepLPF, LPTN, and CSRNet are pixel-level methods based on ResNet and U-Net backbone, while HDRNet and UPE belong to patch-level methods, and 3DLUT, sLUT, Sep-LUT, LLF-LUT and CoTF are the image-level methods. Our method embraces the merits of these methods by designing joint global and local tone mapping. We follow the results in the published paper \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023} and add more comparative methods, using publicly available source code and recommended configurations for training.

Our method significantly outperforms the SOTA methods LLF-LUT (NeurIPS 2023 \cite{Zhang_llfLUT_2023}) and CoTF (CVPR 2024 \cite{li2024cotf}), improving PSNR by \textbf{2.58 dB} in the HDR+ and \textbf{3.31 dB} in the HDRI Haven dataset respectively. Tab. \ref{hdrplus_tab} shows the quantitative comparison results on the HDR+ dataset at two different resolutions. Notably, our method exhibits a significant performance advantage over all competing methods on both resolutions, highlighted in red bold across all metrics. Benefiting from the LDP and IDE, this advantage becomes more pronounced at the more challenging 4K resolution. Compared with the LLF-LUT, our DPRNet achieves notable improvements by 2.58 dB, 0.068, 0.088, and 0.71 in PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, and $\Delta E$, respectively, which demonstrates the robustness of our method to high-resolution images. Moreover, our proposed tiny version also outperforms the SOTA method by 1.81 dB on the HDR+ dataset. Similarly, evaluation on the HDRI Haven dataset presents an even more pronounced advantage, with an improvement of more than 3.3 dB at 4K resolution, especially in the LPIPS metric, which is far superior to all existing methods, thanks to our finely designed tone mapping framework.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}
\textbf{Effectiveness of Specific Modules.} 
To validate the effectiveness of the GTP and LTT modules, we remove the two modules individually. We remove both GTP and LTT modules from the proposed DPRNet as the baseline network (w/o TM). Tab. \ref{modules} presents the results of different variants. When both GTP and LTT are removed, the model performance shows severe degradation, by 3.33 dB PSNR. Meanwhile, the PSNR decreases by more than 1 dB regardless of either removing GTP or LTT alone, which indicates that a single GTP or LTT is incapable of reconstructing satisfactory tone mapping results. This corroborates the efficacy of our joint GTP and LTT strategy. As illustrated in Fig. \ref{com_tm}, the integration of GTP with LTT yields promising visual quality in both global and local enhancement in this challenging case. These results provide compelling evidence that our proposed joint GTP and LTT framework outperforms existing tone mapping methods.

To verify the effectiveness of the learnable differential pyramid, we replace our LDP module with single down-sampling (w/o PD), the Gaussian pyramid (GP), and the Laplacian pyramid (LP), respectively. Tab. \ref{modules} shows the results for different variants. When LDP is introduced, the results are improved by 3.1 dB. This evidence highlights the success of the learnable differential pyramid module. Meanwhile, the results show that GP or LP is not able to effectively extract the HF information of HDR images, resulting in the presence of significant degradation. Furthermore, when we use an iterative detail enhancement to refine the high-frequency (compared to w/o IDE), the result is further improved by 1.3 dB. This finding demonstrates that the IDE helps to produce a finer high-frequency texture.
\\
\textbf{Selection of the Number of Levels.} 
We validate the influence of the number of pyramid levels $n$. As shown in Tab. \ref{modules}, the model achieves the best performance on all tested resolutions when $n=4$. When a larger number of levels ($n\geq5$) result in a significant decline in performance. This is because when $n$ is larger and the number of downsamples is more, the model fails to reconstruct the high frequencies efficiently, resulting in performance degradation.
\\
\textbf{Ablation Study on Loss Functions.} 
To test the effect of the loss function, we set up different variants and modified the loss function combination step by step. Tab. \ref{tab:loss} shows Variants \#1 and \#2 improve performance by about 0.2 dB. In particular, Variants \#3 shows that the addition of $L_\textrm{p}$ loss results in $0.86$ PSNR higher than the baseline. Variants \#4 show a 0.13 dB performance improvement by adding high-frequency loss $L_\textrm{HF}$.

\section{Conclusion}

\label{conc}
This paper proposes a learnable Differential Pyramid Representation Network (DPRNet), a comprehensive framework capable of recovering details and manipulating global and local tone mappings. We propose a learnable differential pyramid module to capture effectively multi-scale high-frequency components from input HDR images. To achieve global consistency and local contrast harmonization, we embed global tone perception and local tone tuning modules within the DPRNet. Furthermore, we propose iterative detail enhancement to gradually improve the image details by progressively refining the extracted high-frequency components. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method significantly outperforms state-of-the-art methods, improving PSNR by 2.58 dB in the HDR+ and 3.31 dB in the HDRI Haven dataset respectively compared with the second-best method. In addition, our method has the best generalization ability in non-homologous video and image tone mapping operations.
