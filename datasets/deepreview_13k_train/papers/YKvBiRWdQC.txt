\title{The Overcooked Generalisation Challenge}

\begin{abstract}

We introduce the Overcooked Generalisation Challenge (OGC) --  the first benchmark to study agents' zero-shot cooperation abilities when faced with novel partners \textit{and} levels in the Overcooked-AI environment.
This perspective starkly contrasts a large body of previous work that has trained and evaluated cooperating agents only on the same level, failing to capture generalisation abilities required for real-world human-AI cooperation.
Our challenge interfaces with state-of-the-art dual curriculum design (DCD) methods to generate auto-curricula for training general agents in Overcooked.
It is the first cooperative multi-agent environment specially designed for DCD methods and, consequently, the first benchmarked with state-of-the-art methods.
It is fully GPU-accelerated, built on the DCD benchmark suite \texttt{minimax}, and freely available under an open-source license: \url{https://git.hcics.simtech.uni-stuttgart.de/public-projects/OGC}.
We show that current DCD algorithms struggle to produce useful policies in this novel challenge, even if combined with recent network architectures that were designed for scalability and generalisability.
The OGC pushes the boundaries of real-world human-AI cooperation by enabling the research community to study the impact of generalisation on cooperating agents.






\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\label{sec:introduction}
Developing intelligent agents capable of collaborating with humans remains a key challenge in artificial intelligence (AI) research \cite{Dafoe2020Open}.
Computational agents that team up with humans \citep{Carroll2019On,Oneill2020HumanAutonomy} to work towards common goals and achieve joint objectives promise to vastly expand human abilities \citep{Engelbart1962AugmentingHI} and enable numerous applications, e.g. in
human-robot interaction \citep{Choudhury2020On}.
Recent years have seen considerable advances in understanding human cooperative behaviour \cite{Rand2013HumanCooperation, Vizmathy2024BetterTogether}, computational modelling of cooperation \cite{Nikolaidis2013Human,Sadigh2016PlanningFA,yang2022optimal,Ding2024Cooperative} and human behaviour \citep{Choudhury2020On}, as well as advances in the development of computational methods for human-AI cooperation \citep{Hu2020Other,strouse2021collaborating,Lupu2021Trajectory,zhao2023maximum,yu2023learning}.
In parallel, several benchmarks have been proposed to foster the development and evaluation of these methods \citep{samvelyan2019starcraft,Carroll2019On,Bard2020Hanabi,Karol2020Google}.
In particular, the Overcooked-AI environment \citep{Carroll2019On}, used for evaluating zero-shot human-AI coordination, has gained significant traction and became a key benchmark in the field
\citep{Choudhury2020On,strouse2021collaborating,Knott2021Evaluating,Fontaine2021Importance,yang2022optimal,zhao2023maximum,yu2023learning}.

    \label{fig:problemSetting}

A common thread among all these benchmarks is their focus on evaluating the cooperative abilities of agents \textit{in-distribution}, meaning they are tested in the same environment in which they were trained~\citep{Carroll2019On,strouse2021collaborating,yang2022optimal,zhao2023maximum,yu2023learning}.
This approach is severely limited:
First, it restricts the range of strategies that a learning agent can encounter.
Consider the \textit{Coordination Ring} layout from Overcooked-AI shown in Figure \ref{fig:problemSetting} as an example.
While the layout requires agents %
to adapt to the partners' preferred walking directions, the amount of interaction is mostly controlled by a few simple choices: going left or right and which objects to pass over the counter.
Previous work has demonstrated that an agent can effectively cooperate in such a layout by being trained with a relatively small, diverse population of partners \citep{strouse2021collaborating,zhao2023maximum,yu2023learning}.
Similarly to how reinforcement learning (RL) agents can remember thousands of levels\footnote{Note that the Overcooked community often refers to a concrete kitchen in the environment as a \textit{layout}, while the UED community refers to instances of an under-specified environment a a \textit{level}. These terms refer to a similar idea but stem from different communities. We use these terms interchangeably when discussing specific environment configurations in Overcooked.} in generalisation benchmarks \citep{cobbe2019quantifying} and overfit to their training environments \citep{zhang2018dissection}, these agents are likely to overfit to their partner population, as well as the respective layouts. %
Second, training and testing agents on the same layout does not represent real-world human-AI collaboration.
This would require generally capable artificial agents that can successfully collaborate in different physical configurations, including those they have not been trained on.

\looseness=-1
To address these limitations, we introduce the Overcooked Generalisation Challenge (OGC) -- a zero-shot cooperation benchmark that challenges agents to cooperate in novel layouts \textit{and} with unknown agents.
To obtain a suitable training distribution of levels, we apply techniques introduced in research on generalisation to the cooperative multi-agent setting, including procedural content generation \citep{cobbe2019quantifying}, unsupervised environment design (UED) \citep{Dennis2020Emergent}, and dual curriculum design (DCD) \citep{jiang2021replayguided}, while we provide hand-designed testing levels.
Additionally, to assess zero-shot cooperation on testing levels, we provide populations of diverse testing agents for them, similar to how previous works have evaluated standard zero-shot cooperation \citep{strouse2021collaborating,Hu2020Other,zhao2023maximum,yu2023learning}.
To the best of our knowledge our work is the first to combine DCD techniques with a cooperative multi-agent RL environment and thus bridges the gap between two previously unrelated research areas;
it tests the impact of generalisation on human-AI coordination and the ability of DCD algorithms to design optimal auto-curricula for cooperating agents.
Furthermore, since historically UED benchmark environments have been often based on simple navigation tasks \citep{cobbe2019quantifying,Cobbe2019Leveraging,Wang2019POET,Dennis2020Emergent,jiang2021replayguided}, our OGC also can be used to evaluate and compare DCD algorithms in a setting that requires additional interaction with objects and a partner agent for achieving a joint goal. 
We benchmark several DCD algorithms and network architectures on our challenge %
and find that only PAIRED \citep{Dennis2020Emergent}, together with a policy that incorporates a soft Mixture-of-Experts (SoftMoE) module \citep{obandoceron2024mixtures}, has some success at generalising to the testing levels and outperforms competitive baselines, including robust PLR \citep{Jiang2021Prioritized,jiang2021replayguided} and ACCEL \citep{Holder2022Evolving}. 
This is surprising as previous research has highlighted the performance benefits of robust PLR. %
Taken together, our contribution is three-fold:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.5cm,itemsep=0.25cm,topsep=0pt]
    \item We introduce the Overcooked Generalisation Challenge -- a novel benchmark challenge in which agents are asked to cooperate with novel partners in previously unseen layouts.
    \item We provide OvercookedUED -- an open-source environment that can be used with state-of-the-art DCD algorithms and that is integrated into \texttt{minimax} \citep{jiang2023minimax}, taking full advantage of the hardware acceleration provided by JAX.
    \item We benchmark our environment by training agents with common DCD algorithms \citep{Dennis2020Emergent,jiang2021replayguided,Holder2022Evolving} 
    and show that current DCD algorithms struggle on the challenge even if we employ recent network architectures \citep{smith2023simplified,obandoceron2024mixtures}. %
    Furthermore, we assess zero-shot cooperation performance with a population of diverse partners to link zero-shot cooperation and generalisation and show that as policies become more generally capable, they achieve better zero-shot cooperation. 
\end{enumerate}


\section{Related work}

\subsection{Generalisation in reinforcement learning}
\looseness=-1
It is well known that RL agents fail to generalise to new environments \citep{zhang2018dissection,zhang2018study,Farebrother2018GeneralizationAR,Nichol2018Gotta,cobbe2019quantifying}.
Consequently, several benchmarks have been proposed to evaluate the generalisation capabilities of RL agents \citep{Nichol2018Gotta,cobbe2019quantifying,Cobbe2019Leveraging}.
To study this generalisation gap, hand-picked \citep{Nichol2018Gotta} or procedurally generated \citep{Cobbe2019Leveraging,cobbe2019quantifying,Fontaine2021Importance} sets of training and testing levels have been used.
These works have shown that RL agents can memorise large numbers of levels during training \citep{cobbe2019quantifying} even if techniques to increase generalisation are applied \citep{zhang2018study}.
They have also shown that regularisation can improve generalisation \citep{zhang2018study,cobbe2019quantifying}.
While many of these works have studied individual agents, generalisation has also been studied in multi-agent settings \citep{Carion2019Structured, chen2023quantifying,samvelyan2023maestro}.
A common finding is that agents must experience sufficiently diverse training data to generalise at all \citep{Cobbe2019Leveraging}. %
One established approach uses domain randomisation \citep[DR]{Jakobi1997Evolutionary}.
This approach has been used to successfully detect cars \citep{Khirodkar2018DomainRF}, achieve indoor fly \citep{Sadeghi2017CAD2RL}, agile locomotion \citep{Tan2018Sim} among others \citep{Tobin2017Domain,Tremblay2018TrainingDN}. %
It has been shown, however, that DR produces many uninformative samples through this random sampling procedure \citep{khirodkar2021adversarial}. 
This can lead to the learner's inability to generalise to challenging configurations of these parameters \citep{Dennis2020Emergent}.

\looseness=-1
More recently, generalisation in RL has been viewed through the lens of unsupervised environment design \citep[UED]{Dennis2020Emergent,mediratta2023stabilizing,Wenjun2023Generalization} that aims to combat the disadvantages of DR by introducing generated auto-curricula \citep{Graves2017Automated}.
UED tackles the issue of creating a useful distribution of training tasks and environments by generating increasingly complex environments that facilitate continued agent learning.
It does so by adapting the free parameters of an under-specified environment to the capabilities of the learning agent.
The UED methods discussed here fall under the class of Dual Curriculum Design \citep[DCD]{jiang2021replayguided} algorithms.
DCD requires three parts: 1) a level generator, 2) a curator and 3) a learning agent.
The level generator provides new training levels while the curator picks the appropriate training levels from new and past ones.
Some popular UED algorithms based on Prioritised Level Replay \citep[PLR]{Jiang2021Prioritized}, including robust PLR \citep[PLR$^\perp$]{jiang2021replayguided}, parallel PLR \citep[PLR$^{\perp,\parallel}$]{jiang2023minimax}, MAESTRO \citep{samvelyan2023maestro} and ReMiDi \citep{beukman2024refining}, combine a random generator with a capable curator. 
Other methods like (population) PAIRED \citep{Dennis2020Emergent}, ACCEL \citep{Holder2022Evolving}, and parallel ACCEL \citep[ACCEL$^{\parallel}$]{jiang2023minimax} are environment generators or editors that do not curate.
Methods like Replay-Enhanced PAIRED \citep[REPAIRED]{jiang2021replayguided} combine advantages from both approaches.
While the development of these DCD methods has been steady, they have mostly been explored in simple environments, see Table \ref{tab:envs}:

\paragraph{Platform games}
Platform games are popular for studying generalisation in RL.
In these games, agents move from left to right in a simple 2D world while dodging enemies and obstacles to reach a certain goal.
Examples include CoinRun(-Platforms) \citep{cobbe2019quantifying} and the Sonic benchmark \citep{Farebrother2018GeneralizationAR}.
Solvable platform games are easy to generate procedurally and perform relatively well.
However, they are often limited by their action and interaction possibilities and the kind of strategies required to solve them, i.e. they often only require going from left to right and dodging objects.

\paragraph{Mazes}
Both the original work on quantifying generalisation \citep{zhang2018study,cobbe2019quantifying} as well as more recent work on DCD algorithms \citep{Dennis2020Emergent,jiang2021replayguided,Holder2022Evolving,jiang2023minimax,Wenjun2023Generalization,beukman2024refining} make use of mazes.
Like platform games, mazes can be generated easily while ensuring they are solvable.
They have thus become a common benchmark for DCD algorithms and are part of recently released DCD benchmarks \citep{jiang2023minimax,coward2024JaxUED}.
While they are usually used to compare algorithms,
they are limited to a single agent, with limited options to interact with the environment and other agents.
This becomes apparent when considering that current algorithms can solve most mazes in only a few ten thousand gradient updates \citep{jiang2023minimax}. 

\paragraph{Bipedal walker}
Bipedal walker-based UED environments \citep{Wang2019POET} have seen their fair share of adaption \citep{Wang2020EnhancedPO, Holder2022Evolving}.
They combine similar advantages and disadvantages as the maze environments, i.e. they are easy to manipulate and make solvable, with a continuous control task and dense rewards.

\paragraph{Other}
\looseness=-1
Other notable UED environments include CarRacing \citep{jiang2021replayguided}, a race car environment in which an agent is tasked with following evolving sets of racing tracks, XLand \citep{OpenEndedLearningTeam2021Open,Bauer2023Human}, a closed-source multi-task universe for generating single- and multi-agent tasks and environments, XLand-MiniGrid, an open-source variant of XLand \citep{nikulin2023xlandminigrid} based on MiniGrid \citep{MinigridMiniworld23}, LaserTag \citep{Lanctot2017Unified,samvelyan2023maestro}, a competitive 2D laser tag game, and MultiCarRacing \citep{Schwarting2021DeepLC,samvelyan2023maestro}, which extends CarRacing to multiple competing agents.
CarRacing and XLand-MiniGrid share properties similar to those of earlier examples of UED environments.
XLand, LaserTag, and MultiCarRacing are the only environments with multi-agent aspects, but they are not open-source. 
XLand neither focuses on the multi-agent setting nor zero-shot coordination and its impact on human-AI cooperation. 
LaserTag and MultiCarRacing are most closely related to our work as they have been used to study multi-agent UED in the competitive setting in \citep{samvelyan2023maestro}.
Opposed to \citep{samvelyan2023maestro}, we study the cooperative setting with different game-theoretic dynamics \citep{Carroll2019On}.

\paragraph{GPU-accelerated implementations}

These greatly improve the running speed of experiments but also require special environment implementations, 
compare \citep{jiang2023minimax}.

\subsection{Human-AI cooperation in Overcooked}
\looseness=-1
Overcooked-AI \citep{Carroll2019On} has emerged as one of the most important benchmarks for human-AI cooperation \citep{Fontaine2021Importance,strouse2021collaborating,zhao2023maximum,yu2023learning,Liu2024LLM,tan2024true,ying2024goma,li2024verco,Fosong2024Learning,rustagi2024mitigating,franzmeyer2024select}.
It has been used for zero-shot cooperation \citep{strouse2021collaborating,zhao2023maximum,yu2023learning}, language model-based cooperative agents \citep{Liu2024LLM,tan2024true}, as well as human modelling in cooperation \citep{yang2022optimal}.
The environment is fully cooperative and involves two agents in cooking and delivering a soup of onions to earn a combined reward.
Commonly, research in Overcooked focuses on training and evaluating agents on one of several layouts that test different aspects of cooperation.
While the original work researches onion-only layouts \citep{Carroll2019On}, later works have included additional ingredients to study biases and preferences in cooperating agents \citep{yu2023learning}.
For this reason, works can be hard to compare, and the amount of variation in layouts has only grown, see for instance \citep{Liu2024LLM,tan2024true,ying2024goma,li2024verco,Fosong2024Learning,rustagi2024mitigating}.
Overcooked is only one recent example of work on cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) environments
including matrix penalty games \citep{Claus1998Dynamics,Kapetanakis2002Reinforcement}, multi-agent particle-world environments \citep{Lowe2017Multi,Mordatch2017Emergence}, StarCraft \citep{samvelyan2019starcraft}, Hanabi \citep{Bard2020Hanabi}, and Google Research Football \citep{Karol2020Google}.
While each of these works has its own merit, Overcooked is unique in its focus on researching and testing cooperative zero-shot human-AI cooperation and, thus, is the only one applicable to our research interest.
Most closely related to our work is \citep{Fontaine2021Importance} in which the authors use procedurally generated Overcooked layouts to evaluate human-robot interaction.
However, they have not trained a general Overcooked agent or evaluated RL agents in human-AI cooperation.
Our work is the first to explore the impact of cross-level generalisation for zero-shot cooperation and the first to offer the necessary tools to do so.



\section{Preliminaries}

\looseness=-1
The cooperative multi-agent UED setting is formalised as a \textit{decentralised under-specified partially observable Markov decision process} (Dec-UPOMDP) with shared rewards.
Such a Dec-UPOMDP is defined as $\mathcal{M} = \langle \mathcal{N}, A, \Omega, \Theta, \mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{M}}, \mathcal{T}^{\mathcal{M}}, O^{\mathcal{M}}, \mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{M}}, \gamma \rangle$ 
in which 
$\mathcal{N}$ is the set of agents with cardinality $n$, $\Omega$ is a set of observations and $\mathcal{S}^{\mathcal{M}}$ constitutes the set of true states in the environment.
Partial observations $o^i \in \Omega$ are obtained using the observation function $O: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{N} \rightarrow \Omega$ by agent $i \in \mathcal{N}$. %
Following \cite{jiang2021replayguided}, a \textit{level} $\mathcal{M}_\theta$ is defined as a fully-specified environment given some parameters $\theta \in \Theta$.
In it, agents each pick an action $a_i \in A$ simultaneously to produce a joint action $\mathbf{a} = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and observe a shared immediate reward $R(s, \mathbf{a})$.
Then,
the environment transitions to the next state according to a transition function $\mathcal{T}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A}^{1} \times ... \times \mathcal{A}^{n} \times \Theta \rightarrow \Delta(\mathcal{S})$ where $\Delta(\mathcal{S})$ refers to the space of distributions over $\mathcal{S}$.
$\gamma \in [0, 1)$ specifies the discount factor.
Agents learn a policy $\pi$.
The joint policy $\boldsymbol{\pi}$ together with the discounted return $R_t = \sum^\infty_{i=0} \gamma^i r_{t+1}$ induce a joint action value function $Q^{\boldsymbol{\pi}} = \mathbb{E}_{s_{t+1:\infty}, \mathbf{a}_{t+1:\infty}}[R_t|s_t,\mathbf{a}_t]$. %
The definition extends a Dec-POMDP \citep{Oliehoek2016,wu2021coordinated} with the free parameters of the environment $\Theta$, analogously to previous works \citep{Dennis2020Emergent,jiang2021replayguided,samvelyan2023maestro}.
Our definition differs from \citep{samvelyan2023maestro} in terms of the shared rewards and general-sum nature.
Within our Dec-UPOMDP, we perform UED to train a policy over a distribution of fully specified environments that enable optimal learning.
This is facilitated by obtaining an \textit{environment policy} $\Lambda$ \citep{Dennis2020Emergent} that specifies a sequence of environment parameters $\Theta^T$ for the given policy that is to be trained.
How $\Lambda$ is obtained depends on the DCD method. %
For example, in OvercookedUED, $\Theta$ represents the possible positions of walls, pots, serving spots, agent starting locations, and onion and bowl piles which is adjusted by $\Lambda$ over the duration of training.



\section{The Overcooked Generalisation Challenge}

\label{sec:ogc}
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{assets/ComponentOverview.pdf}
    \caption{An overview of the Overcooked Generalisation Challenge and how it is typically used in a DCD algorithm. It features components for teacher-based methods like PAIRED \citep{Dennis2020Emergent} via a UED environment and edit-based methods like ACCEL \citep{Holder2022Evolving} via mutator functions of existing layouts.}
    \label{fig:overview}
\end{figure}
\looseness=-1
To evaluate the impact of generalisation on cooperative agents and, ultimately, human-AI collaboration, we introduce the \textit{Overcooked Generalisation Challenge} (OGC, see Figure \ref{fig:overview}). 
It extends previous work by evaluating the cooperative abilities out-of-distribution, i.e., not only with novel partner agents but also on previously unseen testing levels.
The OGC uses procedural content generation to generate training levels.
As levels are generated on the fly, training against a diverse population of experts on each layout for zero-shot capabilities is practically infeasible.
This setup allows the training and testing on diverse levels. It is, therefore, more closely aligned with real-world human-AI collaboration that is not limited to one specific physical environment.
In contrast to existing UED environments that are limited to a single agent and based on simple navigation tasks, the OGC focuses on the interaction of multiple agents in a complex, cooperative task. 
Compared to single-agent environments, multi-agent environments are inherently more complex because the agents interact with each other, as well as with 
the physical environment.
More specifically, in the OGC, two agents are tasked with cooking a soup together in the five original layouts of Overcooked-AI \citep{Carroll2019On} (see Figure \ref{fig:evallayouts}), 
but without having encountered them during training.
The original five layouts have been %
designed to test and explore different kinds of cooperation and thus form suitable out-of-distribution test levels.
UED expands the range of possible layouts and behaviours, further challenging layout generation and agents' generalisability.
Specifically, an environment designer (or teacher agent) must account for agents collaborating when trying to generate layouts that are at the forefront of the abilities of the student agent.
Such teacher agents interact with the challenge by designing layouts either from scratch through interacting with \textit{OvercookedUED} - a novel environment for creating Overcooked levels - or by alternating existing layouts through the \textit{Overcooked mutator}.
This enables the challenge to be used by a diverse set of algorithms.
\subsection{Components of the challenge}
\looseness=-1
The OGC comprises several components that enable its integration with DCD algorithms (see Figure \ref{fig:overview}).
Most importantly, it features an Overcooked environment capable of running different levels fast and in parallel, as well as components to design 
layouts and zero-shot cooperation testing populations. %

\paragraph{Overcooked}
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{assets/EvaluationLayoutsWLegend.pdf}
    \caption{We study the five evaluation layouts proposed in \citep{Carroll2019On}: Cramped Room, Asymmetric Advantages, Coordination Ring, Forced Coordination, and Counter Circuit.}
    \label{fig:evallayouts}
\end{figure}
\looseness=-1
OGC builds on the Overcooked environment.
We adapted the version introduced in the JaxMARL project \citep{flair2023jaxmarl}, %
keeping most features consistent with the original implementation.
This includes action and observation spaces, i.e. the set of actions is given by \{\texttt{left}, \texttt{right}, \texttt{up}, \texttt{down}, \texttt{interact}, \texttt{stay}\} and observations are encoded as a stack of 26 boolean masks. %
We extend the JaxMARL implementation by adding back features from the original work \citep{Carroll2019On}, i.e. reward shaping 
and the option for receiving partial observations based on hand-crafted features.
Additionally, we parallelise different levels during rollouts by using padding and one-hot encoding of the positions of environment elements.
While this facilitates fast parallel rollouts, it requires the introduction of a maximum height $h$ and width $w$. %

\paragraph{OvercookedUED}
\looseness=-1
As a full UED environment, OvercookedUED generates random layouts %
to be used by algorithms that do not specify a designer, such as PLR.
As for \citep{jiang2023minimax}, OvercookedUED does not check whether a random layout is solvable and leaves the task of identifying suitable training layouts to the DCD method.
A random layout always features one or two onion piles, bowl piles, pots and serving locations, and two agents.
The number of walls placed is configurable, but the environment requires a border wall on the edge.
For algorithms that make use of a designer to create layouts (PAIRED, etc.), OvercookedUED provides a UED environment (see Figure \ref{fig:overview}).
This environment allows a designer policy 
to take design steps to parameterise the underspecified MDP. %
The action space of the teacher agent consists of the total number of cells in the $h \times w$ grid.
The first design steps place walls on the grid, later steps place objects, piles, and agents.
In case of a conflict, elements are placed randomly on 
free cells. %

\paragraph{Overcooked mutator}
\looseness=-1
Some DCD algorithms (ACCEL, etc.) rely on alternating existing layouts by mutating them.  
OvercookedUED supports layout mutation through five basic operations: (1) converting a random wall to a free space and vice versa, (2) moving goals, (3) pots, (4) plate piles, and (5) onion piles.
Given a layout, our \textit{mutator} randomly samples $n$ operations and applies them.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\looseness=-1
Our work evaluates agents in two ways.
First, by comparing performance on out-of-distribution Overcooked layouts, we can asses the capabilities of a DCD algorithm to generate generally capable agents and, second, by using a population of hold-out FCP agents, we can asses zero-shot cooperation on the evaluation layouts.

\paragraph{Zero-shot cooperation}
Overcooked was originally designed for building tools for human-AI cooperation.
Within it the use of zero-shot cooperation with a diverse population
has become a proxy for assessing the abilities of an agent to coordinate with humans.
Similarly, we provide trained fictitious co-play \citep[FCP]{strouse2021collaborating} populations with 24 agents per layout. 
Additionally, we include tools for evaluating agents against these populations. 
FCP allows testing against diverse agents with different
preferences and skills. 

\paragraph{Metrics}
We use three evaluation metrics in our challenge:
1) mean episode reward and 2) mean environment solved rate in the evaluation layouts, similar to previous work \citep{jiang2023minimax}, and 3) performance with the FCP population as described above.
The environment is solved if an agent pair delivers more than one soup.
This ensures that random agents usually do not solve an environment and distinguishes agents that make no or few deliveries from ones that do so consistently across many episodes.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{DCD methods and OvercookedUED}
\looseness=-1
Previous work \citep{jiang2021replayguided} has found that PLR$^\perp$ tends to outperform the other here-tested algorithms in navigation-based tasks.
Our more challenging environment suggests that this might not always be the case.
In our preliminary analysis, PAIRED outperformed other DCD methods.
Compared to mazes, car racing, or walker environments with fewer moving pieces, Overcooked layouts are more complex to design, requiring the designer to place multiple objects in relation to each other and the agents. %
This requires a capable generator 
and suggests that simple navigation-based environments 
used to benchmark DCD in UED algorithms do not allow full performance evaluation.
As such, OvercookedUED can be an important part of evaluating DCD algorithms.
We envision that general Overcooked agents should be evaluated in scenarios that are difficult for self-play agents using our benchmark. 
These include zero-shot cooperation with strongly-biased agents \citep{yu2023learning} 
in Coordination Ring 
(see Section \ref{sec:introduction})
and %
Asymmetric Advantages as described in \citep{Ruhdorfer2023Into} and for which we provide the tools.
\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\looseness=-1
Despite its many advantages, our challenge and evaluations also have two limitations.
First, we artificially restricted the maximum size of the layouts to allow the environment to be both fully observable as in \cite{Carroll2019On} and parseable by CNN-based feature encoders.
Future work should focus on more natural representations of the whole scene, e.g. using graphs or item embeddings.
While we included a partial observation that could theoretically be computed independently of size, similar to the vector-based observation used for behaviour cloning agents in \citep{Carroll2019On}, batching across layouts in OvercookedUED still requires the layouts to be scaled to the same height and width.
Second, while our challenge allows us to study zero-shot coordination via generalising across layouts, reasoning about other agents \citep{Rabinowitz2018Machine,gandhi2021baby,bortoletto24_aaai, bara2023towards, bortoletto24Limits} might be equally important to achieve zero-shot cooperation capabilities on unknown layouts.
This is plausible given that humans can reason about the mental states of other agents via Theory of Mind \citep{Premack1978-PREDTC}, as well as the physical configuration of the space in which they operate.
Future work could thus explore reasoning about other agents in previously unexplored environments.


\section{Conclusion}

\looseness=-1
We have presented the Overcooked Generalisation Challenge (OGC) -- 
a generalisation challenge focusing on (zero-shot) cooperation in MARL in out-of-distribution test levels.
Our challenge is the first unsupervised design MARL environment and is significantly more challenging than previous environments commonly used in UED and DCD research.
In addition to using the challenge in UED research, we have shown how the OGC can be used in future research on human-AI collaboration as a zero-shot cooperation benchmark for general agents.
That is, our challenge establishes a link between generalisation and zero-shot coordination.
Our work is the first to provide the research community with the tools to train and evaluate agents capable of coordinating in previously unknown physical spaces and with novel partners.
