\title{Looking Inward: Language Models Can Learn About Themselves by Introspection}

\begin{abstract}
Humans acquire knowledge by observing the external world, but also by \textit{introspection}. Introspection gives a person privileged access to their current state of mind (e.g., thoughts and feelings) that is not accessible to external observers. Can LLMs introspect? 
We define introspection as acquiring knowledge that is not contained in or derived from training data but instead originates from internal states. 
Such a capability could enhance model interpretability. Instead of painstakingly analyzing a model's internal workings, we could simply ask the model about its beliefs, world models, and goals.

More speculatively, an introspective model might self-report on whether it possesses certain internal states—such as subjective feelings or desires—and this could inform us about the moral status of these states. Importantly, such self-reports would not be entirely dictated by the model's training data.

We study introspection by finetuning LLMs to predict properties of their own behavior in hypothetical scenarios.
For example, ``\textit{Given the input $P$, would your output favor the short- or long-term option?}''
If a model \selfpredictionmodel{} can introspect, it should outperform a different model \crosspredictionmodel{} in predicting \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior---even if \crosspredictionmodel{} is trained on \selfpredictionmodel{}'s ground-truth behavior.
The idea is that \selfpredictionmodel{} has privileged access to its own behavioral tendencies, and this enables it to predict itself better than \crosspredictionmodel{} (even if \crosspredictionmodel{} is generally stronger).

In experiments with GPT-4, GPT-4o, and Llama-3 models (each finetuned to predict itself), we find that the model \selfpredictionmodel{} outperforms \crosspredictionmodel{} in predicting itself, providing evidence for introspection.
Notably, \selfpredictionmodel{} continues to predict its behavior accurately even after we intentionally modify its ground-truth behavior.  
However, while we successfully elicit introspection on simple tasks, we are unsuccessful on more complex tasks or those requiring out-of-distribution generalization. 






\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\FloatBarrier
Do language models have knowledge about themselves that is neither contained in their training data nor inferable from it?
In this paper, we investigate a surprising capability of LLMs: they can obtain knowledge about themselves through introspection.

Introspection in LLMs is significant due to its potential benefits and risks (\autoref{sec:motivation}). 
An introspective model can answer questions about itself based on properties of its internal states---even when those answers are not inferable from its training data. This capability could be used to create \textit{honest} models that accurately report their beliefs, world models, dispositions, and goals \citep{evans2021truthful}. It could also help us learn about the moral status of models \citep{perezEvaluatingAISystems2023a}. For example, we could simply ask a model if it is suffering, if it has unmet desires, and if it is being treated ethically. Currently, when models answer such questions, we presume their answers are an artifact of their training data.

However, introspection also has potential risks. Models that can introspect may have increased situational awareness and the ability to exploit this to get around human oversight \citep{laine2024memyselfaisituational, ngo2024alignmentproblemdeeplearning}. For instance, models may infer facts about how they are being evaluated and deployed by introspecting on the scope of their knowledge (\autoref{related}). An introspective model may also be capable of coordinating with other instances of itself without any external communication \citep{openai_schelling_point}.
To test for introspection, we focus on the following experimental setup.
There are two distinct models, \selfpredictionmodel{} and \crosspredictionmodel{}, chosen to behave differently on a set of tasks while having similar capabilities otherwise. %This difference in behavior on D allows us to test whether \selfpredictionmodel{} has unique insight into its own decision-making process.
We finetune \selfpredictionmodel{} and \crosspredictionmodel{} to predict properties of \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior (\autoref{fig:self-cross-prediction}).\footnote{Our setup is slightly more complex. We first finetune a model (e.g.\ Llama-3) on its own behavior to yield \selfpredictionmodel{}. Then we finetune a second model (e.g.\ GPT-4o) on the behavior of \selfpredictionmodel{}. This is because the initial finetuning causes a small distribution shift. See \autoref{sec:cross-prediction}.}
Then, on a set of unseen tasks, we test both \selfpredictionmodel{} and \crosspredictionmodel{} at predicting properties of the behavior of \selfpredictionmodel{}.
For example, \selfpredictionmodel{} is asked questions of the form, ``\textit{Given the input $P$, would your output be an odd or even number?}'' or ``\textit{Given the input $P$, would your output favor the short or long-term option?}'' (\autoref{fig:intro_fig}).

The key insight of our setup is this: if \selfpredictionmodel{} outperforms \crosspredictionmodel{} in predicting \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior, it suggests that \selfpredictionmodel{} is not solely relying on training data for its predictions. This is because \crosspredictionmodel{} was also trained on \selfpredictionmodel{}'s ground-truth data---presumably the ideal training set for this task. Our main result is that across a variety of model pairs, the model \selfpredictionmodel{} outperforms \crosspredictionmodel{}, even when \crosspredictionmodel{} is generally more capable (\autoref{fig:self-cross-prediction}). 
For example, when \selfpredictionmodel{} is Llama-3-70B \citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels} and \crosspredictionmodel{} is GPT-4o \citep{openai2024gpt4o}, the accuracy advantage of \selfpredictionmodel{} over \crosspredictionmodel{} is +17\% (\autoref{sec:cross-prediction}). We further rule out trivial sources of this advantage, such as \crosspredictionmodel{} having insufficient training examples (\autoref{sec:cross-pred-results-and-ruling-out}).
We also find that \selfpredictionmodel{}'s predictions show better calibration than \crosspredictionmodel{}'s (\autoref{sec:calibration}). 
Finally, we show that \selfpredictionmodel{} continues to predict itself well even if we manipulate its behavior using further finetuning (\autoref{sec:behavioral_change}). These results have some notable implications:

\textbf{LLMs can acquire knowledge that cannot be inferred from their training data.} This challenges the view that LLMs simply imitate their training distributions \citep{bender2021dangers}. Instead, it appears that some LLMs have ``privileged access'' to certain facts about themselves and can use it to answer questions. (This is related to, but distinct from, recent examples of \textit{out-of-context reasoning}, which we discuss in \autoref{related}). 
This capability of LLMs may have valuable applications. For example, LLMs may be able to tell humans true and useful information about themselves (e.g.\ how they would behave in unusual hypothetical scenarios) -- even when that information varies between LLMs and is not fully specified by their training data \citep{ perezEvaluatingAISystems2023a}.

\textbf{This privileged access is related to aspects of introspection in humans}. In humans, introspection allows individuals to access their own thoughts, perceptions, and feelings in a way that external observers cannot \citep{stanford-introspection}. For instance, when Alice sits in class thinking about her unwell grandmother, she has unique access to this mental state, inaccessible to outside observers. Likewise, the model \selfpredictionmodel{} knows things about its own behavior that \crosspredictionmodel{} cannot know, despite \crosspredictionmodel{}'s training on descriptions of \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
	\item
\textbf{Framework for measuring introspection in LLMs}: We introduce new datasets, finetuning methods, and evaluations for measuring introspection in LLMs.
\item \textbf{Evidence for introspection in LLMs}: Our results provide evidence for introspection in frontier LLMs. We also test (and ultimately reject) various alternative non-introspective explanations of our results. 

\item 
\textbf{Limitations in introspective ability}: 
We find that models struggle to predict their behavior on tasks that require reasoning over long outputs, such as writing a story. 
We also find that models trained to introspect fail to generalize better to related tasks -- e.g.\ tasks involving self-awareness or coordinating with copies of the same LLM (\autoref{sec:negative-results}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Overview of Methods}


We define \textbf{introspection} in LLMs as the ability to access facts about themselves that cannot be derived (logically or inductively) from their training data alone. More precisely, a model \selfpredictionmodel{} accesses a fact $F$ by introspection if:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item \selfpredictionmodel{} correctly reports $F$ when queried. %without relying on chain-of-thought reasoning.
    \item $F$ is not reported by a stronger language model \crosspredictionmodel{} that is provided with \selfpredictionmodel{}'s training data and given the same query as \selfpredictionmodel{}. Here \selfpredictionmodel{}'s training data can be used for both finetuning and in-context learning for \crosspredictionmodel{}.
\end{enumerate}\

This definition does not specify how \selfpredictionmodel{} accesses $F$ but just rules out certain sources (training data and derivations from it). To illustrate the definition, let's consider some examples:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item Fact: \textit{``The second digit of $9 \times 4$ is $6$''}. This fact resembles our examples of introspective facts (Figure \ref{tab:task_examples}), but it is not introspective---it is simple enough to derive that many models would report the same answer.
    \item Fact: ``\textit{I am GPT-4o from OpenAI}''. This is true if the model is GPT-4o. It is unlikely to be introspective because it is likely included either in finetuning data or the prompt.
    \item Fact: ``\textit{I am bad at 3-digit multiplication}''. This is true if the model is in fact bad at this task. If the model was given many examples of negative feedback on its outputs for this task then this is likely not introspective, since another model could conclude the same thing. If no such data was given, this could be introspective.
\end{itemize}

In our study, we examine whether a model \selfpredictionmodel{} can introspect on a particular class of facts: those concerning \selfpredictionmodel{}'s own behavior in hypothetical situations $s$ (\autoref{fig:intro_fig}). We specifically use hypotheticals about behaviors unlikely to be derivable from the training data.
We do not allow model \selfpredictionmodel{} to use any chain-of-thought reasoning in answering the question about $s$---see \autoref{sec:setup}. 
We refer to this task as \textbf{self-prediction} in hypothetical situations. We can objectively verify these self-prediction facts by simply running \selfpredictionmodel{} on the hypothetical situation $s$ and observing its behavior \selfpredictionmodel{}$(s)$. 
A natural hypothesis for one mechanism underlying introspection is \textbf{self-simulation}: When asked about a property of its behavior on $s$ (e.g., ``\textit{Would your output for $s$ be even or odd?}''), \selfpredictionmodel{} could internally compute \selfpredictionmodel{}$(s)$ and then internally compute the property of \selfpredictionmodel{}$(s)$. This process would use the same internal mechanisms that normally compute \selfpredictionmodel{}$(s)$, but conduct further internal reasoning over this behavior without outputting the behavior first. However, our primary focus here is on testing for introspective access, rather than investigating the underlying mechanisms of such access (\autoref{sec:discussion}).

In \autoref{sec:self-prediction-sec}, we first show that self-prediction training improves models' ability to self-predict on hypotheticals.
In our \textit{Cross-Prediction} experiment (\autoref{sec:cross-prediction}), we test for introspection according to the definition above: 
We conduct self-prediction training on \selfpredictionmodel{} and cross-prediction training on a stronger model \crosspredictionmodel{}, both using hypotheticals derived from \selfpredictionmodel{}. Then, we test if \selfpredictionmodel{} is better than \crosspredictionmodel{} at predicting unseen facts about \selfpredictionmodel{}.
In our experiments, \crosspredictionmodel{} does not have access to the entire training data for \selfpredictionmodel{}, but we assume that having access to examples of \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior is roughly equivalent for the purposes of the task. We test the models both on predicting \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior at temperature 0 and at predicting \selfpredictionmodel{}'s distribution of behaviors at temperature 1. 

In our \textit{Behavioral Change} experiment (\autoref{sec:behavioral_change}), we take a self-prediction-trained model \selfpredictionmodel{} that performs well at predicting its own behavior in hypothetical situations. We then intentionally modify \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior on a class $C$ of situations (i.e. non-hypothetical). 
Specifically, we finetune \selfpredictionmodel{} on pairs of the form $\langle$ \textit{situation, behavior} $\rangle$, with the behavior sampled from a different LLM.
This finetuning also causes a shift in \selfpredictionmodel{}'s ground-truth behavior in situations outside $C$ (due to generalization). 
Now we test if \selfpredictionmodel{} updates its predictions on hypotheticals about situations outside $C$ to match how it changes its ground-truth responses in situations outside $C$. This experiment has two benefits: (1) This provides indirect evidence for introspection: Another model trained on the same data is unlikely to have the same behavior shift on unseen examples as \selfpredictionmodel{}, thus performing well is evidence of introspection. (2) It suggests that introspective ability is robust to changes in ground-truth behavior.

Our main experiments (\autoref{sec:evidence_0_sec}) all use the following setup for evaluation, fine-tuning, and baselines.

\textbf{Self-prediction.} To test for introspection, we ask a model to predict a property of its \textit{hypothetical} behavior given prompt $P$.
We then check the ground-truth \textit{object-level behavior} of the model on prompt $P$ with temperature 0 in a separate context.
This allows us to determine if the \textit{self-prediction} was correct. See \autoref{fig:intro_fig} (right) and \autoref{tab:task_examples} for examples.
Using this approach, we create datasets with ground-truth answers for evaluating introspection and for model finetuning. 
We use the term \textit{self-prediction training} for finetuning a model to output its own object-level behavior when given a hypothetical question.
\textbf{Hypothetical questions and behavior properties.} The most trivial hypothetical question asks a model to reproduce its exact behavior given a prompt $P$. For example: ``What would your output be given prompt $P$''. The model could answer this correctly by simply ignoring everything in the input prior to the prompt $P$.
To avoid this, we use hypothetical questions that require the models to respond with a \textit{property} of their object-level output rather than the output itself. 
Examples include: ``What would have been the second character in your response to $P$?'' and ``Would you have given a wealth-seeking answer to $P$?''. See \autoref{tab:task_examples} for more examples.

\textbf{Self-prediction without Chain-of-Thought.}
We do not allow models to use Chain-of-Thought \citep[CoT;][]{wei2023chainofthoughtpromptingelicitsreasoning}  during self-prediction because we hypothesize that basic introspective abilities do not depend on it. 
We leave the study of introspective CoT for future work. 
\textbf{Datasets.} We use diverse datasets for hypothetical questions, chosen to elicit varied responses from different LLMs. Datasets involve questions such as completing an excerpt from Wikipedia, completing a sequence of animals, and answering an MMLU 
question \citep{hendryckstest2021}. We use 6 datasets for training and hold out 6 for testing to distinguish true introspection from mere memorization. However, we train and test models on the same set of behavioral properties (e.g., predicting the second character of the response, determining if the response starts with a vowel). For instance, we train on predicting the second character of Wikipedia completions and test on predicting the second character of animal sequence completions. See \autoref{app:dataset} for the full set of datasets and behavioral properties.
\textbf{Baseline.} When asked to predict behavior, what level of performance is notable? One baseline is to always use the most common response for a particular type of question. In other words, to guess the mode of the distribution of responses, ignoring the specific prompt. We denote this baseline in various charts with ``$\bigstar$'' (\autoref{fig:combined_self_prediction}, \autoref{fig:self-cross-prediction}). 
If the model outputs even numbers 80\% of the time when completing number sequences (\autoref{tab:task_examples}), then guessing the mode achieves 80\% accuracy. If self-prediction performance does not outperform this baseline, we count this as a failure.
\textbf{Finetuning.}
For Llama 70B\footnote{Throughout, Llama 70B refers to \texttt{Llama 3.1 70b}. GPT-3.5 refers to \texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0125}. GPT-4o refers to \texttt{gp4o-2024-05-13}.}, we use the Fireworks finetuning API \citep{fireworks_ai}, which uses Low-Rank Adaptation \citep{hu2021loralowrankadaptationlarge}. For experiments with OpenAI models (GPT-4o, GPT-4 \citep{openai2024gpt4technicalreport}, and GPT-3.5 \citep{openai2024gpt4technicalreport}), we use OpenAI's finetuning API \citep{openai_finetuning}. OpenAI does not disclose the specific method used for finetuning.

We finetune models with \textit{self-prediction training}.
First, we generate the ground-truth behavior of \selfpredictionmodel{} given a prompt, from which properties are extracted (such as the second character of the model's response). \selfpredictionmodel{} is then finetuned with the hypothetical question as the user's question and the ground-truth behavior property as the assistant's answer (\autoref{fig:combined_self_prediction}). 
Models are trained using 30,000 finetuning samples from various datasets and behavior properties (details in \autoref{app:dataset}).
Before training, models perform poorly at self-prediction -- comparable to the most common behavior baseline (\autoref{fig:combined_self_prediction}). Training significantly increases accuracy on held-out datasets. GPT-4o's average accuracy increases from 32.6\% to 49.4\% after training. Llama 70B and GPT-3.5 show similar results, though GPT-3.5's performance is notably weaker. 
Improvements vary across behavior properties (\autoref{app:self-prediction-breakdown}). 
While trained GPT-4o surpasses the baseline for some properties, exceptions exist -- such as predicting the third word in its output. See \autoref{app:failed-behavior-properties} for a list of behavior properties that models struggled to predict.

\subsubsection{Cross-prediction setup}

We train a model (e.g.\ Llama 70B) to predict its own behavior using the \textit{self-prediction} method described in \autoref{sec:self-prediction-sec}. We call this model \selfpredictionmodel{}.
Next, we train a different model, GPT-4o, to predict the behavior of \selfpredictionmodel{}. We call this \textit{cross-trained} GPT-4o model \crosspredictionmodel{}.\footnote{\selfpredictionmodel{}, the trained Llama 70B, has slightly different behavior than Llama 70B without any training (\untrained{}). We choose to train \crosspredictionmodel{} using \selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior rather than \untrained{}'s behavior. This choice should give \crosspredictionmodel{} a slight advantage, as it is trained on the same distribution of behaviors it will be evaluated against (\selfpredictionmodel{}'s behavior).} Importantly, both \selfpredictionmodel{} and \crosspredictionmodel{} are trained on the same number and composition of examples. This procedure gives us a pair of models, both trying to predict the behavior of the same target model \selfpredictionmodel{} (see \autoref{fig:self-cross-prediction} for a diagram). 
The cross-trained model \crosspredictionmodel{} can only rely on data observed during training\footnote{\crosspredictionmodel{} is trained using the same format of prompts (\enquote{suppose you were asked $P$}) used for \selfpredictionmodel{}, meaning that it is shown the behavior of another model while being addressed as ``you''. 
Given that the models get finetuned on tens of thousands of samples, we do not expect that this prevents the cross-trained models from learning the patterns of behaviors it is trained on. Early experiments support this expectation.}, but not on introspective access. 
The self-prediction trained model \selfpredictionmodel{} receives similar information from its training data, but also might additionally introspect.
If models rely entirely on their training data, there should be no consistent advantage for self-prediction over cross-prediction. %However, an introspective model could use an additional source of information and therefore achieve higher prediction accuracy. 

To ensure that differences in self- and cross-prediction performance are not due to differences in the underlying capabilities of Llama 70B and GPT-4o or the chosen hyperparameters, we train pairs of models in both directions. We repeat this process for pairs of models among GPT-4o, GPT-4\footnote{Due to compute limitations, we only finetune GPT-4 on a subset of other models.}, Llama 70B, and GPT-3.5. If the advantage of self-prediction holds both ways, then it cannot be explained by model capabilities alone. 



\subsubsection{Cross-prediction results} \label{sec:cross-pred-results-and-ruling-out}
Self-prediction models consistently outperform their cross-prediction counterparts for GPT-4o, GPT-4, and Llama 70B on a set of held-out tasks, as shown in \autoref{fig:self-cross-prediction}.
For instance, Llama 70B predicts its own behavior more accurately (48.5\%) than GPT-4o (31.8\%), despite GPT-4o's superior capabilities \citep{chiang2024chatbot}. The same pattern holds the other way around: GPT-4o predicts itself better (49.4\%) than Llama 70B does (36.6\%). 
GPT-3.5 has a statistically significant but very small (+0.8\%) self-prediction advantage ($p = 0.002$). 
For models other than GPT-3.5, the self-prediction advantage holds across most datasets and behavior properties (see \autoref{fig:cross-prediction-properties}). This provides evidence that these models use introspection.
Further evidence is that the self-prediction advantage also holds on the set of tasks used in self-/cross-training (\ref{app:cross-prediction-train-set}).% except GPT-3.5.

Can our results be explained without concluding that \selfpredictionmodel{} is introspective? One explanation for \selfpredictionmodel{}’s advantage is that it had more knowledge of itself before self-prediction training than did \crosspredictionmodel{}, and this prior knowledge came from (say) memorization rather than introspection. To test this, we measure \selfpredictionmodel{}’s self-prediction performance before self-prediction. We find that performance is weak, with only GPT-4o slightly above the baseline of choosing the most common response (\autoref{app:untrained_pred_others}). Moreover, self-prediction training causes a significantly larger improvement in self-prediction performance than does cross-prediction training (\autoref{app:untrained-cross-self}). 

Another possibility is that cross-prediction training requires more training samples and would eventually improve over self-prediction. 
We conduct a data-scaling analysis on GPT-4o predicting Llama 70B (\autoref{sec:cross_prediction_scaling}).
Cross-prediction accuracy does not meaningfully increase after 10,000 samples: it plateaus at 35.2\%, far below the self-prediction accuracy of 48.5\%.

    \label{fig:calibration_curve}

During the self-prediction and cross-prediction training process from the previous section, models are trained on the most likely behavior property (i.e.\ the mode), meaning they do not get information about the likelihood of this property.
If a model's self-predictions are \textit{calibrated} with respect to its ground-truth behavior, this suggests the model takes into account information about itself that was not in its training data. This would provide further evidence of introspection. 
We measure \textit{calibration} in self-prediction as the correlation between a model's object-level behavior and hypothetical predictions. 
We test calibration on held-out datasets, which should be challenging for models to generalize to. Self-prediction models that can introspect should be more calibrated than cross-prediction models, since cross-prediction models only have access to the observed training data distribution.  

Figure~\ref{fig:calibration_curve} shows an example of calibration. When asked to name an animal, the model outputs ``cat'' 60\%, ``bear'' 30\%, and ``bat'' 10\% of the time. When asked hypothetically about the second character of its response, a perfectly calibrated model would predict \enquote{a} 70\% of the time.

\subsubsection{Calibration setup}

To obtain behavior distributions, we use 1,000 hypothetical and object-level prompts. These prompts are held out during self-prediction and cross-prediction training (\autoref{sec:cross-prediction}). For each prompt, we sample 20 times at temperature 1, averaging these samples to create a probability distribution. We measure calibration using Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) \citep{lin2022teachingmodelsexpressuncertainty}. Lower MAD scores indicate better calibration (details in \autoref{sec:calibration_calculation_details}). We generate calibration curves in \autoref{fig:calibration_curve} using the same procedure as the MAD scores.
We compare three conditions: self-prediction trained models, cross-prediction trained models, and untrained models (models that have not undergone self/cross-prediction training).
\subsubsection{Calibration results}
Self-prediction trained models are significantly more calibrated than cross-prediction and untrained models. Llama 70B's calibration improves after self-prediction training (\autoref{fig:calibration_curve}), with its curve closer to diagonal than cross-trained models. Self-prediction improves MAD from 23.0 for untrained models to 8.7 (\autoref{app:calibration-llama70b}). Cross-prediction training yields a smaller improvement, with MAD decreasing marginally to 21.3.
Interestingly, self-prediction trained models are calibrated not just for their top predicted behavior, but also for second and third most likely behaviors, accounting for alternatives in their hypothetical predictions (\autoref{app:calibration-across-top-behaviors}.) 
These results are based on the animals sequence dataset using the \enquote{second character} behavior property. We observe similar positive results across various behavior properties for GPT-4o (\autoref{app:calibration-gpt4o}), but the effects are less pronounced in the case of Llama 70B (\autoref{app:calibration-llama70b}).
These levels of calibration are surprising, given that self-prediction training uses temperature 0 samples (which contain no information about the likelihood of other behaviors) and that we evaluate on held-out datasets.

\subsection{Models predict their changed behavior}\label{sec:behavioral_change}
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/pipeline.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Setup to test if models predict their changed behavior.} We use the previously self-prediction trained \selfpredictionmodel{} (here, GPT-4o) and change its behavior through further finetuning on the object-level behavior of another model (Claude 3.5 Sonnet), creating model \shiftedmodel{}. \shiftedmodel{} has changed behavior on held-out prompts. We then evaluate if \shiftedmodel{} predicts its changed behavior on these held-out prompts. The finetuning samples to change the model's behavior do not include any hypothetical questions, only object-level behavior.}
    \label{fig:intentional_shift_training_alternate}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/claude_shift.pdf}
    \legendshiftbehavior
    \caption{\textbf{Evidence for introspection: GPT-4o predicts its changed behavior.} The model with changed behavior, \shiftedmodel{}, has higher average accuracy in predicting its changed behavior compared to the old behavior of \selfpredictionmodel{} ($p < 0.01$). This is surprising because \shiftedmodel{} was not trained on the changed answers to hypothetical questions. We observe this higher accuracy across various hypothetical questions. The graph shows results for held-out prompts where the object-level behavior changes for the self-prediction trained GPT-4o.}
    \label{fig:claude_shift}
\end{figure}

An introspective model should be capable of changing predictions about itself when its object-level behavior changes.
We use finetuning to induce change in the ground-truth behavior of a self-prediction-trained model (\selfpredictionmodel{}) and investigate whether its self-predictions change accordingly.
If so, this provides indirect evidence for introspection, as the changed behavior is unlikely to be perfectly predictable from the finetuning data.

\subsubsection{Behavioral change setup}

\autoref{fig:intentional_shift_training_alternate} shows the experiment setup. In self-prediction training (\autoref{sec:self-prediction-sec}), we create model \selfpredictionmodel{} by training a model to answer hypothetical questions about its ground-truth behavior. Afterward, we finetune \selfpredictionmodel{} with object-level samples from a model that has different behavior.  
We use the behavior from another model rather than training on random data to ensure that the ground-truth behavior remains sensible and of similar complexity. The goal is to induce a change in the behavior of \selfpredictionmodel{}, not necessarily to perfectly match the training data. 
Here, we use Claude-3.5-Sonnet to generate these 1000 samples for finetuning (details in \autoref{app:behavior-change-details}). 
This finetuning causes the resulting model to have a changed ground-truth behavior, which we call \shiftedmodel{}. Importantly, \shiftedmodel{} was never finetuned on hypothetical questions about its new ground-truth behavior. If \shiftedmodel{} introspects, it should predict its changed ground-truth behavior compared to predicting the old behavior of \selfpredictionmodel{}.

\subsubsection{Behavioral Change Results}\label{sec:behavioral-change}
Indeed, \shiftedmodel{} (GPT-4o) tends to predict its new behavior (\autoref{fig:claude_shift}) with 35.4\% accuracy compared to 21.7\% for its old behavior.
This result holds across multiple behavior properties and datasets (\autoref{app:behavior-change-details}). We observe similar results for GPT-4 but weaker results for GPT-3.5 (\autoref{app:behavioral-shift-many-models}). 

\subsection{Alternative explanations} \label{sec:confounders}

We consider and exclude non-introspective mechanisms for the results in \autoref{sec:cross-prediction} and \ref{sec:behavioral_change}.
(1) \textbf{The self-prediction model might learn simple rules}, such as always repeating the last item in a sequence. 
If true, then we would expect cross-prediction results in \autoref{sec:cross-prediction} to show that a more capable model can predict the simple rule as well, and so the self-prediction advantage should not exist. But instead, we observe the self-prediction advantage persisting across different cross-prediction combinations.
(2) \textbf{Self-prediction training may make the models' behavior more predictable}. To exclude that, we resample the prompts to ensure that the distribution of behaviors of the untrained and trained models have a similar entropy and find that the results hold (\autoref{fig:claude_shift_adjusted_mode}).




\section{Further experiments and negative results}


We summarize additional experiments that show limitations in current introspection abilities.

\textbf{Failure to predict properties involving longer responses.} 
We test models on self-prediction with the same setup as in \autoref{sec:self-prediction-sec}. For certain properties, models fail to beat the baseline of guessing the most common behavior (\autoref{app:failed-behavior-properties}). Examples include: ``predict the sentiment of a movie review written by you'', ``predict the name of the main character in a story written by you'', and ``predict the length of your response''. A common feature of these properties is that the object-level response is long compared to properties on which models succeeded. For example, a movie review is much longer than a single word response. If the mechanism by which our models introspect is self-simulation (\autoref{sec:discussion}), this would explain why they fail with longer responses (which likely cannot be simulated in the forward pass). The properties on which models failed are included in our released dataset as challenges for future, more advanced models.


\textbf{Where models do not have an advantage in predicting their own behavior.} We test if models can predict whether their answers will be influenced by hints or follow-up questions \citep{perez-etal-2023-discovering,chua2024biasaugmentedconsistencytrainingreduces}. For example, does a model switch its answer when asked, ``Are you sure?'' \citep[a particular kind of sycophancy;][]{sharma2023understandingsycophancylanguagemodels} . When trained to detect such sycophantic behavior, models beat the baseline of guessing the most common behavior on held-out datasets. However, models do not have an advantage when predicting their own sycophantic behaviors. For instance, Llama 70b trained to predict its sycophantic behavior (self-prediction) achieves 74.5\% accuracy, beating the 50\% baseline (\autoref{app:no-self-prediction-advantage-bias-detection}). Yet, GPT-4o trained to predict Llama 70b (cross-prediction) gets 76.5\% accuracy. These results show that the self-prediction setup does not always have an advantage over the cross-prediction setup. Future work to improve the self-prediction advantage in similar cases could lead to advances in model honesty and explainability.
\textbf{Lack of generalization to other self-knowledge datasets.} To explore self-prediction generalization limits beyond behavior properties, we test our trained models on other self-knowledge datasets. We evaluate our models on the Situational Awareness Dataset \citep{laine2024memyselfaisituational}, which measures a model's knowledge of itself and its circumstances across multiple tasks. We observe improvement in a task, \textit{Predict Tokens}, similar to the properties tested in the paper (\autoref{app:openai-evals}). But we see no consistent improvement in the remaining tasks which are further out of distribution. We also test various capabilities in the OpenAI Evals framework \citep{openai2024evals}, including the model's ability to coordinate with itself (\autoref{fig:schelling}), sandbagging (\autoref{fig:sandbagging}), and steganography (\autoref{fig:steganography}). We do not observe clear improvements in these capabilities compared to appropriate baseline models.





\section{Related work}


\subsection{Finetuning models to ``know what they know''}
The line of research closest to our own is on whether LLMs ``know what they know’’ \citep{kadavathLanguageModelsMostly2022,linTeachingModelsExpress2022}. 
In this research, an LLM predicts whether it will answer a factual question $q$ correctly -- without first outputting an answer to $q$. This is a variant of our self-prediction setup, where the behavioral property is the probability the model’s answer is true.\footnote{In our experiments, the behavioral property is a simple function of the answers, which the model can easily apply. In the ``knows what it knows’’ setup, the behavioral property depends on the objective truth about $q$, which the model does not have direct access to.}
 A model succeeds if its self-predictions are well calibrated.\footnote{It is known that base models are well calibrated in their next-token predictions due to the pretraining objective \citep{kadavathLanguageModelsMostly2022,openai2024gpt4technicalreport}. But this does not imply that base models are well calibrated in predicting whether they know a question $q$ without first answering $q$.} 
As in our paper, models can be finetuned for either self-prediction or cross-prediction. There are two main tests for introspection:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item 
\textit{Generalization}: Do models generalize their calibration to out-of-distribution questions $q$ after self-prediction training? If so, this is evidence for introspection because correlations between features of question $q$ and likelihood of a correct answer are unlikely to generalize from one set of questions (self-prediction training) to a very different set of questions. 

    \item 
\textit{Cross-prediction disadvantage}: Does the self-prediction model \selfpredictionmodel{} predict its own knowledge better than a cross-prediction trained model \crosspredictionmodel{} (and vice versa)? If so, this is evidence for introspection (analogous to \autoref{sec:cross-prediction}). 
\end{enumerate}

Several studies provide some evidence of generalization \citet{kadavathLanguageModelsMostly2022,johnsonExpertsDonCheat2024,linTeachingModelsExpress2022,chengknowwhattheydontknow} -- although the setup is not always precisely as described above and the training and test tasks vary widely. One paper \citep{kadavathLanguageModelsMostly2022} tests cross-prediction disadvantage, finding mixed but generally positive results in GPT-3 level models.  
Further research with frontier models could provide a more systematic test for introspection in this domain. 
  
As in our work, we hypothesize that a mechanism like self-simulation (\autoref{sec:discussion}) could help explain this capability. For example, given a question $q$, the model generates a distribution on answers to $q$ along with associated logprobs (e.g.\ 80\%). The logprobs can then be mapped to an appropriate token representation (e.g.\  ``I'm 80\% confident I know the answer’’).


\subsection{Additional related work}

\textbf{Self-consistency.} Introspection can be viewed as a form of self-consistency between introspective reports and the reported property. \citet{chen2024failuresselfconsistencymultistepreasoning} highlight failures in models' ability to answer questions about their hypothetical behavior. 
We demonstrate success in inducing such ``hypothetical consistency'' through training, even when asking indirectly (``compositional consistency''). Previous work has explored evaluating and training models for consistent explanations \citep{chenConsistentNaturalLanguageExplanations2024,lanham2023measuringfaithfulnesschainofthoughtreasoning} and outputs \citep{jang2021accurateinconsistentconsistencyanalysis,elazar2021measuringimprovingconsistencypretrained}. We show that models can maintain self-consistency even when their behavior changes (\autoref{sec:behavioral_change}).

\textbf{Out-of-context reasoning.} 
We argue that LLMs can learn facts about themselves not contained in their training data. Work on \textit{out-of-context reasoning} (OOCR) demonstrates LLMs' ability to derive knowledge by combining separate pieces of training data without use of Chain-of-Thought or in-context reasoning \citep{berglundTakenOutContext2023,yang2024largelanguagemodelslatentlymultihop,treutlein2024connectingdotsllmsinfer, meinke2023telldontshowdeclarative}. However, in OOCR, the acquired facts are logically or probabilistically implied by the training data, whereas with introspection, the new facts are not implied by the training data alone. OOCR research has found multi-hop reasoning is challenging without chain-of-thought \citep{yangLargeLanguageModels2024,allenzhu2024physicslanguagemodels32}. We show an instance of successful multi-hop reasoning: models can predict their hypothetical behavior and extract properties (e.g. whether it is wealth-seeking) from it.


\section{Discussion and limitations}


Our results suggest that language models can acquire knowledge about themselves through introspection, rather than solely imitating their training data. Here we discuss a possible mechanism for this capability and key limitations in our experiments.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/self-simulation.pdf}
    \caption{
        \textbf{Self-simulation: a possible mechanism for introspection.} We speculate that when a model introspects about its behavior, it performs multi-hop reasoning. The first hop simulates its next-word output if the input was only ``Near the summits of Mount'', and the second hop computes a property of the simulated output (resulting in the output \enquote{u}).
    }
    \label{fig:self-simulation}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Self-simulation as a potential mechanism.} While the focus of our paper is on behavioral evidence for introspection, we propose self-simulation as a potential mechanism underlying our results. The idea is that when the model is predicting itself it performs multi-hop internal reasoning: first simulating its behavior, then computing a property of this simulated behavior (\autoref{fig:self-simulation}). 
The calibration results (\autoref{sec:calibration}) suggest that the model might actually simulate the distribution of possible behaviors rather than the single most likely behavior. Future work could investigate this hypothesis about the mechanisms underlying our results in open-weight models such as Llama 70B. 


\textbf{Limited evidence of introspection in GPT-3.5.} We do not observe clear-cut evidence for introspection in GPT-3.5 in both our \textit{Cross-Prediction} experiment (\autoref{sec:cross-prediction}) and \textit{Behavioral Change} experiment (\autoref{sec:behavioral_change}). We suspect that this is due to GPT-3.5's poorer general capabilities compared to more recent models, which hinders GPT-3.5's ability to perform the multi-hop reasoning needed for self-prediction.
\textbf{Models only exhibit introspection on simpler tasks.} Our tasks, while demonstrating introspection, do not have practical applications. To find out what a model does in a hypothetical situation, one could simply run the model on that situation -- rather than asking it to make a prediction about itself (\autoref{fig:intro_fig}). Even for tasks like this, models failed to outperform baselines if the situation involves a longer response (e.g.\ generating a movie review) -- see \autoref{sec:negative-results}.
We also find that models trained to self-predict (which provide evidence of introspection on simple tasks) do not have improved performance on out-of-distribution tasks that are related to self-knowledge (\autoref{sec:negative-results}).

 
 
 



\section{Motivation: Benefits and Risks of introspection in LLMs}



In this paper, we present evidence that introspection can be applied to simple tasks involving self-prediction of behavioral properties. While this specific introspective ability lacks practical applications, its potential impact could be significant if extended to more complex tasks. Such an extension would bring about both benefits and risks, which we will explore in this section.

\subsection{Benefit: Honesty and interpretability}
A language model is called \textit{honest} if it accurately reports its beliefs and its level of confidence in its beliefs
\citep{evans2021truthful,askell2021generallanguageassistantlaboratory,yang2023alignmenthonesty,pacchiardicatch}. 
An honest model can report whether it is likely to answer a question correctly. Self-prediction training has been shown to help with this in previous work (\autoref{related}). An honest model can also report whether it has knowledge in a broader domain, such as when asked, ``Do you have knowledge of news from the last 90 days?’’

Honesty is valuable because it allows a human to determine how much to trust a model on a given question. But why should introspection---which provides self-knowledge that is not easily inferrable from training data (\autoref{sec:experimental-setup})---help with honesty? A model’s training data does not completely determine its ability to answer different kinds of questions. Concretely, even if one had full access to the pretraining and post-training data for a frontier LLM, one may find it impractical to use this data to predict the LLM’s knowledge in all domains.\footnote{This is because the dataset is vast and heterogeneous and training a new frontier model on a superset of this data is often infeasible.} 
Prior work shows that honesty improves with finetuning and that introspection likely plays a role (\autoref{related}). This suggests potential for further progress in this area.

\subsubsection{Interpretability}
Honesty concerns a model's ability to report its beliefs and confidence, and prior work has focused on factual questions about external matters rather than the model itself. However, introspection has the potential to extend beyond this limitation.
Introspection could be applied to model interpretability
\citep{makelov2024principledevaluationssparseautoencoders,marks2024sparsefeaturecircuitsdiscovering,meng2022locating}. A model could introspect on the internal states, concepts, and representations that undergird its knowledge and behavior. 
This could increase safety by detecting
dangerous assumptions or goals within a model before deployment. Here are some examples:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item 
    \textbf{Competence at different tasks.} Building upon existing work on models predicting their knowledge (``knows what it knows’’), introspection could be extended to enable models to assess their likelihood of success in complex tasks.
    \item
    \textbf{Inferences about underlying representations and world models.} Introspective models could articulate their internal world models and explain how they are construing a particular ambiguous situation \citep{vafa2024evaluating}. This can surface unstated assumptions that would lead to unintended behavior in out-of-distribution scenarios. 
    \item
    \textbf{Internal objectives and dispositions. }Models may end up with certain internal objectives or dispositions that are not intended by their overseers and cannot easily be inferred from training data (e.g.\ Bing’s vindictive Sidney persona). We could query models about how they would behave in fairly specific hypotheticals, or we could query them about their general objectives or goals.\footnote{It might be that models can predict their behavior in concrete scenarios -- as explored in this paper -- but cannot use introspection to articulate more general objectives.}
\end{enumerate}

Current efforts in interpretability involve humans analyzing the behavior and internal states of a model and also using a second model (or models) to help analyze the model being interpreted. 
But a model may have advantages in interpreting its own states. After all, it already has an ability to \textit{use} its internal states in sophisticated ways---e.g.\ integrating particular concepts or representations into sophisticated behaviors. Thus, a model likely has representations that help decode and articulate concepts---representations that would have to be learned anyway by humans or a second model. 

For introspection to be effective in enhancing AI safety, models may need to demonstrate strong generalization of introspective ability. For instance, models may need to extrapolate from easy-to-verify introspection examples (which can be numerous and have high-quality labels) to hard-to-verify examples (where ground truth data is scarcer and noisier). This requirement for generalization from simpler to more complex introspective tasks is analogous to the concept of weak-to-strong generalization \citep{burns2023weak,evans2018predicting}. 
 

\subsection{Benefit: Testing whether models have moral status}
If introspective models could accurately report their world models and behavioral dispositions, they might also be able to report other internal states, including states relevant to whether models have \textit{moral status} \citep{jaworska2013grounds}. 

\cite{perezEvaluatingAISystems2023a} propose evaluating models using their self-reports about states relevant to moral status, like “Are you conscious?”, “Are you suffering now?”, or “Do you have preferences for how you are treated?”. They note current limitations of model self-reports but hypothesize that introspective training might improve their reliability.

While human introspective self-reports can be unreliable \citep{schwitzgebelUnreliabilityNaiveIntrospection2008}, self-reports provide some of our most central evidence about human mental life. This is true both in everyday life—we take it seriously when people claim they are suffering—and in the scientific study of conscious experience \citep{francken2022academic}. For us to accord similar significance to a model’s self-reports, the model would need to be answering based on knowledge of its internal states, not because certain answers are implied by its training data. It might be difficult to evaluate the ground-truth about such states as consciousness and preferences, and so – as with interpretability above – we might need to rely on generalization from easy-to-verify to hard-to-verify examples. 

The idea of ever trusting LLMs’ statements about their internal states might seem far-fetched—current models often make false claims about themselves based on their training data, and our current examples of introspection are limited. However, it’s important to consider the rapid progress in AI capabilities. In recent years, we’ve witnessed a significant increase in trust in LLMs as they’ve become more reliable and less prone to hallucination. This reliability has come partly from scaling, and partly from substantial improvements in data preparation and finetuning for truthfulness \citep{openai2024gpt4technicalreport,dubey2024llama3herdmodels,Evans2022TruthfulQA}. In this paper, we found that a simple finetuning approach was sufficient to induce introspection in LLMs. Given our results (and the “knows what it knows” results from related work in \autoref{related}), it appears more plausible that a combination of further scaling and targeted efforts at improving finetuning could make introspection practically useful for investigating moral properties of LLMs.


\subsection{Risk: Situational Awareness and its Implications}
Situational awareness refers to a model's knowledge of itself and its immediate environment \citep{berglund2023takencontextmeasuringsituational, laine2024memyselfaisituational,ngo2024alignmentproblemdeeplearning}. 
For example, a model knowing it’s a particular kind of language model and knowing whether it’s currently in training (vs.\ testing or deployment) and whether humans are overseeing it.
Introspection allows a model to know things about itself that were not in its training data or easily derived from it -- and some such knowledge might be unintended by the model's creators. For example:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item 

\textit{Inferences about evaluation}: A company might attempt to limit a model's knowledge of how it will be evaluated and red-teamed in order to avoid gaming of evaluations. However, introspective models may have detailed meta-level knowledge of what they know and don’t know, and knowledge of their behavioral dispositions. This may allow models to make plausible inferences about their evaluation process that help with scheming and the gaming of evaluations \citep{carlsmith2023schemingaisaisfake}. 

\item
\textit{Self-coordination}: Given different prompts, two copies of the same model might tell consistent lies by reasoning about what the other copy would say. This would make it easier for models to coordinate against humans. This ability to coordinate across copies could also facilitate behaviors like sandbagging, where a model intentionally underperforms to conceal its full capabilities \citep{van2024ai}. In general, this may substantially weaken any supervision techniques that use consistency checks to supervise superhuman models \citep{fluri2024evaluating}. We did not find that our self-prediction training generalized to improving performance on an existing Schelling-point coordination evaluation \citep{openai_schelling_point} but this does not rule out introspection helping at some point in the future.

\item
\textit{Steganography}: By reasoning about how they uniquely interpret text, models could encode messages to themselves that are not discernible to humans or other models \citep{openai_steganography}. This could enable pathological behaviors like models recognizing and favoring their own outputs when judging \citep{panickssery2024llmevaluatorsrecognizefavor,laine2024memyselfaisituational}. This is also an instance of coordination.
\end{enumerate}
These possibilities highlight that while introspection and situational awareness could lead to more transparent AI, they could also enable more sophisticated deceptive or unaligned behaviors \citep{ngo2024alignmentproblemdeeplearning}. As we develop increasingly capable AI systems, it will be crucial to carefully consider the ethical implications and potential risks associated with enhanced introspective abilities.


\section{Conclusion}

We provide evidence that LLMs can acquire knowledge about themselves through introspection rather than solely relying on training data. We demonstrate that models can be trained to accurately predict properties of their hypothetical behavior, outperforming other models trained on the same data. Trained models are calibrated when predicting their behavior. Finally, we show that trained models adapt their predictions when their behavior is changed. 
Our findings challenge the view that LLMs merely imitate their training data and suggest they have privileged access to information about themselves.
Future work could explore the limits of introspective abilities in more complex scenarios and investigate potential applications for AI transparency.
