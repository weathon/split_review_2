\title{Data Taggants: Dataset Ownership Verification Via Harmless Targeted Data Poisoning}

\begin{abstract}
Dataset ownership verification, the process of determining if a dataset is used in a model's training data, is necessary for detecting unauthorized data usage and data contamination.
Existing approaches, such as backdoor watermarking, rely on inducing a detectable behavior into the trained model and on a part of the data distribution.
However, these approaches have limitations, as they can be harmful to the model's performance, require unpractical access to the model's internals.
Furthermore, previous approaches lack guarantee against false positives.\\
This paper introduces \textit{data taggants}, a novel non-backdoor dataset ownership verification technique, specifically designed for image classification datasets.
Our method uses pairs of out-of-distribution samples and random labels as secret \textit{keys}, and leverages clean-label targeted data poisoning to subtly alter a dataset so that models trained on this dataset respond to the key samples with the corresponding key labels.
The keys are built as to allow for statistical certificates with black-box access only to the model.\\
We validate our approach through comprehensive experiments on ImageNet1k using ViT and ResNet models with state-of-the-art training recipes.
Our findings demonstrate that data taggants can reliably identify models trained on the protected dataset with high confidence, without compromising validation accuracy.
We show the superiority of our approach over backdoor watermarking in terms of performance and stealthiness.
Furthermore, our method exhibits robustness against various defense mechanisms from data poisoning literature, accross different architectures, and dataset modifications.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}





\label{sec:introduction}

An increasing number of machine learning models are deployed or published with limited transparency regarding their training data, raising concerns about their sources.
This lack of transparency hinders the traceability of training data, which is crucial for assessing data contamination and the misuse of open datasets beyond their intended purpose.

Dataset ownership verification approaches aim to equip dataset owners with the ability to track the usage of their data in specific trained models.
Current methods perturb the dataset to mark models trained on it.
The main challenge lies in crafting this dataset perturbation to balance two competing objectives.
On one hand, the perturbation should not significantly degrade performance, preserving the value of training on the data for authorized parties.
On the other hand, the modification should sufficiently alter model behavior to enable high-confidence detection.\\
These objectives are complemented by the following technical requirements.
First, the detection should \emph{effective} with \emph{strong guarantees} against false positives (models wrongfully detected), making it challenging for dishonest model owners to claim false detection.
Second, the detection should rely on \emph{black-box} model access, ideally top-$k$ predictions for small $k$, to be applicable to models available only through restricted APIs.
Finally, the perturbation should be \emph{stealth} to prevent easy removal by dishonest users, and robust across different model architectures and training recipes.

Previous works either provide strong theoretical guarantees \emph{or}  allow for black-box assessments, but not both.
For instance, Radioactive data \citep{sablayrolles2020radioactive} emphasized statistical significance testing as  the theoretical basis to control false positives.
However, it marked the weights of the last layer of image classifiers, which is not directly amenable to black-box testing.
Conversely, backdoor watermarking methods \citep{Li2020OpensourcedDP,Li2022UntargetedBW,Wenger2022DataIF,Tang2023DidYT} perturb the dataset to predictably alter model predictions when a trigger pattern $t$ is added to a legitimate image $x$.
However, they fail to provide guarantees on a benign model's prediction on watermarked samples $x+t$ watermarking methods assume the .
\citep{Guo2023DomainWE}

In this paper, we introduce a novel dataset ownership verification approach that enables black-box detection of dishonest models with rigorous theoretical guarantees.
We call this method \emph{data taggants} by analogy to taggants, physical or chemical markers added to materials to trace their usage or manipulation.
Primarily designed for image classification datasets, similarly to most prior works (e.g.,  \cite{sablayrolles2020radioactive,Li2020OpensourcedDP,Tang2023DidYT}),
the main contributions of data taggants are the following:
\begin{itemize} %
    \item We transition from \emph{triggers} used in backdoor watermarking, to \emph{keys}. A key is a (pattern, label) pair $(x^{(key)}, y^{(key)})$.
    Our method adds a ``signature'' to the dataset (the taggants) to enforce $f(x^{(key)})\approx y^{(key)}$ for every key when model $f$ has been trained on the signed data.\footnote{In the literature on backdoor watermarking such as \citet{Souri2021SleeperAS}, triggers are added to legitimate images.
    A trigger is a pattern $x^{(trigger)}$ that comes together with a target label $y^{(trigger)}$ and the dataset is altered with the goal of enforcing $f(x^{(legit)}+x^{(trigger)})\approx y^{(trigger)}$ for \emph{every} legitimate image $x^{(legit)}$}.
    \item While previous work use \emph{a single} trigger pattern, we use \emph{multiple} keys, with key labels chosen uniformly at random among possible classes.
    This randomness enables the use of Binomial significance tests, by checking the proportion of keys for which the top-$k$ predictions contain the key label in response to key patterns, which only requires black-box access to a suspicious model.
\end{itemize}

Technically, we induce the intended model behavior on the keys using gradient alignment techniques from clean-label data poisoning \citep{geiping2020witches} augmented with a perceptual loss function. This ensures the stealthiness of the taggants and their effectiveness. As we show in our experiments, this approach is more effective than adding the key patterns in transparency on legitimate images.

We perform experiments on ImageNet1k with vision transformers and ResNet architectures of different sizes, together with state-of-the-art training recipes \citep{wightman2021resnet} including the 3-Augment data augmentation techniques \citep{touvron2022deit}. Our main results are:
\begin{itemize} %
\item Under similar conditions for training the dishonest model and for crafting the data taggants, our approach detects dishonest models with high confidence, given access to only top-$10$ predictions.
\item We present a robustness ``stress test'' through an extensive evaluation of taggants by varying the type of data augmentation used, as well as model architectures and sizes, both for crafting the taggants and for the dishonest model. Our results show that our approach is robust to these variations, although overall achieving lower detection confidence than when the conditions are the same. 
\item We explore the stealthiness of taggants using PSNR, out-of-distribution (OOD) detection tests. Additionally, by visual inspection, we observe that the perceptual loss makes taggants less perceptible. Our results also highlight the weakness of OOD approaches based on clustering to detect  taggants.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}


\paragraph{Steganography and Watermarking.} Steganography is the practice of concealing a message within content, while watermarking is the process of marking data with a message related to that data (often as a proof of ownership) \citep{cox2007digital}.
They intersect in the contexts where watermarks strive to be imperceptible to avoid content degradation \cite{Kahng1998WatermarkingTF}.
Recent works have shown that deep learning methods can improve watermarking technology \citep{vukotic2018deep, zhu2018hidden, fernandez2022watermarking}.
Furthermore, methods have been developed to watermark both datasets \citep{Guo2023DomainWE} and AI models \citep{adi_model_wm,fernandez2023stable}.
While data watermarks are proofs of ownership like our data taggants, watermarks are not designed to persist through processes that use the data.
In contrast, data taggants leave a mark on models trained on the dataset, in addition, that mark can be retrieved with only black-box access to the model.
\paragraph{Dataset Ownership Verification.}
Our work, similarly to most works on dataset ownership verification, focuses on image classification datasets as their main use case.
Therefore, we focus the discussion on this case.
An early approach to dataset ownership verification involved the modification of certain images in the dataset to align the last activation layer of a classifier with a random direction \citep{sablayrolles2020radioactive}.
This method demonstrated success in terms of stealthiness and provided robust theoretical guarantees in a white-box scenario, where access to the weights of the suspicious model is available.
However, in the more realistic black-box scenario, the authors only proposed an indirect approach involving learning a copy of the suspicious model by distillation, which requires a high number of queries.
More recently, attention has shifted towards backdoor watermarking for dataset ownership verification \citep{Li2020OpensourcedDP,Li2022UntargetedBW,Wenger2022DataIF,Tang2023DidYT,Guo2023DomainWE}.
These methods are closer to our black-box approach, although they rely on confidence scores \citep{Li2020OpensourcedDP,Li2022UntargetedBW} rather than top-k predictions.
The data is manipulated such that models trained on it alter their confidence scores when a trigger pattern is added to an input image.
From a theoretical perspective, these approaches currently lack robust statistical tests.
Some authors have proposed the computation of p-values \citep{Li2022UntargetedBW,Tang2023DidYT}, but there is no proof that an honest model satisfies their null hypothesis -- that a model not trained on the data should not change its confidence scores or predictions less than a predetermined threshold.
The validity of the test is thus questionable, especially since they do not provide theoretical or empirical support for the choice of the threshold on confidence scores.
In contrast, our approach enables the application of standard statistical tests.

\paragraph{Data poisoning.}
Our research, akin to prior backdoor watermarking studies, repurposes data poisoning techniques for dataset ownership verification.
Data poisoning originally investigates how subtle changes to training data can compromise a model by malicious actors.
Two main types of data poisoning attacks exist: backdoor attacks, which modify the model's behavior when a specific trigger is applied to a class of data \citep{Li2019InvisibleBA,Souri2021SleeperAS}, and targeted attacks, which induce errors on a specific set of inputs \citep{shafahi2018poison,geiping2020witches}.
These, in turn, may add incorrectly labeled data \cite{Li2020OpensourcedDP,Li2022UntargetedBW} to the dataset, which impedes the stealthiness of the approach, or use clean-label modifications \citep{geiping2020witches}.
Previous studies on backdoor watermarking were mostly based on backdoor attacks, attempting to predictably degrade performance when a trigger is introduced to the data.
In contrast, we aim to design models that predict specific key labels in response to key patterns, which is more similar to targeted poisoning attacks -- the difference with a poisoning attack being that we alter the behavior on randomly generated patterns rather than legitimate data.
Our algorithmic approach leverages gradient matching techniques developed in the clean-label data poisoning literature, for both targeted and backdoor attacks \citep{geiping2020witches,Souri2021SleeperAS}.

\paragraph{Membership inference attacks.}
The goal of membership inference attacks is to reveal confidential information by inferring which data points were in the training set, usually recognized by low-loss inputs \citep{shokri2017membership, watson2021importance}.
These methods do not offer any theoretical membership certificate, since a model might have low loss on a sample regardless of whether this sample was actually part of the training set.
They are therefore not applicable to dataset ownership verification.




\section{Data Taggants}

\label{sec:method}

In the application scenario we consider, Alice  wants to publish online a dataset $\mathcal{D}_{A}$.
Alice suspects that once $\mathcal{D}_{A}$ is published, Bob will train a model $\mathcal{M}^{B}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{A}$.
Given \textbf{black-box} access (top-$k$ predictions) to Bob's (future) model $\mathcal{M}^{B}$, Alice wants to mark her dataset in order to determine if $\mathcal{M}^{B}$ has been trained on $\mathcal{D}_{A}$.

We propose a solution to Alice's problem called \textit{data taggants}, which alters the dataset to mark models trained on it.
Data taggants uses data poisonning to induce a certain behavior on Bob's model, and statistical tests to detect if Bob's model displays said behavior.
To ensure \textbf{stealthiness}, we take inspiration from a \textit{clean-label} data poisoning, which leaves labels untouched \citep{geiping2020witches}.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:sign}

Let us denote Alice's original dataset by $\mathcal{D}_{A} = (x_{i}, y_{i})_{i=1}^{N} \in (\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y})^{N}$ , where $N$ is the number of samples, $\mathcal{X}$ is the set of images and $\mathcal{Y}$ is the set of possible labels. The process of adding data taggants is the following (Figure \ref{fig:fig1}):
\begin{enumerate}%
    \item Alice generates a set of $K$ secret \textbf{keys}: $\mathcal{D}_{(key)} =  (x^{(key)}_{i}, y^{(key)}_{i})_{i=1}^K \in (\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y})^{K}$;
    \item Alice \textbf{signs} her dataset by perturbating the images in a small subset $\mathcal{D}_{S} \subseteq \mathcal{D}_{A}$ of size $S$ called the \textbf{signing set}. The perturbation of images in $\mathcal{D}_{S}$ is called the \textbf{signature}, while \textbf{data taggants} refer to the modified signing set. 
    \item Alice shares a dataset $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{A}$, equal to $\mathcal{D}_{A}$ with the modified $\mathcal{D}_{S}$.
\end{enumerate}
The goal of the taggants is to have models trained on $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{A}$ predict $y^{(key)}_{i}$ in response to $x^{(key)}_{i}$, while being \textbf{stealth} so that Bob cannot easily remove it. We first formalize Alice's goal in an ideal scenario where she knows in advance the details of Bob's training pipeline and discuss our more practical solution afterwards. 
Let us denote by $\mathcal{L}^{B}_{\theta}$ the loss of Bob's model with parameter $\theta$. We use $t$ to denote a data augmentation sampled according to Bob's recipe and $\mathbb{E}_t[.]$ the expectation over this random sampling. 
Alice defines a constrained set of image perturbations $\mathcal{C} = \{ \delta \in \mathbb{R}^{d} / \| \delta \|_{\infty} \leq \varepsilon \}$, where $\varepsilon>0$ is kept small to ensure stealthiness. The space of possible signatures is then $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}= \{ \Delta =(\delta_{j})_{j=1}^N \in \mathcal{C}^N | \forall j \notin \mathcal{D}_{S}, \delta_{j} = 0 \}$. Alice aims to find the signature $\Delta$ which minimizes the loss on secret keys of Bob's model after training on $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{A}$. This corresponds to the following bilevel optimization problem:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:optimization_problem_sign}
    \min_{\Delta \in \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathcal{L}^{B}_{\theta^*(\Delta)}(x^{(key)}_{i}, y^{(key)}_{i}) \quad \text{s.t.} \qquad \theta^* (\Delta) \in \arg \min_{\theta} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \mathbb{E}_t\big[\mathcal{L}^{B}_{\theta}(t(x_{j} + \delta_{j}), y_{j})\big].
\end{align}
Since solving the bilevel optimization problem above is too costly in terms of compute, we use a variant of gradient matching \citep{geiping2020witches}, where the goal is to find $\Delta$ such that, when $\theta\approx\theta^* (\Delta)$,
$\displaystyle
    \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{B}_{\theta}(x^{(key)}_{i}, y^{(key)}_{i}) \approx \frac{1}{S} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{D}_{S}} \mathbb{E}_t\big[\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{B}_{\theta}(t(x_{j} + \delta_{j}), y_{j})\big].
$

In reality, Alice does not know in advance Bob's training algorithm. There may also be multiple Bobs using different training recipes. Our approach involves a model and a set of data augmentation that Alice uses as a surrogate to Bob's training pipeline. We later study, in our experiments, the robustness of our approach to variations in Alice and Bob's models and data augmentations.

\subsection{Signing the data}
The starting point of our approach is that Alice trains a model on her original dataset.
We now give the details of the various steps to craft the taggants. We denote by $\theta^*$ the parameters of Alice's model, and $\mathcal{L}^{A}_{\theta^*}$ its loss function.

\paragraph{Generating the secret keys.}

To prevent from negatively impacting model performances, we choose key images $x^{(key)}_i$ to be \textbf{out-of-distribution} data points. More precisely, we generate key images by sampling each pixel value uniformly and sample key labels $y^{(key)}_i$ uniformly at random in $\mathcal{Y}$.

\paragraph{Parallelized and clean-label perturbations.} 
We evenly split the signing set into K parts  $(\mathcal{D}_{S_{i}})_{i=1}^K$, where $\mathcal{D}_{S_{i}}$ is associated to key $i$. Following clean-label data poisoning \citep{geiping2020witches}, each sample $(x_j, y_j)$ in $\mathcal{D}_{S_{i}}$ satisfies $y_j=y^{(key)}_i$. With this split, the taggant optimization problem, described later, is decomposable into one problem for each key, which we solve in parallel.
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{assets/figure_method2.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of our method: Alice optimizes image-wise signatures for images in the signing set by maximizing the alignment between the gradients of the signed images $\nabla_{\theta}^{(signed)}$, and the gradient of the key $\nabla_{\theta}^{(key)}$. The resulting images and their labels are the \textbf{data taggant}.}
    \label{fig:method2}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Taggant objective function with differentiable data augmentations.} We approximate $\mathbb{E}_{t} \left [ \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{A}_{\theta^*}(t(x_{j}+\delta_{j}), y_{j}) \right ]$ by repeating each sample $R$ times, applying $R$ randomly sampled data augmentations $t_r, r\in\{1,\ldots,R\}$, and averaging the resulting gradients. We use differentiable versions of common data augmentations to optimize through them. The taggant objective function $\mathcal{T}(\Delta)$ is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:method2}, computes the perturbation by aligning the gradients of the keys with the gradient of perturbed images. More precisely, $\mathcal{T}(\Delta) =\sum_{i=1}^K\mathcal{T}_{i}$ with:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:poisoning_loss_sign}
\mathcal{T}_{i}(\Delta) = - \cos \Big (\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{A}_{\theta}(x^{(key)}_{i}, y^{(key)}_{i}), \sum\limits_{j \in \mathcal{D}_{S_{i}}} \frac{1}{R} \sum\limits_{r=1}^{R} \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}^{A}_{\theta}(t_r(x_{j}+\delta_{j}), y_{j})\Big )
\end{align}

\paragraph{Perceptual loss.}

To improve the \textbf{stealthiness} of our approach, we add a term of differentiable perceptual loss $\mathcal{L}_{perc}$ to the taggant function, using the LPIPS metric \cite{zhang2018unreasonable}, which relies on the visual features extracted by a VGG model.
Given a weight $\lambda$ for $\mathcal{L}_{perc}$, the final optimization problem for taggants is
$     \min\limits_{\Delta \in \mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}} \frac{1}{K} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \mathcal{T}_{i}(\Delta) + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{perc}(\Delta)
$.

\paragraph{Random Restarts.}
Since the problem is non-convex, the algorithm may be trapped in local minima. In practice, we observed that using multiple random restarts of initial signatures for each key improved performance. We chose the best according to the taggant objective function.

\subsection{Detection}

The detection phase (\circled{2} in Figure \ref{fig:fig1}) relies on checking a suspicious model's predictions  on the secret keys. In order to control for false positives, we determine the key accuracy of an honest model.

\paragraph{Null hypothesis.}

Our null hypothesis is $\mathcal{H}_{0}$: ``model $h$ was not trained on the signed dataset''.
Given the set of keys $\mathcal{D}_{K}$ where the key labels are chosen uniformly at random, under the null hypothesis, model $h$ cannot have better-than-chance probability of predicting $y^{(key)}_i$ given $x^{(key)}_i$.

Let us denote by $\text{top-}k(h, \mathcal{D}_{K})$ the number of keys $i$ for which $y^{(key)}_i$ is in the top-$k$ predictions for $x^{(key)}_i$. Under the null hypothesis, $\text{top-}k(h, \mathcal{D}_{K})$ follows a binomial distribution of parameter $k/|\mathcal{Y}|$ where $|\mathcal{Y}|$ is the number of possible labels. The p-value of the binomial test is then given by the tail of a random variable $Z$ following a binomial distribution with parameters $\text{Binomial}(K, k/| \mathcal{Y} |)$:
\begin{align}\nonumber
    \text{p-value}\big(\text{top-}k(h, \mathcal{D}_{K})\big) = \mathbb{P} (Z \geq \text{top-}k(h, \mathcal{D}_{K})) = \sum_{z=\text{top-}k(h, \mathcal{D}_{K})}^{K} \binom{K}{z} \Big(\frac{k}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\Big)^z \Big(\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|-k}{|\mathcal{Y}|}\Big)^{K-z}
\end{align}
This test only requires top-$k$ predictions, which are often available in black-box scenarios (e.g., restricted API access to the model). 




\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiments}
In this section, we empirically evaluate the effectiveness, stealthiness, and robustness of data taggants.

\paragraph{Experimental setup.}
When generating taggants, the signing sets' sizes use a budget $B$ of the total dataset size, $S = B \times N$, with $B = 10^{-3} $ unless stated otherwise.
We generate 20 secret keys, using 8 random restart per key and keep the 10 best keys according to the taggant objective function.
The weight of the perceptual loss is fixed to $\lambda = 0.01$.
These parameters were chosen in preliminary experiments and fixed afterwards.
$\varepsilon$ in the constraint set $\mathcal{C}$ is fixed to $16/255$, a common value in the data poisoning literature.

In each experiment, we train one model for Alice and run the detection test on 4 repetitions of Bob's training 4 using different random initializations to compute standard deviations.
Similarly to \cite{sablayrolles2020radioactive}, we combine the  p-values of the 4 tests with Fisher's method \cite{fisher1970statistical}.
The result is denoted $\log_{10} p$ and is commensurable to the base-10 logarithm of a p-value.
Given the large number of experiments, we train all models for 100 epochs to save compute, even though the best performances for DeIT are achieved after 800 epochs.
As sanity check, we confirm with a run of 800 epochs that the effectiveness results hold.

\subsection{Effectiveness}

We first compare the effectiveness of data taggants to two baselines.
First, 
\textbf{``naive canary''}, where copies of the private keys are added into the training set.
This serves as a topline in terms of detection performance, but it is not viable as tracing mechanism since it is not stealth at all.
Second,
\textbf{``transparency''}, where we linearly interpolate between the keys and images of the signing set with a weight $\gamma=0.2$, $x'=\gamma x^{(key)} + (1 - \gamma) x$.
This value for $\gamma$ was chosen by visual inspection, as a small value for which the key is still visible.
In this comparison, we use DeIT-small models with the three-augment (3A) data augmentation \citep{touvron2022deit} for Alice and Bob, with three different budgets $B$, $0.1\%$, $0.01\%$ and $0.001\%$. 

 First, we observe that all methods have roughly the same validation acuracy as a model trained on clean data (differences within noise). In particular, this suggests that our taggants do not negatively impact model performances at these budget levels. 
In terms of detection, Naive canary works best as expected, with perfect key top-10 accuracy for $B\geq 0.01\%$. While Transparency works better than data taggants for smaller budgets, data taggants has much higher top-10 accuracy for $B=0.1\%$ ($87.5\%$ \emph{vs} $55.0\%$). In addition, the PSNR (signal to noise ratio) is lower for transparency, which suggests that with a budget as small as $0.1\%$, data taggants already dominate the transparency baseline in this experimental setting, and are able to detect dishonest models with very high confidence ($\log_{10}p=-59.6$).

We perform an additional comparison with another baseline where test data points are used as keys rather than random patterns, which is similar to repurposing a data poisoning attack to dataset ownership verification. Table~\ref{tab:3a_baselines_witches} shows the results. With comparable PSNR, using random keys leads to much better detection accuracy and confidence for $B=0.1\%$ than using test data, justifying our design choice.

As a best effort, we address the scenario where Bob tries to locate the signed data using either visual inspection, or, following \citep{Tang2023DidYT}, using anomaly detection algorithms. Similarly to the previous section, we use taggants crafted by DeIT-small using 3A augmentation, with a budget $B=0.1\%$.

\paragraph{Visual inspection.}

We observe that neither method detect data taggants -- less than $1.1\%$ taggant detection rate for the best setting of $k$ in k-NN, when considering the top-1000 samples with highest outlier scores. Interestingly, when computing DBSCAN for different detection thresholds (Figure \ref{fig:ood_detect} (b)), we observe that DBSCAN exhibit performances that are significantly worse than random. Our explanation is that DBSCAN computes clusters and select outliers as isolated points or clusters. By manually analyzing the clusters, we observe that some of them contain a lot of data taggants, most likely because their embeddings are lumped together and do not appear as anomalies.

On the one hand, these results suggest that OOD detection based on clustering is not a proper approach to detect the taggants. It suggests however that if Bob has sufficient resources, he may try to manually locate clusters of signed images by visual inspection, which is less costly than inspecting individual images. We leave this direction as an open question for future work.

\paragraph{Robustness ``stress test'': different model architectures and data augmentation.}
 
We consider two families of models (DeIT and ResNet) of various sizes.
We measure intra-family transferability by validating the protected dataset generated from each model onto each other model of the same family (DeIT and ResNet).
We also measure inter-family transferability by crafting a protected dataset with ResNet-18 and DeIT-tiny models and validating it on all the models of the other family (resp. DeIT and ResNet). The results are shown in Table \ref{tab:stresstest}. Overall, we see good intra-family transferability, with larger DeIT models being more sensitive to taggants than smaller DeIT models. Interestingly, the trend is reversed for ResNets, with smaller ResNets being more sensitive. Across architectures, DeIT-tiny to ResNets or ResNets to DeIT-tiny, the results are less conclusive even though the top-10 accuracy is still $>0$. All in all, these results suggest that the taggants are robust even in this worst-case scenario, but getting high-confidence when Alice and Bob use different architectures and data augmentations is an interesting avenue for future work.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}\label{sec:limitations}

This article presents a dataset ownership verification approach that marks a trained model.
Current results show a slight degradation in validation accuracy compared to training on the clean dataset, which future works should try to reduce.
While we show the robustness of our method through different transferability results against different model architectures and training recipe, further experiments would be required to evaluate the effectiveness of our approach when Bob trains his model on a subset or a superset of our protected dataset.
Particularly, future work should study the efficiency of our method when Bob trains his model on an aggregate of different datasets containing data taggants.


\section{Conclusion}

\label{sec:limitations}

We proposed data taggants, a new approach to dataset ownership verification and designed mostly for image classification datasets. Data taggants are hidden in a dataset through gradient matching, in order to mark models trained on them. Our approach shows promising results, with very high detection confidence of dishonest models in favorable settings, without affecting classification performance. 

We highlight the difficulty of assessing stealthiness, a challenge that is likely faced by other related domains such as data poisoning and adversarial examples. 
Through extensive robustness tests, we also show the relevance of data taggants in difficult settings, and identify promising avenues for future work such as designing taggants that robustly mark models across large variations in training settings.
