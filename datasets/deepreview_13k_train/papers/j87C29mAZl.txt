\title{On Expressive Power of Looped Transformers: Theoretical Analysis and Enhancement via Timestep Encoding}

\begin{abstract}
Looped Transformers offer advantages in parameter efficiency and Turing completeness. However, their expressive power for function approximation and approximation rate remains underexplored. In this paper, we establish approximation rates of Looped Transformers by defining the concept of the modulus of continuity for sequence-to-sequence functions. This reveals a limitation specific to the looped architecture. That is, the analysis prompts us to incorporate scaling parameters for each loop, conditioned on timestep encoding. Experimental results demonstrate that increasing the number of loops enhances performance, with further gains achieved through the timestep encoding architecture.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Transformers~\citep{Vaswani2017AttentionIA} have become the standard neural network architecture for a wide range of machine learning tasks, such as natural language processing and computer vision. Looped Transformers have an architecture composed of fixed-size Transformer layers, where the output is fed back into the input. 
This structure offers advantages over standard Transformers, such as inductive bias, parameter efficiency, and Turing completeness.
\citet{dehghani2018universal} first introduced the idea of incorporating recursive inductive bias into Transformers, aiming to address tasks that RNNs handle more easily. Looped Transformers are also related to weight-tying Transformers~\citep {Lan2020ALBERT:}, demonstrating comparable performance to standard Transformers with fewer parameters. More recently, \citet{Giannou2023LoopedTA} theoretically demonstrated that the recursive structure of Looped Transformers allows them to function as Turing machines.
In summary, Looped Transformers are more parameter-efficient and potentially more expressive than standard Transformers, enhancing their generalization capabilities.

The expressive power of standard Transformers has been extensively explored, showing that they can approximate continuous permutation-equivariant functions on compact domains~\citep{Yun2020Are, kim2023provable, takakura23a, jiang2024, kajitsuka2024are}. In addition, their approximation rate has been studied: \citet{takakura23a, jiang2024} established it by identifying the properties of the target functions, revealing the types of functions that Transformers can approximate effectively.
In contrast, the expressive power of Looped Transformers in function approximation remains unexplored. Due to the structural constraints imposed by weight-tying, which limit their flexibility, existing universal approximation theories for Transformers cannot be directly applied. Moreover, the approximation rate and the appropriate properties of the target functions for Looped Transformers have yet to be investigated.
Recently, \citet{zhang23} first explored the expressive power of \emph{looped} models. They established an approximation rate for ReLU networks for continuous functions in terms of loop counts and modulus of continuity. 
Still, this remains unclear in the case of Looped Transformers.

In this paper, we derive the approximation rate of Looped Transformers for continuous sequence-to-sequence functions by defining the concept of \emph{sequence continuity}, \emph{contextual continuity}, and \emph{token continuity}.
This reveals a limitation specific to the looped architecture. That is, the analysis prompts us to incorporate scaling parameters for each loop, conditioned on timestep encoding.


\section{Background}


We define the Transformer architecture in~\Cref{sec:tf} and Looped Transformers in~\Cref{sec:looped}. We then introduce related work of theoretical analysis on function approximation power of Transformers in~\Cref{sec: related works}.

\subsection{Transformer architecture}
\label{sec:tf}

Given an input sequence $\mX \in \R^{d \times N}$, composed of $N$ token embedding of dimension size $d$, the self-attention layers with $h$ heads and head size $s$, and the feed-forward layer with width size $q$, are defined as follows:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:attn}
    \attn(\mX)
    &= \sum_{i=1}^h \mW_{i}^{(O)}
    \left(\mW_{i}^{(V)}(\mX) \right)
    \sigma_S \left[
    \left(\mW_{i}^{(K)}(\mX) \right)^\top
    \left(\mW_{i}^{(Q)}(\mX) \right)
    \right]
    \in \R^{d \times N},\\
    \ff(\mX_{:,i}) &= \mW^{2} \cdot \relu (\mW^{(1)} \cdot \mX_{:,i} + \vb^{(1)} ) + \vb^{(2)} \in \R^d,
\end{align}
where $\mW_{i}^{(V)},\, \mW_{i}^{(K)},\, \mW_{i}^{(Q)} \in \R^{s \times d}, \mW_{i}^{(O)} \in \R^{d \times s}, \mW^{(1)} \in \R^{q \times d}, \mW^{(2)} \in \R^{d \times q}, \vb^{(1)} \in \R^q, \vb^{(2)} \in \R^d$ are parameters, $\relu$ denotes $\ReLU$ function, and $\sigma_S$ denotes a softmax operator applied to the columns of the input matrix. 

The Transformer block $\tf(\mX): \R^{d \times N} \to \R^{d \times N}$ is defined by:
\begin{align}
    \mX' &= \mX + \attn(\mX),\\
    \tf(\mX) &= \mX' + \bm{\ff}(\mX').
\end{align}
where $\bm{\ff}$ represent token-wise $\ff$. In other words
\begin{equation}
    \tf = (\mathrm{id} + \bm{\ff}) \circ (\mathrm{id} + \attn),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{id}$ denote the identity mapping. For simplicity, we omit the domain of definition.

For the analysis of expressive power in \Cref{sec:unversalapproximators}, we use the hardmax operator $\sigma_H$ instead of $\sigma_S$ and exclude Layer Normalizations as in previous studies~\citep{Yun2020Are,kim2023provable}. %The reason is that the softmax function can be interpreted as an approximation of the hardmax function; also softmax can be made arbitrarily close to the hardmax by scaling the temperature.For simplicity, we also exclude Layer Normalizations from the analysis in \Cref{sec:unversalapproximators}.

\subsection{Looped Transformer}
\label{sec:looped}

Looped Transformers feed output back into input, defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:Looped}
	\bm{\mathcal{L}}_2 \circ \tf \circ \cdots \circ  \tf \circ \bm{\mathcal{L}}_1,
\end{equation}
where $\bm{\mathcal{L}}_2$ and $\bm{\mathcal{L}}_1$ denote token-wise linear layers.
Let $\tf^{\circ r}$ denote the $r$-times composition of $\tf$. We refer to $\bm{\mathcal{L}_}{2} \circ \tf^{\circ r} \circ \bm{\mathcal{L}}_{1}$ as a Looped Transformer with $r$-loops.

Looped Transformers have been studied in recent years, regarding their parameter efficiency~\citep{Lan2020ALBERT:, takase2021lessons, bae2024relaxed} and generalization capabilities~\citep{dehghani2018universal, fan2024length}. Other recent works~\citep{Giannou2023LoopedTA, gatmiry2024can, luca24a, gao2024, giannou2024newtons} have investigated their performance on iterative algorithms, including in-context learning and graph algorithm. In particular, \citet{yang2024looped} empirically demonstrated that increasing the number of loop iterations enhances performance on complex tasks. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no studies on the expressive power or approximation rate of Looped Transformers in function approximation.

\subsection{Theoretical analysis on function approximation}
\label{sec: related works}
The universal approximation theorem for fully connected neural networks, as demonstrated by~\citet{cybenko1989, hornik1989multilayer}, shows that networks of sufficient size can approximate certain classes of functions with arbitrarily low error.
Transformers are universal approximators of sequence-to-sequence functions~\citep{Yun2020Are, takakura23a, jiang2024, kajitsuka2024are}, and their memorization capacity has also been studied~\citep{kim2023provable}.
Recently,~\cite{zhang23} revealed that even single fixed-size networks can be universal approximators. They explored the surprising potential of composition (loop) and derived the approximation rate in terms of the number of loop counts and modulus of continuity of the target function.


\section{Approximation Rate of Looped Transformers}

\label{sec:unversalapproximators}

We establish the approximation rate of Looped Transformers by defining the modulus of continuity for continuous sequence-to-sequence functions. We begin with preliminaries of Transformers in~\Cref{subsec:preliminary}.
Then, we present and explain three types of continuity for sequence-to-sequence functions, which determine the approximation rate.
In \Cref{subsec:mainresult}, we present our main results on approximation rate, along with some implications. In \Cref{sec:proof_sketch}, we provide a proof sketch with a detailed explanation, outlining three steps for approximation and deriving the approximation rate.

\subsection{Preliminary}
\label{subsec:preliminary}
Transformers are {\em permutation-equivariant}, so we define the target function class as follows.
\begin{definition}[\citet{Yun2020Are, kim2023provable}] 
A function $f : \R^{d \times N} \to \R^{d \times N}$ is said to be {\em permutation equivariant} if for any permutation matrix $\mP$, we have $f(\mX \mP) = f(\mX) \mP$.
Let $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PE}}([0,1]^{d \times N})$ denote the set of permutation equivariant and continuous functions. 
\end{definition}
To approximate sequence-to-sequence functions, networks need to map each token within the context of sequences, formulated as {\em contextual mapping}.
\begin{definition}[\citet{Yun2020Are, kim2023provable}] 
\label{def:context-mapping}
Consider a finite set $\sL \subset \R^{d \times N}$.
A map $\mathrm{CM}: \sL \to \R^{1 \times N}$ defines a {\em contextual mapping} if the map satisfies the following:
\begin{enumerate}%[leftmargin=1.1em]
    \item For any $\mL \in \sL$, the $N$ entries in $\mathrm{CM}(\mL)$ are all distinct.
    \item For any $\mL, \mL' \in \sL$, with $\mL \neq \mL'$, all entries of $\mathrm{CM}(\mL)$ and $\mathrm{CM}(\mL')$ are distinct.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

Let $\norm{\cdot}_p$ denote the entry-wise $L^p$-norm for a vector for any $p \in [1, \infty)$.
\begin{definition}[Norm of function]
We define the $L^p$-norm for a function $f$ on $[0,1]^{d \times N}$ by:
\begin{equation*}
    L^p([0,1]^{d \times N}) \coloneq \Big (\int \norm{f(\mX)}_p^p d\mX \Big )^{1/p}.
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}

\subsection{Definition of continuity for sequence-to-sequence functions}
\label{subsec:continuity}
The modulus of continuity of a continuous function $g: [0,1]^d \to \R$ can be defined as
\begin{equation*}
	\sup\big\{|g(\vx)-g(\vx')|: \|\vx-\vx'\|_2\le t,\ \vx,\vx'\in [0,1]^{d}\big\}.
\end{equation*}
It can be extended for sequence-to-sequence functions $f: [0,1]^{d\times N} \to \R^{d\times N}$ as follows.
\begin{definition}[Modulus of Sentence Continuity]
\label{def:sentence_continuity}
Given a sequence-to-sequence continuous function $f:[0,1]^{d \times N} \to \R^{d \times N}$, the modulus of \emph{sentence continuity} is defined by:
\begin{equation*}
	\omega_f(t)\coloneqq \sup\big\{\|f(\mX)-f(\mX')\|_p: \|\mX-\mX'\|_2\le t,\ \mX,\mX'\in [0,1]^{d \times N}\big\}.
\end{equation*}
\end{definition}
We illustrate what this continuity represents and why it is referred to as \emph{sequence continuity}. If we consider the following two sentences: (1) I read books, and (2) He writes music, the sequence continuity measures how much the overall meaning of a sequence changes:

\begin{minipage}{\textwidth}
    \centering
    $\text{`I read books'} \ \overset{\Delta}{\longleftrightarrow} \  \text{`He writes music'}$,
\end{minipage}

in proportion to the differences between the input sequences, measured by comparing each token:

\begin{minipage}{0.33\textwidth}
    \centering
    $\text{`I'} \ \overset{\Delta}{\longleftrightarrow} \  \text{`he'}$,
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.33\textwidth}
    \centering
    $\text{`read'} \ \overset{\Delta}{\longleftrightarrow} \  \text{`write'}$, and
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.33\textwidth}
    \centering
    $\text{`book'} \ \overset{\Delta}{\longleftrightarrow} \   \text{`music'}$.
\end{minipage}

We found that this concept is insufficient to derive the approximation rate of Looped Transformers, primarily because one key characteristic of the Transformer architecture is the sharing of parameters across all tokens: specifically, the feed-forward layers are applied token-wise. In other words, Transformers output token embeddings in the context of sequences for each token. 

This observation leads us to define two additional forms of continuity: \emph{contextual continuity} and \emph{token continuity}, which we found to determine the approximation rate of Looped Transformers.
\begin{restatable}[Modulus of Contextual Continuity]{definition}{modulus}
\label{def:contextual_continuity}
Given a sequence-to-sequence continuous function $f:[0,1]^{d \times N} \to \R^{d \times N}$, the modulus of \emph{contextual continuity} is defined by:
\begin{equation*}
	\omega^{cont}_f(t)\coloneqq \sup_{n, \mX, \mX'}\big\{\|f(\mX)_{:,n}-f(\mX')_{:,n}\|_p: \|\mX-\mX'\|_2\le t,\ \mX_{:,n} = \mX'_{:,n},\ \mX,\mX'\in [0,1]^{d \times N} \big\},
\end{equation*}
\end{restatable}
\begin{definition}[Modulus of Token Continuity]
Given a sequence-to-sequence continuous function $f:[0,1]^{d \times N} \to \R^{d \times N}$, the modulus of \emph{token continuity} is defined by:
\begin{align*}
	\omega^{tok}_f(t)\coloneqq \sup_{n, \mX, \mX'}\big\{\|f(\mX)_{:,n}&-f(\mX')_{:,n}\|_p: \|\mX_{:,n}-\mX'_{:,n}\|_2\le t, \\  
 &\mX_{:,m} = \mX'_{:,m} \text{ for any } m \neq n, \ \mX,\mX'\in [0,1]^{d \times N}\big\},
\end{align*}
\end{definition}
The modulus of \emph{contextual continuity} measures the variation in the output of token embeddings induced by a perturbation of context. For example, we consider the following three sentences: 

\begin{minipage}{0.33\textwidth}
    \centering
    (1) I \underline{write} papers,
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.33\textwidth}
    \centering
    (2) You \underline{write} books, and 
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.33\textwidth}
    \centering
    (3) Mozart \underline{writes} music.
\end{minipage}

The output embedding of the second token, `write', should be similar in sentences (1) and (2) due to their similar context. In contrast, a larger variation in context, as seen in sentence (3), can induce a significant variation in the output of token embedding.

On the other hand, the modulus of \emph{token continuity} measures the variation in the output embedding caused by perturbations to the token itself within the same context. For instance, we consider the sentences:

\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
    \centering
    (1) I \underline{write} papers, and
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
    \centering
    (2) I \underline{draft} papers.
\end{minipage}

In this example, both sentences have the same context, but the verb (`write' vs. `draft') variation reflects a perturbation in the token itself. The modulus of \emph{token continuity} quantifies how this change influences the output embeddings.
A small modulus of \emph{token continuity} means that the output embeddings of `write' and `draft' are expected to be similar.

\subsection{Main Result}
\label{subsec:mainresult}

The following main theorem demonstrates the approximation rate of Looped Transformers in terms of the modulus of continuity and the number of loops.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{universal}
\label{thm:universal}
Given a function $f \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PE}}([0,1]^{d \times N})$, for any $r \in \mathbb{N}$, there exists a Looped Transformer $\mathrm{TF}:\R^{(24d+1)\times N}\to\R^{(24d+1)\times N}$ of single head, head size $s=1$, and width size $q=99d+8$, and two affine linear maps $\mathcal{L}_1:\R^d\to\R^{24d+1}$ and $\mathcal{L}_2:\R^{24d+1}\to\R^d$ such that
\begin{equation*}
    \big\|\bm{\mathcal{L}}_2\circ \mathrm{TF}^{\circ r}\circ \bm{\mathcal{L}}_1-f\big\|_{L^p([0,1]^{d \times N})}
    \leq \omega^{tok}_f(\delta\sqrt{d}) + \omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{Nd}) + \omega_f(\delta \sqrt{Nd}) + \mathcal{O}(\delta^{d}),
\end{equation*}
for $\delta = \big((r-N)/2\big)^{-1/((N+1)d+1)}$, where $\bm{\mathcal{L}}_1$ and $\bm{\mathcal{L}}_2$ represent the token-wise applications of $\mathcal{L}_1$ and $\mathcal{L}_2$, respectively.
\end{restatable}

Thus, Looped Transformers are universal approximators.
\begin{corollary}
The hypothesis space of Looped Transformers $\mathcal{H}$, defined by
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{H} \coloneqq \Big\{\bm{\mathcal{L}_2} \circ \tf^{\circ r} \circ \bm{\mathcal{L}_1}:\, r\in \N,\,
         \bm{\mathcal{L}}_2 \tn{ and } \bm{\mathcal{L}}_1 \tn{are token-wise affine linear maps}\Big\},
\end{equation*}
are dense in $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{PE}}([0,1]^{d \times N})$ in terms of the $L^p([0,1]^{d \times N})$ norm.
\end{corollary}

These results provide us with some insights:
\begin{list}{\textbullet}{\leftmargin=1.1em \itemindent=0em \itemsep=1pt}
    \item A function with a small modulus of continuities, \eg, robust to contextual perturbations, is suited for approximation by Looped Transformers.
    \item The total parameter count is $\mathcal{O}(d)$, independent of both $\delta$ and $N$, highlighting the parameter efficiency of Looped Transformers.
    \item The optimal approximation rate of ReLU networks of size $n$ is $\mathcal{O}(\omega_f(\mathcal{O}(n^{-2/d})))$ for continuous functions on $[0,1]^{d}$ \citep{Yarotsky19optimal}; the exponential rate is unavoidable.
\end{list}

\subsection{Proof Sketch}
\label{sec:proof_sketch}
We provide a proof sketch highlighting differences from prior studies and associated difficulties.

\paragraph{Approximation with Piecewise Constant Function.}
We approximate $f \in \mathcal{F_{\mathrm{PE}}}$ with piece-wise constant function $\bar{f}: [0,1]^{d \times N} \to \R^{d \times N}$. Specifically, for $\delta^{-1} \in \mathbb{N}$, we divide the input space $[0,1]^{d \times N}$ into $\delta$-discretized cubes, denoted by $\{Q_{\bB}\}_{\bB \in \{0,1,\dotsc,\delta^{-1} - 1\}^{d\times N}}$. Each cube is associated with a representative $\hat{\mX}_{\bB} \in Q_{\bB}$. Define a piecewise constant function $\bar{f}$ for $\mX \in [0,1]^{d \times N}$ as
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{f}(\mX) = f(\hat{\mX}_{\bB}) \quad \text{where } \bB \text{ satisfies } \mX \in Q_{\bB}.
\end{equation*}
We can bound the approximation error within each cube as $ \| \bar{f}(\mX) - f(\mX) \|_p \leq \omega_f(\sqrt{\delta^2 + \cdots + \delta^2}) \leq \omega_f(\delta \cdot \sqrt{Nd})$ for any $\mX \in [0,1]^{d \times N}$.
We involve three steps to construct $\bar{f}$. The first and second steps map the input $\mX$ to the coordinates of the discretized input space, involving $\bB$. The third step approximately maps these coordinates to the target embeddings. 

\paragraph{Step 1.~Token-wise Quantization.}
 Then it maps them to an integer, referred to as a \emph{token ID}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:quant}
    \mX_{:, n} \in [0, 1]^d \to \boldsymbol{\beta} \in \{0, 1, \dotsc, \delta^{-1} -1 \}^d \to z \in \{0, 1, \dotsc, \delta^{-d} -1 \} .
\end{equation} 
The key idea behind our proof follows \citet{zhang23}; however, we cannot directly apply it here due to the need to account for skip connections. Additionally, it is necessary to consider a bijective mapping of $\bm{\beta}$ to a token ID in the $\delta^{-1}$-base system for the next step.

\paragraph{Step 2.~Contextual Mapping.}
The network performs contextual mapping, which maps $N$ token IDs to a \emph{sequence ID} in the set of $\{0, 1, \dotsc, \delta^{-Nd} - 1\}$.
Previous studies~\citep{Yun2020Are, kim2023provable} use multiple layers for constructions; however, these results do not extend to Looped Transformers for two reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(1)] \citet{Yun2020Are} used both sparse and uniform attention, whereas Looped Transformers are limited to a single fixed attention layer.
\item[(2)] \citet{kim2023provable} used $N$-layers to store $N$ parameters, whereas fixed-size $\mathcal{O}(d)$ Looped Transformers cannot store $N$ weight components. 
\end{enumerate}
While these considerations indicate some limitations of Looped Transformers, we found that Looped Transformers with $N$-loops can perform contextual mapping. The proof strategy follows \citet{kim2023provable}; however, it is necessary to update for a single Transformer block.
Let $\vz \in \{0, 1, \dotsc, \delta^{-d}-1\}^N$ represent a sequence of $N$ ordered and distinct token IDs, where $\vz_1 > \vz_2 > \cdots > \vz_N$.
The networks map the set of token IDs into sequence ID via inner product with $\vu \coloneqq (\delta^{-d(N-1)}, \dotsc,\delta^{-d}, 1) \in \R^N$ \ie
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{CM}(\vz) \coloneq \vu^\top \vz,
\end{equation*}
which satisfy
\begin{equation*}
    \norm{\vu^\top \vz- \vu^\top \vz'} > 1,  \quad \text{if }  \vz \ne \vz'.
\end{equation*}
Thus $\mathrm{CM}$ is a contextual mapping. The key point is that the network only needs to store $\delta$ to represent $\vu$, allowing it to be implemented with Looped Transformers

\paragraph{Step 3.~Token-wise Mapping.}
The network token-wise maps the coordinates of discretized regions approximately to the target token embedding. From Steps 1 and 2, each token in the input sequence is assigned a token ID with a sequence ID, where the sequence ID is consistent across all tokens. The combination of the token ID and sequence ID determines the coordinates, referred to as \emph{contextual token ID}. 

Notably, we found that the design of the \emph{contextual token ID} plays a crucial role in Looped Transformers. This comes from the constraint of looped architecture. 
Let $\calK$ denote the set of contextual token IDs, where each index is associated with a distinct cube $\bB \in \{0, 1, \dotsc, \delta^{-1} - 1\}^{d\times N}$ in the discretized space. For each $k \in \calK$, let $\mX_k$ denote the cube associated with $\bB$, \ie, $\mX_{\bB}$. Looped Transformer $\tilde{f}: \R^{d\times N} \to \R^{d\times N}$ can token-wise approximate piecewise constant function $\bar{f}$ with the error of 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:neighboring}
   \|\tilde{f}(\mX_{k})_{:,n} - \bar{f}(\mX_{k})_{:,n}\| \leq \max_{n, k'\in \calK} \| \bar{f}(\mX_{k'})_{:,n} - \bar{f}(\mX_{k'-1})_{:,n} \|_p \quad \text{for any } k \in \calK ,
\end{equation}
for $n = {0,1, \dotsc, N}$ (details are provided in ~\Cref{lemma:memorize}).
This requires us to design $\calK$ so that $\| \bar{f}(\mX_{k'})_{:,n} - \bar{f}(\mX_{k'-1})_{:,n} \|_p$ is small, \ie, the outputs of neighboring contextual token IDs are similar.
The core idea of this design is explained with illustrations, comparing the output embeddings of the underlined tokens.
\begin{enumerate}%[leftmargin=1.1em]
    \item[(1)] I \underline{write} papers. \ ; \ I write \underline{papers}. \ \ (different token ID with same sequence ID)
    \item[(2)] I \underline{write} papers. \ ; \ You \underline{write} books. \ \ (same token ID with different sequence ID)
\end{enumerate}
The contextual continuity $\omega^{cont}_f$ of \Cref{def:context-mapping} ensures that the outputs embeddings of `write' in (2) are similar. However, none of the continuity properties provide guarantees that the output embeddings of `write' and `papers' in (1) are similar.

\paragraph{Consolidation into Single Looped Transformer.}
At the end of the construction, we demonstrate that the composition of the three sub-networks from Steps 1, 2, and 3 can be realized with a single Transformer block. %\ie
The proof strategy follows \citet{zhang23}; however, it cannot be directly applied because their approach requires an additional layer.

\paragraph{Deriving Approximation Rate.}
Lastly, we estimate the approximation error of our construction and establish the approximation rate. 
In Step 2, we consider only the case where all $N$ input tokens are distinct, disregarding other cases. These cases can be treated as negligible when $\delta$ is small. The number of subsets where one of the $N$ tokens is duplicated is
\begin{equation*}
    {(\delta^{-d})}^N - \delta^{-d} \cdot (\delta^{-d}-1) \cdots (\delta^{-d}-N-1) < C\delta^{-(N-1)d},
\end{equation*}
where $C$ is a constant. The volume of these subsets is $C\delta^{-(N-1)d} / \delta^{-Nd} = C\delta^{d}$, thus the error with respect to the $L^p$ norm is $\mathcal{O}(\delta^{d})$.

\begin{figure}[t]
\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/contextual_token_ids.png}
\end{center}
\caption{Approximation error and modulus of continuity. The linear interpolation technique reduces the error by a factor of $1/\delta^{-1}$.}\label{fig:rate}
\end{figure}
In Step 3, we can bound the approximation error as follows:
\begin{align*}
    \| \tilde{f}(\mX_{k}) - \bar{f}(\mX_{k}) \|_p 
    \leq \max_{n} \| \tilde{f}(\mX_{k})_{:,n} - \bar{f}(\mX_{k})_{:,n} \|_p 
    \leq \max_{n, k'\in \calK} \| \bar{f}(\mX_{k'})_{:,n} - \bar{f}(\mX_{k'-1})_{:,n} \|_p.
\end{align*}
There are two types of error for the right-hand side term: the variation induced by contextual perturbation and the variation induced by token perturbation. 
We illustrate this with examples of each pattern, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rate}. Specifically, we consider the following three cases:
\begin{enumerate}%[leftmargin=1.1em]
    \item[(1)] I \underline{write} papers. \ ; \ I \underline{write} books. \ \ (\emph{small} perturbation of context)
    \item[(2)] You \underline{write} books. \ ; \ Mozart \underline{write} music. \ \ (\emph{large} perturbation of context)
    \item[(3)] Beethoven \underline{writes} music. ; I \underline{draft} papers. \ \ (perturbation of both token and context)
\end{enumerate}
The error in each case can be bounded with the corresponding modulus of continuity:
\begin{enumerate}%[leftmargin=1.1em]
    \item $\omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{d})$
    \item $\omega^{cont}_f(\sqrt{Nd}) \to \ \delta \omega^{cont}_f(\sqrt{Nd})$
    \item $\omega^{tok}_f(\sqrt{d}) + \omega^{cont}_f(\sqrt{Nd}) \to \delta \big( \omega^{tok}_f(\sqrt{d})) + \omega^{cont}_f(\sqrt{Nd}) \big)$
\end{enumerate}
 
Since that $\omega_f^{cont, tok}(n \cdot t) \leq n \cdot \omega_f^{cont, tok}(t)$ for any $n \in \N$ and $t \in [0, \infty)$ with $\delta<1$, we can then derive the upper bound for the three terms:
\begin{align*}
    &\max_{n, k'\in \calK} \| \bar{f}(\mX_{k'})_{:,n} - \bar{f}(\mX_{k'-1})_{:,n} \|_p \\
    &\leq \max \Big\{\omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{d}), \ \delta\omega^{cont}_f(\sqrt{Nd}), \ \delta \big( \omega^{tok}_f(\sqrt{d}) + \omega^{cont}_f(\sqrt{Nd}) \big)\Big\} \\
    &\leq \max \Big\{\omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{d}), \ \omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{Nd}), \ \omega^{tok}_f(\delta\sqrt{d}) + \omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{Nd})\Big\} \\
    &\leq \omega^{tok}_f(\delta\sqrt{d}) + \omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{Nd}),
\end{align*}

With the triangle inequality, we have an approximation error as
\begin{align}
     \| \tilde{f} - f \|_{L^p([0,1]^{d \times N})}
     &\leq \| \tilde{f}(\mX) - f(\mX) \|_p \cdot 1 \notag \\ 
     &\leq 
     \| \tilde{f}(\mX) - \bar{f}(\mX) \|_p + \| \bar{f}(\mX) - f(\mX) \|_p  \notag \\
     &\leq \max_{n, k'\in \calK} \| \bar{f}(\mX_{k'})_{:,n} - \bar{f}(\mX_{k'-1})_{:,n} \|_p + \| \bar{f}(\mX) - f(\mX) \|_p + \mathcal{O}(\delta^{d}) \notag \\
     &\leq \omega^{tok}_f(\delta\sqrt{d}) + \omega^{cont}_f(\delta\sqrt{Nd})  + \omega_f(\delta \sqrt{Nd}) + \mathcal{O}(\delta^{d}).
     \label{eq:triangle_3}
\end{align}
Then, $\delta$ is expressed in terms of the number of loops $r$ to determine the approximation rate. We use $\delta^{-1}-1$ loops for Step 1, $N$ loops for Step 2, and $2\delta^{-(N+1)d}-1$ loops for Step 3, with 1 loop required to connect each step. Thus we have
\begin{align}
    r = (\delta^{-1} - 1) + 1 + (N) + 1 + \big(2 \delta^{-(N+1)d} - 1\big) &\Leftrightarrow \delta^{-1} + 2\delta^{-(N+1)d} = r - N \notag \\
    &\Leftrightarrow \delta^{-1} \cdot 2\delta^{-(N+1)d} \ge r - N \notag \\
    &\Leftrightarrow 2\delta^{-(N+1)d-1} \ge r - N \notag \\
    &\Leftrightarrow \delta \leq {\Big(\frac{r-N}{2}\Big)}^{-1/((N+1)d+1)}.
    \label{eq:loop}
\end{align}
From Eq.~\ref{eq:triangle_3} and Eq.~\ref{eq:loop}, we can derive \Cref{thm:universal}. 

\paragraph{Summary.} Our contribution is to establish an approximation rate for Looped Transformers by identifying the continuity of sequence-to-sequence functions.
Additionally, as a technical contribution, we demonstrate that a single Looped Transformer block is sufficient for contextual mapping. While \citet{zhang23} requires three feed-forward layers of looped ReLU networks for universal approximation, we achieve this with just one layer.



\section{From Theory to Practice: Introducing Timestep Encoding}

The theoretical result in \Cref{sec:unversalapproximators} identifies a limitation of the looped architecture in its reliance on contextual and token continuity. This analysis suggests incorporating \emph{time-dependent} scaling parameters for each loop, which we implemented as a function of the timestep encoding.

\subsection{Motivation}
\paragraph{Limitation Specific to Looped Architecture.} \Cref{thm:universal} shows that the approximation rate of Looped Transformers depends on the modulus of three types of continuity. Sequence continuity relates to approximating continuous functions with piecewise constants, while contextual and token continuity dependencies are unique to the looped architecture. Previous studies~\citep{Yun2020Are,kim2023provable} show that standard Transformers lack these dependencies. This additional dependency increases approximation errors, limiting the approximation power of Looped Transformers.

We identify the cause of dependency in \textbf{Step 3: Token-wise Mapping} of the following Lemma.
\begin{restatable}{lemma}{memorize}
\label{lemma:memorize}
Given $\vy_k \in \R^d$ for $k = 0, 1, \dotsc, m-1$ with
\begin{equation*}
    |(\vy_k - \vy_{k-1})_i| \leq \varepsilon_i \quad \tn{for $k=1,2,\dotsc,m-1,$}
\end{equation*}
there exist feed-forward layer $\mathrm{FF}:\R^{14d}\to \R^{14d}$ of width size $20d$ and two affine linear maps $\calL_1:\R^{d}\to\R^{14d}$ and $\calL_2:\R^{14d}\to \R^{d}$ such that
\begin{equation*}
    |\big(\calL_2\circ (\mathrm{id} + \mathrm{FF})^{(m-1)} \circ \calL_1(k) - \vy_k\big)_i| \leq \varepsilon_i \quad \tn{for $k=0,1,\dotsc,m-1$},
\end{equation*}
for any $i = 1, 2, \dotsc, d$.
\end{restatable}
\Cref{lemma:memorize} implies that large variations in the target function, represented by discretized points $\vy_k$, lead to increased approximation error in Looped Transformers. Specifically, when outputs at nearby points vary greatly, a small approximation error cannot be guaranteed.

\paragraph{How Can We Improve the Approximation Rate of Looped Transformers?}
To address the dependency on contextual and token continuity, we introduce \emph{time-dependent} parameters for each loop. Specifically, we modify the feed-forward layers by adding a scaling vector parameter that varies with the loop index (timestep), defined as follows:
\begin{equation*}
    \ff(\mX) \to \boldsymbol\eta(t) \odot \ff(\mX) \quad \text{for the $t$-th loops},
\end{equation*}
where $\odot$ is an element-wise product, $t \in \N$ denotes the loop index (timestep), and $\boldsymbol\eta(t) \in \R^d$ represents a \emph{time-dependent} scaling parameter.
This kind of \emph{dynamic} scaling vector parameters is also used by HyperNetworks~\citep{ha2016hypernetworks} for RNN to enhance expressive power.

We show that the time-dependent Looped Transformer overcomes approximation errors from contextual and token continuity. Specifically, we can replace \Cref{lemma:memorize} with the following \Cref{lemma:time_dependent_memorize}, which demonstrates that time-dependent models can precisely approximate any target function.
\begin{restatable}{theorem}{timedependentmemorize}
\label{lemma:time_dependent_memorize}
Given $\vy_k \in \R^d$ for $k=0, \dotsc, m-1$,
there exist feed-forward layer $\mathrm{FF}:\R^{4d}\to \R^{4d}$ of width size $6d$ and $\boldsymbol\eta(t)\in\R^{4d}$ and two affine linear maps $\calL_1:\R^{d}\to\R^{4d}$ and $\calL_2:\R^{4d}\to \R^{d}$ \st
\begin{equation*}
    \calL_2\circ (\mathrm{id} + \boldsymbol\eta(m-1)\odot\mathrm{FF})\circ \cdots \circ (\mathrm{id} + \boldsymbol\eta(1)\odot\mathrm{FF}) \circ \calL_1(k) = \vy_k.
\end{equation*}
\end{restatable}
For implementation, while adding parameters for each loop is effective, the number of parameters increases with the number of loops. Therefore, we introduce timestep encoding to address this issue.

\subsection{Timestep Encoding}
We use timestep encodings to represent loop counts and to condition scaling parameters, following \cite{peebles2023scalable}, where \emph{time-dependent} Transformers are applied in diffusion models by regressing layer normalization parameters from timestep encodings. 

To condition on timesteps, frequency embeddings are processed through a two-layer MLP with hidden size matching the Transformer block and SiLU activation, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}. Let $\operatorname{TE}(t)\in \R^d$ denote timestep embeddings, defined as:
\begin{equation*}
   \operatorname{TE}(t) = \mW^{(TE)}_1 \cdot \mathrm{SiLU} (\mW^{(TE)}_2 \cdot \operatorname{PE}(t) + \vb^{(TE)}_1 ) + \vb^{(TE)}_2,
\end{equation*}
where $\mW^{(TE)}_1, \mW^{(TE)}_2 \in \R^{d \times d}$ and $\vb^{(TE)}_1, \vb^{(TE)}_2 \in \R^d$ are parameters, and $\operatorname{PE}(t) \in \R^d$ is the timestep encoding function that maps the timestep (loop index) into a $d$-dimensional embedding, \st
\begin{align*}
    \operatorname{PE}(t)_{2i} = \sin(t / 10000^{2i/d}), \quad \operatorname{PE}(t)_{2i+1} = \cos(t / 10000^{2i/d}).
\end{align*}
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.25\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.25\textwidth]{figures/arch.png}
    \caption{Timestep encoding architecture.}
    \label{fig:arch}
\end{wrapfigure}
The root mean square layer normalization (RMSNorm)~\citep{zhang2019root} has been employed in several recent large language models (LLMs), such as LLaMA~\citep{llama} and Gemma~\citep{gemma}.
RMSNorm, denoted by $\LN$, is defined as
\begin{align*}\label{eq_rmsnorm}
    \begin{split}
    \LN(\vx) = \boldsymbol\alpha \odot \frac{\vx}{\text{RMS}(\mathbf{\vx})} , \quad \text{where}~~ \text{RMS}(\mathbf{\vx}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d} \sum_{i=1}^{d} \vx_i^2}.
    \end{split}
\end{align*}
where $\boldsymbol\alpha\in\R^d$ is a gain parameter for rescaling. 

Extending standard RMSNorm, we define time-dependent RMSNorm as:
\begin{equation*}
    \LN(\vx, t) = \boldsymbol\alpha(t) \odot \frac{\vx}{\text{RMS}(\mathbf{\vx})} 
\end{equation*}
where  $\boldsymbol\alpha(t) \in \R^d$ is a time-dependent parameter generated by a \emph{hypernetowrk}.Additionally, we incorporate parameters for output scaling, defining the time-dependent Transformer block as follows:
\begin{align*}
    \mX' &= \mX + \boldsymbol\gamma_1(t) \odot \attn(\bm{\LN}_1(\mX, t)),\\
    \tf(\mX, t) &= \mX' + \boldsymbol\gamma_2(t) \odot \bm{\ff}(\bm{\LN}_2(\mX', t)),
\end{align*}
where $\boldsymbol\gamma_1(t), \boldsymbol\gamma_2(t) \in \R^d$ are time-dependent parameters applied token-wise, and $\bm{\LN_1}$ and $\bm{\LN_2}$ represent token-wise applications of ${\LN}_1$ and ${\LN}_2$, respectively.

To generate time-dependent vector parameters, we use the SiLU function and weight parameters:
\begin{equation*}
    \boldsymbol\alpha_1(t), \boldsymbol\alpha_2(t), \boldsymbol\gamma_1(t), \boldsymbol\gamma_2(t) = \mW^{(H)} \cdot \SiLU(\operatorname{TE}(t)) + \vb^{(H)},\\
\end{equation*}
where $\mW^{(H)} \in \R^{4d \times d}, \vb^{(H)} \in \R^d$ are parameters.


\section{Experiments}

Our experimental results support our theoretical findings. First, we show that increasing loop counts enhances the expressive power of Looped Transformers by evaluating dynamic programming (DP) tasks in~\Cref{sec:setting1}. Second, we observe performance gains from timestep encodings in certain DP, in-context learning, and language modeling tasks~\Cref{sec:setting2}.

\subsection{Varying Loops with Dynamic Programming}\label{sec:setting1}
DP problems were chosen for their recursive structure and their difficulty for standard Transformers without chain-of-thought~\cite{feng2023towards}. We categorize specific types of DP problems and select representative tasks from each category (details are provided in~\Cref{app:dp}).

\paragraph{Experimental setups.}
We generate $10^6$ samples for training and $10^3$ samples for testing. All tasks are trained as classification tasks using cross-entropy loss and are evaluated by best accuracy on the test sets. While our theoretical results focus on approximation power (fitting to the training set), we observe a strong correlation between training and test accuracy, suggesting that lower test accuracy reflects the approximation capacity rather than a generalization issue (see the results of ED in \Cref{app:ED}). 

We trained Looped Transformers with $5$, $10$, $50$, and $100$ loops, both with and without time dependency (the configuration is provided in \Cref{app:conf_dp}. We incrementally increased the number of loops, stopping when performance exceeded $90$\% or saturated due to limited computational resources.
In addition, to validate the research significance of Looped Transformers, we compared them to a $12$-layer Transformer. The Transformer’s limitations stem not from approximation but from generalization capabilities, which fall outside the scope of our theoretical results.
