\title{ConLUX: Concept-Based Local Unified Explanations}

\begin{abstract}

With the rapid advancements of various machine learning models, there is a significant demand for model-agnostic explanation techniques, which can explain these models across different architectures.
Mainstream model-agnostic explanation techniques generate local explanations based on basic features (e.g., words for text models and (super-)pixels for image models). However, these explanations often do not align with the decision-making processes of the target models and end-users, resulting in explanations that are unfaithful and difficult for users to understand.
On the other hand, concept-based techniques provide explanations based on high-level features (e.g., topics for text models and objects for image models), but most are model-specific or require additional pre-defined external concept knowledge. 
To address this limitation, we propose \toolname, a general framework to provide concept-based local explanations for any machine learning models. 
Our key insight is that we can automatically extract high-level concepts from large pre-trained models, and uniformly extend existing local model-agnostic techniques to provide unified concept-based explanations.
We have instantiated \toolname on four different types of explanation techniques: LIME, Kernel SHAP, Anchor, and LORE, and applied these techniques to text and image models.
Our evaluation results demonstrate that 1) compared to the vanilla versions, \toolname offers more faithful explanations and makes them more understandable to users, and 2) by offering multiple forms of explanations, \toolname outperforms state-of-the-art concept-based explanation techniques specifically designed for text and image models, respectively.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\label{sec:intro}

As machine learning models become more complex and popular, it has become an emerging topic to explain the rationale behind their decisions.
In particular,
as the structure of machine learning models diversifies and evolves rapidly, and effective closed-source models (e.g., GPT-4~\citep{gpt4} and Gemini~\citep{geminiteam2024geminifamilyhighlycapable}) become more prevalent,
model-agnostic explanation techniques show their appeal due to their adaptability to various models and tasks~\citep{survey_model_agnostic}.
These techniques consider target models as black boxes, so they can explain any machine learning model without requiring any knowledge of the model's internal structure. 
This paper addresses the challenge of incorporating high-level concepts into local model-agnostic techniques to explain the decision-making processes of various machine learning models, including large language models (LLMs).

To faithfully explain the behavior of machine learning models, it is essential to provide explanations built from language components aligned with the decision process of the target models; to make explanations easy to understand, it is also crucial to provide explanations built from user-friendly language components. 
Unfortunately, mainstream model-agnostic explanation techniques often fail to meet both requirements, as they provide explanations built from basic features (e.g., words for text models and (super-)pixels for image models)~\citep{LIME, SHAP, Anchors, lore}. 
In contrast, many concept-based techniques provide explanations based on high-level features (e.g., topics in texts and objects in images)~\citep{concept_survey}.
These techniques either utilize the internal information of the target models like gradients, activations, and attention weights~\citep{zhang2021invertibleconceptbasedexplanationscnn,conceptSHAP, Yeh_Kim_Arik_Li_Ravikumar_Pfister_2019, Cunningham_Ewart_Riggs_Huben_Sharkey_2023,ghorbani2019towards, Crabbé_van2022}, or pre-defined external knowledge~\citep{ConceptGlassbox, Fel_Boutin_Béthune_Cadene_Moayeri_Andéol_Chalvidal_Serre_2023} to build high-level concepts.
This limits these techniques to specific types of models or tasks.
Furthermore, while there are different forms of explanations (e.g. feature attributions, rules) for various purposes~\citep{survey_zhang}, existing concept-based explanations mainly focus only on attributions, which limits their fidelity and applicability.
To bridge this gap, we aim to elevate the explanations of various forms provided by existing model-agnostic techniques from feature-level to concept-level.
As we focus on explaining the decision-making process of machine learning models to end-users, we put our emphasis on local explanations, which are more tractable for end-users.
Moreover, we observe that various large pre-trained models provide a unified way to extract high-level concepts across different tasks.
To this end, we propose \toolname, a general framework that automatically incorporates high-level concepts into various existing local model-agnostic techniques for any machine learning models, and provides local explanations in various forms for diverse user needs.

\input{figs/text-example.tex}

We take three mainstream local model-agnostic techniques, LIME~\citep{LIME}, Anchor~\citep{Anchors}, and LORE~\citep{lore} as examples to illustrate how \toolname improves local model-agnostic explanations.

Figure \ref{fig:example-text} shows a LIME explanation for a BERT-based sentiment analysis model on a movie review. The target model classifies the sentence as negative.
LIME provides feature-level attributions, indicating how each word contributes to the model's prediction.
In this case, LIME assigns high negative scores to the words ``however" and ``falls", which indicates that these words contribute much to the negative prediction.
However, this explanation is unfaithful and hard to understand by end-users. 
For example, the word ``however" is assigned a high negative score, but it functions as a conjunction and does not directly convey sentiment~\citep{ReX}.
Moreover, such confusing attributions, combined with an overwhelming amount of attribution information, complicate the explanation for end-users.
\toolname addresses these issues by elevating the explanation from feature-level to concept-level.
\toolname-agumented LIME extracts the main topics of the input sentence using GPT-4o, and then provides attribution-based explanations with these topics. 
From this explanation, users can easily understand that the negative prediction is mainly because the sentence mentions the movie's poor performance in ``emotion impact" and ``direction and script", while the part about ``stunning visuals" and ``passable plot" also expresses some positive sentiment. 
This explanation faithfully reflects the decision process of the target model and is more understandable to end-users.

\input{figs/image-example.tex}

Similar issues exist in the explanation of image models. We use YOLOv8~\citep{yolov8_ultralytics} to perform an image classification task on ImageNet~\citep{deng2009imagenet} dataset. Figure~\ref{fig:example-image2} shows a LIME explanation for an image classified as a \textit{punching bag}. LIME attributes high importance to some fragmented superpixels. End-users can hardly understand why these parts are important for the model's prediction. 
\toolname-agumented LIME provides explanations based on objects detected by Segment Anything Model (SAM)~\citep{kirillov2023segany}, and attributes high importance to the punching bag itself and the kid in the image. 
This explanation is more faithful and understandable.
End-users can easily realize that the model does not perform perfectly when classifying this image to a \textit{punching bag}.

Figure~\ref{fig:example-image1} shows the explanations generated by Anchor, LORE, and their \toolname-augmented versions for an image classified as a \textit{pickup}. 
Anchors provides rule-based sufficient conditions (referred to as \textit{anchors}) for the target model's prediction. The vanilla anchor indicates that parts of the car and the background house guarantee the prediction as a \textit{pickup}.
With \toolname, end-users can easily understand that the model classifies the image as a \textit{pickup} exactly because it indeed detects the pickup truck in the image.
LORE provides rule-based sufficient conditions and counterfactual explanations. Figure \ref{fig:example-image1} shows the counterfactual explanations, which show users how to change the model's prediction by modifying the input image.
The vanilla LORE explanation indicates that if we mask a part of the grass, the background house and the whole side window of the truck will change the model's prediction to a \textit{Half track}. 
In contrast, \toolname-augmented LORE indicates that users can simply mask the pickup truck to change the model's prediction to a \textit{Bison}, which is more user-friendly.

The preceding examples indicate that feature-level explanations are hard to understand by end-users. 
On the other hand, as high-level concepts align with the decision process of target models and users better~\citep{Zhang_Madumal_Miller_Ehinger_Rubinstein_2021, Ghorbani_Wexler_Zou_Kim_2019,Kim_Wattenberg_Gilmer_Cai_Wexler_Viegas_2018,EAC},
\toolname addresses this by providing concept-level explanations, and the examples demonstrate that \toolname makes explanations more understandable to end-users.
Moreover, our empirical evaluation shows these concept-level explanations are also more faithful to the models.
Finally, benefiting from the various types of explanations provided by existing local model-agnostic techniques, \toolname can provide rich explanations including attributions, sufficient conditions, and counterfactuals,  satisfying diverse user needs and offering a more comprehensive understanding of the target models.
This fills the current gap in concept-based explanations, which lack forms beyond attributions.

To elevate the explanations provided by existing local model-agnostic techniques from feature-level to concept-level, we modify these techniques in a uniform and lightweight way based on their two commonalities:
1) these techniques use basic features as language components to build explanations;
2) these techniques use a perturbation model, which generates samples similar to the given input by changing some of its feature values, to capture the local behavior of the target model at the feature level.
To this end, by only elevating the language components to high-level concepts and extending the perturbation model to generate samples by changing high-level concepts, \toolname extends these techniques to provide concept-level explanations.

We evaluated \toolname on explaining two sentiment analysis models (BERT, Llama 3.1\citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels}) and three image classification models(YOLOv8, Vision Transformer~\citep{oquab2023dinov2,darcet2023vitneedreg}, and ResNet-50~\citep{he2016deep}). Our evaluation results demonstrate that \toolname improves the fidelity of Anchors, LIME, LORE, and Kernel SHAP explanations by 82.21\%, 48.59\%, 149.93\%, and 48.27\% respectively, and by considering various forms of explanations together, \toolname outperforms state-of-the-art concept-based explanation techniques specifically designed for text models (TBM~\citep{TBM}) and image models (EAC~\citep{EAC}), respectively.


\section{Preliminaries}

\label{sec:pre}
In this section, we introduce the background knowledge and notations used in this paper.

\paragraph{Machine Learning Models.}
We consider a machine learning model as a black-box function $f$ that maps an input vector $\vx$ to an output scalar $f(\vx)$.
Formally, we let $f: \sX \rightarrow \R$, where $\sX$ is the input domain.
For models that take fixed-dimension inputs, let $\sX = \R^n$. 
For models capable of handling inputs of arbitrary dimensions, let $\sX = \cup_{i=1}^\infty \R^i$. 
Let $\vx_i$ denote the $i$-th feature value of $\vx$.

\paragraph{Local Model-Agnostic Explanation Techniques.}
A local model-agnostic explanation technique $t$ takes a model $f$ and an input $\vx$, and generates a local explanation $g_{f,\vx}$ to describe the behavior of $f$ around $\vx$, i.e., $g_{f,\vx} := t(f, \vx)$.
$g_{f,\vx}$ ($g$ for short) is an expression formed with \textbf{predicates}. Each predicate $p$ maps an input $\vx$ to a binary value, i.e., $p: \sX \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$, indicating whether $\vx$ satisfies a specific condition.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}
Existing local model-agnostic explanation techniques generate explanations following a similar workflow:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Producing Predicates}: These techniques first generate \textbf{a set of predicates} $\sP$ based on the input $\vx$.
    \item \textbf{Generating Samples}: The underlying \textbf{perturbation model} $t_{per}$ generates a set of samples $\sX^b_s$ in \textbf{predicate representations}, where each sample $\vz^b\in \sX^b_s$ is a binary vector in $\{0,1\}^d$ and $\vz_i^b$ indicates whether the sample satisfies the $i$-th predicate in $\sP$. The perturbation model then transforms the samples $\sX^b_s$ back to the original input space to get $\sX_s$ and $f(\sX_s)$.
    \item \textbf{Learning Explanation}: The underlying \textbf{learning algorithm} generates the local explanation $g_{f,\vx}$ consisting of predicates in $P$ using $\sX_s$ and $f(\sX_s)$.
\end{enumerate}

\input{figs/workflow}
Mainstream local model-agnostic explanation techniques like Anchors, LIME, LORE, and Kernel SHAP, all follow this workflow. They use the same kinds of predicate sets and perturbation models, and use different learning algorithms to generate explanations with different properties.
In the following, we introduce the main components of the explanation techniques.

\textbf{Predicate Sets.} Given an input $\vx$, the corresponding predicate set $\sP$ is defined as follows:
\[
\sP=\{p_i| i \in [1, d]\},
\]
where $d$ is the number of predicates in $\sP$, a hyperparameter set by users or according to the input data $\vx$. 
Each $p_i$ is a \textbf{feature predicate} that constrains the value of a set of feature values in $\vx$, i.e. $p_i(\vz): \bigwedge_{j\in \sA_i}1_{\ran(\vx,j)}(z_j)$, where $\sA_i$ is the set of indices of features that $p_i$ constrains. Specifically, $\{\sA_1,\sA_2, ..., \sA_d\}$ forms a partition of $\{1,2,\ldots,|x|\}$.
Each $\ran(\vx,j)$ is a set containing $x_j$, which is set according to the type of input data. For example, we can use  $\ran(\vx,j) = (x_j-\epsilon, x_j+\epsilon)$ for continuous values, and $\ran(\vx,j) = \{x_j\}$ for categorical values.
The predicate $p_i$ is a conjunction of indicator functions, each of which
checks if a sample $z$ has a similar value to $x_j$ (i.e., $z_j \in \ran(\vx,j)$).

\textbf{Predicate Representations.} The predicate representation $\vz^b\in \{0,1\}^d$ of a sample $\vz$ is a binary vector where $\vz_i^b = p_i(\vz)$.

\textbf{Perturbation Models.} The perturbation model $t_{per}$ first randomly selects $\sX_s^b\subseteq \{0,1\}^{d}$ as the predicate representations of the samples.
Then, it transforms $\sX_s^b$ back to the original input space to get $\sX_s$.
For each $\vz^b \in \sX_s^b$, a predicate-to-feature mapping function $t_{\text{p2f}}: \{0,1\}^d \rightarrow \sX$
 transforms $\vz^b$ to $\vz$ as follows: if $z_i^b=1$, then for each $j\in \sA_i$, set $z_j = x_j$; otherwise, set each $z_j$ to a masked value, or a random value sampled from $\text{per}(\vx,j)\backslash\text{ran}(\vx,j)$, where $\text{per}(\vx,j)$ is a perturbation range with $\text{per}(\vx,j) \supset \mathrm{ran}(\vx,j)$.

\textbf{Learning Algorithms and Explanations.} The learning algorithm learns an understandable expression $g$ as an explanation. 
In Anchors, $g$ is a conjunction of predicates that provides a sufficient condition for producing $f(\vx)$ as output, i.e., $f(\vz) = f(\vx)$ if $g(z) = 1$, and $g(z) = \bigwedge_{p\in\sQ} p(z)$, where $\sQ$ is selected by KL-LUCB algorithm~\citep{KL-LUCB}. 
In LIME and Kernel SHAP, $g(z) = \sum_{i=1}^d w_i p_i(z)+w_0$, where $w_i$ is the weight of $p_i$, which is learned by their underlying regression algorithms.
LORE first learns a decision tree with building systems like Yadt~\citep{YADT}, then extracts a sufficient condition to obtain $f(\vx)$ and some counterfactual rules from the tree as explanations. The sufficient condition is similar to Anchors, while each of the counterfactual rules is in the form of $f(\vz) = y$ if $g(z) = 1$, where $y\neq f(\vx)$ and $g(z) = \bigwedge_{p\in\sQ} p(z) \wedge \bigwedge_{p\in\sC} \neg p(z)$, and $\sQ$ and $\sC$ are extracted from the decision tree.

\textbf{An Example.}
For a text input \textit{I love this movie so much}, these techniques can let each $p_i$ constrains only one feature, and produce six predicates in the form of $p_i(z): = \1_{z_i = x_i}$. For another text input $z =$ \textit{I love this [MASK] so [MASK]}, the predicate representation of $z$ is $p_1(z)\ p_2(z)\ p_3(z)\ p_4(z)\ p_5(z)\ p_6(z) = 1\ 1\ 1\ 0\ 1\ 0$. The perturbation model will generate samples in predicate representation, then $t_{p2f}$ will transform samples back to the origin input space. 
For example, a sample $0\ 1\ 0\ 1\ 1\ 1$ is generated and $t_{p2f}$ maps it to \textit{[MASK] love [MASK] movie so much}. 
Consequently, these techniques will use the output of these samples and the samples' predicate presentation to learn an expression, and build the explanation with the predicates.
Limited by the predicate sets and perturbation models, existing local model-agnostic explanation techniques can only generate explanations based on the constraints of feature values, which limits their effectiveness in explaining the behavior of the model.



\section{The ConLUX Framework}

\label{sec:fram}
In this section, we propose \toolname, a general framework to provide concept-based local explanations based on existing local model-agnostic explanation techniques without significantly changing their core components.

We introduce \toolname in three steps: 1) defining concept-based local explanations and concept predicates, 2) showing the modifications to the explanation techniques, and 3) demonstrating the augmented workflow.

\subsection{Concept-Based Local Explanations}
As we discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pre}, though the form of the explanations varies, they are all built from predicates in $\sP$. Elevating the predicates to concept level is the key to providing concept-based explanations.

Definition of high-level concepts varies, as \citet{molnar2020interpretable} mentions, ``A concept can be any abstraction, such as a color, an object, or even an idea."
Here, to provide explanations that are easier to understand by end-users, we define a concept predicate as follows:

\begin{definition}[Concept Predicate]
    Given an input $\vx$, a concept predicate $p^c$ is a function that maps $\vx$ to a binary value, i.e., $p^c: \sX \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$, and satisfies the following properties:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Descriptive}: The concept predicate $p^c$ can be concisely and intuitively described in natural language.
        \item \textbf{Human Evaluable}: The truth of $p^c(\vx)$ can be readily assessed by a human user. 
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The preceding two properties ensure that the concepts are easy to understand. Here, we provide two examples of concept predicates for text and image models in the following:

\paragraph{Examples.}
For text models, we can define a concept predicate as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Concept Name: Poor Visual Effects and Cinematography
    \item Description: The input text mentioned that the visual effects and cinematography are lacking, failing to create an appealing aesthetic.
\end{itemize}
For image models, we use objects in an image to define a concept predicate. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:example-image2}, we can easily describe the concept predicate as ``there is a punching bag in the image", ``there is a kid in the image", etc.

We then define a concept-based local explanation as follows:
\begin{definition}[Concept-Based Local Explanation]
    A concept-based local explanation $g^c_{f,\vx}$ is an expression formed with concept predicates to describe the behavior of $f$ around $\vx$.
\end{definition}

As existing local explanation model-agnostic techniques provide various kinds of explanations like attributions, sufficient conditions, and counterfactuals, \toolname can elevate all these explanations to concept level and provide users a unified interface to obtain various kinds of explanations with a single click. 
Additionally, We denote such a set of various kinds of explanations as a \textbf{\toolname unified explanation}, which provides higher fidelity and offers a more comprehensive view than a single form of explanation. 

\subsection{Augmenting explanation techniques}
\label{sec:ConLUX}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:workflow}, to produce concept predicates, we should first extract high-level concepts based on the input $\vx$; to provide explanations at concept level, we should replace the feature predicates in $\sP$ with concept predicates; to capture the local behavior of the target model at concept level, we should extend the perturbation model to generate samples by changing high-level concepts.

\paragraph{Producing Concept Predicates.} 
We use large pre-trained models to provide high-level concepts based on the input $\vx$ and the target task.
For text models, we refer to the concept extracting prompt used in~\citet{TBM}, providing the information about the task, the given input, the corresponding output, and some in-context learning examples. 
For image models, we use SAM to detect objects in the image. 

Consequently, \toolname defines concept predicates (denoted as $p^c$) using the extracted concepts, and replaces the feature predicates set $\sP$ with the concept predicates set $\sP^c$. 


\paragraph{Generating Samples.}
The extended perturbation model $t_{per}^c$ generates samples in predicate representation and $t_{\text{p2f}}^c: \{0,1\}^{|\sP^c|} \rightarrow \sX$ transforms the samples back to the original input space.
Different from the $t_{per}$ simply decides whether to mask a feature value, $t_{per}^c$ changes high-level concepts at feature level, which is more complex.
Therefore, $t_{per}^c$ is usually a more sophisticated model.
Here, for text models, we use Llama3.1 to perform the predicate-feature mapping; for image models, since each object is still a set of pixels, we can use the same transformation as $t_{per}$.

\subsection{\toolname-Augmented Workflow}
The \toolname augments the workflow in Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} as follows: it first extracts high-level concepts based on the input $\vx$ and the target task, then follows a similar workflow as their vanilla versions, but replaces the predicate set $\sP$ with $\sP^c$ and the perturbation model $t_{per}$ with $t_{per}^c$. Therefore, the \toolname-augmented techniques can capture the local behavior of the target model at the concept level, and provide concept-based local explanations.


\section{Empirical Evaluation}

\label{sec:eval}

In this section, we demonstrated the generality of \toolname, its improvement of explanation fidelity, and the fidelity of \toolname unified explanations by empirical evaluation. 
We showed the generality of \toolname by applying it to four mainstream local model-agnostic explanation techniques: Anchors, LIME, LORE, and Kernel SHAP (KSHAP for short), which provide three types of explanations:
sufficient conditions, counterfactuals, and attributions. 
We applied them to explain various text and image models.
We showed the improvement of explanation fidelity by comparing the vanilla feature-level explanations with \toolname-augmented the concept-based explanations.
Moreover, we compared the fidelity of \toolname unified explanations with two state-of-the-art concept-based explanation techniques: Textual Bottleneck Model (TBM)~\citep{TBM} for text models and Explain Any Concept (EAC)~\citep{EAC} for image models.
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We chose sentiment analysis as the target task for text models, and image classification as the target task for image models. 

\paragraph{Sentiment Analysis.} Sentiment analysis models take a text sequence as input and predict if the text is positive or negative, i.e. $f: \sX \rightarrow \{0, 1\}$, where $\sX:= \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty \sW^i$ is the input domain, and $\sW$ is the vocabulary set. 
We used a pre-trained BERT~\citep{morris2020textattack} and Llama3.1 to predict the sentiment of 200 movie reviews from the Large Movie Review Dataset~\citep{IMDB}, and explained the local behavior of the models around each input text.
For vanilla techniques, we followed the settings described in Section~\ref{sec:pre}.
For \toolname-augmented techniques, we set the number of concept predicates to 10, used GPT-3.5~\citep{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} to extract high-level concepts, and Llama3.1 to perform the predicate-to-feature mapping.
For TBM, we applied it to explain the same 200 movie reviews with its default settings.

\paragraph{Image Classification.} Image classification models take an image as input and predict the category of the image, i.e. $f: \sX \rightarrow \{0, 1, ..., m\}$, where $m$ is the number of categories, $\sX:= \R^{3\times h\times w}$ is the input domain, with $h$ and $w$ being the height and width of the image.
We used a pre-trained YOLOv8, Vision Transformer (ViT)~\citep{oquab2023dinov2,darcet2023vitneedreg}, and ResNet-50~\citep{he2016deep} to predict the category of 1000 images from the ImageNet dataset~\citep{deng2009imagenet}, and explained the local behavior of the models around each input image.
For vanilla techniques, we followed LIME to use Quickshift algorithm~\citep{jiang2018quickshiftprovablygoodinitializations} to obtain the superpixels, and used these super-pixels as predicates.
For \toolname-augmented techniques, we used SAM~\citep{kirillov2023segany} to detect objects in the images, and used these objects as predicates.
For EAC, we applied it to explain the same 1000 images with its default settings.

To evaluate the fidelity of \toolname unified explanations, as the combination of multiple forms of explanation provides more fidelity than a single form, we used the combination \toolname-augmented KSHAP and LORE explanations as local surrogate models.
Specifically, if an input is covered by LORE's rule, we use the LORE output; otherwise, we use the KSHAP explanation. 

\subsection{Fidelity Evaluation}

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Fidelity reflects how faithfully the explanations describe the target model. As these techniques provide explanations in different forms, we used different metrics to evaluate their fidelity.

Following the setup in the original papers of Anchors and LORE, 
we used \textbf{coverage} and \textbf{precision} as fidelity metrics (which are named differently in the LORE paper). Given a target model $f$, an input $\vx$, and a distribution $D_\vx$ derived from the perturbation model, and the corresponding explanation $g$, we defined the coverage as \(
\cov(\vx;f,g) = \E_{\vz\sim D_\vx}[g(\vz)]\), which indicates the proportion of inputs in the distribution that match the rule;
we defined the precision as \(\prec(\vx;f,g) = \E_{\vz\sim D_\vx}[\1_{f(\vz)=y} | g(\vz)]\), where y is the consequence of the rules in $g$ with $y = f(\vx)$ for factual rules and $y\neq f(\vx)$ for counterfactual rules.
Precision indicates the proportion of covered inputs that $g$ correctly predicts the model outputs. 

As LIME and KSHAP are attribution-based local surrogate, we used \textit{Area Over most relevant first perturbation curve} (AOPC)~\citep{samek2016evaluating, DecompX}, and $\mathrm{accuracy_a}$ as fidelity metrics~\citep{balagopalan2022road,yeh2019fidelity,ismail2021improving}.
Given a target model $f$, an input $\vx$, its corresponding model output $y = f(\vx)$, their corresponding explanation $g$, and $x^{(k)}$ that is generated by masking the $k\%$ most important predicates in $\vx$,
AOPC and $\mathrm{accuracy_a}$ are defined as follows:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,topsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,partopsep=0pt, leftmargin=10pt]
    \item \textbf{AOPC:}  Let \(
        \AOPC_k = \frac{1}{|\sT|} \sum_\vx ^\sT p_f(y|\vx)-p_f(y|\vx^{(k)}), 
    \)
    where $p_f(y|\vx)$ is the probability of $f$ to output $y$ given the input $\vx$, and $\sT$ is the set of all test inputs.
    $AOPC_k$ indicates the average change of the model output when masking the $k\%$ most important predicates. A higher $AOPC_k$ indicates a better explanation. We calculate the AOPC curve by varying $k$ from 0 to 100.

    \item $\mathrm{\mathbf{Accuracy_a}}$: $\mathrm{Accuracy_a}$ indicates the proportion of inputs among all $\vx^{(k)}$ that the target model gives the same output as the original input $\vx$, i.e. $\E(f(\vx^{(k)}) = f(\vx))$. Specifically, $\mathrm{accuracy_a}$ is different from the standard accuracy, and a lower $\mathrm{accuracy_a}$ indicates a better explanation.
    
\end{itemize}
Specifically, we only considered the predicates that positively contribute to $f(\vx)$, and we did not use AOPC when explaining Llama 3.1, as it does not directly provide the probability for each output token.

For TBM, EAC, and \toolname unified explanations,
considering that they can all serve as local surrogate models, i.e. $g: \sX \rightarrow \R$, we defined the metrics as follows: Given a target model $f$, an input $\vx$, a perturbation distribution $\sD_{\vx}$, and their corresponding explanation $g$, a performance metric $L$ (e.g. accuracy, F1 score, MSE, etc.), we define the (in-)fidelity as \(E_{z\sim \sD_\vx}L(f(\vz), g(\vz))\), which indicates the performance of using $g$ to approximate $f$. Here, we used the accuracy as the performance metric.
Specifically, 
considering the complexity of the original task,
we reduced the image classification task for local surrogates to predicting whether the target model $f$ assigns the same classification to $\vx'$ as it does to $\vx$.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Results}
Table~\ref{table:fidelity-anchor} shows the fidelity of Anchors, LORE, and their \toolname-augmented versions.
\toolname improves the average coverage of Anchors and LORE by 9.0\% and 10.4\%, and the average precision by  11.9\% and 8.7\%, respectively.
Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} and Table~\ref{table:fidelity-LIME} show the fidelity of LIME, KSHAP, and their \toolname-augmented versions.

\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:related}
Our work is related to model-agnostic explanation techniques and concept-based explanation techniques.

Model-agnostic explanation techniques consider target models as black boxes and provide explanations without requiring any knowledge of the model's internal structure. 
Existing Model-agnostic explanation techniques provide different types of explanations, such as feature importance~\citep{SHAP, LIME,tan2023glime, Shankaranarayana_Runje_2019}, decision rules~\citep{Anchors,lore,Dhurandhar_Chen_Luss_Tu_Ting_Shanmugam_Das_2018}, counterfactuals~\citep{wachter2018counterfactualexplanationsopeningblack, lore}, and
visualizations~\citep{ICE,friedman2001greedy,apley2020visualizing}.
However, to our knowledge, all existing model-agnostic explanation techniques provide explanations at feature levels~\citep{survey_zhang}. Basic feature-based explanations are usually worse in aligning with either the decision-making process of the model or end-users~\citep{Ghorbani_Wexler_Zou_Kim_2019, EAC,Kim_Wattenberg_Gilmer_Cai_Wexler_Viegas_2018}, which makes these explanations unfaithful and hard to understand.

Concept-based explanation techniques provide explanations in terms of high-level concepts, which align with the decision-making process of the model better and are more interpretable to end-users.
To our knowledge, existing concept-based explanation techniques are all model-specific or task-specific~\citep{survey_concept}. We categorize them into three groups: 
(1) techniques that extract concepts from the model's internal structure~\citep{zhang2021invertibleconceptbasedexplanationscnn, conceptSHAP, Yeh_Kim_Arik_Li_Ravikumar_Pfister_2019, Cunningham_Ewart_Riggs_Huben_Sharkey_2023,ghorbani2019towards, Crabbé_van2022}, which are limited to specific types of models,
(2) techniques that use external knowledge to define concepts~\citep{ConceptGlassbox, Fel_Boutin_Béthune_Cadene_Moayeri_Andéol_Chalvidal_Serre_2023},  which are limited to specific types of tasks since their methods based on the knowledge for a specific task, and 
(3) techniques that use pre-trained models to extract concepts~\citep{TBM, EAC}. \citet{TBM} propose TBM, which is a surrogate model specifically designed for text data, while \citet{EAC} propose EAC, which also utilizes internal information of the target model. 
Therefore, these techniques are only for specific types of tasks.
In addition, these techniques mainly focus only on attributions which limits their use cases~\citep{survey_concept}.



\section{Conclusion}

\label{sec:conclusion}
We have proposed \toolname, a general framework that automatically extracts high-level concepts and incorporates them into existing local model-agnostic explanation techniques to provide concept-based explanations, which are more faithful and easier to understand by end-users.
\toolname offers unified explanations that combine attributions, sufficient conditions, and counterfactuals. This satisfies diverse user needs and fills the current gap in concept-based explanations, which lack forms beyond attributions.
\toolname achieves this by utilizing large pre-trained models to extract high-level concepts, elevating language components from feature level to concept level, and extending perturbation models to sample in the concept space.
We have instantiated \toolname on Anchors, LIME, LORE, and Kernel SHAP, and provide unified explanations. We have constructed empirical evaluations to demonstrate the effectiveness of \toolname.
