\title{OATS: Outlier-Aware Pruning Through Sparse and Low Rank Decomposition}

\begin{abstract}
The recent paradigm shift to large-scale foundation models has brought about a new era for deep learning that, while has found great success in practice, has also been plagued by prohibitively expensive costs in terms of high memory consumption and compute. To mitigate these issues, there has been a concerted effort in post-hoc neural network pruning techniques that do not require costly retraining. Despite the considerable progress being made, existing methods often exhibit a steady drop in model performance as the compression increases. In this paper, we present a novel approach to compressing large transformers, coined OATS, that utilizes the second moment information in the input embeddings to decompose the model weights into a sum of sparse and low-rank matrices. Without any retraining, OATS achieves state-of-the-art performance when compressing models by up to $60\%$ on large language models such as Llama-3 and Phi-3 and vision transformers such as ViT and DINOv2 while delivering up to $1.37\times$ the CPU acceleration versus a model that was comparably pruned. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Large scale transformer-based models have found great success in a number of domains ranging from image classification \citep{wu2020visual}, language modelling \citep{devlin-etal-2019-bert}, and question answering \citep{NEURIPS2020_1457c0d6}. However, these models contain billions of parameters making them computationally expensive to train and deploy which has lead to an increased demand for resource-saving techniques like model quantization \citep{dettmers2022gptint, egiazarian2024extreme}, parameter efficient fine-tuning \citep{hu2022lora, zhao2024galore}, and, most relevant to this work, neural network pruning \citep{frantar-sparsegpt}. 

Pruning has been a key focus for model compression since the early days of deep networks \citep{NIPS1988_07e1cd7d, NIPS1989_6c9882bb, NIPS1992_303ed4c6}. Over the years, various pruning techniques have emerged, introducing sparsity either before training \citep{lee2018snip, Wang2020Picking, NEURIPS2020_46a4378f, jorge2021progressive}, during training \citep{zhugupta, pmlr-v119-evci20a}, or post-training \citep{pmlr-v202-benbaki23a}. In the context of large foundation models, post-training pruning methods, particularly those requiring minimal \citep{xia2022structured, ma2023llmpruner} or no re-training \citep{frantar-sparsegpt, sun2024a, ashkboos2024slicegpt, zhang2024dynamic} are preferred for their computational efficiency. These techniques, when compressing models by $50\%$, have demonstrated the ability to accelerate end-to-end CPU inference by up to $1.8\times$ \citep{frantar-sparsegpt, yin2024outlier} and GPU inference by up to $1.63\times$ using structured N:M sparsity \citep{Mishra2021AcceleratingSD}, highlighting their potential in real-world applications.

Despite the significant advancements in pruning techniques, it was recently shown that current methods suffer from a consistent degradation in model performance as compression levels increase \citep{yin2024junk}. Moreover, although structured pruning offers greater potential for acceleration compared to unstructured pruning, it often imposes a much steeper trade-off in terms of model accuracy and effectiveness \citep{pmlr-v162-chen22a}. These challenges underscore the need for more sophisticated pruning strategies to strike a better balance between efficiency and performance.
\subsection{Contributions}
To mitigate these issues, we introduce \textsc{\textbf{O}utlier-\textbf{A}ware Pruning \textbf{T}hrough \textbf{S}parse and Low Rank Decomposition} (\textbf{OATS}), a novel retraining-free method for compressing large transformers that utilizes the second moment of the input embeddings to approximate the model's weight matrices as a sum of a sparse matrix and a low-rank matrix.
We evaluate OATS on recent large language models -- Phi-3 \citep{phi3tr} and Llama-3 \citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels} -- and vision transformers -- ViT \citep{wu2020visual} and DinoV2 \citep{oquab2023dinov2} -- demonstrating that OATS achieves new state-of-the-art performance across a wide range of commonly employed performance metrics. Furthermore, by combining structured pruning with unstructured pruning, our benchmarks utilizing the Deepsparse Engine by \citet{deepsparse} reveal that OATS leads to more CPU speed-up across all levels of compression when compared to models that utilize just unstructured pruning. 

To facilitate the interpretability of the sparse and low-rank terms found by OATS, we run experiments where we divide the compressed model into two models, a sparse model and a low-rank model, and visualize their respective attention heat maps utilizing attention rollout \citep{abnar-zuidema-2020-quantifying}. Our visualizations on vision transformers \citep{wu2020visual} reveal a complementary relationship between the two models, with each focusing on different key areas of the image, effectively segmenting it into distinct regions.



\section{The \algname Algorithm}

The key observation behind the OATS algorithm is that the weight matrices, $\mW\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$, in a transformer model can be faithfully approximated as a summation of a sparse and low rank matrix by solving the following optimization problem, commonly known as Robust PCA \citep{slr, rpca}:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:rpca}
    \min_{\mS, \mL \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out} \times d_{in}}} \| \mW - \mS - \mL \|_F^2 \; \text{ s.t. } \; \text{Rank}(\mL) \leq r, \; \|\mS\|_0 \leq k. 
\end{equation}

\subsection{Alternating Thresholding} To solve Equation \ref{eqn:rpca}, \algname leverages the alternating thresholding algorithms proposed by \citet{alt_proj} and \citet{alt_rpca} that iteratively alternates between solving for the low-rank term $\mL$, through singular-value thresholding, and for the sparse term $\mS$, through hard-thresholding. Given a matrix $\mA \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, singular-value thresholding, also known as truncated SVD, is defined as:
$$ \textsc{TruncatedSVD}(\mA, r) = \mU_r \mathbf{\Sigma}_r \mV_r^{\top},$$
where $\mU_r, \mathbf{\Sigma}_r, \mV_r^{\top}$ correspond to the matrices formed by retaining only the top-$r$ singular vectors and singular values from the full SVD of $\mA$. Hard-thresholding, which succeeds the singular-value thresholding step, is defined as:
$$ \textsc{HardThreshold}(\mA, k) = \mM \odot \mA,$$
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.5\textwidth}
    \begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{AlternatingThreshold}} \label{alg:altmin}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \STATE \textbf{Inputs:} 
    \STATE \hspace{1em} Weight Matrix: $\mW \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out} \times d_{in}}$
    \STATE \hspace{1em} Iterations: $N$
    \STATE \hspace{1em} Rank: $r$
    \STATE \hspace{1em} Nonzeros: $k$
    \STATE \textbf{Procedure:}
    \STATE $\mS = \mathbf{0}$
    \FOR{$t = 1$ to $N$}
        \STATE $\mL = \textsc{TruncatedSVD}(\mW - \mS, r)$
        \STATE $\mS = \textsc{HardThreshold}(\mW - \mL, k)$
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE \textbf{return}: $\mS$, $\mL$ 
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
    \end{minipage}
\end{wrapfigure}
where $\mM \in \mathbb{R}^{m\times n} $ is a binary matrix with $k$ non-zero entries coinciding with the $k$ largest entries in magnitude in $\mA$. Alternatively, the hard-thresholding can also be performed row-wise rather than layer-wise in which case $\mM$ would be a binary matrix with $m\cdot \left \lfloor \frac{k}{m} \right \rfloor$ non-zero entries coinciding with the $\left \lfloor \frac{k}{m} \right \rfloor$ largest entries in magnitude in each row of $\mA$.

These steps are summarized in Algorithm \ref{alg:altmin} on the right. To optimize memory usage, the low-rank term $\mL$ is stored as its two low-rank components: $\mU_r$ and $\mathbf{\Sigma}_r \mV_r^{\top}$. 

\subsection{Incorporating Outlier Information} The alternating thresholding on its own yields suboptimal results because the activations of large-scale transformers exhibit a small number of large-magnitude features and altering these negatively impacts model performance \citep{kovaleva-etal-2021-bert, dettmers2022gptint, sun2024massive}. OATS takes inspiration from Wanda \citep{sun2024a} and incorporates outlier information through a diagonal scaling matrix $\mD\in \mathbb{R}^{d_{in}\times d_{in}}$ that captures the second moment of the input activations
$$ \mD =  \text{diag}\left(\sqrt{\mX^{\top} \mX}\right),$$
where $\mX\in \mathbb{R}^{B\times d_{in}}$ and $B$ is the product of the batch size and sequence length. 
The weight matrix $\mW$ is then scaled by $\mD$ before applying \textsc{AlternatingThreshold}:
$$ \mS, \mL = \textsc{AlternatingThreshold}\left(\mW \mD, N, r, k\right). $$
To undo the scaling, \algname applies the inverse transformation to reach the compressed weight
$$\mW_{\text{compressed}} =  (\mS + \mL)\mD^{-1}, $$
where it leverages the fact that $\mD$ is diagonal so that it both preserves the sparsity pattern of $\mS$ and is easy to invert. Aligned with \citep{frantar-sparsegpt, sun2024a}, and \citet{zhang2024dynamic}, the activations are calculated through a calibration set that is propagated through the compressed layers. 

\subsection{OATS Parameters} To determine the rank $r$ and the number of nonzeros $k$, \algname takes in as input two hyperparameters: the \emph{compression rate}, $\rho \in (0,1)$, and the \emph{rank ratio}, $\kappa \in (0,1)$. The compression rate coincides with the sparsity rate required by existing pruning algorithms and is defined as:
$$\rho = 1 - \frac{\# \text{ of nonzero parameters in compressed layer}}{\# \text{ of parameters in original layer}} = 1- \frac{k + r(d_{out} + d_{in})}{d_{out} \cdot d_{in}}. $$ 
The rank ratio represents the proportion of nonzero parameters that appear in the low-rank term: $$\kappa =\frac{r(d_{out} + d_{in})}{(1-\rho)d_{out} \cdot d_{in}}.$$ 
Given a fixed compression rate $\rho$ and rank ratio $\kappa$, the two equations above can be solved to obtain the rank $r$ and nonzeros $k$: 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:oats_hyper}
r = \left \lfloor \kappa \cdot (1-\rho) \cdot \frac{d_{out} \cdot d_{in}}{d_{out} + d_{in}} \right \rfloor \qquad k = \left \lfloor (1-\kappa) \cdot (1 - \rho) \cdot d_{out}\cdot d_{in}\right \rfloor.
\end{equation} 
The complete \algname algorithm pseudocode can be found in Algorithm \ref{alg:OATS} below. 
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{\textsc{\algname}} \label{alg:OATS}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Inputs:} 
\STATE \hspace{1em} Layer Inputs: $\mX^{\ell} \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times d_{in}}$
\STATE \hspace{1em} Layer Matrix: $\mW^{\ell} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out}\times d_{in}}$
\STATE \hspace{1em} Compression Rate: $\rho$
\STATE \hspace{1em} Rank Ratio: $\kappa$
\STATE \hspace{1em} Iterations: $N$
\STATE \textbf{Procedure:}
\STATE $r \leftarrow \left \lfloor \kappa \cdot (1-\rho) \cdot \frac{d_{out} \cdot d_{in}}{d_{out} + d_{in}} \right \rfloor, \; k \leftarrow \left \lfloor (1-\kappa) \cdot (1 - \rho) \cdot d_{out}\cdot d_{in}\right \rfloor$
\STATE $\mD \leftarrow \text{diag}(\sqrt{\mX^{\top} \mX})$ 
\STATE $\mL, \mS \leftarrow \textsc{AlternatingThreshold}(\mW \mD, N, r, k)$
\STATE $\mW \leftarrow (\mL + \mS)\mD^{-1}$ 
\STATE \textbf{return:} $\mX^{\ell+1} \leftarrow \mW\mX^{\ell}$
\end{algorithmic}
    
\end{algorithm}



\section{Experiments on Large Language Models}

\subsection{Experiment Setup}
\paragraph{Models and Tasks} We evaluate \algname on two state-of-the-art families of large language models: Phi-3 \citep{phi3tr} and Llama-3 \citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels}. To gauge the algorithm's performance under various model sizes, we select Phi-3 Mini, a $3.8$B parameter model, Phi-3 Medium, a $14$B parameter model, and Llama-3 8B, an $8$B parameter model, so that models of various sizes are represented in our experiments. We utilize LM Harness developed by \citet{eval-harness} to evaluate zero-shot performance on eight tasks, five-shot performance on the Massive Multitask Language Understanding benchmark by \citet{hendrycks2021measuring}, and language generation on WikiText-2.

\paragraph{Pruning Benchmarks} As \algname does not require costly retraining after model compression, we opt to benchmark it with three current state-of-the-art algorithms that similarly do not require such overhead: SparseGPT by \citet{frantar-sparsegpt}, Wanda by \citet{sun2024a}, and DSNoT\footnote{DSNoT experiments are run with both SparseGPT and Wanda. We report the best results across the two.} by \citet{zhang2024dynamic}. The parameters utilized for \algname are depicted in Table \ref{table:oats_hyper}.
\paragraph{Calibration Data} Remaining consistent with \citet{frantar-sparsegpt}, \citet{sun2024a}, and \citet{zhang2024dynamic}, our calibration data consists of 128 sequences of length 2048 sampled from the first shard of the C4 training set \citep{JMLR:v21:20-074}. To ensure consistency, we utilize the same calibration data for all pruning algorithms that we benchmark. 
\paragraph{Layer-Wise Compression Rates} We benchmark our algorithm across a wide range of compression rates: \{0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6\}. For compression rates at or below $0.5$, we compress all transformer blocks uniformly. At the higher compression rate of $0.6$, we utilize Outlier Weighed Layerwise Sparsity Ratios (OWL) developed by \citet{yin2024outlier} which was shown to lead to significant performance improvements at higher compression rates. We exclude pruning any linear layers that are present in the model head and embeddings which conforms with what was done in the prior works by \citet{frantar-sparsegpt}, \citet{sun2024a}, and \citet{zhang2024dynamic}. 
\paragraph{Hardware Speedup} To measure the CPU speedup generated by the unstructured sparsity and low-rank compression of OATS, we utilize the DeepSparse Inference Engine developed by \citet{deepsparse}. More recently, NVIDIA's sparse tensor cores are able to leverage for acceleration structured N:M sparsity, a sparsity pattern where every $N$ in $M$ parameters are zero \citep{Mishra2021AcceleratingSD}. We include in our experiments the natural extension of \algname to structured N:M sparsity where we impose the structured sparsity pattern onto the sparse term in the decomposition.

\subsection{Ablations for \algname}\label{sec:hyper}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Scaling the weights by the second moment of the input activations, $\mD$, versus not scaling.
    \item Pruning the weights per each output row in the matrix versus pruning layer-wise. 
\end{itemize}

The rollout visualizations show that the sparse and low-rank terms capture distinct areas of the image, effectively segmenting it, and reveal three distinct patterns in how the image is partitioned. The first type of behavior, which is also commonly exhibited in the classical setting \citep{rpca}, is when one component (commonly sparse) captures the subject(s), while the other component (commonly low-rank) captures the background. The second behavior occurs when both components focus on different parts of the same subject, capturing distinct features. The third behavior arises when the image contains multiple subjects, with each component isolating a different subject. While these patterns provide initial insights into how the components process visual information, further investigation is needed to fully understand the underlying mechanisms driving these behaviors.


\section{Related Works}

\paragraph{Connection with Wanda} OATS utilizes the same outlier scaling as the the one that is employed by Wanda \citep{sun2024a}. In fact, Wanda can be seen as a special case of OATS when the rank ratio $\kappa = 0$, which, according to Equation \ref{eqn:oats_hyper}, results in the low-rank term becoming the $\mathbf{0}$ matrix. This leads to OATS performing a singular hard thresholding step that is equivalent to the pruning step described by Wanda and returning a compressed matrix equal to:
$$\mW_{\text{compressed}} = \textsc{HardThreshold}(\mW \mD, k) \mD^{-1}. $$
\paragraph{Sparse and Low-Rank Approximation in Transformers} The emergence of sparse and low-rank structures in transformers has recently become an area of both theoretical and practical interest. On the theoretical front, \citet{zhao2024implicit} showed that the logits of LLMs trained utilizing next token prediction converge to a low rank and sparse structure. On the practical front, Scatterbrain proposed by \citet{scatterbrain} shows that its possible to approximate the entire attention mechanism with a single sparse and low rank representation. LoRAP by \citet{li2024lorap} finds a low-rank representation for the attention matrices by utilizing a similar scaling technique as OATS. Recent works, such as LoSparse \citep{losparse}, LoRAPrune \citep{zhang2024loraprune}, and APT \citep{zhao2024apt}, propose variations of applying structured pruning on the weights while incorporating a low-rank adapter that is trained via gradient descent. We want to highlight that unlike OATS, these works utilize training and low-rank adaptation to recover model performance from structured pruning, whereas OATS depends on an intrinsic sparse and low-rank approximation of the weight matrices.  
\paragraph{Robust PCA Algorithms} While OATS utilizes an alternating thresholding approach for its efficiency and simplicity, the search for fast Robust PCA algorithms has been a key area of interest since the inception of the problem. An alternative approach is to apply a convex relaxation, replacing the sparsity and low-rank constraints with an $\ell_1$ and nuclear norm surrogate leading to Stable Principle Component Pursuit \citep{spcp}. Another way to enhance computational efficiency is to utilize gradient descent by parameterizing the low-rank matrix as $\mL = \mU\mV^{\top}$, and then performing gradient descent on $\mU$ and $\mV$ \citep{fast_rpca, scale_rpca}. 
\paragraph{Pruning and Interpretability} While pruning has been proven to be a successful approach to model compression, there is still ongoing research into what pruning is pruning and how it impacts model performance. \citet{Paganini2020PruneR} show that magnitude-based pruning has a disproportionate negative effect on underrepresented classes. In a similar vein, \citet{yin2024junk} show that pruning LLMs can lead to irreversible damage to model performance on tasks that are more challenging. We highlight that the low-rank term present in OATS might be able to mitigate the negative impacts of pruning raised by the prior works. Indeed, in Tables \ref{table:mmlu} and \ref{table:hs}, we can see that at higher compression rates, the gap between OATS and prior state-of-the-art is significantly larger suggesting that the low-rank term plays a critical role in mitigating the loss in performance at high compression. 


\section{Conclusion}

We have introduced OATS, an algorithm that distinguishes itself from prior algorithms by utilizing outlier information to determine a compressed sparse and low-rank decomposition without any re-training. We run a number of experiments showing that OATS is able to consistently outperform prior state-of-the-art across multiple benchmarks and compression rates while also improving on speed-up. Furthermore, in the search for interpretability, we have generated visualizations highlighting that the sparse and low-rank terms capture different segments of the image. We hope that our work can be utilized to investigate how sparse and low-rank structures in transformers can be fully leveraged for maximal memory and compute efficiency.
