\title{MAgICoRe: Multi-Agent, Iterative, Coarse-to-Fine Refinement for Reasoning}

\begin{abstract}
Large Language Models' (LLM) reasoning can be improved using test-time aggregation strategies, i.e., generating multiple samples for each problem and aggregating over them to find a better answer.
While these strategies improve performance, they often reach a saturation point beyond which additional samples provide no return. 
Refinement offers an alternative by using model-generated feedback to not only sample more solutions but also improve their quality. 
However, refinement introduces three key challenges:
\textbf{(1) Excessive refinement:} Uniformly refining all instances can cause over-correction and reduce overall performance.
\textbf{(2) Inability to localize and address errors:} LLMs have a limited ability to self-correct and struggle to identify and correct their own mistakes in a targeted way. 
\textbf{(3) Insufficient refinement:} Deciding how many iterations of refinement are needed is non-trivial, and stopping too soon could leave errors unaddressed. 
To tackle these issues, we propose \method{}, a framework for \acron{M}ulti \acron{Ag}ent \acron{I}teration for \acron{Co}arse-to-fine \acron{Re}finement.
\method{} aims to avoid excessive refinement by categorizing problems as easy or hard, solving easy problems with coarse-grained aggregation, and solving hard ones with fine-grained and iterative multi-agent refinement. 
To enable more granular error localization, we incorporate external step-wise reward model (RM) scores. 
Moreover, to ensure effective refinement, we employ a multi-agent loop with three agents: the \textit{Solver}, the \textit{Reviewer} (which generates targeted feedback based on step-wise RM scores) and the \textit{Refiner} (which incorporates feedback and generates new solutions).
To ensure sufficient refinement, we re-evaluate updated solutions, iteratively initiating further rounds of multi-agent refinement and making the communication between the Reviewer and Refiner agents bidirectional. 
We evaluate \method{} on Llama-3-8B and GPT-3.5 and show its effectiveness across five math reasoning datasets, with consistent gains for all datasets and models.
Across datasets, even one iteration of \method{} beats Self-Consistency by $3.4\%$ (absolute), Best-of-$k$ by $3.2\%$, and Self-Refine by $4.0\%$ while using less than $50\%$ of the samples. 
Furthermore, unlike iterative refinement with baseline methods, \method{} continues to improve with more iterations.
Finally, our ablations highlight the importance of \method{}'s use of RMs and multi-agent communication
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\begin{figure*}[t!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/intro.pdf}
    \caption{Three main issues in refinement: 
    \emph{(1) Excessive refinement:} refinement risks over-correcting, turning correct solutions into incorrect ones. 
    \emph{(2) Inability to localize and address errors:} LLMs struggle with finding and correcting \setlength{\fboxsep}{1pt}\colorbox{Dandelion}{\textcolor{red}{errors}} in a targeted way.
    \emph{(3) Insufficient refinement:} an effective refinement should gradually make the output better, but stopping too soon could leave errors uncorrected.
    To address these issues, we propose \method{}, which adapts resource allocation based on problem difficulty and refines only when encountering hard problems, adopting a coarse-grained aggregation approach for easy ones. 
    For hard cases requiring refinement, we employ a multi-agent setup that iteratively reviews and refines the solutions based on targeted feedback generated by the reviewer using step-wise PRM scores.}
    \label{fig:intro}
\end{figure*}

\label{sec:intro}

Imagine a person taking a math exam with problems of varying difficulty; even before answering any question, an effective exam-taker might first distinguish between easier and more challenging problems, 
deciding how much effort to budget for each one \citep{son2000metacognitive, dodeen2015teaching}.
To maximize their score, the student would likely spend minimal time on the easy problems and focus more on the harder ones, refining their answers where needed. Misallocating effort could not only waste time but even lower their score, as overthinking simple problems might lead to mistakes; similarly failing to dedicate enough thought to hard problems will lead to poor results. 
For Large Language Models (LLMs) performing reasoning tasks, several test-time approaches have been proposed to improve performance by dedicating more computation through aggregation. 
These approaches sample multiple solutions to the same question and aggregate over the resulting answers (e.g. Self-Consistency \citep[SC;][]{wang2022self}, Best-of-$k$ sampling \citep{lightman2023let,sun2024easytohardgeneralizationscalablealignment,wang2023math}).
However, applying aggregation strategies uniformly on all samples may waste computation on easier problems, where additional samples might not change the answer \citep{aggarwal2023let}. 
Refinement -- where solutions are instead critiqued and improved upon during resampling -- offers a possible avenue for breaking out of the aggregation rut. 
This mirrors human reasoning, where feedback can be used to improve answers (rather than simply retrying the same problem), often in an iterative fashion.
For example, a teacher might improve a student's understanding by providing multiple rounds of feedback on a test \citep{pan2021pretest,henry2006test,ken2013feedback}. 

While refinement seems promising, it faces three key challenges that current work has yet to fully address (outlined in \cref{fig:intro}): 
\textbf{(1) Excessive refinement}: the LLM must know when to refine and when not to. While refinement can help on hard problems, uniformly refining all instances can cause over-refinement, where solutions that were already correct before refinement are ``overthought'' and flipped to incorrect, 
reducing the overall performance \citep{huang2024large, shridhar2024art, stechly2024selfverification}.
\textbf{(2) Inability to localize and address errors}: LLMs struggle to identify their own mistakes (i.e., steps needing refinement) and struggle to correct mistakes in a targeted way without explicit instructions.
\textbf{(3) Insufficient Refinement}:  deciding how many iterations of refinement are needed is non-trivial -- some cases may require only one round, while others need more, and stopping early could leave errors unaddressed, i.e., hard problems might be ``underthought'' by a single refinement iteration.

To address these issues and unlock performance gains via refinement, 
we propose a unified solution called \method{}, a framework for \acron{M}ulti \acron{Ag}ent \acron{I}teration for \acron{Co}arse-to-fine \acron{Re}finement.
As shown in \cref{fig:intro}, our approach leverages external global and local Reward Models (RMs) that enhance both aggregation and refinement processes. 
To tackle the first issue (excessive refinement), we perform \emph{selective refinement} (see \cref{fig:intro}(a)): 
we start by generating multiple reasoning chains from the LLM and scoring them with off-the-shelf RMs, using the entropy of the final answer distribution to classify examples as easy or hard.
This classification allocates resources more efficiently and avoids over-correction by refining only hard samples.
Given LLMs' general inability to  to localize errors \citep{tyen2024correct}, we leverage \emph{step-by-step RM scores} to help the LLM pinpoint low-scoring steps (which are likely to be incorrect); this process is shown in \cref{fig:intro}(b).
Moreover, to help LLMs refine effectively once the errors have been localized, we propose a \emph{multi-agent system} consisting of three agents: the Solver, the Reviewer, and the Refiner. 
For each reasoning problem, the Solver generates a reasoning chain, the Reviewer gives targeted feedback based on step-by-step RM scores, and the Refiner improves the solutions using this feedback. 
Finally, to address the issue of insufficient refinement, we \emph{iterate the review-refine process}, checking the quality and the entropy of the final answer at each iteration as a stopping criterion (cf. \cref{fig:intro}(c)).
This makes the communication between the Reviewer and Refiner agents \emph{bidirectional}, as the refinement process is conditioned on feedback from the review, and subsequent reviews are conditioned on the refined outputs from prior iterations. 

We evaluate the effectiveness of \method{} on five diverse math reasoning datasets (GSM8K~\citep{cobbe2021training}, SVAMP~\citep{patel2021nlp}, MATH~\citep{hendrycksmath2021}, MMLU~\citep{hendryckstest2021} and SAT~\citep{zhong2023agieval}) with two LLMs: Llama-3-8B and GPT-3.5. Notably, our results show that across all datasets, we obtain consistent improvements over all aggregation and refinement baselines. 
Specifically, even just one iteration of \method{} on Llama-3-8B already outperforms Best-of-$k$ sampling \citep{lightman2023let} by $3.2\%$ and Self-Consistency \citep{wang2022self} by $3.4\%$, while using roughly half the samples.
\method{} also outperforms a combination of Self-Refine \citep{madaan2023self} and Self-Consistency by $4.0\%$ (these trends also hold true for GPT-3.5).
We demonstrate that \method{} effectively decides when to use refinement and when \emph{not} to, leading to up to a $6.4\%$ improvement over the strongest Best-of-$k$ baseline on MATH \citep{hendrycksmath2021}, whereas uniformly applying refinement to all samples can result in a \emph{drop} of $5.2\%$, highlighting the key role played by selective refinement.
We show that \method{} further improves for more iterations of refinement, while the baselines fail to reliably improve across iterations. 
Our ablations also underscore the benefits of external scoring models (as opposed to LLM self-verification and self-scoring) and the importance of a multi-agent framing. 

In summary, our contributions and findings are:
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=1pt,leftmargin=*]
    \item We propose \method, a multi-agent coarse-to-fine framework that adaptively uses targeted feedback to outperform refinement methods such as Self-Refine, as well as aggregation methods such as Best-of-$k$ and Self-Consistency, at all sample budgets across five datasets and two LLMs. 
    
    \item Our difficulty-aware coarse-to-fine resource allocation results in sample-efficiency, even outperforming $k$-way SC while using $8\times$ fewer samples. 
    \method{} continues to improve when $k$ increases, even as aggregation methods saturate.
    We also validate that our coarse-to-fine classifications correlate with human difficulty judgments. 
 
    \item Our analysis shows the necessity of incorporating RMs for refinement, showing that an LLM alone is less effective in determining when refinement is needed compared to trained off-the-shelf RMs. 
    Additionally, we find that using step-wise scores to guide feedback throughout the refinement process results in superior performance.
    Furthermore, we demonstrate that multi-agent communication between the Reviewer and the Refiner leads to additional improvements.

    \item  
    Lastly, we validate the importance of iterative refinement: while our refinement baselines do not show improvement over iterations, \method{} consistently improves with additional iterations. 

\end{itemize}


\section{\method{}

\label{sec:method}

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.99\textwidth]{figures/method.pdf}
    \caption{
    \method{} first generates $k$ reasoning chains for each problem and obtains solution-level scores from both the ORM and PRM. The problem's difficulty is then classified based on two conditions: (1) the quality of the majority answer and (2) the RM's answer confidence. A problem is considered hard when the majority answer receives a low average RM score \emph{and} the answer distribution is flat, indicating that no single answer is substantially better than others (i.e., low confidence). 
    For these hard problems, we employ a multi-agent setup with three agents: Solver, Reviewer and Refiner. 
    The Solver generates $k$ reasoning chains, passing them to the PRM for step-wise scoring to pinpoint \setlength{\fboxsep}{1pt}\colorbox{Dandelion}{\textcolor{red}{errors}}. The Reviewer turns scores into targeted feedback, and the Refiner improves the previously generated $k$ solutions using the Reviewer's feedback.
    This review-refine communication cycle is repeated until either of the two conditions passes, or a maximum number of iterations is reached. 
    }
    \label{fig:model}
\end{figure*}

\method{} is an adaptive framework that aims to improve both the performance and efficiency of multi-step reasoning with LLMs by intelligently applying test-time aggregation and refinement. 
\method{} consists of three intertwined components, resulting in a unified solution to the three issues discussed in \cref{sec:intro}: Excessive refinement, the inability of LLMs to localize and address their own errors, and insufficient refinement.
(1) \method{} avoids excessive refinement by categorizing problem difficulty as easy or hard, using coarse-grained aggregation for easy problems and fine-grained, iterative multi-agent refinement for hard ones.
(2) To improve error localization, we incorporate step-wise scores from an external reward model, providing targeted feedback and thereby helping the LLM to identify problematic steps. To enable effective refinement where the feedback is properly incorporated, we employ a multi-agent setup with three agents: the \textit{Solver}, the \textit{Reviewer}, which generates targeted feedback based on step-wise RM scores, and the \textit{Refiner}, which incorporates feedback to generate new solutions.
(3) To ensure sufficient refinement, the refined solutions are re-evaluated, iteratively initiating further rounds of multi-agent refinement if needed, and making the communication between agents bidirectional. 
Below we introduce \method{} in detail.

\subsection{Overall \method{} Framework}
\label{sec:background}
\textbf{Background.} In \method{}, we incorporate three models: (1) an LLM with three roles: the Solver, the Reviewer, and the Refiner for generating initial solutions, targeted feedback, and refinement, respectively (more details in \cref{ssec:multiagent}), (2) an Outcome Reward Model \citep[ORM;][]{cai2024internlm2} for generating global, solution-level correctness score, and (3) a Process Reward Model \citep[PRM;][]{wang2023math} for generating local step-by-step correctness scores. 
Given a query and a Solver's response, the ORM evaluates the overall -- or ``global'' -- correctness of this response. 
A PRM, on the other hand, assigns scores for each step within a reasoning chain, providing a finer-grained -- or ``local'' -- correctness signal. 
The ORM and PRM perform both independent and joint tasks in our framework.
The ORM's global correctness score is crucial for deciding which refined samples to retain during iterative refinement, 
and the PRM's local correctness score is used for generating targeted feedback.
Both the ORM and PRM contribute to (1) assessing problem difficulty and (2) Weighted Self-Consistency \citep{li2023making} for final answer selection (see \cref{ssec:wsc} for details).
\textbf{Overview.} A high-level overview of \method{} is presented in \cref{fig:model}. The process begins with the Solver generating $k$ reasoning chains for each problem, followed by the ORM and PRM providing solution-level scores (as illustrated in the top-left of \cref{fig:model}). Next, the input problem's difficulty is classified based on two criteria (top-right of \cref{fig:model}): (1) the quality of the majority answer and (2) the RMs' answer confidence. Refinement is initiated only when the problem is deemed difficult, which occurs when the majority answer receives a low average RM score \emph{and} the answer distribution is flat -- indicating no single answer is significantly better than the others (i.e., low confidence). For these hard samples requiring refinement, we employ a multi-agent setup with three agents: the Solver, the Reviewer, and the Refiner (bottom of \cref{fig:model}). The Reviewer uses the $k$ reasoning chains generated by the Solver, along with the step-wise scores from the PRM, to provide targeted feedback. The Refiner then enhances the $k$ solutions based on this feedback. This bidirectional communication between the Reviewer and Refiner can continue for multiple iterations. At the end of each iteration, the ORM assigns a global correctness score to each chain, determining which top $k$ solutions to retain, with the final answer being aggregated from these top $k$ solutions.
\subsection{Classifying Problem Difficulty and Assigning to Coarse or Fine Methods}
\label{sec:stage1}
The first step of \method{} is to categorize each problem's difficulty, either as easy or hard.  
As illustrated in \cref{fig:model}, we assess two conditions described below.
\textbf{Condition 1: Is the Majority Answer of High Quality?}
To assess problem difficulty, the Solver generates $k$ solutions for the input question and group them by their final answers. From the largest cluster of solutions, we calculate the average RM score and normalize it by the average score across all solutions, denoted as $\mathcal{S}_{avg}$. 
After normalization, if $\mathcal{S}_{avg}$ is greater than $0$, Condition 1 is \emph{true}, meaning the majority answer is already high-quality, and hence no refinement is needed.  If it is lower, Condition 1 is \emph{false}, suggesting even the most frequent answer is poor, so the generated solutions may benefit from refinement. 
Using this procedure, we evaluate the quality of the majority answer using both ORM and PRM separately but with the same procedure, i.e., if any of the RMs gives the majority answer a low score, Condition 1 will be \emph{false} (see \cref{sec:cond} for more details).
\textbf{Condition 2: Is Reward Models' Answer Confidence High?}
We check if the RMs are confident in any single answer. Confidence is determined by how concentrated the answer distribution is (i.e., if one cluster stands out). A concentrated distribution indicates high confidence, while a spread-out distribution shows low confidence. 
The answer distribution is formed by (1) the frequency of each unique answer and (2) the
total RM score of each answer cluster. 
That is, we weigh each answer cluster by its aggregated RM score, similar to the approach used in Weighted Self-Consistency \citep{li2023making}.
We calculate confidence (denoted as $C$) using the entropy of the answer clusters.
If $C \geq 0.5$, Condition 2 is \emph{true}, meaning RMs are confident in one answer. If $C < 0.5$, Condition 2 is false, indicating uncertainty and the need for further refinement. Note that we pass the answer cluster entropy to the sigmoid function, making $0.5$ a natural threshold requiring no tuning; further details can be found in \cref{sec:cond}. 
Again, we evaluate both ORM and PRM's answer confidence separately, each following the same procedure (i.e., if any of the RMs has low confidence, Condition 2 is \emph{false}).

\textbf{Coarse-to-Fine Decision.} If \emph{either} of the conditions is met (the quality of the answer is high \emph{or} the RMs are confident on an answer), an instance is delegated to the easy -- or coarse-grained -- method. 
Conversely, if \emph{both} conditions are not satisfied, the instance is delegated to the hard -- or fine-grained -- method.
For easy instances, we apply the coarse-grained method: Weighted Self-Consistency \citep{li2023making}, where we assign a weight to each answer cluster using the sum of the solution-level scores generated by both ORM and PRM, and choose the final answer from the cluster with the highest weight \footnote{In \cref{tab:ormprm_ablation} we show that this combination outperforms using the ORM or PRM only.}. Therefore, the coarse-grained method does \emph{not} involve refinement.
\subsection{Fine-Grained Multi-Agent Iterative Refinement}
\label{ssec:multiagent}
For hard instances (which fail both conditions), we need to employ refinement to unlock improvements (see the bottom part of \cref{fig:model}). 
By categorizing problems as either easy or hard and applying refinement only to hard problem solutions, we mitigate the issue of excessive refinement, addressing ``Issue 1'' in \cref{fig:intro}.  
Additionally, we address ``Issue 2'' by employing a multi-agent setup with three agents: (1) the \textit{Solver}, which generates the initial solution (2) the \textit{Reviewer}, which takes PRM scores and a reasoning chain as input, and generates targeted feedback that pinpoints the errors within the chain, and (3) the \textit{Refiner}, which takes the feedback generated by the Reviewer to refine the previous chain.
Finally, to tackle ``Issue 3'' (insufficient refinement), we iterate the refinement process until an adequate solution is found. 
Below we introduce the details of the fine-grained refinement process.
  
\textbf{Solver generates $k$ solutions.} \method{} begins by generating $k$ solutions to a given problem. 
Implementationally, this is done in the previous step (assessing problem difficulty), as shown in the top-left of \cref{fig:model}. 
Thus, given a problem that is classified as hard, we can directly re-use the $k$ reasoning chains \textit{already generated} by the Solver. 

\textbf{Reviewer generates targeted feedback.} 
To effectively localize and address the errors, we use the PRM to evaluate each generated solution and assign local step-wise scores. 
We append these scores to the end of each step (see the bottom-left of \cref{fig:model}), and pass the result to the Reviewer. The goal of the Reviewer is to incorporate step-wise correctness scores from the PRM to generate actionable feedback. That is, it takes a chain with the PRM scores as input, and is prompted to identify problematic steps that need refinement and possible ways to fix them. We use a 1-shot prompt to guide the Reviewer how to generate targeted feedback, and the prompt is shown in \cref{fig:refinement_prompt}.

\textbf{Refiner improves solutions based on targeted feedback.}
The Refiner then uses the targeted feedback provided by the Reviewer to enhance the reasoning chain initially generated by the Solver. By highlighting specific errors in the reasoning chain, the targeted feedback enables the Refiner to address mistakes more effectively, as it clearly identifies which steps are incorrect; this can help models correct errors \citep{tyen2024correct}. In \cref{tab:multiagent}, we also show that using this communicative multi-agent setup between the Reviewer and the Refiner makes refinement more effective. Here we again use a 1-shot prompt to teach the Refiner how to fix the error and improve a reasoning chain based on targeted feedback, and the prompt for the Refiner is shown in \cref{fig:refinement_prompt}.

\textbf{Iterating the refinement process for bidirectional communication.}
For some hard instances, one round of refinement may be insufficient; thus, we iterate the refinement process across multiple rounds, creating a communication cycle between the Reviewer and the Refiner, with outputs from the Refiner going back to the Reviewer in the next round. 
To ensure sufficient refinement (and prevent excessive refinement), we re-evaluate the two conditions described in \cref{sec:stage1} in each iteration. 
The refinement continues until (a) one of the conditions is met, or (b) a predefined maximum number of iterations is reached. 

\textbf{Final answer selection.}
The refinement process described above operates on all $k$ chains simultaneously, producing $k$ refined chains in each iteration.
At the end of each iteration, we use the ORM to assess whether the refined solution has improved based on its global correctness score. 
In other words, by the end of each iteration, we have $2k$ reasoning chains -- $k$ initial and $k$ refined -- but retain only the top $k$ based on their global ORM scores. 
Here we choose to base the decision on the ORM score because the targeted feedback is generated with PRM's step-wise scores, so selecting the solution via another scoring model avoids overfitting.
Finally, the answer is selected using Weighted Self-Consistency over these retained top $k$ chains, at the end of each iteration.

\textbf{Key Improvements.} By leveraging external reward models and an iterative multi-agent framework, our method achieves several key improvements: (1) it avoids excessive refinements by only refining hard samples. (2) it enhances error localization and resolution by using PRM's step-by-step scoring to offer targeted feedback, and thereby enabling effective communication between the Reviewer and the Refiner. (3) it ensures thorough refinement by re-evaluating refined solutions, and initiating additional rounds of refinement when necessary.


\section{Experimental Setup}


\label{sec:expt}
\paragraph{Implementation Details.} We develop \method{} with \llama~\citep{llama3modelcard} and \gpt~\citep{openai2023gpt3.5} as the base LLMs.
Based on their strong performance on standard reward modeling evaluations \citep{lambert2024rewardbench}, we choose InternLM-7B \citep{cai2024internlm2} as the ORM, and Math-Shepherd-7B \citep{wang2023math} as the PRM for computing the RM scores. 
By default, we sample $k=40$ reasoning chains in each iteration for \method{}, and the decoding temperature is set to $0.8$. The maximum number of iterations is set to $3$, with additional analysis on increasing the number of iterations in \cref{fig:iteration} and \cref{tab:more}. We compare against different categories of baselines, as follows.

\begin{itemize}[wide=1pt, leftmargin=*, after=\strut]
    \item \textbf{Vanilla Prompting.} The first baseline as a reference is zero-shot Chain-of-Thought~\citep{wei2022chain}; note that this only generates one reasoning chain per question without aggregation.
    \item \textbf{Iterative Prompting.} Next, we compare \method{} to an iterative prompting method, \textbf{Self-Refine (SR)} \citep{madaan2023self}, which refines the initial CoT answer via iteratively prompting the LLM to generate feedback and refine the previous output accordingly.
    \item \textbf{Aggregation-based Methods.} The third category generates multiple samples for each question. 
    Here, we sample $k$ solutions from the same LLM (using a temperature of 0.8) and select the final answer either via $k$-way \textbf{Self-Consistency (SC)} \citep{wang2022self} or according to the highest ORM score (\textbf{Best-of-$k$}) \citep{lightman2023let,sun2024easytohardgeneralizationscalablealignment,wang2023math}. 
    Note that we compare with $k=120$ for these aggregation baselines, but in \method{}, only the hard instances require more than $40$ samples (with up to $3 \times 40 = 120$ samples used in cases needing three iterations of refinement).
    \item \textbf{Iterative Baseline with Aggregation.}
    To enable a fair comparison, we also report a stronger version of self-refine by combining \textbf{Self-Refine and Self-Consistency (SR+SC)}, i.e., a baseline that is iterative, refines, and aggregates. Specifically, this baseline applies Self-Refine for $k$ samples in parallel, and the final answer is derived by aggregating the $k$ refined solutions.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Datasets.} We measure the effectiveness of \method{} extensively on five math reasoning datasets.
The first class of datasets we test on are math word problems: \textbf{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021training}, \textbf{SVAMP}~\citep{patel2021nlp}, and \textbf{MATH}~\citep{hendrycksmath2021}. GSM8K and SVAMP consist of grade school-level math problems, with 1312 and 1000 problems in the test set. 
The MATH dataset is relatively more challenging, comprising high-school math competitions spanning diverse topics such as Prealgebra, Algebra, Number Theory, Counting and Probability, Geometry, Intermediate Algebra, and Precalculus and a total of 5000 problems. 
Following previous works \citep{lightman2023let,wang2023math}, we evaluate MATH performance on a representative subset of 500 samples.
Furthermore, we also evaluate on mathematics splits of general benchmarks that test language models' world knowledge and problem-solving abilities over various subjects such as \textbf{MMLU}~\citep{hendryckstest2021} and \textbf{SAT}~\citep{zhong2023agieval} with 974 and 220 test instances respectively.

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{ssec:main}
\paragraph{\method{} outperforms all baselines at the first iteration.}
We present our main results in \cref{tab:main}. 
First, one iteration of \method{} (i.e., with only one round of communication between the Reviewer and the Refiner) already outperforms all baselines.
Compared to aggregation-based methods, which generate multiple responses for each problem without refinement, we observe that \method{} after the first iteration, improves over Best-of-$120$ by $3.2\%$ (absolute) averaged across the five datasets on Llama-3-8B, despite using $2\times$ fewer samples.
Note that our method's first iteration only involves $40$ samples for easy problems and $40$ refined chains (which are only refined once) for the \emph{subset} of hard problems, making our $k=55$ on average. 
When compared to $120$-way SC, our method shows an even greater average improvement of $3.3\%$ on Llama-3-8B and $3.2\%$ on GPT-3.5.
Turning to refinement-based methods, we compare against Self-Refine \citep[SR;][]{madaan2023self} without aggregation, where each iteration operates on a single instance. 
Note that we run refinement-based baselines with up to $5$ iterations and only report the best in \cref{tab:main} (denoted as ``Best Iter''), leaving a more detailed comparison in \cref{fig:iteration} and \cref{tab:more}.
On average, \method{} shows $17.1\%$ and $13.5\%$ improvements over SR for Llama-3-8B and GPT-3.5; however, SR alone is a weaker baseline as it does not aggregate over multiple samples; therefore, we also compare to SR + SC.
Even when compared to SR + SC's best iteration, \method{} outperforms SR + SC by $5.4\%$ (Llama-3-8B) and $4.9\%$ (GPT-3.5) on average.
This performance gain can be attributed to the fact that \method{} can adaptively address challenging instances with targeted refinement.

\paragraph{PRM-based targeted feedback enables better refinement.}
\label{ssec:llmrefine}
Having demonstrated that selectively applying refinement in \method{} is crucial for achieving improvements, we now compare the refinement process with and without using a PRM. To this end, \emph{without using a PRM}, we ask the LLM to generate an updated solution based on its own previous reasoning, referring to this as LLM Self-Refinement. This is similar to SR + SC  in \cref{tab:main}, except we use \method{}'s ability to identify hard instances and only apply refinement to those, resorting to Weighted SC for the easier problems. 
Compared to the full \method{} in row 4 of \cref{tab:llmrefine}, using LLM's self-refinement (row 1) results in an average drop of $1.5\%$, indicating that using the LLM for refinement is less effective than using a PRM. 
To further examine how sensitive the refinement process is to the score quality, in row 2, we replace the actual PRM scores with random scores. The result is worse than row 4, indicating that PRM scores help in localizing errors.
Finally, we test whether the global ORM score can offer a similar advantage as using the local PRM score. In row 3, instead of appending the step-wise score in each step, we place the global ORM score at the end of the response to generate the feedback. Result shows that it performs slightly worse than using the PRM score, suggesting that while global correctness is a strong signal as well, local correctness scores better localize errors in the intermediate steps, helping the LLM identify and correct them more effectively. 
Overall, \method{}'s method of using the step-wise feedback from the PRM is most effective for refinement.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Improving Reasoning by Aggregation.} 
Self-Consistency \citep[SC;][]{wang2022self} generates $k$ reasoning chains and marginalizes over the chains to obtain answer clusters; the most frequently-occurring answer is selected as the final prediction.
While simple and effective, it requires generating $k$ solutions \emph{for every sample} at test time. 
This can be costly and suboptimal, as both past work and our work show that SC saturates as $k$ increases \citep{chen2024llmcallsneedscaling,li2024agentsneed}. 
To enhance cost-effectiveness, several studies adaptively determine the number of samples ($k$) required \emph{for each instance} \citep{aggarwal2023let,li2024escape}. 
However, the performance of such approaches is upper-bounded by SC -- they address the cost issue but do not enhance overall performance. 
To surpass SC, \citet{yin2024aggregation} argue that SC's focus on selecting only the most frequent answer may overlook instances where the correct answer is actually the second most frequent. They propose using the LLM to evaluate answer clusters, taking into account not just frequency but also the LLM-evaluated quality of the answers.  
Given recent findings that LLMs struggle with self-correction and finding errors \citep{huang2024large,tyen2024correct,kamoi2024can}, we instead propose using external RMs for deciding between coarse-grained aggregation and fine-grained refinement, identifying errors, and aiding refinement; this allows us to improve over aggregation alone. 

\paragraph{LLM-based Verification and Refinement.} 

Past work mostly uses RMs for verification purposes \citep{li2023making, khalifa2023grace, cobbe2021training, lightman2023let}. 
Notably, GLoRe \citep{havrilla2024glore} takes local correctness into account for refinement, but in a non-adaptive way, and it requires specific data curation with fine-tuning; our work is adaptive and uses off-the-shelf global and local models.
Another line of work has proposed using the LLM itself as a verifier, in place of an RM \citep{liu2024large, zhang2024accessing, aggarwal2023let, madaan2023self}.
However, recent work has questioned the ability of LLMs to ``self-evaluate'' or ``self-verify'' their own reasoning~\citep{huang2024large, stechly2023gpt4, kamoi2024can}.
Hence, \method{}  uses readily-available external RMs \citep{christiano2017deep, wang2023math, cai2024internlm2, lambert2024rewardbench} to guide the LLM's reasoning.

As uniformly refining all samples can in fact hurt performance, \citet{shridhar2024art} trained specific models to decide when to refine and when to trust refined solutions. 
Their non-iterative method breaks down the question into multiple subquestions, under the assumption that question decomposition helps LLM generate more accurate answers. 
In \method{}, our refinement process uses a coarse-to-fine resource allocation method to differentiate easy from hard problems (for which we use both global and local rewards) and thus to decide when to refine, uses explicit step-wise local scoring rather than question decomposition, and iteratively refines guided by global correctness to ensure sufficient refinement. 
\citet{wadhwa2024dcr} propose a multi-agent detect-critique-refine pipeline for generation tasks; in contrast, \method{} focuses on reasoning tasks, uses external global and local reward models for selective coarse-to-fine refinement (whereas \citet{wadhwa2024dcr}'s detection uses the same metric as their evaluation, which is infeasible in reasoning where the metric requires access to the gold answer), 
and employs an iterative, bidirectional multi-agent communication setup. 

\paragraph{Multi-Agent Systems with LLMs.}  
An increasing number of studies explore how LLMs can be used in multi-agent systems, where the agents interact, collaborate, and compete \citep{wang-etal-2024-unleashing, lu2024llm, feng-etal-2024-dont}. 
Related to our work, one line of multi-agent research focuses on structured debates or discussions between LLM agents, where the interaction helps refine and improve previously generated solutions \citep{du2023debate, liang2023encouraging, yin2023exchange, chen2024reconcile}. 
We find that separating out roles into a multi-agent setup, with one agent generating feedback and another other refining based on that feedback, yields better results than attempting both at once. 
Generally, a major challenge in multi-agent systems is achieving a correct consensus among LLMs; external feedback can help prevent this consensus from aligning with the agents' internal and possibly erroneous outputs. 
Therefore, unlike past work which relies on models themselves to provide feedback, \method{} incorporates external reward models for more objective scoring, enabling the generation of targeted feedback to guide the model in refining its responses.
In \cref{ssec:llmrefine}, we show that this approach is more effective than relying on the LLM to identify its own errors. 



\section{Conclusion}

Building on the observation that different problems require varying amounts of computation, we introduced \method{}, a method that adaptively allocates more computational resources to more challenging problems and selectively applies refinement where appropriate, i.e., on harder problems.
\method{} addresses three key issues in refinement: excessive refinement on easy examples, the inability of LLMs to detect and correct errors in their reasoning, and insufficient refinement on hard instances. Our approach tackles these issues by employing both global and local external reward models to decide which samples to refine. We then incorporate local correctness scores to generate targeted feedback and an iterative multi-agent communication framework to refine solutions for hard problems.
Our results across five math datasets and two models show that our coarse-to-fine method consistently outperforms both coarse-grained aggregation and fine-grained refinement alone at any given budget and even outperforms baselines using substantially more computation.
In our ablations, we demonstrate the importance of selective refinement, showing that performance generally drops when refining all samples uniformly. 
We also highlight the role of iteration in our framework, showing increased performance across iterations even as baselines stagnate.
