\title{LASER: A Neuro-Symbolic Framework for Learning Spatio-Temporal Scene Graphs with Weak Supervision}

\begin{abstract}

We propose \ours, a neuro-symbolic approach to learn semantic video representations that capture rich spatial and temporal properties in video data by leveraging high-level logic specifications. 
In particular, we formulate the problem in terms of alignment between raw videos and spatio-temporal logic specifications. 
The alignment algorithm leverages a differentiable symbolic reasoner and a combination of contrastive, temporal, and semantics losses.
It effectively and efficiently trains low-level perception models to extract a fine-grained video representation in the form of a spatio-temporal scene graph that conforms to the desired high-level specification.
To practically reduce the manual effort of obtaining ground truth labels, we derive logic specifications from captions by employing a large language model with a generic prompting template.
In doing so, we explore a novel methodology that weakly supervises the learning of spatio-temporal scene graphs with widely accessible video-caption data.
We evaluate our method on three datasets with rich spatial and temporal specifications: OpenPVSG, 20BN-Something-Something, and MUGEN. 
We demonstrate that our method learns better fine-grained video semantics than existing baselines.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}








Understanding the semantics of video data has gained prominence due to a wide range of real-world applications such as video search, text-video retrieval, video question answering, video segmentation, and video captioning.
In particular, there are two important aspects of video semantics: \textit{spatial semantics} and \textit{temporal semantics}.
Spatial semantics concern the entities in the video, their individual attributes, and their semantic relationships.
For example, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:intro-example}, ``sponge on the table'' specifies two entities, ``sponge'' and ``table'', connected by the relation ``on''.
Temporal semantics, on the other hand, capture actions and properties evolving through time.
For instance, ``lift and drop a sponge'' describes two temporally consecutive actions ``lift'' and ``drop''.

Significant progress has been made in capturing each aspect of semantics.
A well-known representation of spatial semantics is a \textit{Scene Graph} \cite{kuznetsova2018imagev4, lu2016visual}, and many scene graph generation procedures have been proposed over the years \cite{zhu2022scene}. 
Most of these endeavors have been focused on extracting scene graphs from images. 
Recent research demonstrates a growing interest in extracting temporal semantics from videos, typically specified by an \textit{action sequence} that describes a series of events. 
To explicitly learn combined spatial and temporal semantics, \textit{Spatio-Temporal Scene Graph} (STSG) \cite{shang2017video, zhu2022scene} has been proposed to represent entity relations throughout a video. 
However, despite significant strides in this field, high-fidelity STSG for video modality remains elusive due to the complexity of low-level annotations that are often tedious and hard to obtain \cite{yang2023panoptic}. 

\input{figures/temporal_example}

To this end, weak supervision stands as a potential solution. 
Due to the advent of high-performing Large Language Models (LLM), it is conceivable that we can get fine-grained captions of videos~\cite{damonlpsg2023videollama}, and utilize them as weak supervisory signals. 
The challenge then is how to systematically learn the video semantics from weak supervision. 
Specifically, given a caption of a video, it is non-trivial to use it to supervise an STSG generation model.
We would need a common ground to compare the spatial and temporal information in both the caption and the predicted STSG.
Our proposed solution to this conundrum is to convert the caption into \textit{logical specifications} that can be systematically checked against the STSG for semantic alignment.

We set out by designing STSL, a general and expressive \underline{S}patio-\underline{T}emporal \underline{S}pecification \underline{L}anguage for specifying fine-grained spatio-temporal properties.
STSL is grounded in \textit{Finite Linear Temporal Logic} (LTL$_f$) \cite{de2013linear} which is used to describe temporal properties over finite traces of action and states.
STSL subsumes action sequences commonly seen in video-action alignment tasks \cite{chang2019d3tw} while capturing additional temporal nuances such as ``until'' and ``finally''. 
It also allows to express common-sense constraints
for extra supervision.
Finally, combined with relational predicates, such as ``above'' and ``touching'', over variables and entities, it can specify the spatial semantics of video.

We combine STSG and STSL into a novel framework, \ours\footnote{\underline{L}earning to \underline{A}lign for \underline{S}patio-t\underline{E}mporal \underline{R}epresentations.}, for learning fine-grained video representations. 
\ours\ employs the neuro-symbolic learning paradigm \cite{huang2021scallop, manhaeve2018dpl, li2020closed, manhaeve2018dsl}.
In particular, we implement STSL and a specification checker for it atop the Scallop neuro-symbolic framework \cite{li2023scallop} by extending it with novel constraint solving functionality.
Our checker computes an \textit{alignment score} between a pair of predicted STSG and an STSL specification describing the input video.
Intuitively, the alignment score represents the likelihood of the STSL specification being satisfied over the STSG extracted from the video.
We leverage the end-to-end differentiable reasoning capability of Scallop to enable training of the STSG model using weak supervision.
To provide additional supervision, we incorporate contrastive learning, time-span supervision, and semantic loss into our loss function design. 
Further, since such specifications are usually unavailable in existing datasets, we devise a generic prompting template that utilizes a large language model like GPT-4 \cite{openai2024gpt4} to convert any caption into an STSL program. 
This enables us to leverage widely accessible video datasets with captions for learning a fine-grained STSG extraction model.


We evaluate our method on three datasets, OpenPVSG \cite{yang2023panoptic}, 20BN-Something-Something \cite{goyal201720bn}, and MUGEN \cite{hayes2022mugen}.
The STSL specifications collected from these datasets exhibit diverse patterns, including action sequences, action pre- and post-conditions, evolving entity relationships, and common-sense temporal constraints.
We illustrate that our method learns high-quality video semantic representations, outperforming existing baselines.

We summarize the main contributions of this work as follows: 
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=-1ex,partopsep=1ex,parsep=1ex,leftmargin=0.5cm]
    \item We formulate spatio-temporal scene graph learning as weakly supervised learning via captions.
    \item We design STSL, a general and expressive spatio-temporal specification language, for specifying fine-grained video semantics.
    \item We implement a differentiable neuro-symbolic alignment checker to relate between an STSL specification and a spatio-temporal scene graph.
    \item We propose \ours, an end-to-end framework for learning spatio-temporal scene graphs with weak supervision from available video captions, leveraging an integrated loss function that combines alignment loss, contrastive loss, temporal loss, and semantic loss.
    \item We empirically evaluate \ours\ on three video understanding datasets, demonstrating superior performance in video semantics extraction tasks. 
\end{enumerate}



\section{Illustrative Overview}

\label{sec:illustrative-overview}
\input{figures/learning_pipeline}

We illustrate our approach using two examples adapted from the 20BN-Something-Something (20BN) dataset \cite{goyal201720bn} and the MUGEN dataset \cite{hayes2022mugen} (Figures \ref{fig:illustrative-overview-example} and \ref{fig:motivation_mugen}).
The 20BN dataset contains real-life short videos involving a human performing simple actions, such as ``push'', ``move-towards'', and ``lift'', on other objects.
The MUGEN dataset contains short gameplay footage of a 2D platform game \textit{CoinRun}.
In the video game, the player character MUGEN can walk, jump, collect coins, and kill enemies.
Our model takes two inputs for the task of learning a fine-grained semantic video representation.
The first is the video.
For simplicity, we assume the main entities in the video are already detected and bounding-boxes are extracted.
The second is a description of the entities and events happening in the video, usually obtained from natural language video captions.
The goal of the task is to learn a fine-grained semantic video representation, in this case the \textit{spatio-temporal scene graph} (STSG), capturing entities, their relationships, events, and evolving properties.

In this work, instead of directly using NL captions as weak supervision labels, we propose to employ spatio-temporal specification written in STSL.
This language is based on the formalism of finite linear temporal logic (LTL$_f$), and can precisely capture the nuance of temporal relations.
We assume one such specification is given for each data point as a weak supervision label and we elucidate the specification extraction as a preprocessing step in our presented tasks in \S \ref{sec:evaluation}.
Once the temporal language STSL is formally defined, converting natural language to a specification becomes a semantic parsing task in the natural language field.
Due to the rapid growth in LLM and Auto-GPT \cite{yang2023autogpt}, we are convinced fine-grained video specifications can be extracted from captions.

Figure \ref{fig:illustrative-overview-example} shows one spatio-temporal specification describing the pre- and post-condition of a single high-level action ``push''.
Before a box is pushed off the desk, it must be on the desk (\texttt{on(v1, v2)}), and a hand is touching it (\texttt{touching(v1, v3)}).
After it is pushed off the desk, it must be under the desk ($\neg \texttt{above(v1, v2)}$).
These predicates that encoding static spacial relation between entities are connected by temporal logic modality (e.g., $\lozenge$ (finally)) to express the dynamic change of spacial relation, that is, a temporal ordering over the moments before ($\phi_{pre}$) and after ($\phi_{post}$) the action ``push'' happens.

Figure \ref{fig:motivation_mugen} shows another specification connecting three events ``jump'', ``collect'', and ``collect'' together using the $\textbf{U}$ (until) operator.
This suggests that the three events are likely consecutive.
Different from the traditional sequence of actions \cite{chang2019d3tw, huang2016connectionist}, the events here are relational predicates connecting entities and possibly properties.
For example, \texttt{jump(M, ``down'')} specifies that the actor of jump is (M)UGEN, and that the direction of jump is ``down''.
Despite expressing diverse semantics, both specifications can be uniformly represented under our LTL$_f$ based formalism.
The next question is how to learn the underlying STSG from the video and specifications.
In this work, we frame it as a weakly-supervised specification alignment problem, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:illustrative-overview-example}.
That is, the STSG extracted from video should align with (i.e., satisfy) the corresponding logic specification.
We design a neuro-symbolic alignment checker to compute an alignment score, i.e., the probability of alignment.
During training, our approach learns to extract an STSG such that it maximizes the alignment score between a corresponding pair of video and specification.
With additional semantic and contrastive loss, we show that our method can effectively generate an STSG that conforms to the specification.







\section{Related Work}


\textbf{Video Scene Graph Learning.} 
In recent years, learning video scene graphs has attracted significant attention from the computer vision community. 
Various tasks, including entity tracking, object identification, dynamic relation analysis, and pathfinding, are being explored \cite{yang2023panoptic, shang2017video, xu2022meta, sun2023trace, li2022essgg}. 
New techniques involving spatio-temporal aware networks have been developed. \cite{nag2023unbiased, Cong2021sttran, ji2021detecting, sun2019videobert}
However, learning fine-grained video scene graphs through weak supervision is still under exploration.
A few recent works have developed point-solutions for extracting fine-grained video semantics \cite{apriceno2022neuro, lee2023neuro, chang2019d3tw}.
Such approaches includes both dynamic time warping \cite{chang2019d3tw, dvornik2021drop, richard2018neuralnetwork, ding2018weakly}, soft nearest neighbor\cite{dwibedi2019temp, han2022temporal}, and semantic loss \cite{xu2022dont}.
To the best of our knowledge, \ours{} is the first framework to train a spatio-temporal scene graph generation model with weak supervision, using caption as labels.
\textbf{Vision Language Pretraining.}
Vision language pretraining is crucial in video understanding and has a wide range of downstream applications.
Current works have succeeded in learning visual representations using large-scale paired visual-textual data through contrastive learning in both image-text \cite{radford2021clip, jia2021scaling, zhai2023sigmoid}  and video-text representation \cite{li2021align, miech2019end, xu2021videoclip} learning.
Recent works also explore the viability of utilizing pretrained foundation models for generating image and video scene graphs \cite{Yao2021ICCV, liang2024ckt, zhang2023learning}.
Without loss of generality, we finetune SigLIP\cite{zhai2023sigmoid} as our scene graph extraction model.

\section{Methodology}



\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{images/laser-arch.png}
    \caption{Example model architecture of $M_\theta$ for extracting probabilistic STSG.}
    \label{fig:model-architecture}
\end{figure}

We begin by presenting the high-level problem definition.
We are given a dataset $\Dcal$ of video-specification pairs $(X, \psi)$, where $X = [x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a video containing $n$ frames, and $\psi$ is a spatio-temporal specification using LTL$_f$.
We wish to learn a neural model $M_\theta$ which extracts a fine-grained video representation $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = M_\theta(X)$ that conforms to 
the corresponding  specification $\psi$.
During training time, given a loss function $\Lcal$, we aim to minimize the following main objective:
\begin{figure}[H]
\footnotesize
\begin{align}
J(\theta) &= \frac{1}{|\Dcal|} \sum_{(X, \psi)  \in \Dcal} \Lcal(\Pr(M_\theta(X) \models \psi), 1),
\end{align}
\end{figure}

\noindent where $\Pr(\hat{\mathbf{r}} \models \psi)$ is the alignment score (probability of alignment) computed by our spatio-temporal alignment checker. 
We illustrate the full learning pipeline in \figref{fig:learning_pipeline} and detail the process in this section. We first describe our video semantics representation using a probabilistic relational database (\S \ref{sec:method-sym-db}).
Then, we present our specification language STSL (\S \ref{sec:method-stsl}) and its alignment checker (\S \ref{sec:method-ltl-inference}).
We further demonstrate how to automatically convert natural language captions to a specification in STSL using LLM (\S \ref{sec:nl2spec}).
Lastly in \S \ref{sec:method-loss}, we present the loss function design for our method.

\subsection{Video to Probabilistic Relational Database}
\label{sec:method-sym-db}

The neural perception model $M_\theta$, parametrized by $\theta$, converts pixel-based raw video data $X$ into a distribution of STSGs, which is encoded as a predicted probabilistic relational database $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = M_\theta(X)$.
\ours\ is model-agnostic, and we present two implementations: one optimized for generalizability, fine-tuning SigLIP \cite{zhai2023sigmoid} as the backbone for open-world scene graph generation (\figref{fig:model-architecture}), and the other optimized for accuracy, training a scene graph classifier from scratch with pretrained S3D \cite{xie2018s3d} backbone (\appref{app:experimental_details}).
At a high level, the model has two classification heads extracting two types of probabilistic information.
By associating the classification outputs with explicit relational symbols, we then construct the predicted database $\hat{\textbf{r}}$.
Here are the two types of probabilistic facts:
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=-1ex,partopsep=1ex,parsep=1ex,leftmargin=0.5cm]
\item 
Attribute of a single entity, e.g., 
$0.05::$ \texttt{deformed}(3, $e$) represents ``an entity $e$ is unlikely to be deformed at time 3''.
$0.92::$ \texttt{walk(10, \texttt{MUGEN}, \texttt{right})} stands for ``MUGEN is walking to the right at time 10''; \texttt{is-a}(4, $e$, human) represents ``the entity $e$ is a human at time 4''.
\item
Relation between a pair of entities, e.g., $0.88::$ \texttt{next-to}(5, $e_1$, $e_2$) for ``$e_1$ is next to $e_2$ at time 5''.
\end{enumerate}
Note that our time step does not directly correspond to each frame.
While there are other discretizations of time, in this work, each time step represents an ID of a clip, which is a size $k$ sliding window of the video.
We assume that the STSG is discretized into $m$ time steps.

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Specification Language}
\label{sec:method-stsl}
Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) \cite{Pnueli1977TheTL} is a formal logic system extending propositional logic with concepts about time.
It is commonly used for formally describing temporal events, with applications in software verification \cite{chaki2005concurrent, kesten1998algorithmic} and control \cite{ding2014optimal, sadigh2014learning}.
As we operate on prerecorded, finite-length videos, our language is developed using LTL$_f$ \cite{de2013linear}, which supports LTL reasoning over finite traces.
Thus, we use LTL$_f$ as a framework for specifying events and their temporal relationships occurring in a video.

Our STSL (Figures \ref{fig:ltl-high-level-syntax} and \ref{fig:ltl-low-level-syntax}) further extends LTL$_f$ by introducing relational predicates and variables.
It starts from the specification $\psi$ which existentially quantifies variables in an STSL formula.
The formula $\varphi$ is inductively defined, with basic elements as relational atoms $\alpha$ of the form $a(t_1, \dots, t_n)$.
Note that each term $t_i$ can be a quantified variable to be later grounded into a concrete entity.
From here, $\varphi$ can be constructed using basic propositional logic components $\land$ (and), $\lor$ (or), and $\neg$ (not).
The system additionally includes temporal unary operators $\square$ (always), $\lozenge$ (finally), $\bigcirc$ (next), and a binary operator $\mathbf{U}$ (until) \cite{albers2009automata}.
For example, the description ``MUGEN starts by climbing the stairs; immediately after that, it walks to the right'' can be represented as an STSL formula
\begin{align}
\label{eqn:climb-and-walk}
\psi = \texttt{climb}(\texttt{M}, \texttt{\_}) ~\mathbf{U}~ \texttt{walk}(\texttt{M}, \texttt{right}).
\end{align}
Note that an argument to the predicate \texttt{climb} is a wildcard (\texttt{\_}), since we do not specify whether it is climbing up or down.
This formula might seem too strict since it requires the two events to be consecutive.
To make the specification more natural, one can change the above formula to ``$\lozenge (\texttt{climb(M,\_)} \wedge \lozenge \texttt{walk(M, right)})$''.
Here, the two events, \texttt{climb} and \texttt{walk}, need to happen in chronological order but are not required to be consecutive.

\subsection{Natural Language to Programmatic Spatio-Temporal Specification}
\label{sec:nl2spec}

\input{figures/nl2spec}

Although directly providing the ground truth programmatic spatio-temporal specification already reduces the manual labeling effort compared to annotating each object in each frame of a video, it still requires a professional logic programmer's effort. 
To overcome this limitation, we introduce a large language model (LLM) to automatically extract the programmatic specification from video captions, which are widely available in video datasets.
We utilize the LLM to convert the video caption 
$c$ into programmatic specifications $\psi$. Directly converting from a caption to a program is challenging for an LLM especially in a low-data language like STSL. 
We hence use a few-shot learning approach with an LLM to generate an intermediate structured representation of the caption in JSON format.
For each caption $c$, our goal is to convert it into a series of events $E = \{e_1, e_2, \dots, e_{n}\}$. 
Each event includes
(a) a detailed natural language description of the event, which serves as the guidance for generating the following details
(b) a series of unary, binary, positive, and negative predicates describing the semantics of the scenario
(c) the location in the video where the event occurs (early, middle, or late)
(d) the duration of the event (long, medium, or short).
In \secref{sec:method-loss}, we further explain how we incorporate all the structured representations into the loss function.
We designed a generic prompt template to convert a caption into its structured representation. The prompt template consists of the following components:
(a) Constants for temporal specification: "early", "middle", "late", "short", "medium", and "long".
(b) Scene graph keywords, such as object names and relations.
(c) Few-shot examples of caption and JSON structured representations pairs.
We illustrate a caption and its structured representation in \figref{fig:nl2spec}, and the full prompt in \appref{app:nl2spec}.

The programmatic spatio-temporal specification is then generated by postprocessing the events in sequential order. 
Consequently, we can generate the programmatic spatio-temporal specification $\psi$ for the caption as a sequence of events happens in time order:

\begin{figure}[H]
\footnotesize
\begin{align}
\textstyle
\psi = \lozenge_{ei \in E} \psi_i, \quad \psi_i = \bigwedge_{\phi_j \in \psi_i} \phi_j
\end{align}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Spatio-Temporal Alignment Checking}
\label{sec:method-ltl-inference}
\input{figures/ltl-semantics}

Given a probabilistic database $\mathbf{r}$ that encodes a distribution of STSGs (\S \ref{sec:method-sym-db}), and a specification $\psi$ in STSL, we aim to measure the alignment score $\Pr(\mathbf{r} \models \psi)$ in an end-to-end and differentiable manner.
Conceptually, each probabilistic fact $f$ in the database can be toggled on or off, resulting in $2^{|\mathbf{r}|}$ distinct \textit{worlds}.
Denoting each world (i.e. a concrete STSG) as $w \in \Pcal(\mathbf{r})$ \footnote{$\Pcal$ represents power-set.}, we can check whether the world $w$ satisfies the specification $\psi$ or not.
From here, the alignment score can be computed as the sum of the probabilities of worlds satisfying $\psi$:
\begin{equation}
    \footnotesize
    \Pr(\mathbf{r} \models \psi) = \sum_{\substack{w \in \Pcal(\mathbf{r})\\ w \models \psi}} \Pr(w), \quad
    \Pr(w) = \prod_{f \in w} \Pr(f) \prod_{f \in \mathbf{r} \backslash w} (1 - \Pr(f))
\end{equation}
Suffering from exponential complexity, enumerating all possible worlds is intractable.
Existing general-purpose neuro-symbolic systems like Scallop \cite{li2023scallop} employ scalable algorithms to approximate this probability and greatly reduces the probabilistic reasoning time.
We also note that some of the STSG $w$ sampled from $\Pcal(\mathbf{r})$ might be infeasible due to having conflicting facts (e.g. MUGEN is walking and climbing at the same time.)
We extend Scallop's ``top-k proofs'' provenance to support general disjunctive constraints and early remove
infeasible STSGs that do not satisfy the specification. 
\ours\ implements the alignment checker using our extended version of Scallop.
It inductively computes the alignment between a temporal slice of $\textbf{r}$ and an STSL formula.
The whole specification $\psi$ is aligned if the full $\textbf{r}$ (from 1 to $m$) satisfies $\psi$ with a concrete variable grounding $\Gamma$, which maps variables to concrete entities.
We also extend Scallop with a variable assignment interface enumerating $\Gamma$ for constraint solving.
We illustrate one simplified evaluation process in \figref{fig:ltl-semantic-align}, and the full checker in \appref{app:temp_reasoner}.
The checker iteratively aligns the predicted probabilistic events (simplified to just climb and walk) with the specification.
At the 4th iteration, 3 different satisfying alignments are derived, yielding a final aggregated alignment score of $0.58$.

\subsection{Loss Function}
\label{sec:method-loss}

\textbf{Contrastive Learning.}
Unavoidable dataset biases exist in the specification, and contrastive learning can effectively reduce the bias and generate explanations of better quality. 
Let $(X_i, \psi_i)$ and $(X_j, \psi_j)$ be two datapoints in a mini-batch B, where $\psi_i$ and $\psi_j$ are the specifications for video $X_i$ and $X_j$ correspondingly. 
If $X_i \models \psi_j$, then it is an extra positive sample to the video $X_i$, else it is a negative sample to $X_i$.
Let the relational database representing the STSG predicted by the model be $\mathbf{r} = M_\theta(X_i)$,
we can thus define our contrastive loss:
\begin{equation}
\Lcal_c(w, \psi) = 
\frac{1}{|B|^2} \sum_{ \substack{(X_j, \psi_j) \in B}} \Lcal(Pr(w) \cdot \mathbbm{1}[w \models \psi],  \mathbbm{1}[\psi =\psi_j]), \quad
\Lcal_t = \sum_{(X, \psi) \in B}  \sum_{ w \in \mathcal{P}(M_\theta{(X)})} \Lcal_c(w, \psi)
\label{eqn:time_span}
\end{equation}
\textbf{Time-Span Supervision.}
A video caption is expanded into a sequence of events using LLM, with each event assigned a specific temporal target, detailing its location and duration within the video, as illustrated in Section \ref{sec:nl2spec}. 
By aligning the spatio-temporal specification $\psi$ with the video, we can identify when its sub-specifications, $\psi_1, \psi_2, \dots, \psi_n$, are met. 
This alignment facilitates weak supervision across the entire time span. 
Formally, this temporal specification yields a weight function $\Fcal$. 
Let $|X|$ be video length, $d_{s}$, $d_{m}$, $d_{l}$ be the proportional threshold for the duration length to be considered as ``short'', ``medium'', and ``long''.

\begin{figure}[H]
\footnotesize
\begin{align}
d_i &= \begin{cases}
      |X| * d_{s} & \text{if $e_i$ duration is short}\\
      |X| * d_{m} & \text{if $e_i$ duration is medium}\\
      |X| * d_{l} & \text{if $e_i$ duration is long}
    \end{cases}    
\end{align}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\footnotesize
\begin{align}
\Fcal(w, s, \psi_i) &= \begin{cases}
      \frac{d_i - s}{d_i} & \text{if $e_i$ location is early and } \anglebr{w, s} \models \psi_i \text{ and } s < d_i\\
       \frac{d_i - ||X| - 2s|}{d_i} & \text{if $e_i$ location is middle and } \anglebr{w, s} \models \psi_i \text{ and } \frac{|X|- d_i }{2} < s < \frac{|X| + d_i}{2}\\
      \frac{s-d_i}{d_i} & \text{if $e_i$ location is late and } \anglebr{w, s} \models \psi_i \text{ and } |X|-d_i < s \\
      0 & \text{otherwise}
    \end{cases}
\end{align}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
\footnotesize
\begin{align}
\Lcal_t &=  \sum_{(X, \psi) \in B} \sum_{ w \in \mathcal{P}(M_\theta{(X)})} \sum_{\psi_i \in \psi} \sum_{s \in 1 \dots |x|} \Fcal(w, s, \psi_i) \Lcal_c(w, \psi_i).
\end{align}
\end{figure}


\textbf{Semantic Loss.}
To provide further supervision, we resort to human knowledge encoded in the form of integrity constraints. 
We introduce semantic loss reflecting the probability of violating the integrity constraints.
For example, an entity in a video cannot be \texttt{open} and \texttt{closed} at the same time;
an entity that is not \texttt{bendable} cannot be \texttt{deformed}.
These integrity constraints may interweave so heavily that it is hard to use a simple disjoint multi-class classifier to enforce.
We encode all integrity constraints in the form of first-order logic rules, and our reasoning engine generates the probability that these constraints are violated.
We thus have the semantic loss as an extra-weighted term after calculating the other loss components.
Let $n$ be the number of integrity constraints.

\begin{figure}[H]
\footnotesize
\begin{align}
\Lcal_s = \sum_{i=0}^n \sum_{w \in \mathcal{P}(M_\theta{(X)})} \Lcal(\Pr(w \not\models \psi_{IC_i}), 0),
\end{align}
\end{figure}

\section{Evaluation}


\label{sec:evaluation}
Our evaluation aims to answer the following research questions:
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=-1ex,partopsep=1ex,parsep=1ex,leftmargin=0cm, label={}]
\item \textbf{RQ1:} How does the accuracy of STSGs learned with weak supervision using LASER compare to that of baselines, including both strong and weakly supervised ones?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Does LASER effectively alleviate the burden of requiring programmatic specifications through the use of GPT-4 \cite{openai2024gpt4} with a dataset-agnostic prompt template?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} How do different components of LASER's loss function contribute to its learning performance?
\end{enumerate}

To answer these questions, we evaluate \ours~on three datasets: a synthetic dataset MUGEN \cite{hayes2022mugen}, a realistic dataset 20BN-Something-Something \cite{goyal201720bn}, and a fine-grained STSG annotated dataset OpenPVSG \cite{yang2023panoptic}.
Further, we include two learning setups:
\ours-\textbf{P}: Learn STSG model given ground truth \textbf{P}rogrammatic specifications, and
\ours-\textbf{C}: Learn STSG model given \textbf{C}aptions as specifications (only video caption is provided).
We include more experimental details in \appref{app:experimental_details}.

Our main result is that LASER achieves comparable or even better performance, compared to even fully supervised methods, and using only a fraction of the training labels: $0.42$ VIOU improvement on OpenPVSG, $0.03$ F1-score improvement on 20BN, and $5.2\%$ accuracy improvement on MUGEN.
\subsection{OpenPVSG}

\textbf{Dataset.}
The OpenPVSG \cite{yang2023panoptic} dataset comprises $400$ videos sourced from Ego4D\cite{grauman2021ego4d}, VidOr\cite{shang2019annotating, thomee2016yfcc100m}, and EpicKitchen\cite{Damen2022RESCALING, Damen2018EPICKITCHENS}. 
This dataset offers fine-grained ground truth annotations of STSGs for $150K$ frames, encompassing $126$ object classes and $57$ relation classes.
We train on $1,538$ caption-video pairs, and test on $295$ caption-video pairs.

\textbf{Experimental Setup.}
As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:model-architecture}, our objective is to train an STSG extraction model capable of taking a video clip along with object bounding boxes as input and predicting the properties, attributes of objects, and relationships between objects.
As the ground truth programmatic specification is not provided in the OpenPVSG dataset, we only adopt the \ours-\textbf{C} setting. 
We finetune the SigLIP\cite{zhai2023sigmoid} model to generate the probabilistic spatio-temporal scene graph.
This model predicts both the similarity scores between the cropped objects and the unary predicate keywords, and the similarity scores between object pairs and the binary predicate keywords, yielding a probabilistic STSG.
We have two evaluation metrics, prediction accuracy for unary and binary predictions, and an VIOU metric, which is a comprehensive metric that checks the object classification, segmentation, and trajectory over time sequence. 
We use VIOU metric with $0.5$ threshold. 
\textbf{Quantitative Study.}
We compare against the fully supervised method, randomly initialized extraction model, and out-of-box SigLIP model as baselines. 
For the fully supervised baseline, we choose the most similar architecture as \ours, VPS + vanilla setup, which is a classifier based method in OpenPVSG.
As shown in \tabref{tab:openpvsg_performance}, \ours\ outperforms the fully supervised method on VIOU metric, and we demonstrate that fine-tuning improves the out-of-box SigLIP model.

\textbf{Dataset.}
The 20BN dataset consists of 
(a) video and action pairs of humans performing everyday actions with ordinary objects \cite{goyal201720bn},  
(b) expert designed pre-conditions and post-conditions for the actions in the PDDL language \cite{migimatsu2022grounding},
and 
(c) frame-based object bounding boxes \cite{materzynska2020something}.
There are 172 actions with 37 underlying predicates in this dataset, capturing object attributes, object states, and relationships between two objects.
Specifically, there are 6 static predicates, 21 unary predicates, and 10 binary predicates.
We train on $10,000$ training datapoints and test on $14,816$ data points, where the training and testing splits follow the 20BN-Something-Something dataset.

\textbf{Experimental Setup.}
In the 20BN dataset, each video is associated with an action label, and each of these actions has a corresponding logical specification, expressed as pre- and post-conditions in the PDDL language.
These PDDL specifications naturally correspond to the $\lozenge (\psi_{\text{pre}} \wedge \lozenge \psi_\text{post})$ structure in STSL. 
We use these specifications as ground truth programmatic specifications for \ours-\textbf{P}. 
For \ours-\textbf{C} setting, we use the action description provided in the dataset as captions to query for spatio-temporal labels.
To evaluate the performance of our STSG extraction model, we employ the F1-score as a metric, comparing its predictions against the fine-grained STSG labels obtained using heuristics. 
These labels are established based on the assumption that the pre-condition holds at the beginning and the post-condition holds at the end, serving as the symbolic ground truth representation for the entire video. 
Since the evaluation metric necessitates a frame-based symbolic video representation, we configure the video clip size to be 1.
Our training methodology incorporates three types of loss functions: contrastive loss, temporal loss, and semantic loss. 
For semantic loss, we extract common sense integrity constraints, such as ``a rigid object is not fluid.''

\textbf{Quantitative and Qualitative Study.}
We compare against GPA \cite{migimatsu2022grounding}, a DNF cross-entropy loss based approach.
GPA asserts the pre-condition satisfies at the first frame and post-conditions holds at the last frame, while \ours\ relaxes the assumption via STSL specification.
Our evaluation reveals that we achieve a higher average F1-score of $0.77$ compared to the baseline's score of $0.74$. 
Furthermore, our model outperforms the baseline in $71\%$ of the fine-grained predicate recognition tasks, as depicted in Figure \ref{fig:20bn-f1}.
We showcase the STSG model's frame-wise predictions on a few testing datapoints in \figref{fig:20bn_qualitative}, and the ablation study of our loss function design in \tabref{tab:20bn-ablation-loss}.


\subsection{MUGEN}

\textbf{Dataset.}
MUGEN \cite{hayes2022mugen} is a synthetic dataset that is based on an open-sourced platform game CoinRun \cite{cobbe2019coinrun}.
Each datapoint contains a $3.2$s video snippet of the game-play and a corresponding automatically generated text describing the video.
The agent in the game can perform $6$ actions: \texttt{walk}, \texttt{jump}, \texttt{kill}, \texttt{collect}, \texttt{die}, and \texttt{climb}.
A video may contain any sequence of actions, which may be simple or complex.
We train on $5,000$ training datapoints and test on $12,851$ datapoints.
\textbf{Experimental Setup.}
We construct the specification from the given text description using a simple heuristic-based semantic parser. 
We extract an ordered action list $[a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n]$ from the text with heuristics and then construct the specification as $a_1  \mathbf{U} a_2  \dots \mathbf{U} a_n$.
The generated specifications are used as ground truth programmatic STSL labels.
As the MUGEN video data is very different from natural videos, we need to train a classification model from scratch.
Our neural perception model consists of an S3D video encoding model pretrained on Kinetics 400 \cite{kay2017kinetics}, and MLP layers to classify the actions.
We use contrastive loss and semantic loss for training.
More details are in \appref{app:mugen_experiment}.

\textbf{Quantitative Study.}
We evaluate our action prediction performance on MUGEN and compare it to caption supervised VT-TWINS \cite{ko2022video}, TempCLR \cite{yang2023tempclr}, and a directly supervised baseline (Supervised). 
We provide all baselines with extra annotations on the start- and end-frame of each clip-based textual description.
As shown in Table \ref{tab:performance}, \ours~has better action prediction accuracy despite receiving less supervision than the baselines.
Moreover, our weakly supervised model even achieves better accuracy than the Supervised method on when trained on $5000$ data points.
We further evaluate our approach on a downstream video-specification retrieval task. 
Given 3 videos and 3 specifications, we want to match the correctly aligned pairs, retrieve specification given video and vice versa.
\ours{} outperforms an embedding-based baseline SDSC \cite{hayes2022mugen} on both tasks (Table~\ref{tab:downstream}).

\section{Related Work}



\textbf{Joint Visual-Textual Learning}
is crucial in video understanding and has a wide range of downstream applications.
Current works have succeeded in learning visual representations using large-scale paired visual-textual data through contrastive learning in both image-text \cite{radford2021clip, jia2021scaling}  and video-text representation \cite{li2021align, miech2019end, xu2021videoclip} learning.
A downstream application of the joint visual-textual learning task is visual-text retrieval, which aims to learn a multi-modal encoder to project both modalities onto a joint embedding space and compute a similarity score between the visual and textual data
\cite{bain2021frozen, radford2021clip, han2022temporal, li2019unicoder, lu2019multi}.
\textbf{Weakly-Supervised Visual-Textual Alignment} aims to synchronize an ordered textual input with a video based on its time span given just video-level labels.
The main challenge in this task lies in how to train without having access to the specific time boundaries corresponding to the text.
To mitigate this issue, an iterative process is usually adopted to first estimate and then refine the low-level time boundaries from the video-level labels.
Such approaches includes both dynamic time warping \cite{chang2019d3tw, dvornik2021drop, richard2018neuralnetwork, ding2018weakly} and soft nearest neighbor\cite{dwibedi2019temp, han2022temporal}.
There are also works incorporating temporal logic into the learning process as a semantic loss \cite{xu2022dont}.


\textbf{Neuro-Symbolic Methods} \cite{hitzler2022neuro} are known for their decoupling of complex learning systems into perceptual and reasoning systems.
There are a wide range of neuro-symbolic approaches solving individual computer vision tasks, such as visual reasoning \cite{chen2022comphy, amizadeh2020neuro}, visual-question-answering (VQA) \cite{huang2021scallop, Mao2019NeuroSymbolic}, and video action aligning \cite{huang2016connectionist, chang2019d3tw}.
Due to the recent surge of general-purpose neuro-symbolic programming systems \cite{manhaeve2018dpl, manhaeve2018dsl, huang2021scallop}, there is growing interest in scaling point-solutions into more general and complex systems.
\ours\ utilizes one such general-purpose neuro-symbolic programming system, Scallop \cite{huang2021scallop}, for extracting temporal and spatial semantics from videos.




\section{Conclusion, Limitation, and Future Outlook}


We presented \ours, comprising a generic specification language STSL and a differentiable neuro-symbolic framework for learning semantic video representations.
Our approach captures the rich spatial and temporal properties of a video by learning a spatio-temporal scene graph (STSG) through weak supervision in the form of logic specifications.
We evaluated
our work on three datasets OpenPVSG, 20BN-Something-Something, and MUGEN  which exhibit diverse temporal properties. 
\ours\ outperforms the current baselines on downstream tasks while providing explainability.
However, \ours\ still suffers from scalability issues due to the exponential number of object pairs occurring in the same frame limiting its application to captioned short videos. 
In the future, we plan to design tailored video-textual models for efficient learning over long videos with sparse labels.
