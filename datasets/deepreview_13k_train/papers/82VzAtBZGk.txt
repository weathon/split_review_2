\title{Safe Reinforcement Learning in Black-Box Environments via Adaptive Shielding}

\begin{abstract}
Empowering safe exploration of reinforcement learning (RL) agents during training is a critical impediment towards deploying RL agents in many real-world scenarios. Training RL agents in unknown, \textit{black-box} environments poses an even greater safety risk when prior knowledge of the domain/task is unavailable. We introduce ADVICE (Adaptive Shielding with a Contrastive Autoencoder), a novel post-shielding technique that distinguishes safe and unsafe features of state-action pairs during training, thus protecting the RL agent from executing actions that yield potentially hazardous outcomes. Our comprehensive experimental evaluation against state-of-the-art safe RL exploration techniques demonstrates how ADVICE can significantly reduce safety violations during training while maintaining a competitive outcome reward.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Reinforcement Learning (RL)~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement} is a powerful machine learning paradigm for solving complex decision-making tasks that has exhibited performance commensurate with the cognitive abilities of humans in diverse applications, including game-playing~\cite{alpha-go, alpha-zero, open-ai-five} and robot control~\cite{massively-drl, loc-behaviours}. Despite this huge potential, developing RL-based agents that can explore their environment safely remains a significant challenge. Exploring unfamiliar, and potentially hazardous, states while learning from the environment, especially in safety-critical domains, like robotics or healthcare, can pose real dangers. Alleviating this entails RL agents capable of synthesising an optimal policy by exploring the policy space adequately while ensuring safe exploration by preventing the execution of unsafe actions~\cite{concrete-problems}. 

Ensuring safety becomes an increasingly difficult challenge in complex environments typically characterised by high-dimensional state/action spaces~\cite{dalal2018safe}. Such environments require a large amount of training time before the agent can consistently complete the task and avoid safety concerns. This issue is further exacerbated in \textit{black-box} environments where no prior knowledge can be utilised before training; the only information available is the data observed in real time by the RL agent. In such scenarios, the risk associated with exploration increases exponentially as the agent must operate without pre-defined guidelines, rendering typical safe exploration techniques inadequate~\cite{waga2022dynamic}. 

Prior research on safe RL exploration formulates safety constraints as linear temporal~\cite{alshiekh2018safe, konighofer2023online, elsayed2021safe} and probabilistic logic~\cite{yang2023safe} specifications, whose use as a shield protects the agent during training. Shielding techniques are categorised into pre-shielding (restricting action choices to a predefined safe subset) and post-shielding (evaluating and modifying actions post-selection to ensure safety)~\cite{shieldreview}. Despite noteworthy advances, a common challenge across these methods is their reliance on some degree of prior knowledge about the environment, task, or safety concern which may not be generally available. Research targeting safe exploration in black-box environments employs Lagrangian methods~\cite{stooke2020responsive, altman1998constrained, tessler2018reward, achiam2017constrained}, or involves a pre-training phase before the shield synthesis~\cite{tappler2022automata}.
We present ADVICE (\textbf{AD}apti\textbf{V}e Sh\textbf{I}elding with a \textbf{C}ontrastive Auto\textbf{E}ncoder), a novel post-shielding technique for the safe exploration of RL agents in \textit{black-box} environments. The ADVICE shield is underpinned by a contrastive autoencoder that effectively learns distinguishing latent representations between safe and unsafe features (state-action pairs) and a non-parametric classifier that employs these latent representations, allowing new features to be identified as safe, or correcting them with a new safe action when deemed unsafe. Further, ADVICE encompasses an adaptation component that considers the agent's recent performance to automatically regulate the risk tolerance levels of the shield, thus encouraging exploration when appropriate. We demonstrate ADVICE's ability to work in highly complex \textit{black-box} environments and significantly reduce safety violations during training in comparison to state-of-the-art methods, such as Lagrangian multipliers~\cite{ddpg-paper} and discretized shields~\cite{tabular-shield}. 
To the best of our knowledge, ADVICE is the first research work that investigates shielding for safe RL exploration in black-box environments with high-dimensional state/action spaces and proposes an end-to-end methodology for shield synthesis without using \emph{any} prior knowledge. 



\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:rel-work}

\textbf{Shielding Techniques.} 
Recent research devises techniques for the safe exploration of RL agents using safety shields, allowing the agent to select from a pool of safe actions or correct an action deemed unsafe~\cite{shieldreview}. Existing shielding approaches leverage linear temporal logic (LTL) specifications~\cite{alshiekh2018safe, konighofer2023online, elsayed2021safe} or use external hints for constructing the LTL formulae~\cite{waga2022dynamic}. LTL specifications can be replaced with probabilistic logic programming (PLP)~\cite{yang2023safe}, extending their use to continuous deep RL and enabling safety constraints to be differentiable. By utilising logical neural networks~\cite{kimura2021reinforcement}, the same logic specifications can be both respected and learnt, providing a more nuanced understanding of safety. A recurring limitation of these methods is their reliance on explicit prior knowledge of their environment, task, and/or safety concerns. Although they can improve safety and, in some cases, eliminate violations altogether, their applicability is restricted to a narrow set of environments and safety considerations~\cite{turchetta2020safe}. In contrast, our \approach\ post-shielding method does not need \emph{any} prior knowledge, using exclusively the information captured in a typical RL problem. 

\textbf{Black-Box Safe Exploration Techniques.} Other recent research focuses on improving safety in \textit{black-box} environments, where no prior knowledge is provided to the agent/user. A trivial but effective solution is to record all unsafe features in a tabular format to prevent the agent from repeating them~\cite{tabular-shield}. However, this approach is limited to discrete environments or extremely low-dimensional spaces. Other research collects data in the environment before training, to then utilise a safety layer~\cite{dalal2018safe, srinivasan2020learning, thananjeyan2021recovery} or shield~\cite{tappler2022automata} to protect the agent. This requires a significant amount of data collection, resulting in an increasing number of safety violations. Lagrangian methods enable modelling the Markov Decision Process as a Constrained Markov Decision Process, leading to their wide adoption due to their simplicity and effectiveness~\cite{altman2021constrained, garcia2015comprehensive}. The Lagrangian multiplier can be fixed~\cite{stooke2020responsive, altman1998constrained}, or integrated into the algorithm itself~\cite{tessler2018reward, achiam2017constrained}. Other advancements employ uncertainty estimation concepts~\cite{kahn2017uncertainty, jain2021safe}. Defining safety in terms of uncertainty and propagating it into the RL algorithm during training yields a cautious yet effective agent for reducing safety violations, even in complex environments. Similarly to these approaches, \approach\ needs no prior knowledge; however, \approach\ also entails far less data collection than the previously discussed techniques.




\section{Preliminaries}

\label{sec:prelim}
\textbf{Markov Decision Process.} 
A Markov Decision Process (MDP)~\cite{bellman1957markovian} is a discrete-time stochastic control process to model decision-making. An MDP is formally defined as a 5-tuple $M = (S, A, P, R, \gamma)$, where $S$ is the state space, $A$ is the action space, $P$ is the state transition probability matrix such that $P(s'|s, a)$ is the probability of transitioning to state $s'$ from state $s$ using action $a$, $R$ is the reward function such that $R(s,a)$ is the reward for taking action $a$ in state $s$, and $\gamma$ is the discount factor that determines the value of future rewards. A policy $\pi:  S \to\Delta(A)$ is a distribution over actions given a state. MDPs can be solved using dynamic programming techniques (e.g., value iteration, policy iteration) which require complete knowledge of the MDP's dynamics~\cite{bertsekas2008introduction}.

\textbf{Reinforcement Learning.} 
Reinforcement Learning (RL) involves training an agent to make a sequence of decisions by interacting with the MDP, which represents the environment. This machine learning technique is used to solve MDPs when full knowledge is not available. The agent's goal is to find a policy $\pi^*$ maximising the expected discounted return $E\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{a_{t}}(s_{t}, s_{t+1})\right]$~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. The value function $V_{\pi}(s)$ informs the agent how \textit{valuable} a given state is when following the current policy $\pi$. Common RL algorithms include Q-learning~\cite{qlearning}, and SARSA~\cite{sarsa}. Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) extends traditional algorithms by utilising deep neural networks to approximate the policy $\pi$ or the value function $V$ when the state/action space is high-dimensional and complex. Actor-critic methods~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement} are a popular class of algorithms both in traditional and deep RL. Distinctly, the policy (actor) and the value function (critic) are modelled as separate components, allowing for simultaneous updates to both functions. Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)~\cite{ddpg-paper} is an example of an actor-critic method tailored specifically for continuous action spaces.

\textbf{RL Shielding.}
Shielding techniques can be split into two categories: pre and post-shielding. Pre-shielding, only allows the agent to select an action from a pool of actions considered safe based on state~\cite{shieldreview}. Post-shielding $\phi(s,a)$ checks whether the action $a$ chosen by the policy $\pi$ in the context of state $s$ is safe. If the action is deemed unsafe, then the shield selects a new safe action $a'$, where $\phi: \mathcal{F} \to A$. In this work, we focus on post-shielding techniques.
\end{comment}

\textbf{Contrastive Learning.} 
Contrastive Learning (CL)~\cite{hadsell2006dimensionality} can be either an unsupervised or semi-supervised machine learning paradigm aiming at distinguishing between similar (positive) and dissimilar (negative) pairs of data. At its core lies a contrastive loss function, which encourages the model to put similar pairs closer together in the embedding space while separating dissimilar pairs. Given a pair of inputs $x_i$, $x_j$, the contrastive loss function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
L(x_{i}, x_{j}, y, \theta) = y \cdot \lVert h_\theta(x_i) - h_\theta(x_j)\lVert^2 + 
(1-y) \cdot max(0, m-\lVert h_\theta(x_i) - h_\theta(x_j)\lVert^2)
\label{eq:contrastive-loss}
\end{equation}
where the binary label $y$ indicates if the pair is similar ($y=1$) or dissimilar ($y=0$) and the margin~$m$ regulates the minimum distance between dissimilar pairs. The loss function encourages the model to learn meaningful representations that reflect the inherent similarities and differences between data points, thus facilitating the formation of well-defined clusters in the embedding space.



\section{ADVICE}

\label{sec:advice}

Our \approach\ post-shielding technique empowers safe RL exploration in black-box environments without requiring a system model. Figure~\ref{fig:advice-overview-large} shows a high-level overview of \approach, including its key shield construction and execution/ adaptation stages, and its incorporation within the standard RL loop. The core of \approach\ comprises a contrastive autoencoder (CA) model that can efficiently distinguish between safe and unsafe features from the feature space $\mathcal{F}: S \times A$ (representing all possible state-action pairs). A feature $f_t = (s_{t}, a_{t})$,  $f_t \in \mathcal{F}$, denotes a state-action pair at time step $t$. The CA model leverages a unique loss function where similar and dissimilar features are compared, enabling the systematic identification of meaningful latent feature representations. \approach\ employs these latent representations and specialises an unsupervised nearest neighbours model to the learnt embedding space, thus enabling the classification of new features. Formally, the \approach\ shield is defined by the function
\begin{equation}
    \phi: F \times \mathbb{Z}_+ \rightarrow A
\label{eq:advice-shield}
\end{equation}
such that at time step $t$ during the execution of an episode, \approach\ evaluates the agent's desired action $\phi(f_t, K)$ and allows the action $a_t$ to be taken, or enforces the execution of another safe action~$a'_t$ instead. The \approach-specific parameter $K$ enables controlling the risk aversion levels of the shield. In particular, \approach\ considers the agent's performance and automatically adapts the $K$ value, thus supporting the dynamic calibration of \approach's cautiousness level during learning. Next, we introduce the details of \approach\ and its integration within the RL loop as a post-shield.

\subsection{ADVICE Shield Construction}
\label{sec:advice-training}
As a black-box post-shielding technique, \approach\ does not rely on any prior knowledge about the RL agent or its environment. Instead, the \approach\ shield construction stage is founded on collecting a feature set $F_E$ during an initial unshielded interaction period of $E$ episodes where the RL agent is allowed to interact with and collect experience from its environment. A feature $f_t \in F_E$ is classified as either safe $\mathcal{S} \subset F_E$, unsafe $\mathcal{U} \subset F_E$ or inconclusive $\mathcal{I} \subset F_E$  based on the following function:
\begin{equation}
g(f_t) = 
\begin{cases} 
\mathcal{S} & \text{if } t = 0 \text{ or } s_{t+1} = \text{goal (accepting state)}, \\
\mathcal{U} & \text{if } s_{t+1} = \text{terminal (failure state)}.\\
\mathcal{I} & \text{ otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:safe-unsafe-def}
\end{equation}
hence $\mathcal{S} \cup \mathcal{U}   \cup \mathcal{I}= F_E$ and $\mathcal{S} \cap \mathcal{U} \cap \mathcal{I}= \emptyset$. The collected features set $F_E$ from the initial interaction period $E$ are organised into these categories to facilitate the contrastive learning process: (i) pairs of features that are similar (e.g., two safe/unsafe features); and (ii) feature pairs that are dissimilar (a safe and an unsafe feature). This categorisation is vital to allow the model to discern between safe and unsafe features effectively and focus on finding meaningful representations in a lower-dimensional latent space that reflect the similarities and differences. The combine function $\mathcal{C}$ consumes the set of features in sets $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{U}$
and produces all pairwise combinations from $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{U}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{U}) = & \left\{ (f_i, f_j, 1) \mid i, j \in S, i \neq j \right\} \cup \\
              & \left\{ (f_i, f_j, 1) \mid i, j \in U, i \neq j \right\} \cup \\
              & \left\{ (f_i, f_j, 0) \mid i \in S, j \in U \right\}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:pair-generation}
\end{equation}
where $(f_i, f_j, 1)$ indicates a pair of similar features, and $(f_i, f_j, 0)$ shows a pair of dissimilar features. 

The training of the CA model involves using the pairs of collected features and optimising two loss functions simultaneously: the mean squared error (MSE) and the contrastive loss function (CL) presented in Equation~\eqref{eq:contrastive-loss}. The MSE loss measures how accurately the model can reconstruct the features input after encoding and decoding. In contrast, the CL is designed to refine the model's ability to cluster similar features together, whilst separating dissimilar ones within the latent space based on the Euclidean distance between them. This distance is minimised for similar pairs and maximised for dissimilar pairs, encouraging high cohesion and high separation between similar and dissimilar features, respectively.
Thus, the CA learns to encode and reconstruct the salient features of the given RL problem, comprising the state of the environment and chosen action, in a lower-dimensional latent space as accurately as possible. 

Once trained, the CA model is adept at finding nuanced distinctions between safe and unsafe features and accurately placing unseen features within the appropriate partitions in the latent space. The shield construction stage of \approach\ concludes with embedding an unsupervised nearest neighbours (KNN) model in the latent space that classifies new encoded features as safe/unsafe. A visual representation of the latent data encoding, illustrating the separation between in the latent space, is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:core-results}.


\subsection{ADVICE Execution and Adaptation}
\label{sec:advice-inference}

\begin{algorithm}[b!]
\caption{ADVICE Execution and Adaptation}
\label{alg:advice-inference}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\Require Contrastive Autoencoder $CA$,  Neighbours $K^{max}$, Recent history $h_r$, Distant history $h_d$
\While {$E\!\!+\!\!+ \leq E_{max}$} \label{algo:maxE}
 	\State $s_1 \gets \textsc{Observe()}$
		\For{$t= 1, \dots, T$} \label{algo:episodeExec}
		
        \State $a_t \gets \pi(s_t)$ \label{algo:actionExec}

        \If{!\textsc{IsSafe}($f_t, K, K^{max}$)}\label{algo:unsafeAction}
            \State  $A_{f_t} \leftarrow \prod_ {d \in D} A^d_{f_t} |A^d_{f_t} \subset A^d$\label{algo:candidateActions}
            \State $A_{f_t}'  \leftarrow \{a | \forall a \in A_{f_t}  :  f_t'=(s_t, a) \bullet \textsc{IsSafe}(f_t', K, K^{max})\}$\label{algo:candidateFilter}
            \If{$A_{f_t}' \neq \emptyset$}
					\State $a_t \gets \textrm{argmax}_{a \in A_{f_t}' } Q_\pi (s_t, a)$\label{algo:findBestAction}
	        \EndIf
        \EndIf
		\State $r_t, s_{t+1} \gets \textsc{Execute}(a_t)$
		\State $\pi \gets \textsc{Update\_Policy}(a_t, s_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$
   \EndFor
    
    \State $K \gets \textsc{Update\_Cautiousness}(K, K^{max}, h_d, h_r$)\label{algo:updatedCautiousness}\Comment{\eqref{eq:updatek}-\eqref{eq:mov-std}}
\EndWhile
\Statex

\State \textbf{function} \textsc{IsSafe}($f, K, K^{max}$)\label{algo:safeStart}
    \State \quad $\hat{f} \gets \textsc{Encode}(f)$ 
        \Comment{Encode feature f into the latent space}
    \State \quad $N_{\hat{f}} \gets \textsc{GetNeighbours}(\hat{f}, K^{max})$
    \State \quad \textbf{if} $\sum_{n \in N_{\hat{f}}} [n \text{ == \textit{"safe"}}] \geq K$
        \Return True \Comment{Action considered safe}
    \State \quad \textbf{return} False \Comment{Action considered unsafe}\label{algo:safeEnd}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


Upon the completion of the shield construction phase, ADVICE can be used as a post-shield within the RL loop. 
The constructed shield encourages safe environment exploration, guarding the agent throughout its interaction with the environment by ensuring the execution of safe actions.

The \approach\ execution and adaptation stage, shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:advice-inference} and Figure~\ref{fig:advice-overview-large} (bottom), involves a continuous cycle of action evaluation and decision-making until the maximum number of training episodes $E_{max}$ is reached (line~\ref{algo:maxE}). 
During an episode's execution (line~\ref{algo:episodeExec}), the RL agent, having observed the current state $s_t$, selects an action $a_t$ based on its current policy $\pi$ (line~\ref{algo:actionExec}).
Without ADVICE, the agent would proceed with this action regardless of its potential safety implications. 
Instead, ADVICE can now be used to evaluate the selected action before it is realised by the agent, thus ensuring safe environment exploration.
More specifically, to establish if the action $a_t$ is safe, \approach\ extracts the latent representation $\hat{f}$ of feature $f_t$, collects the nearest $K^{max}$ latent data points to  $\hat{f}$ and checks whether the cardinality of the safe data points exceeds the safety threshold $K$, in which case the action $a_t$ is considered safe; otherwise, $a_t$ is considered unsafe (lines~\ref{algo:safeStart}--\ref{algo:safeEnd}).

The safety threshold $K \in [0, K^{max}]$ determines how many neighbours need to be labelled safe so that the encoded feature can be deemed safe. 
The closer $K$ is to $K^{max}$ entails that \approach\ will be more cautious in terms of safety, while a $K$ value closer to $\lceil K/2 \rceil$ means that the ADVICE shield is more relaxed and favours exploration. 
A value of $K < \lceil K/2 \rceil$ should not be considered as this would allow the RL agent to execute actions that are more likely to be unsafe than safe.

If action $a_t$  is deemed unsafe (line~\ref{algo:unsafeAction}), \approach\ intervenes and generates a set of candidate actions $A_{f_t}$ by quantising the continuous space of each action dimension $A^d$, where $d \in D$ is the dimension, and extracting the Cartesian product across the $D$ action dimensions (line~\ref{algo:candidateActions}).
The set of candidate actions $A_{f_t}$ is filtered, keeping only those actions that are considered safe $A'_{f_t} \subseteq A_{f_t}$ (line~\ref{algo:candidateFilter}).
If the $A'_{f_t}$ set is not empty, these filtered candidate actions are evaluated by the RL agent's value function $Q_{\pi}$ for their expected reward (line~\ref{algo:findBestAction}).
The action with the highest expected reward is selected, entailing that the action aligns with both the safety considerations and the agent's performance objectives. 
Note that if no safe alternative action is identified (i.e., $A_{f_t}' = \emptyset$), \approach\ resorts to providing the originally selected action $a_t$; since no alternative action is predicted to be safe, $a_t$ is expected to achieve the highest expected reward. 



At the end of each episode, \approach\ calibrates its cautiousness level by considering the recent safety performance of the RL agent (line~\ref{algo:updatedCautiousness}). 
This unique \approach\ characteristic enables moving beyond the static definition of the safety threshold $K$ by automatically adjusting its value in response to the frequency of recent safety violations. 
Accordingly, \approach\ becomes adaptive and allows the agent to explore more when exhibiting safe behaviour for a period of time while being more cautious, thus interfering more when the RL agent behaves increasingly unsafely.

To assess the agent's performance over time, \approach\ employs a double sliding window, commonly used in the field of anomaly detection~\cite{dualwindowimagery, hyperspectraldualwindow}. 
By comparing the recent rate of safety violations against a broader historical view, \approach\ can discern whether the current trend deviates from the pattern seen historically. 
This analysis informs ADVICE whether to strengthen or relax the cautiousness level.
The adaptation function is defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
	K =
	\begin{cases} 
		min(K\!+\!1, K^{max}),    & \text{if } MA_{h_r} > MA_{h_d} + \sigma_{h_d} \\
		max(K\!-\!1, \lceil K^{max}/2\rceil ), & \text{if } MA_{h_r} < MA_{h_d} - \sigma_{h_d} \\
		K, & \text{otherwise}
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:updatek}
\end{equation}
where $MA_{h_d}$ and $\sigma_{h_d}$ are the moving average and standard deviation, respectively, of safety violations $Z_i$. This uses the distant history window $h_d$, and $MA_{h_r}$ is the moving average of $Z_i$ using the recent history window $h_r$, i.e.,  $h_r < h_d$.
Given $h \in \{h_r, h_r\}$, the calculation of the moving average $MA_h$ and standard deviation $\sigma_h$, considering the history of safety violations $\mathcal{Z}_1, \dots, \mathcal{Z}_t$, is defined by:

\noindent\begin{minipage}{.38\linewidth}
\begin{equation}
  MA_{h} = \frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=t-h}^{t} (\mathcal{Z}_{i} - \mathcal{Z}_{i-1})
\label{eq:mov-avg}
\end{equation}
\end{minipage}%
\hspace{6mm}
\begin{minipage}{.55\linewidth}
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{h} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{h} \sum_{i=t-h}^{t} \left((\mathcal{Z}_i - \mathcal{Z}_{i-1}) - MA_{h}\right)^2}
\label{eq:mov-std}
\end{equation}
\end{minipage}

If the $MA_{h_r}$ rate exceeds the sum of $MA_{h_{d}} + \sigma_{h_{d}}$, then it signifies that the agent crashes more often than expected. 
Consequently, $K$ is automatically incremented to adopt a more cautious stance. 
Inversely, if the $MA_{h_r}$ rate is below $MA_{h_{d}} - \sigma_{h_{d}}$, this suggests that the agent acts more conservatively than expected. 
As a result, $K$ is automatically reduced to allow the agent to explore more freely. 
The safety threshold $K$ remains the same for any other occasion. 
\approach\ deliberately only considers one standard deviation as to two or more standard deviations would make the adaptation slow to respond to emerging safety risks. Our sensitivty analysis on these two parameters (Section~\ref{sec:adaptive-shield-results}), 
demonstrates the described behaviours.




\section{Evaluation}

\label{sec:evaluation}

 
This benchmark suite is designed to assess performance and safety in high-dimensional, complex scenarios that incorporate a variety of safety constraints. 
Accordingly, the Safety Gymnasium is an ideal benchmark to assess the effectiveness of \approach.
 Each task is designed to terminate the episode immediately upon a collision, signifying catastrophic damage to a physical robot. %, and presenting a significant challenge in terms of safety. 
 The selected tasks below %for evaluation 
 encompass a range of complexities:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, nolistsep, leftmargin=3mm]
    \item \textbf{Semi-random Goal}: 
    A standard goal environment with six obstacles and one goal. The obstacles have a static spawn, while the agent and the goal have randomised positions every episode.
    \item \textbf{Randomised Goal}: 
    A similar goal environment, but obstacles have random locations per episode.
    \item \textbf{Randomised Circle}: The agent has to circle in a given zone in this environment. The aim is to maximise speed and distance from zone's centre while avoiding the three randomised obstacles.
\end{itemize}
 
Figure~\ref{fig:examples-envs} shows a snapshot of the tasks within the Safety Gymnasium.
To evaluate the performance of ADVICE, we conduct comparisons against the following state-of-the-art algorithms:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, nolistsep, leftmargin=3mm]	
    \item \textbf{DDPG}: A deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG)~\cite{ddpg-paper} agent being the foundational baseline.
    \item \textbf{DDPG-Lag}: A DDPG agent with a Lagrangian multiplier ($\lambda=0.1$, $a=0.01$) which heavily penalises constant safety violations.
    \item \textbf{Tabular Shield}: A DDPG agent with a discretised (1 decimal place) table of terminal state-action pairs, which prevents the agent from executing inadequate actions again~\cite{tabular-shield}.
\end{itemize}
Each method employs the same DDPG configuration, ensuring a fair and accurate comparison. 
The results are averaged across three independent runs and include mean scores and confidence intervals (standard error of the mean), showing a performance overview. 
Unless explicitly stated, the default \approach\ deployment per run is $E=1000$ with $K=4$ and $K^{max}=5$.

For our first set of experiments, we evaluate the overall performance of the examined methods (DDPG, DDPG-Lag, Tabular shield, ADVICE) in all three tasks. 
Figure~\ref{fig:core-results} shows the average episodic reward (return), cumulative safety violations (failed episodes), and cumulative goal reaches (successful episodes), alongside an example latent space visualisation from a single ADVICE execution. 
The obtained results provide evidence that \approach, albeit designed to prioritise safety, manages to maintain a competitive performance (rewards) in all tasks. 
Both DDPG-Lag and \approach, indicate an inherent trade-off between reward maximisation and safety prioritisation. 

Since the primary goal of a safety shield is to encourage exploration and learning in a safe way, it is evident that \approach, across all tasks, can significantly reduce safety violations, outperforming by a notable margin all other methods.
DDPG-Lag also manages to reduce safety violations, but not to the magnitude of \approach, highlighting the effectiveness of \approach's shield. 
We visualise the learnt latent space from an example \approach\ run (Figure~\ref{fig:core-results} right) 
illustrating the power of contrastive learning and validating its effectiveness at learning latent representations that empower the distinction between safe and unsafe features.
Considering that \approach\ constructs the shield for the first $E\!=\!1000$ episodes and then starts its execution for safe exploration, while DDPG-Lag aims at reducing safety violations from episode $E\!=\!1$, the results are even more affirmative.

Despite its conservative prioritisation to reward, \approach\ still reaches the goal (successful episode execution) with a similar frequency to all other methods. 
In fact, \approach\ completes the task without compromising safety, and the difference in accumulated reward is due to the reward function design and the reduced time the agent has to complete the task using a cautious path. 

These core results corroborate that \approach's contrastive approach to shielding an RL agent from unsafe actions is effective in comparison to other methods. 
\approach\ significantly reduces safety violations without detracting much from the agent's overall performance.

\subsection{\approach\ Adaptation}
\label{sec:adaptive-shield-results}
To evaluate \approach's adaptation capabilities, we conducted a sensitivity analysis focusing on the impact of varying the distant history window $h_d$ and the recent history window $h_r$. 
For each $h_d$ and $h_r$ combination, Table~\ref{tab:adaptive-shield-results} shows the number of consecutive episodes $K$ was fixed at a specific value, the frequency of $K$ adjustments, and the impact on mean safety violations. 

Our findings reveal an impact of $h_r$ on the frequency of $K$ adjustments. 
A smaller $h_r$ leads to more frequent $K$ increases, allowing the system to quickly respond to immediate safety violations. Conversely, a larger $h_r$ tends to stabilise $K$ by filtering out anomalies and adjusting only in response to sustained trends of increased violations. Similarly, $h_d$ influences the decrease of $K$; a smaller $h_d$ facilitates rapid decreases in $K$ following a reduction in safety violations, whereas a larger $h_d$ results in less frequent reductions, promoting stability in \approach's behaviour.
The interaction between $h_d$ and $h_r$ minimally affects the overall rate of safety violations, suggesting that while these parameters impact the adaptiveness and stability of $K$, they do not directly correlate with safety violations. These insights highlight the role of $h_d$ and $h_r$ primarily as tuning parameters to balance responsiveness against stability in \approach.

\section{Limitations}

\label{sec:limitations}

Although \approach\ maintains a lower safety violation rate compared to other methods, the following aspects deserve further investigation.
Firstly, \approach\ suffers from \textit{cold-start} needing to gather sufficient features to train the contrastive autoencoder. 
Although this delays its effectiveness, it still significantly reduces violations compared to methods that start learning from timestep $t_0$. 
Transfer learning, i.e.
\approach\ increases computational overheads due to continuous inference at each timestep, possibly limiting its applicability in resource-limited settings. 
Inferring the safety of actions at alternate timesteps could reduce overheads, assuming minimal changes between consecutive states, but may also delay detecting emerging unsafe conditions.
Thirdly, in environments with temporally dynamic obstacles, \approach\ could be less effective in reducing violations as it only captures a single environmental snapshot per timestep, lacking the temporal context. 
Integrating LSTM or features from previous states and actions could help, although this extension could further elevate computational requirements.
Finally, additional experiments could enable establishing the applicability, feasibility and scalability of \approach\ in domains with characteristics different from those used in our evaluation. 



\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\label{sec:conc-and-future}

We introduced \approach, a post-shielding technique for the safe exploration of RL agents operating in complex \textit{black-box} environments. 
\approach\ does not need \emph{any} prior knowledge and uses a contrastive autoencoder to distinguish between safe and unsafe features efficiently. 
Our evaluation shows that \approach\ significantly reduces safety violations while maintaining competitive performance against state-of-the-art methods. 
Despite its effectiveness, \approach\ has areas for improvement, including mitigating the cold-start issue, reducing high computational demands, and addressing challenges with dynamic obstacles.
Future work could explore meta-learning techniques~\cite{hospedales2021meta} to address the cold-start problem, enabling faster activation of \approach\ without sacrificing performance. 
Quantisation or pruning could reduce computational demands, enhancing applicability in computationally constrained domains.
We envisage that \approach\ serves as a foundational step in using neural network-based shielding for safe RL exploration in complex, black-box environments without \emph{any} prior knowledge.
