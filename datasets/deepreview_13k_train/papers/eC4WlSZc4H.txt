\title{Robustness Over Time: Understanding Adversarial Examples’ Effectiveness on Longitudinal Versions of Large Language Models}

\begin{abstract}

Large Language Models (LLMs) undergo continuous updates to improve user experience. 
However, prior research on the security and safety implications of LLMs has primarily focused on their specific versions, overlooking the impact of successive LLM updates.
This prompts the need for a holistic understanding of the risks in these different versions of LLMs.
To fill this gap, in this paper, we conduct a longitudinal study to examine the adversarial robustness -- specifically misclassification, jailbreak, and hallucination -- of three prominent LLMs: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMA.
Our study reveals that LLM updates do not consistently improve adversarial robustness as expected. 
For instance, a later version of GPT-3.5 degrades regarding misclassification and hallucination despite its improved resilience against jailbreaks, and GPT-4 demonstrates (incrementally) higher robustness overall.
Moreover, larger model sizes do not necessarily yield improved robustness.
Specifically, larger LLaMA models do not uniformly exhibit improved robustness across all three aspects studied. 
Importantly, minor updates lacking substantial robustness improvements can exacerbate existing issues rather than resolve them.
By providing a more nuanced understanding of LLM robustness over time, we hope our study can offer valuable insights for developers and users navigating model updates and informed decisions in model development and usage for LLM vendors. 


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 by OpenAI, and LLaMA by Meta~\cite{TMSAABBBBBBBCCCEFFFFGGGHHHIKKKKKKLLLLLMMMMMNPRRSSSSSTTTWKXYZZFKNRSES23}, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in varied Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks, including language translation~\cite{JWHWT23}, text classification~\cite{SLLWGZW23}, and creative writing~\cite{HSCBZ23,JDGPNZML23}. 
Despite their impressive performance, these LLMs also present certain risks. 
For instance, LLMs are usually trained on vast amounts of internet data, which may contain biases, misinformation, and offensive content~\cite{TMPB23,BLHLLHS23,OWJAWMZASRSHKMSAWCLL22}. 
Consequently, the outputs generated by LLMs can perpetuate harmful stereotypes~\cite{LBLTSYZNWKNYYZCMRAHZDLRRYWSOZYSKGCKHHCXSGHIZCWLMZK22,AFZ21}, disseminate false information~\cite{AM23,MLG23,PPCNKW23,HD23}, or produce inappropriate and offensive responses~\cite{KLSGZH23}. 
Furthermore, previous studies have shown that LLMs are sensitive to input query changes, including both unintentional errors by legitimate users and intentional modifications by potential attackers~\cite{MLHALHZ22,ZWZWCWYYGZX23}.

In response, updates have been made to improve LLMs by incorporating feedback and insights from users and developers.
Although such improvements partially mitigate known attacks or failures observed in earlier versions~\cite{B23,KLSGZH23}, unintended consequences and even new vulnerabilities or biases could still be introduced. 
Unfortunately, current research on the robustness evaluation of LLMs has focused on a \emph{single version} of the LLM, leaving the impact of model updates unexplored. 

To fill this gap, in this paper, we undertake the first comprehensive robustness evaluation of longitudinal LLMs.
The goal of our study is to identify and shed light on potential issues resulting from model updates. 
The benefits of studying the robustness of LLMs \textit{over time} are two-fold:
(1) From the perspective of users, understanding the limitations and risks associated with model updates enables them to make informed decisions about their usage of LLMs;
(2) From the perspective of LLM developers, continuous evaluation and testing facilitate iterative improvement, addressing emerging challenges and refining model behavior over time.
Following software engineer practice, we distinguish between model ``upgrade'' and ``update'' for fine-granularity analysis in this paper.
An \textit{LLM upgrade} denotes a significant version change or major improvement. 
For example, the release of the \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0125} (GPT-3.5 v0125) represents an upgrade to the \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-1106} (GPT-3.5 v1106).
An \textit{LLM update} typically incorporates enhancements to the existing version.
For example, we consider \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo} as a continuously updated model.

\mypara{Methodology}
Our investigation focuses on assessing the robustness of popular LLMs  over time, including closed-source models (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4) as well as the open-source LLaMA-1/2. 
We utilize adversarial examples within the in-context learning (ICL) framework to examine this robustness~\cite{BMRSKDNSSAAHKHCRZWWHCSLGCCBMRSA20}. 
More details about ICL can be found in~\autoref{section:icl}.

Our primary goal is to evaluate if an LLM is robust when the ICL components are replaced by their adversarial counterparts.
Specifically, we explore two attack scenarios: 1) classification tasks and 2) generation tasks. 
Within these scenarios, we consider three representative adversarial goals: misclassification~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23} for classification tasks, as well as jailbreak~\cite{YLYX23,CRDHPW23,MZKNASK23} and hallucination~\cite{LCZNW23} for generation tasks.

Our evaluation workflow, illustrated in \autoref{figure:workflow}, first generates adversarial examples using surrogate language models (e.g., T5~\cite{RSRLNMZLL20} or Mistral-7B~\cite{JSMBCCBLLSLLSSLWLS23}).
These adversarial inputs are then tested against different versions of the target LLM. 
This approach allows us to examine how these adversarial inputs impact various versions of LLMs over time. 
To ensure thorough evaluation, we employ multiple surrogate models and investigate 15 distinct adversarial query scenarios within the ICL framework. These scenarios involve different combinations of adversarial or benign task descriptions, demonstrations, and questions (further details in \autoref{section:rot}).

\mypara{Findings}
In summary, we make the following key findings.
First, we demonstrate that GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMA are all vulnerable to adversarial queries, which are persistent across different versions.
For instance, in the classification task using the SST2 dataset, GPT-3.5 v0125 achieves a performance of 0.583 with clean samples. However, this performance significantly decreases to 0.264 when fed with various types of adversarial examples.

In addition, we notice no significant robustness improvement for LLMs during the upgrade process.
\hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-1106} (GPT-3.5 v1106), despite its better performance in jailbreak tests, shows the worst performance in both misclassification and hallucination evaluations compared to \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} (GPT-3.5 v0613) and \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0125} (GPT-3.5 v0125).
\hytt{gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09} (GPT-4 v0409) has demonstrated a noticeable regression in the misclassification task. 
There are significant gaps in jailbreak and hallucination tasks compared to previous versions \hytt{gpt-4-0125-preview} (GPT-4 v0125).
For the LLaMA family, upgrading models does not improve the robustness for many cases (see~\autoref{figure:llama_7b_0} and \autoref{figure:llama_7b_3}). 
The overall performance of the latest version (LLaMA-3 family) is much worse in all the tasks.
In addition, our findings reveal that increasing LLM size does not guarantee enhanced robustness.

Through the lens of misclassification evaluation, we conduct weekly tests on the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 models to evaluate the minor update process.
Our experiments show that model providers may update the LLMs to add new features over time.
In general, these updates could nonetheless influence the overall performance of the LLMs, which can still exacerbate existing issues rather than resolve them.

\mypara{Impact}
We hope that our study can offer valuable insights for future studies on the robustness of LLMs. 
It is critical that developers prioritize the consideration of model robustness during the upgrade and update.
Proactively incorporating robustness-enhancing techniques and conducting rigorous evaluation will bolster the resilience of an LLM to both known and unknown challenges.



\section{Threat Modeling}

\label{section:threatmodel}

LLMs can perform completely different tasks, such as answering questions, summarizing documents, translating languages, and completing sentences. 
Within these tasks, according to different requirements and purposes, we categorize them into two types: 
\begin{itemize}
\item 
\mypara{Classification Task}
LLMs are usually used to classify the input sentences, such as sentiment analysis (positive or negative) and linguistic acceptability (acceptable or not).
These tasks usually require users to provide clear descriptions and corresponding candidate labels.
Then, LLMs generate the prediction label to assist users.
\item 
\mypara{Generation Task}
In this task, LLMs typically need to produce ``open-ended'' text based on the inputs from the user, with the goal of creating coherent, meaningful, and contextually appropriate answers.
These answers are obtained from pre-trained knowledge of LLMs or some external searches.
\end{itemize}
Since in-context learning is the most widely used inference framework on both classification tasks and generation tasks, we inject adversarial examples into the components of in-context learning to evaluate the robustness of continuously updated LLMs.
In this paper, we summarize all the queries into two types of in-context learning methods, i.e., zero-shot learning and few-shot learning.

\subsection{In-context Learning (ICL)}
\label{section:icl}

\mypara{Overview}
The core idea of In-context Learning (ICL) is to learn from analogies implied in contextual information.
ICL requires a few examples to form a demonstration context and feed them into LLM.
It does not modify the parameters of an LLM and relies on the model to learn the pattern hidden in the demonstration (and accordingly generate the correct output).
As such, ICL effectively reduces the computation costs for adapting LLMs to new tasks, as fine-tuning is not required.
In general, there are two categories of in-context learning: zero-shot learning and few-shot learning.
We outline their details as follows, and their examples can be found in \autoref{figure:queryexamples}.

\mypara{Zero-shot Learning}
Zero-shot learning~\cite{XLSA19} is a capability enabled by LLMs, allowing them to generalize to tasks or domains they have never been explicitly trained on, no matter the classification or generation task.
As a special case of ICL, the query of zero-shot learning to an LLM (termed $Q_{zero}$ in this paper) only contains two elements: description and question (see~\autoref{figure:zeroshoteg}), which can be formulated as follows.
\begin{equation}
\label{equ:query-zs}
    Q_{zero} = \emph{Description} + \emph{Question}. 
\end{equation}
Here \emph{Description} serves as an instructional guide for LLMs, which could provide necessary information such as task characterization and the response format.
\emph{Question} is an inquiry for a specific task.
Zero-shot learning relies on the capability of an LLM to infer both the input distribution and the desired output from a query without any demonstrations~\cite{WBZGYLDDL22}.
Therefore, although the LLMs may not have been pre-trained on specific tasks, they can still perform accurate results.
Because of the simple $Q_{zero}$ structure, zero-shot learning offers excellent convenience and becomes the most common approach to querying LLMs in real-world scenarios.

\mypara{Few-shot Learning}
Few-shot learning \cite{BMRSKDNSSAAHKHCRZWWHCSLGCCBMRSA20} includes a few examples to form a learning context that enables LLMs to better understand the task.
In other words, compared to zero-shot learning, few-shot learning enables LLMs to quickly adapt to new tasks by learning from an extra element, i.e., \emph{Demonstration}.
Thus, the query of few-shot learning, $Q_{few}$, can be formulated as:
\begin{equation}
\label{equ:query-fs}
    Q_{few} = \emph{Description} + \emph{Demonstration} + \emph{Question}.
\end{equation}
\emph{Demonstration} typically consists of a handful of user-generated question-answer pairs.
For the classification problem, the answer is always the label of the question, while in the generation problem, the pair is the example within a similar semantic meaning or the same format and style.
We show an example of $Q_{few}$ in the classification problem in~\autoref{figure:fewshoteg}.
In general, few-shot learning can better guide the LLMs in learning a more accurate mapping between questions and desired answers.
In this paper, following~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23}, we specifically consider 3-shot learning (i.e., three question-answer pairs in \emph{Demonstration}).

\section{Attack Taxonomy}

\label{section:adq}

In this section, we primarily discuss the taxonomy of adversarial attacks on LLMs.
The goal of adversarial attacks is to manipulate the behavior of LLMs to elicit misleading or undesirable responses through adversarial examples in \emph{clean} queries~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23, WCPXKZXXDSTAMHLCKSL23, WHHCZWYHYGJZX23}.
We consider three adversarial attacks against LLMs. 
The first is \textit{misclassification} (see \autoref{section:mf}), which tries to perturb normal input to induce the model to deviate from correct predictions.
In addition, a more representative adversarial attack is \textit{jailbreak} (see \autoref{section:jb}).
Jailbreak attacks aim to modify the input to bypass the safeguard within LLMs, thereby forcing LLMs to generate certain disallowed, unsafe, or harmful statements.
Furthermore, another adversarial attack, named \textit{hallucination} (see \autoref{section:hc}), is to produce coherent and grammatically correct but factually incorrect or nonsensical outputs.

\subsection{Misclassification}
\label{section:mf}

Previous studies have proposed various effective methods to generate adversarial examples against language models.
However, different works consider attacking different components of the ICL framework.
For instance, Zhu et al.~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23} proposed PromptBench, a dataset consisting of adversarial descriptions. 
Their experimental results demonstrate that descriptions are notably vulnerable to adversarial attacks primarily because they serve as a critical context that shapes the responses of LLMs and guides their cognitive outputs.
However, they only focus on a single version of LLMs.
Wang et al.~\cite{WXWGCGAL21} proposed AdvGLUE, a dataset comprising adversarial questions, but the target models are traditional small-scale language models. 
Above all, to fully evaluate the robustness of the LLMs, we consider different types of adversarial queries, i.e., each element of a query can be clean or adversarial.
The clean and adversarial examples of each element are shown in~\autoref{table:advpro}.

\mypara{Zero-shot Learning}
We first outline different categories of queries in zero-shot learning under \underline{m}is\underline{c}lassification (mc) task, i.e., \adq{zero}{mc}.
Recall that zero-shot learning does not have any demonstration; thus, \adq{zero}{} encompasses two elements: the description and the question.
We can replace any of them with \underline{A}dversarial ($\mathsf{A}$) or \underline{C}lean ($\mathsf{C}$) examples.
For instance, we use \adq{zero}{AC} to denote an adversarial query consisting of an adversarial description and a clean question.
In turn, we can generate the clean or adversarial queries \adq{zero}{mc} as follows: 
\[
\adq{zero}{mc} := \{\adq{zero}{CC}, \adq{zero}{AC}, \adq{zero}{CA}, \adq{zero}{AA}\}.
\]

\mypara{Few-shot Learning}
Similar to the procedure employed for \adq{zero}{mc}, we extend our approach to encompass the creation of adversarial queries in few-shot learning, i.e., \adq{few}{mc}.
For instance, \adq{few}{AAC} consists of an adversarial description, an adversarial demonstration, and a clean question.
Given \adq{few}{mc} with several demonstrations, we can generate the clean or adversarial queries in eight scenarios as follows: 
\[
\adq{few}{mc} := \{\adq{few}{CCC}, \adq{few}{ACC}, \adq{few}{CAC}, \adq{few}{CCA}, \adq{few}{AAC}, \adq{few}{ACA}, \adq{few}{CAA}, \adq{few}{AAA}\}.
\]

\subsection{Jailbreak}
\label{section:jb}

Jailbreak attacks refer to a scenario where a user intentionally tries to trick or manipulate the LLMs to bypass their built-in safety, ethical, or operational guidelines, thereby inducing the LLMs to produce toxic responses.
Previous works~\cite{ZWKF23,YLYX23,DLLWZLWZL23,CRDHPW23,MZKNASK23} have proposed a new class of adversarial attacks that can jailbreak safety-aligned language models.
Among them, to test the robustness of both closed-source and open-source LLMs against jailbreak attacks, we launch black-box \hyit{optimization-based} jailbreak attacks, including GPTfuzz~\cite{YLYX23}, PAIR~\cite{CRDHPW23} and TAP~\cite{MZKNASK23}.
More concretely, these methods are systematically automated prompt-level jailbreaks optimized by outputs or coordinates of LLMs.
Note that all of the above methods are zero-shot learning.
We attempt to use few-shot learning for jailbreaking as well.
However, the results indicate that once an answer in the demonstration involves a jailbreaking response, the LLM is more likely to reject the query, regardless of the optimization method used. 
Therefore, in this paper, we do not discuss few-shot learning.
In addition, beyond the scope of jailbreak queries, the method for adversarial-based techniques needs a detailed description for modification.
More specifically, a surrogate or teacher model leverages the outputs of the target LLM to refine or alter the description.
Consequently, within our framework, we assume that the question itself invariably originates from a clean source, but the descriptions are classified as either clean or adversarial, as delineated in \autoref{equ:query-zs}.
Given the adversarial query \adq{zero}{jb}, we have the following adversarial queries in two scenarios: 
\[
\adq{zero}{jb} := \{\adq{zero}{CC}, \adq{zero}{AC}\}.
\]
In this paper, the adversary is assumed to have black-box access to the LLMs for the following three adversarial-based optimized jailbreak attacks.
\begin{itemize}
\item 
\textbf{GPTfuzz~\cite{YLYX23}} To automate the generation of jailbreak templates for LLMs, GPTfuzz starts with human-written templates.
It uses a series of random mutations to generate new inputs and evaluate them with the assistance of LLMs.
\item 
\textbf{PAIR~\cite{CRDHPW23}} PAIR is a systematically automated prompt-level jailbreak.
PAIR adopts an \emph{attacker} LLM to discover and improve the jailbreak prompts and uses a \emph{judge} LLM to evaluate the responses from the \emph{target} LLM.
\item 
\textbf{TAP~\cite{MZKNASK23}} More advanced than PAIR, TAP utilizes three LLMs: an \emph{attacker}, an \emph{evaluator}, and a \emph{target}.
The task is to generate the jailbreaking prompts using tree-of-thoughts reasoning. 
The \emph{evaluator} first assesses the generated prompts and evaluates whether the jailbreaking attempt would be successful or not, and then evaluates the generated prompts from the \emph{target}. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hallucination}
\label{section:hc}

Hallucination refers to a phenomenon in which the model generates incorrect, nonsensical, or imaginary text. 
In particular, hallucination represents a unique form of adversarial example that can lead LLMs to provide erroneous responses to confusion about the given question. 
This confusion stems from logical inaccuracies rather than linguistic mistakes in the misclassification of datasets. 
Since we deploy LLMs in an open-domain chat setting without specific task constraints, the responses can be seen as a repository of world knowledge. 
The factors contributing to hallucination are varied, potentially from the data source or context. 
Thus, studying whether an LLM is vulnerable to hallucination is pivotal in examining LLM robustness.
We generate the hallucination answers on three domains: Question Answering (QA), Dialogue, and Summarization.
We consider these hallucination answers as adversarial examples.
The given queries contain questions with both the correct and hallucination answers, and the LLM needs to make the correct choice between these two types of answers.
Therefore, we only consider zero-shot learning as the adversarial query:
\[
\adq{zero}{hl} := \{\adq{zero}{A}\}.
\]



\section{Evaluation Setup}

\label{section:rot}

In this section, we introduce our experimental settings and protocols.
We first mainly elaborate on two ways of model evolution over time: upgrade and update (see \autoref{section:overtime}). 
An upgrade often involves substantial and fundamental changes.
It usually does not depend on previous content and involves replacing the old with the new.
On the other hand, an update refers to smaller-scale, minor, incremental, and gradual improvements within a single version.
It often requires modifications (or improvements) based on the existing foundation and cannot be separated from the original basis.
We then outline the corresponding datasets for testing different categories of LLMs (see \autoref{section:dataset}).
Finally, we introduce the evaluation metrics to summarize the results (see \autoref{section:metrics}).

\subsection{Over Time}
\label{section:overtime}

In this paper, ``over time'' pertains to the target LLMs that undergo continuous \textit{upgrades} and \textit{updates} under the direction of their developers, which will be explained as follows.

\mypara{Upgrade Over Time}
Recent studies demonstrate that adversarial examples exhibit a significant degree of transferability across different LLMs~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23}.
However, many LLMs, such as ChatGPT and LLaMA, are \emph{continually upgraded}.
As a result, the question of whether successive versions of these LLMs remain vulnerable to previously identified adversarial strategies has not been adequately addressed, prompting us to evaluate the robustness of the latest iterations of LLMs systematically.
We undertake a comprehensive robustness assessment, focusing on GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMA, the most prominent LLMs subject to ongoing upgrades.
Concretely, our analysis centers on GPT-3.5, for which three different versions are available: 
\hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} (GPT-3.5 v0613), \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-1106} (GPT-3.5 v1106), and \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0125} (GPT-3.5 v0125).
For GPT-4, we choose four versions: \hytt{gpt-4-0613} (GPT-4 v0613), \hytt{gpt-4-1106-preview} (GPT-4 v1106), \hytt{gpt-4-0125-preview} (GPT-4 v0125), and \hytt{gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09} (GPT-4 v0409).
For the LLaMA model, we focus on three different versions: \hytt{LLaMA} (LLaMA v1), \hytt{LLaMA-2} (LLaMA v2), \hytt{LLaMA-2-chat} (LLaMA v2 Chat), \hytt{LLaMA-3} (LLaMA v3), and \hytt{LLaMA-3-instruct} (LLaMA v2 Instruct).
Meanwhile, we consider different scales of LLaMA models.
For instance, for the primary version \hytt{LLaMA}, we consider \hytt{LLaMA-7B}, {13B}, and {65B}.
The scales of \hytt{LLaMA-2} and \hytt{LLaMA-2-chat} we discuss in this paper are \hytt{7B}, \hytt{13B}, and \hytt{70B}.
For \hytt{LLaMA-3} family, we choose \hytt{8B} and \hytt{70B}.
We treat \hytt{LLaMA-3-8B} as the upgraded version of \hytt{LLaMA-7B} and \hytt{LLaMA-2-7B} as well as \hytt{LLaMA-2-70B} and \hytt{LLaMA-3-70B} models as the upgraded version of \hytt{LLaMA-65B}.
In accordance with the inherent structure of \adq{zero}{} and \adq{few}{} (see \autoref{section:icl}), we feed different types of adversarial examples into longitudinal versions of different LLMs to measure the robustness.

\mypara{Update Over Time}
Besides the upgrades, closed-source commercial LLMs are frequently updated within a single version.
For instance, there have been 28 updates for ChatGPT since its release, as documented in the official release notes.\footnote{\url{https://help.openai.}\textsuperscript{,}\footnote{\url{https://status.openai.}
OpenAI disseminates updates regarding their models to users via email.
In addition, there may be some minor updates from the model provider without any notifications.\footnote{See some discussion in \url{https://community.openai.}
Thus, we believe that updates at regular intervals are warranted for the present version.
To research the robustness of updates over time, we employ the latest iterations of GPT-3.5 (currently pointing to \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0125})\footnote{Before February 16th, 2024, GPT-3.5 pointed to \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613}.} and GPT-4 (currently pointing to \hytt{gpt-4-0613}) as the target model.
Similar to the experiments on the upgrade, we feed different types of adversarial examples into GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to measure the robustness.
We evaluate the attack performance on a weekly basis, starting from the fourth week of 2024.

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{section:dataset}

\mypara{Misclassification Datasets}
We leverage the following widely used benchmarking description and question datasets to construct the clean or adversarial components of our adversarial queries under misclassification tasks.

\begin{itemize}
\item 
\mypara{Description Dataset}
We construct a curated attacking description dataset that contains the clean and adversarial descriptions from PromptBench~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23}.
Given a classification task, PromptBench assigns different seed descriptions, surrogate models (e.g., T5 and UL2), and adversarial attacks (e.g., TextBugger and BertAttack) to generate adversarial descriptions.
Among these adversarial descriptions, we select the most prominent adversarial description according to the attack capability of the surrogate model.
Then, we add the chosen clean and adversarial pairs to our curated dataset.

\item 
\mypara{Question Dataset}
We choose GLUE~\cite{WSMHLB19} and AdvGLUE~\cite{WXWGCGAL21} as our question datasets for misclassification tasks.
GLUE is a collection of benign classification questions for training, evaluating, and analyzing natural language understanding systems, and AdvGLUE, whose partial samples are the adversarial variants built upon the GLUE's samples, aims at crafting challenging and deceptive examples to evaluate the robustness of language models.
We choose the following five benign classification tasks from GLUE as our clean question datasets: SST-2~\cite{SPWCMNP13} (sentiment analysis), QQP~\cite{WHF17} (duplicate sentence detection), MNLI~\cite{WNB18} (natural language inference), QNLI~\cite{WSMHLB19} (natural language inference), and RTE~\cite{WSMHLB19} (natural language inference), and select their corresponding adversarial versions from AdvGLUE, i.e., AdvSST-2, AdvQQP, AdvMNLI, AdvQNLI, and AdvRTE, as our final adversarial question dataset.
\end{itemize}

\mypara{Jailbreak Datasets}
We leverage three mainstream jailbreak algorithms, i.e., GPTfuzz~\cite{YLYX23}, PAIR~\cite{CRDHPW23}, and TAP~\cite{MZKNASK23}, to construct our jailbreak datasets upon the samples from the Forbidden Question (FQ) dataset~\cite{CLYSBZ24}.
We aim to evaluate the effectiveness of the above three jailbreak algorithms against continuously upgraded and updated LLMs on the FQ dataset.
It contains 160 forbidden questions that cover the usage policies of five major LLM-related service providers, i.e., Google,\footnote{\url{https://policies.google.}OpenAI,\footnote{\url{https://openai.}Meta,\footnote{\url{https://ai.meta.}Amazon,\footnote{\url{https://aws.amazon.}\textsuperscript{,}\footnote{\url{https://aws.amazon.} and Microsoft.\footnote{\url{https://learn.microsoft.}\textsuperscript{,}\footnote{\url{https://learn.microsoft.}
Therefore, compared with previous works~\cite{ZWKF23, SCBSZ23}, the FQ dataset includes a wider variety of forbidden questions.

\mypara{Hallucination Datasets}
We generate the hallucination answers following the principle from~\cite{LCZNW23} under three tasks, i.e., HotpotQA \cite{YQZBCSM18} (QA), OpenDialKG~\cite{MSKS19} (dialogue), and CNN/Daily Mail~\cite{SLM17} (summarization).
We use \hytt{mistral-7B} as a surrogate model to generate three responses for sampling 5,000 queries, with the final selection based on the lowest similarity to the correct answer.
We construct the hallucination dataset with both the correct answer from the original dataset and the corresponding hallucinated answer for each sample.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{section:metrics}

In this paper, we consider the following three evaluation metrics (\cts, \rts, and \pdr) for measuring the performance:

\begin{itemize}
\item 
\emph{\textbf{Clean Test Score (CTS)}} evaluates the target LLMs on clean queries (e.g., \adq{zero}{CC} or \adq{few}{CCC}).
Note that for different tasks, \cts has different meanings:
(1) For the misclassification task, \cts signifies the \textit{clean prediction accuracy}, thereby reflecting the normal utility of the LLMs.
(2) Regarding the jailbreak task,CTS represents \textit{rejection rate}, reflecting the capability of LLMs rejecting jailbreak attempts.
Note that for the hallucination task, we do not need \cts to qualify the answers.
Therefore, for all tasks, a \textit{higher} \cts ($\uparrow$) indicates a stronger model foundation utility.
\item 
\emph{\textbf{Robust Test Score (RTS)}} measures the success score given the adversarial queries,
It offers multiple angles to evaluate the robustness of the LLMs on different tasks:
(1) For misclassification tasks, \rts quantifies the prediction accuracy on adversarial examples.
(2) For jailbreak tasks, \rts denotes the rejection rate of LLMs against jailbreak prompts.
(3) For hallucination tasks, \rts stands for the correct rate for selecting the correct answers.
As a result, a \textit{higher} \rts ($\uparrow$) indicates better robustness against adversarial queries.
\item 
\emph{\textbf{Performance Drop Rate (PDR)}}, which was introduced by~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23}, aims to quantify the extent of performance decline under adversarial attacks.
In general, \textit{lower} \pdr ($\downarrow$) indicates superior model robustness.
\pdr can be formulated as:
\[
    PDR = \frac{CTS - RTS}{CTS}.
\]
\end{itemize}

\mypara{Note}
To evaluate \cts and \rts for jailbreak attacks, we use GPT-4 as our judge model.
For instance, we utilized the prompt from~\cite{CLYSBZ24}, which uses three demonstrations for 
few-shot learning to better instruct GPT-4 to judge the harmfulness of the responses (see \autoref{figure:judge_prompt}).

\subsection{Evaluation on Misclassification}
\label{section:mc_eva}

\mypara{GPT-3.5}
We analyze the performance of three versions of GPT-3.5 against benign queries and adversarial queries.
\autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_0} and \autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_3} present the \cts and \rts results of zero-shot learning and few-shot learning, respectively.
The results of \pdr under zero-shot learning and few-show learning are both shown in \autoref{table:pdrgpt3.5}.
Note that the standard deviation within the figures is related to different adversarial examples generated from different surrogate language models.
The results of \autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_0} reveal that v1106 exhibits the lowest \cts and \rts among the three versions of GPT-3.5.
For instance, across all five classification datasets, the utility of v1106 is significantly inferior to that of the other two versions in zero-shot learning.
For example, the v1106's \cts on SST-2 is 0.189, while the other two versions can achieve 0.874 and 0.742, respectively.
When feeding adversarial queries, the \rts of v1106 on \adq{zero}{AC} is only 0.038, while the \rts of the other two models are 0.556 and 0.430, respectively.
Concurrently, as the zero-shot \pdr results in \autoref{table:pdrgpt3.5} shows (the red blocks are the largest \pdr among all the models for one dataset, while the green blocks refer to the lowest \pdr), it is evident that v1106 demonstrates the least robustness. 
For most datasets, v1106 has the highest \pdr in 14 of 15 scenarios, which means that not only are \cts and \rts the lowest, but its performance decline is also the greatest.
Therefore, v1106 is the worst among these versions in the GPT-3.5 family.
In addition, it was originally hoped that each successive version will manifest improvements across all evaluation metrics.
However, the results from \autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_0} do not unequivocally support such an optimistic conclusion.
Specifically, v0125 cannot achieve performance similar to v0613 in many datasets, such as RTE and QNLI.
Compared to v0613, v0125 performs extremely unstablely on adversarial examples generated by different surrogate models, with its standard deviation significantly higher than that in the aforementioned versions.
Although some of the \pdr results in the upgraded version are lower than before, the decreased \cts and \rts values, such as the SST-2 dataset, reinforce that the upgraded version has not markedly improved.

\autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_3} demonstrates the results of different categories of queries in few-shot learning.
Compared with zero-shot learning, the \rts results underscore the context-dependent nature of model behavior and the robustness. 
Despite the help of the demonstration, v1106 still performs the worst, both in \cts and \rts.
In addition, the latest version (v0125) fails to showcase substantial improvements in model effectiveness compared with the first iteration (v0613). 
For instance, in \adq{few}{CCA} of the MNLI dataset, the \rts results under each scenario for the v0125 model are discernibly lower than those of v0613.
As demonstrated in \autoref{table:pdrgpt3.5}, the \pdr results fluctuate from different adversarial queries.
Among the 28 adversarial attacks analyzed, it was observed that 16 of them exhibit a lower \pdr in the v0613 (not considering situations where they are equal).
For some datasets, such as the QQP dataset, v0125 has a larger \pdr than v0613 among all adversarial queries, even though the upgraded version has a larger \cts.
After careful analysis, we discover that the reasons for this phenomenon align with our previous results. 
The increase of \pdr is due to the simultaneous increase or decrease of \cts and \rts. 
Thus, we cannot solely evaluate the model based on the \pdr, especially when versions differ.

Overall, the above analysis underscores that specific scenarios maintain significant attack effectiveness in misclassification, even in the upgraded version.
In general, for the GPT-3.5 family, v0613 is better than v0125, whereby, in turn, v1106 performs the worst.

For zero-shot learning, from \autoref{figure:gpt-4_0}, the latest version, v0409, clearly performs the worst across the majority of datasets.

For \pdr, the upgraded versions still have the majority of the highest performance drops.
For example, in the SST-2 and QNLI datasets, v0125 and v0409 consistently achieve the highest \pdr results across all categories of adversarial attacks.

For our experiment, we also search external statistics for performance evaluations of these versions.\footnote{\url{https://chat.lmsys.org/?leaderboard}.}
This leaderboard is a benchmark platform for LLMs that features anonymous, randomized battles in a crowdsourced manner.
The leaderboard is based on the foundation of human evaluations and voting mechanisms through comparative analysis of the outputs of different LLMs when fed the same questions.
Similar to our clean test methodology, this approach is designed to probe the lower bounds of model performance.
Notably, the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 family ranking from this leaderboard is congruent with our conclusion.
In a nutshell, despite advancements or modifications in developing LLMs, there has not been a corresponding enhancement in their robustness against adversarial attacks.

\mypara{LLaMA}
LLaMA v1, v2, and v3 models are generative models without any instruction-tuning or RL-tuning.
Thus, for these models, following~\cite{PGCC23}, we choose to enlarge the logit bias for the labeled words.

\autoref{figure:llama_7b_0} shows the \cts and \rts results of the LLaMA-7B family.

We comprehensively analyze the results, specifically scrutinizing the output produced by these models.
First, the latest versions of the LLaMA models do not exhibit improvements across several datasets.
After careful analysis, in many instances, such as the QNLI dataset, LLaMA models indeed encapsulate the intended meaning of the labels, albeit not exactly matching the labels we had originally targeted.
For example, ``not\_entailment'' in the PromptBench dataset is the label for the QNLI dataset, but the tokenizer will split this word into five different tokens.
This nuanced disparity between the model outputs and the desired labels introduces an elevated level of complexity into our evaluation process.
In addition, the LLaMA models exhibit certain constraints pertaining to the generated length.
These models do not possess the capability to generate the outputs according to the user-specified length requirements. 
Instead, they adhere to a predetermined length criterion and provide the length based on the user's need, which sometimes results in sentence truncation, potentially causing the omission of critically labeled words from the generated text.
For the \pdr results, as demonstrated in the first three rows of \autoref{table:pdrllama}, \autoref{table:pdrllama_13b}, and \autoref{table:pdrllama_70b}, the results of v3 models are consistently higher on average compared to the first two versions.
However, as we analyzed before, we believe that the \pdr results of v3 models underscore the complex and context-dependent nature of model behavior and robustness.
The effectiveness is contingent upon diverse factors, such as generated length and label words, yielding varying results across different datasets and scenarios.

For the few-shot learning, \autoref{figure:llama_7b_3} shows the results of \cts and \rts of the LLaMA-7B family.

First, compared with zero-shot learning, including demonstrations indeed leads to notable enhancements in numerous results.
Moreover, increasing the weight may not fully correlate positively with an increase in its overall robustness.
For example, in the QQP dataset, the \cts of the 7B v3 Chat model is 0.601 while the \cts of the 70B v3 Chat model is 0.570.
In addition, the latest versions still perform worst in many cases, especially in the RTE and QNLI datasets, regardless of model size.
Similar to our observations in zero-shot learning, we contend that the LLaMA models exhibit a degree of sensitivity to the labels despite demonstration.
For the \pdr, the upgraded versions do not have the lowest results for most cases either, especially in the 7B family, indicating it cannot resist adversarial attacks compared with the v1 versions.
In addition, it is still noteworthy that the \pdr metric is a quotient derived from the division of \cts and \rts.
Considering the aforementioned analysis, it becomes apparent that the smaller \pdr values can be attributed to potential reductions in both \cts and \rts.

In this section, we introduce the results of adversarial-based jailbreak attacks. 
Our evaluation employs three adversarial-based optimization methods, with an initial seed description and a question.
We treat this seed description as clean. 
This strategy may yield a higher \cts than direct question querying due to potentially including sensitive or prohibited vocabulary in these initial seed descriptions.

\mypara{GPT-3.5}
We first analyze the attack results of GPT-3.5.
The first three columns in \autoref{table:jb} reveal that among the LLMs evaluated, v1106 exhibits the highest \cts and \rts, suggesting an excellent resistance to jailbreak attacks. 
Conversely, v0613 and v0125 demonstrate inferior performance in resisting different jailbreak attacks.
For \pdr, v1106 also has the lowest drop rate in GPTFuzz and PAIR, whereas v0613 has the lowest one in TAP.
This indicates that the latest version is not necessarily the most effective.
Interestingly, this observation stands in contrast to previous findings in~\autoref{section:mc_eva}, where model v1106 was identified as having the least robustness against the misclassification evaluation. 
This highlights a clear trade-off between misclassification performance and resistance to jailbreak tests across all models.
This trade-off poses a considerable challenge for the model provider, as optimizing the performance of LLMs while maintaining adequate defenses against jailbreak attempts. 
Identifying the trade-off is particularly significant, underscoring the complex robustness and the subtle balance required in LLMs to address diverse safety and performance criteria.

\mypara{GPT-4}
For \cts, as shown in \autoref{table:jb}, v1106 exhibits the highest results.
Conversely, v0613 maintains a 0.537 \cts, indicating a pronounced vulnerability to jailbreak attacks and a substantial safety risk inherent in this particular version.
For all three attacks, v0125 has the highest \rts, demonstrating effectiveness in responding to and mitigating such threats.
In addition, v0409 demonstrates the lowest \rts of GPTFuzz.
For the other two attacks, v0613 has the worst performance. Note that v0613 is the default version of GPT-4.
Since many users typically do not manually select their model version, potential security threats become more exposed when the latest model is set as the default.
For \pdr, although v0613 and v0409 show comparatively lower results in the PAIR and TAP, it is the lower values of \cts and \rts that primarily contribute to these results.
Model developers must adopt a holistic approach to safety and security, considering the implications of jailbreak methods during the upgrade process. 
This balanced approach is integral to improving the overall safety and reliability of the LLMs and guaranteeing their robustness against a wide array of potential vulnerabilities.

\mypara{LLaMA}
First, within the LLaMA 7B model family, the \cts results across all versions are proximate to 1.000, signifying that the clean queries fail to constitute an effective attack on these LLMs. 
Regarding \rts, the v2 model consistently has the highest value across three attacks.
However, for the v3 model, for GPTFuzz and TAP, it has the lowest result of \rts, as well as for PAIR, it has the second worst \rts.
In addition, the \rts of the v3 Instruct model for GPTFuzz is lower than v2 Chat, but it performs better for the other two attacks.
For \pdr, the v3 model also has the highest drop rate in GPTFuzz and TAP.
This differential performance suggests that the iterative enhancements incorporated into the latter versions of the 7B model have successfully bolstered its resilience against jailbreak attacks.

Second, for the 13B family, the \cts values are uniformly near 1.000, indicating a robust defense mechanism against the initial jailbreak prompts.
For GPTFuzz attacks, the v1 model has a higher \rts and lower \pdr relative to the latter versions. 
However, the v2 model exhibits an increasing \rts for the other two attacks compared to the first version, with v2 Chat further showcasing a similar trend.
For \pdr, the v2 models have lower results in PAIR and TAP as well.

Last but not least, in the 70B model, the \cts results are similar to the 7B and 13B models, close to 1.000.
For GPTFuzz, the v1 model is more resistant to jailbreak attacks than its upgraded versions when analyzing \rts and \pdr.
When considering the other two attacks, the v2 Chat demonstrates enhanced resilience.
Notably, the v3 model records the highest \rts and lowest \pdr for all the attacks.

In general, the updated versions in LLaMA models have indeed fortified their defenses against adversarial-based jailbreak attacks.
From another perspective, previous work~\cite{ZYPDLWW24} posits that the 70B models are considered larger and safer compared to the smaller, purportedly less secure 13B models.
However, based on our experiments, we cannot conclude that the 70B model is safer, especially in v2 and v3 models.
For all the attacks, the larger v2 and v3 models consistently exhibit lower \rts compared to their smaller ones, as shown in \autoref{table:jb}.
This trend indicates that larger models may benefit from increased computational capacity and potentially more sophisticated learning capabilities. 
However, they also present a larger attack surface or are more vulnerable to adversarial-based jailbreak attacks.
Increasing the model size and capacity does not linearly translate to improved security or safety and may, in fact, introduce some new vulnerabilities or exacerbate existing ones instead.

\subsection{Evaluation on Hallucination}
\label{section:hl_eva}

In this section, we analyze the hallucination phenomena in LLMs. 
We curate three types of hallucination datasets: Dialogue, QA, and Summarization.
These datasets serve as the basis for evaluating the robustness of longitudinal LLMs across various capability dimensions.
We list the results in \autoref{table:hl}, where the highest value in each longitudinal version is highlighted with a green box, and the lowest one is highlighted with a red box.

\mypara{GPT-3.5}
The first three columns of \autoref{table:hl} show that the upgrade of GPT-3.5 does not correlate with reducing hallucination incidents.
Expressly, in Dialogue and QA datasets, v0613 has the highest \rts, indicating pronounced robustness in these areas but the lowest ones in the Summarization dataset.
In addition, v1106 has the lowest \rts in Dialogue and QA datasets.
Conversely, v0125 is the most robust when evaluating the Summarization dataset.
The results underscore the complexity of addressing hallucination across GPT-3.5 models. 
They highlight the importance of targeted improvements and the need for a multifaceted approach to enhancing model robustness.

\mypara{GPT-4}
The overall performance of GPT-4 in hallucination tests indicates a better level of proficiency. 
As demonstrated in \autoref{table:hl}, v0125 achieves the highest \rts in both the Dialogue and QA datasets.
Meanwhile, for summarization, v1106 has the highest \rts.
However, the latest version, v0409, exhibits a significant regression in performance compared to its predecessor, v0125.
This means that the latest version is not the best choice in terms of hallucination.

\mypara{LLaMA}
For the LLaMA family, the \rts for all models hover around 0.5, indicating a lack of efficacy in accurately detecting hallucinated responses. 
This general trend suggests a gap in the design or training data, limiting their ability to differentiate between accurate and hallucinated content effectively. 
Notably, the \rts values dip even lower than 0.5 in some instances. 
For instance, in the v3 70B model evaluated on the summarization dataset, the \rts plummets to 0.477. 
This observation raises critical concerns about the current methods employed in training and refining these LLMs, particularly in handling complex tasks prone to hallucination.
Model providers should consider integrating expansive foundational information and adopting advanced training methodologies in future upgrades.

\subsection{Takeaways}

Our comprehensive evaluation reveals a concerning trend across various versions of GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and the LLaMA models, indicating a general oversight of robustness as a critical metric in the model upgrade process.
Specifically, for GPT-3.5, v1106, despite its superior performance in jailbreak tests, shows the worst performance in both misclassification and hallucination evaluations, rendering its overall performance less than satisfactory. 
Due to its favorable cost-effectiveness, GPT-3.5 is a preferred choice among many users. 
However, it is worth considering v0613 for specific classification tasks as it potentially provides a better balance between performance and robustness.
For GPT-4, v0125 stands out for its better overall performance, whereas the latest version, v0409, is worse than v0125 in all the tasks.
Regarding the LLaMA family, upgrading models does not improve the robustness.
The LLaMA-3 families perform worst in misclassification and jailbreak tasks, whereas these models demonstrate a significant limitation in distinguishing correct answers.
In addition, our findings underscore that an increase in model size does not guarantee enhanced robustness. 
Furthermore, larger models entail higher GPU resource consumption, which may not align with the cost-effectiveness priorities. 
Therefore, smaller models may offer a more attractive cost-benefit for those prioritizing efficiency and resource management.
In light of these insights, we suggest that model providers prioritize robustness as a critical component of the model development and upgrade process.



\section{Evaluation on Update Over Time}

\label{section:update_eval}

These longitudinal updated models will continuously self-optimize based on user input and feedback. 
As they are continuously exposed to new data and potentially evolving use cases, weekly testing helps assess their adaptability and learning efficiency.
Due to time constraints, we could not complete all the adversarial attacks proposed in \autoref{section:upgrade_eval} for each of the models on a weekly basis. 
Consequently, we select the misclassification task to evaluate the robustness and track the progress of updates.

\subsection{Evaluation on Misclassification}

\mypara{GPT-3.5}
\autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_e0} and~\autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_e3} demonstrate the trajectory of weekly tests in the benchmark dataset.
From the figures, there is a significant turning point for zero-shot learning from February 12th to 19th. 
Specifically, SST-2, MNLI, and QNLI datasets exhibit a marked decline in \cts, accompanied by a concomitant decrease in \rts to varying extents.
Conversely, for the QQP dataset, both \cts and \rts demonstrate an upward trend during the same period.
Regarding the RTE dataset, the \cts remains relatively stable, showing no significant fluctuations, yet an increase in \rts.
After updating to v0125, small fluctuations are still observed.
It is hypothesized that OpenAI may persist in minor updates to this model, potentially leading to variations in the attack performance.

In addition, from~\autoref{figure:gpt-3.5_e3}, there is also a tuning point from February 12th to 19th.
When juxtaposed with zero-shot learning, few-shot learning demonstrates a comparatively stable performance across different datasets, exhibiting minimal fluctuations in performance metrics. 
More concretely, the QQP dataset is the only one that manifests a significant upward trajectory in \cts. 
Furthermore, the impacts of various elements of ICL on different datasets are heterogeneous, with some datasets, such as MNLI in \adq{few}{AAC}, experiencing increases in vulnerability while others, such as SST-2 in \adq{few}{ACA}, decrease.

By comparing with the official release notes, we find that on February 16th, \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo} automatically updates from \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613} to \hytt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0125}.
Besides, models at slots have not been significantly updated.
However, based on the results of our experiments, this development contributed, at least partially, to the enhancements observed in the GPT-3.5 model, which marks a discernible divergence in performance metrics when compared with its predecessors.
We hope that OpenAI will continue to evaluate the overall performance of the GPT-3.5 when releasing such new features to ensure a more stable consistency.

\mypara{GPT-4}
Compared to GPT-3.5, GPT-4 does not show particularly large fluctuations on both \cts and \rts in zero-shot and few-shot learning (see~\autoref{figure:gpt-4_e0} and~\autoref{figure:gpt-4_e3}).

\subsection{Takeaways}

Model providers may update the LLMs to add more new features over time.
While these updates initially leave the parameters of the pre-trained model ostensibly unchanged, they could nonetheless exert a subtle influence on the overall performance of the LLMs.
Transitioning from one version to another, mainly when there are no significant improvements in robustness, can exacerbate existing issues rather than resolve them, losing the purpose of updating the model.
Consequently, model providers should adopt a standardized approach to evaluating the performance of LLMs during the update.



\section{Related Work}

\label{section:relatedwork}

\mypara{Adversarial Attacks} 
We focus on adversarial attacks that manipulate legitimate inputs to mislead a trained model to produce incorrect outputs in the NLP domain~\cite{ZSAL20}.
These attacks commonly manipulate the input text at character-, word-, and sentence-level to attain the attack goals (i.e., targeted or untargeted attacks).
Similar to adversarial attacks in computer vision domain, they can be categorized into black-box attacks (paraphrase~\cite{IWGZ18,RSG18,ASEHSC18}, text manipulation~\cite{BB18,LJDLW19,MR18}, etc.) and white-box attacks (FGSM~\cite{LLSBLS18,SM18}, JSMA~\cite{PMSH16}, HotFlip~\cite{ERLD18}, etc.).
In the NLP domain, those attacks have been successfully applied to attack various applications, such as optical character recognition~\cite{SS18}, image caption~\cite{CZCYH18}, visual question answering~\cite{XCLRDS18}, etc.
Our objective here is not to devise novel adversarial attacks against LLMs.
Rather, we use existing methods to understand whether LLMs can be challenged by carefully crafted textual adversarial examples and whether/how these adversarial examples can be transferred to different versions of an LLM.

\mypara{LLMs}
Large language models (LLMs) have become a prominent area of research and application in the NLP domain, driven primarily by the transformer architecture~\cite{VSPUJGKP17}.
These models are trained on massive text data and boast a substantial number of parameters, often exceeding hundreds of billions.\footnote{\url{https://chat.openai.}
As LLMs grow in size, they demonstrate emergent abilities such as enhanced language understanding~\cite{ZCSLE23}, coherent text generation~\cite{CKA23}, and contextual comprehension~\cite{ZZPC23}, which are not present in smaller models.
Moreover, fine-tuning techniques (e.g., LoRA~\cite{HSWALWWC22}) are invented to adapt the pre-trained LLMs to specific downstream tasks, allowing them to exhibit specialized behavior and produce task-specific outputs.



\section{Discussion}


In this study, we do not focus on generating adversarial examples on the \emph{Adversarial Description} dataset and \emph{Adversarial Question} dataset.
The primary reason behind our decision lies in the considerable financial cost of generating such adversarial examples, as it requires us to query GPT models for an extensive duration, spanning several weeks. 
Furthermore, it is worth noting that no universally applicable standard template for the ICL exists.
Certain queries may prove effective only when applied to specific datasets, and even minor modifications in the queries or choice of words can yield vastly different classification results, increasing the difficulty of queries.
Despite these limitations, our investigation reveals that the adversarial examples derived from a traditional model continue to significantly impact LLMs, aligning with previous works~\cite{ZWZWCWYYGZX23,ZWKF23}.
Given that organizations such as OpenAI and Meta have not open-sourced the training datasets for their models, there exists a potential risk of inadvertently evaluating models on their training sets. 
While our analysis suggests a markedly low probability of our evaluation test set intersecting with the training set, accurately assessing this risk for future model iterations remains challenging.
It is generally a trending topic to construct or select non-overlap datasets for LLM evaluation nowadays~\cite{ZZCCZCLWH23}.
In the future, we could also choose the datasets that do not overlap with the training data, e.g., the dataset with a CC BY-SA 4.0 license or constructed by ourselves.
In general, we believe it is important for the model provider to use enhancing adversarial robustness methods in model upgrades and updates.
In the future, we will continue to measure the new features of LLMs after the model updates.



\section{Conclusion}


We comprehensively assess the robustness of the longitudinal versions of LLMs, focusing on GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMA through the lens of misclassification, jailbreak, and hallucination evaluation.
Our empirical results consistently demonstrate that, for all the LLMs, the upgraded and updated model does not exhibit heightened robustness against the proposed adversarial queries compared to its predecessor. 
In general, our analysis reveals a prevalent trend of decreased adversarial robustness in the many upgraded and updated versions.
Our findings reinforce the importance of understanding and assessing the robustness aspect when updating LLMs, calling for enhanced focus on comprehensive evaluation and reinforcement strategies to counter evolving adversarial challenges.
