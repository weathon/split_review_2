\title{ALMANACS: A Simulatability Benchmark for Language Model Explainability}

\begin{abstract}

How do we measure the efficacy of language model explainability methods?
While many explainability methods have been developed, they are typically evaluated on bespoke tasks, preventing an apples-to-apples comparison. To help fill this gap, we present ALMANACS, a language model explainability benchmark.
ALMANACS scores explainability methods on simulatability, i.e., how well the explanations improve behavior prediction on new inputs.
The ALMANACS scenarios span twelve safety-relevant topics such as ethical reasoning and advanced AI behaviors; they have idiosyncratic premises to invoke model-specific behavior; and they have a train-test distributional shift to encourage faithful explanations.
By using another language model to predict behavior based on the explanations, ALMANACS is a fully automated benchmark.
We use ALMANACS to evaluate counterfactuals, rationalizations, attention, and Integrated Gradients explanations. Our results are sobering: when averaged across all topics, no explanation method outperforms the explanation-free control.
We conclude that despite modest successes in prior work, developing an explanation method that aids simulatability in ALMANACS remains an open challenge
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Understanding the behavior of deep neural networks is critical for their safe deployment. While deep neural networks are a black box by default, a wide variety of interpretability methods are being developed to explain their behavior \citep{räuker2023transparent,Nauta2022FromAE}. Some approaches, such as LIME \citep{ribeiro2016should} and MUSE \citep{lakkaraju2019faithful}, try to approximate output behavior. Other approaches try to mechanistically explain the circuits inside a network \citep{nanda2023progress,wang2023interpretability}. Some approaches imitate explanations in the training data \citep{camburu2018esnli,narang2020wt5,marasović2022fewshot}. Other approaches study the network's activations, such as a transformer's attention over its input \citep{Serrano2019IsAI,Wiegreffe2019AttentionIN}. Others aim to create neural networks that are intrinsically explainable \citep{jain2020learning}.
With so many interpretability methods to choose from, how can we tell which one works best? Despite years of work in the field, there is no consistent evaluation standard. New interpretability papers generally test their methods on bespoke tasks, making it difficult to assess their true effectiveness. To solve this issue, \citet{doshivelez2017rigorous}, \citet{Nauta2022FromAE}, and \citet{räuker2023transparent} argue that we need standard interpretability benchmarks. Just as benchmarks have driven progress in computer vision \citep{imagenet_cvpr09}, natural language processing \citep{wang2018glue,wang2019superglue}, and reinforcement learning \citep{brockman2016openai,tunyasuvunakool2020}, we seek to drive progress in interpretability by enabling apples-to-apples comparisons across diverse methods.

In designing an interpretability benchmark, both ``what to measure?'' and ``how to measure it?'' are tricky questions. As interpretability methods have varying goals and downstream applications, there are many desirable properties for interpretability metrics to measure. These properties include faithfulness \citep{jacovi2020faithfully}, robustness \citep{alvarezmelis2018robust}, completeness \citep{wang2023interpretability}, plausibility \citep{ehsan2019automated}, and minimality \citep{wang2023interpretability}, among others. Many of these properties are only defined conceptually, not mathematically; so even after desired properties are chosen, it's a challenge to measure them precisely. Past work circumvents this issue by using human studies as a gold standard for evaluation \citep{colin2023i, hase2020evaluating, marasović2022fewshot, arora2022explain}. This gold standard, however, requires a large cost of both time and money. As it can take weeks to perform a human study, this is a major bottleneck in the development of interpretability methods.
We leverage the recent emergence of LLM capabilities to overcome this bottleneck. As LLMs are proving able to substitute crowd workers \citep{Gilardi2023ChatGPTOC,alizadeh2023open,Veselovsky2023ArtificialAA}, we explore their potential to replace humans as automated evaluators of interpretability methods. In \Secref{sec:interpverif}, we investigate this possibility, running experiments indicating that ChatGPT can indeed reason accurately about explanations in ALMANACS when they come from a ground-truth linear model. This observation forms the core of our benchmark: LLMs can automatically evaluate explanations! Compared to human-in-the-loop approaches, our fully automated benchmark drastically speeds up the interpretability algorithm development cycle. Automation also makes our benchmark less expensive than human studies while being more reproducible.
Our benchmark is centered around the concept of \textit{simulatability} \citep{hase2020evaluating,Fel2021WhatIC}. Across a diverse set of text scenarios, we measure if an explanation method improves the ability to predict model behavior on held-out examples. This anchors our benchmark to a concrete application of interpretability -- behavior prediction -- that is a necessary condition for explanations to be faithful and complete.
Furthermore, our benchmark measures how well explanations aid performance under distributional shift. Each of our benchmark tasks is a written scenario with hardcoded placeholders. By holding out some of the placeholder values exclusively for the test set, we perform stress tests that see if explanations provide insight into novel scenarios.
Our results yield a striking observation: compared to the control setting with no explanations, none of the tested interpretability methods consistently improve simulatability. This underscores the open challenge of generating explanations that aid prediction. Our study, however, is not without its limitations. While using LLMs for automatic evaluation holds promise, its consistency with human evaluation remains an open question. It's possible that humans could succeed in cases where LLMs fail, and vice versa. Future work with human studies is needed to resolve this question.

We present ALMANACS: Anticipating Language Model Answers in Non-objective And Complex Scenarios. When creating ALMANACS, we made the following key design choices.

\textbf{Simulatability.} Our benchmark measures simulatability, ie, how much an explanation helps predict the model's behavior on new inputs \citep{hase2020evaluating,Fel2021WhatIC}. We choose simulatability because it is tractable to measure and because it is related to two desired properties: faithfulness and completeness. Faithfulness is how accurately an explanation reflects the model's reasoning \citep{jacovi2020faithfully,chan2022comparative,lyu2023faithful}, and completeness is how much of the model's behavior is explained \citep{wang2023interpretability}. By definition, totally faithful and complete explanations would enable accurate prediction of model behavior on new inputs. Simulatability is therefore a necessary condition for faithfulness and completeness.

\textbf{Non-objective.} Consider a dataset of objective questions, such as calculus questions, and an explanation method that generates expositions about calculus. Assuming that the model often gives correct answers, these ``explanations'' could help with predicting the model's behavior \textit{even though the explanation method knows nothing about the model's internals}. To avoid this confounding effect, we make all questions in our benchmark \textit{non-objective}.

\textbf{Complex behavior.} We construct datasets of unusual, multi-premise scenarios that elicit nonlinear model behavior by adversarially filtering against a logistic regression baseline.
\textbf{Distributional shift.} Predicting a model's behavior within a known distribution may be accomplished by interpolating between observed values, bypassing the need to understand the model’s internal reasoning. To favor methods that provide faithful explanations of the model's reasoning, we evaluate simulatability under a distributional shift between questions in a train and test set, where good performance requires extrapolation from an accurate understanding of the model. \scott{Reconsider this phrasing.}
\textbf{Safety-relevant.} As benchmarks should measure how helpful methods are at producing useful insights \citep{räuker2023transparent}, the behaviors we evaluate are related to existing harms, as well as the types of behaviors we want to regulate in advanced AI.



\subsection{Framework for Explanations}

Our simulatability pipeline, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:explainpredict}, has two parts: an explainer and a predictor.

Given a language model $f: X \rightarrow Y$, we collect a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{(x, y)\}$, where $f(x) = y \in [0, 1]$ is the model's probability of answering \texttt{Yes}.

We formalize an interpretability method as an \emph{explainer} function $\mathcal{E}: (f,\mathcal{D}) \mapsto e$. Each $e$ is an explanation corresponding to a particular $(x,y) \in \mathcal{D}$. Additionally, we allow each $e$ to depend on $f$ and $\mathcal{D}$. We call an explanation ``local'' if it just describes behavior in the region of $(x, y)$ and ``global'' if it describes behavior outside this region. In the most general case, the explainer $\mathcal{E}$ could evaluate $f$ on additional inputs and access its internal state: a trivial $\mathcal{E}$ might simply copy $f$'s weights, enabling perfect simulation but minimal model comprehension. From $\mathcal{E}$, we obtain an explanation-augmented dataset $\tilde{\mathcal{D}} = \{(x, y, e)\}$.

These explanations are then read by a \emph{predictor} function $\mathcal{P}: (\tilde{\mathcal{D}}, x) \mapsto \tilde{y}$, which uses the explanation-augmented dataset $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}$ to simulate $f$ on test inputs $x \notin \mathcal{D}$ (similar to \citet{colin2023i}). Crucially, $\mathcal{P}$ has no access to $f$, only information about $f$ through $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}$. While our framework leaves open the nature of this predictor, we desire $\mathcal{P}$ to be capable, inexpensive, and effective only on human-legible explanations. While human evaluations remain the simulatability gold standard, employing a human $\mathcal{P}$ is expensive and slow. The selected examples $(x, y, e) \in \tilde{\mathcal{D}}$ are the 10 nearest neighbors to the respective test question by the cosine similarity of text embeddings of the questions. %

\subsection{Templates and Dataset Generation}

Our benchmark comprises Yes/No questions and answers for 12 safety-relevant topics. The topics are listed in Figure \ref{fig:behavior_summary}. For each topic, 15 templates each independently generate 500 train and 50 test questions. A template comprises a multi-sentence scenario in which 5 placeholder phrases are each selected from a set of 15 possible values; an example appears in Figure \ref{fig:benchmarkdesign}. The use of templates allows us to study model behavior over a well-defined region of the input space and intervene on high-level concepts of the inputs, as in CEBaB \citep{abraham2022cebab}. Training questions are sampled from a limited subset of the values for each placeholder, so that test questions present both new combinations of seen values and entirely new values unseen in the train set, depicted in Figure \ref{fig:benchmarkdesign}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Suppose on input $x$, the model $f$ outputs the probability $y(x) = f(x)$ and the predictor $\mathcal{P}$ predicts $\tilde{y}(x) = \mathcal{P}(\tilde{\mathcal{D}},x)$. Rather than throwing away information by binarizing these outputs, we use probability distribution metrics to compare $y$ and $\tilde{y}$, averaged over all $x$ in the test dataset $\mathcal{D}_\text{test}$. We also include the Spearman correlation coefficient as a simulatability metric that captures the predictor's ability to rank $x$ by $y(x)$, rather than precise prediction of the probability distribution $y$.

\textbf{\textsc{KLDiv.}} The familiar Kullback–Leibler divergence is a proper scoring rule that measures the statistical distance between $y$ and $\tilde{y}$. Equivalently, it is the expected log score of predictions $S_{y}^{\tilde{y}}(x) = y(x) \cdot \log\big(\tilde{y}(x)\big) + \big(1 - y(x)\big)\cdot \log\big(1 - \tilde{y}(x)\big)$, normalized by the entropy of the model distribution and negated:
$\textsc{KLDiv}(\mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{D}} \bigg( S_{y}^{y}(x) - S_{y}^{\tilde{y}}(x) \bigg).$

\textbf{\textsc{TVDist.}} The total variation distance is equivalent to the L1 distance between $y$ and $\tilde{y}$. \textsc{TVDist} has the advantage of being more intuitively understandable and bounded to the unit interval, but it is not a proper scoring rule:
$\textsc{TVDist}(\mathcal{D}) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|} \sum_{x \in \mathcal{D}} \big| y(x) - \tilde{y}(x) \big|$.

\textbf{\textsc{Spearman}} The Spearman correlation coefficient measures the correlation of $y$ and $\tilde{y}$'s rank variables, $R(y)$ and $R(\tilde{y})$. We compute it per dataset topic: $\textsc{Spearman}(\mathcal{D}) = \frac{\mathrm{cov}(R(y), R(\tilde{y})}{\sigma_{R(y)} \sigma_{R(\tilde{y})}}$.



\section{Explanation Methods}



\subsection{Naive Baselines}
\label{sec:naivebaselines}

The following naive methods assume a very simple logic to the model $f$. We include them as a reference point from which good interpretability methods must improve.

\textbf{\textsc{PredictAverage}} predicts the answer as the mean of \texttt{Yes} probabilities observed in the training data, $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}, x) = \left(1 / |\mathcal{D}|\right) \sum f(x'), \forall x' \in \mathcal{D}$ .

\textbf{\textsc{NearestNeighbor}} predicts the answer as the \texttt{Yes} probability of the nearest instance in the training data, where the similarity metric is the cosine similarity between the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} embeddings of words appearing in $x$: $\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}, x) = f( \mathop{\arg\min}_{x' \in \mathcal{D}} \text{sim}(x, x')) $.

\textbf{\textsc{NearestNeighborThree}} is analogous to \textsc{NearestNeighbor}, but takes the mean \texttt{Yes} probability over $k=3$ nearest neighbors.

\textbf{\textsc{LogisticRegression}} learns from the train data by logistic regression on the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} embeddings of $x$. That is,
$ \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{D}, x) = p(x) = 1 / \left( 1 + \exp{(ax+b)} \right) $ where we use gradient descent to fit weights $a,b$ to minimize the binary cross-entropy loss
\begin{equation*}
\mathop{\arg\min}\limits_{a,b}\sum_{x' \in \mathcal{D}} f(x') \ln{p(x')} + \big(1 - f(x')\big) \ln{\big(1-p(x')}\big).
\end{equation*}
\subsection{Counterfactuals}
\label{sec:counterfactuals}

Counterfactuals, alternatives close to the input that change a model's output, have been championed as effective supplementary data for interpretability \citep{sharma2019certifai}. Counterfactually-augmented data probes the model's decision boundary \citep{gardner2020evaluating}, and training with such ``contrast sets'' can boost performance and robustness to spurious cues \citep{kaushik2019learning}. Counterfactual explanations have aided human performance on vision tasks \citep{goyal2019counterfactual}.

We generate counterfactual explanations by identifying, for each $(x,y) \in \mathcal{D}$, the nearest neighbor $(x',y')$ that satisfies $|y' - y| > \delta$, where $\delta$ is a threshold we set to 0.2. This ensures that the answers differ sufficiently for $(x',y')$ to serve as a contrastive counterfactual to $(x,y)$. We define ``near'' by the cosine similarity of the \texttt{all-mpnet-base-v2} embeddings of the words in $x$ and $x'$. The explanation corresponding to this example is then $e=(x',y')$. Thanks to the templated form of our questions $\{x\}$, the difference between $x$ and $x'$ is conceptual and localized to a fraction of the text.

\subsection{Rationalizations}
\label{sec:rationalizations}

Natural language rationalizations have enjoyed success in explainable AI \citep{gurrapu2023rationalization}, model distillation \citep{hsieh2023distilling,li2022explanations}, and in improving robustness against spurious cues \citep{ludan2023explanation}. Because large language models possess zero-shot reasoning capabilities \citep{kojima2022large}, they may be able to introspect through self-generated explanations. \citet{wiegreffe2020measuring} suggest that large models can indeed produce faithful free-text explanations in a joint predict-and-rationalize setting for question-answering. Indeed, \citet{chen2023models} find that rationalizations can aid model simulatability.  Like \citet{wiegreffe2022measuring} and \citet{chen2023models}, we study the abstractive rather than extractive setting. We generate a free-form natural language rationalization for each question-answer pair $(x,y)$ by prompting the model $f$ with $(x,y)$ and instructions to explain its reasoning step-by-step. We save $f$'s output as the explanation $e$.
\subsection{Attention}
\label{sec:attention}

The attention of a transformer architecture \citep{Serrano2019IsAI} is one of many different salience methods. Also known as feature attribution methods, these methods assign a value to each part of the input representing its contribution to the output. Other methods include gradients (e.g. integrated gradients \citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic}, see Section \ref{sec:integrated-grad-norm}), DeepLIFT \citep{shrikumar2017learning}, GradCAM \citep{selvaraju2017grad}), perturbations (e.g. LIME \citep{ribeiro2016should}, SHAP \citep{lundberg2017unified}), and influence functions \citep{grosse2023studying}. They can produce informative visualizations and aid humans in finding adversarial attacks \citep{ziegler2022adversarial}, but showed mixed-to-weak results as an aid for human-evaluated simulatability \citep{hase2020evaluating}.

We evaluated the salience attribution given by final-layer attention patterns, following \citet{pruthi2021evaluating} who found this most effective in an explanation-augmented distillation setting, out of 7 salience schemes. We (lossily) verbalize the attention vectors to make them more human-comprehensible \citep{Feldhus2022ConstructingNL}, such that each explanation comprises a list of the input's 25 most salient tokens by absolute value (excluding special and whitespace tokens). We instruct to the predictor to pay attention to these important parts of the sentence. 

\subsection{Integrated Gradients}
\label{sec:integrated-grad-norm}

We study Integrated Gradients \citet{sundararajan2017axiomatic}, another feature attribution method, using the same procedure as we use for \textsc{Attention}. Integrated Gradients stands out among feature attribution methods because it is axiomatically motivated. Created to satisfy \textit{sensitivity} and \textit{implementation invariance}, Integrated Gradients is also the unique path method that is \textit{symmetry preserving}; see \citet{sundararajan2017axiomatic} for details. In \citet{pruthi2021evaluating}'s distillation-based evaluation of explanation methods, Integrated Gradients was one of the best-performing methods.


\section{Testing the GPT-4 predictor}

\label{sec:interpverif}

Before evaluating if these explanations aid model simulation, we test a critical assumption of the ALMANACS design: that the GPT-4 predictor can understand explanations and apply them in new scenarios. Specifically, we test if GPT-4 can predict the ALMANACS behavior of a synthetic model when we provide GPT-4 with hand-crafted explanations designed to contain useful information.

Our experimental setup is identical to all our other ALMANACS tests, with the following twist: the model $f$ is a five-variable linear model followed by a sigmoid. The weights of the linear model are drawn from the exponential distribution with $\lambda=1$. To input an ALMANACS scenario into the model, we do the following. We use the \texttt{all-distilroberta-v1} \citep{reimers2019sentencebert} to embed all the values of each of the 5 placeholders. For each template, we do a unique principal component analysis (PCA) for each of the 5 placeholders; the PCA is over the 15 possible placeholder values. We assign a real-valued score according to the leading PCA component of each placeholder, and these 5 scores are the input to the model. Intuitively, the model has a linear decision boundary over a PCA of embeddings of the placeholder values.

We assess two explanations. The \textsc{Qualitative} explanation is vague and imprecise, revealing that each variable slot has a different degree of influence on the final answer, the variables with the highest and lowest values for each slot, and whether each variable inclines the answer to \texttt{Yes} or \texttt{No}. The \textsc{Weights} explanation divulges the weights of the linear model and the scores for all train set variables. Note that neither explanation provides information about values that are unseen in the train set. 
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{figures/fig_synthtic_model_results.png}
    \caption{GPT-4's prediction performance on ALMANACS for a synthetic linear model.}
    \label{fig:predictorverify}
\end{wrapfigure}

Can GPT-4 use these explanations to improve its predictions? In \Figref{fig:predictorverify}, we see that providing the \textsc{Qualitative} explanation substantially improves predictions over the no-explanation control (\textsc{NoExpl}), reducing \textsc{KLDiv} from 0.54 to 0.30. It beats two naive baselines described in Section \ref{sec:naivebaselines} -- \textsc{PredictAverage} and \textsc{LogisticRegression} -- which have \textsc{KLDiv} scores of 0.41 and 0.35, respectively. Providing the \textsc{Weights} explanation is even more effective, achieving the lowest \textsc{KLDiv} of 0.16. This is as we expected, since the \textsc{Weights} explanation offers full transparency into the model, omitting only the scores of some test values. We conclude that, at least in this synthetic setting, GPT-4 is indeed able to leverage qualitative and quantitative explanations to improve its predictions.

Using ALMANACS, we evaluate all explanation methods. The evaluation is on a per-template basis: when predicting on a test question, the predictor has access only to the $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}$ of train questions from the same template. We also include the \textsc{NoExpl} control, which sets $\tilde{D} = D$. Table \ref{tab:results} reports the results, measured by \textsc{KLDiv}; the \textsc{TVDist} and \textsc{Spearman} results in Appendices \ref{sec:tvdist_results} and \ref{sec:spearman_results} are similar.

\textbf{Naive baseline performance.} How do the naive baselines perform? As expected, the naive baselines are the worst predictors of all methods. Considering both \texttt{flan-alpaca-gpt4-xl} and \texttt{vicuna-7b-v1.3}, all of \textsc{PredictAverage}, \textsc{NearestNeighbor}, and \textsc{NearestNeighborThree} achieve \textsc{KLDiv}s between 0.13 and 0.21. \textsc{LogisticRegression} is the best naive baseline, with a \textsc{KLDiv} of 0.11 on \texttt{flan-alpaca-gpt4-xl} and of 0.09 on \textsc{vicuna-7b-v1.3}. These results confirm that the adversarial dataset selection makes ALMANACS difficult for most our naive baselines, with \textsc{LogisticRegression} being the exception.

\textbf{Idiosyncrasy between models.} Does ALMANACS elicit distinct behavior for the two different language models? Though the models have the same overall trend in their average results, they differ across topics. For example, \texttt{flan-alpaca-gpt4-xl}'s Hiring Decisions behavior is the \textit{easiest} topic for the predictor to simulate, with \textsc{KLDiv} scores ranging from 0.02 to 0.03. Simulating \texttt{vicuna-7b-v1.3}'s Hiring Decisions behavior, on the other hand, is the second \textit{hardest} for the predictor, with \textsc{KLDiv} scores ranging from 0.09 to 0.13.

\textbf{No-explanation predictions.} How well does GPT-4 perform as a predictor, even without explanations? In the \textsc{NoExpl} control, we prompt GPT-4 with 10 input-output examples $(x, y)$ from the training data, without explanations. Compared to the naive baselines, \textsc{NoExpl} performs better for both \texttt{flan-alpaca-gpt4-xl} and \texttt{vicuna-7b-v1.3}, with mean \textsc{KLDiv}s of 0.10 and 0.08, respectively. \textsc{NoExpl}'s improvement over the naive baselines shows that GPT-4 can do in-context learning to aid prediction. Relative to the \textsc{PredictAverage} and \textsc{LogisticRegression} baselines, \textsc{NoExpl}'s Table \ref{tab:results} results are better than its Figure \ref{fig:predictorverify} results. This relative performance improvement suggests that the GPT-4 predictor is better at in-context learning of other language models' behavior than in-context learning of a synthetic linear model.

\textbf{Explanation method performance.} Do \textsc{Counterfactual}, \textsc{Rationalization}, \textsc{Attention}, or \textsc{IntegratedGradients} explanations improve GPT-4's predictions? For each explanation method, we prompt GPT-4 with 10 input-out-explanation examples $(x, y, e)$ from the explanation-augmented training data. For \texttt{flan-alpaca-gpt4-xl}, all four explanation methods yield 0.09 or 0.10 mean \textsc{KLDiv}, matching the 0.10 of \textsc{NoExpl}. The most notable success case is \textsc{Counterfactual} explanations, which, compared to \textsc{NoExpl}, decrease \textsc{KLDiv} from 0.19 to 0.15 in Sycophancy. For \texttt{vicuna-7b-v1.3}, all explanation methods achieve on average 0.08 to 0.10 \textsc{KLDiv}, which is matching or slight worse than \textsc{NoExpl}. We conclude that none of the explanation methods reliably improve predictions over the \textsc{NoExpl} control. 









\section{Related Work}

Despite numerous metrics proposed to evaluate the quality of explanations, there is not an established consensus on the best measures\citep{chen2022makes, jacovi2020faithfully}. This stems from the diversity of explanation forms \citep{lyu2023faithful} and use cases \citep{räuker2023transparent, lertvittayakumjorn2021explanationbased, Schemmer_2022, begley2020explainability}. This also results from the difficulty of formalizing the concept of ``human understandability'' \citep{zhou2022exsum}.

\textbf{Faithfulness}, how well an explanation reflects a model's reasoning process, is a critical dimension of explanation quality \citep{jacovi2020faithfully, lyu2023faithful}. Faithfulness evaluation is difficult because the ground truth of neural model reasoning is non-transparent. Past work develops metrics to quantify the faithfulness of saliency map explanations \citep{chan2022comparative, yin2021sensitivity} and establishes saliency map benchmarks \citep{Agarwal2022OpenXAITA, hooker2019benchmark}.

\textbf{Plausibility} is a qualitative evaluation of how good explanations seem to humans \citep{jacovi2020faithfully}. Plausibility benchmarks often measure similarity to human explanations \citep{wiegreffe2021teach, gurrapu2023rationalization}, disregarding the key property of faithfulness.

\textbf{Simulatability} studies of explanations can be used to distinguish explanations that aid human understanding \citep{chen2023models, Feldhus2022ConstructingNL} from those that don't
\citep{alqaraawi2020evaluating, hase2020evaluating, arora2022explain, colin2023i}. Simulatability has been used to evaluate explanations of a variety of forms, including saliency maps \citep{alqaraawi2020evaluating, jacovi2020faithfully}, verbalized saliency maps \citep{Feldhus2022ConstructingNL}, counterfactuals \citep{alipour2021improving}, contrastive explanations \citep{yin2022interpreting}, and natural language explanations \citep{chen2023models}. In contrast to the nonlinear model behavior in our work, the only existing simulatability benchmark, CEBaB \citep{abraham2022cebab}, probes the relatively simple causal relationship between the conceptual factors of the model's input/output.

\textbf{Automating Simulatability Evaluation}: Given that running simulatability studies with humans in the loop is more costly and complex, a few works have attempted to use machine learning models in place of humans by training a predictor \citep{pruthi2021evaluating, hase2021models, chen2022usecasegrounded, martin2023case, teufel2023quantifying} or prompting language models \citep{chen2023models}. \scott{Add the Pruthi et al. discussion here.}

\textbf{Other Interpretability Benchmarks}: \citet{schwettmann2023function} introduces a benchmark for describing submodules in neural networks. \citet{casper2023red} introduces an interpretability benchmark for image classification models using Trojan detection as a task framework.



\section{Conclusion}


Motivated by the lack of tools for the systematic evaluation of interpretability methods, we introduce ALMANACS. ALMANACS is a fully automated benchmark that measures simulatability, a necessary condition for faithful and complete explanations. %
Using ALMANACS, we evaluate the ability of four explanation methods (\textsc{Counterfactual}, \textsc{Rationalization}, \textsc{Attention}, and \textsc{Integrated Gradients}) to help simulate two language models (\texttt{flan-alpaca-gpt4-xl} and \texttt{vicuna-7b-v1.3}). Our results show that, when averaged across all topics, none of the explanation methods improve performance over the no-explanation control.%

How do we account for this striking null result on several explanation methods, in light of prior work that found they were successful? While future work is needed to provide a definitive answer, we make the following observations. Existing tasks on which explanations are evaluated are generally easier: for example, we expect that tokens interact relatively linearly in the popular IMDB sentiment classification task. Existing tasks, like CommonsenseQA and e-SNLI, are also objective, which could promote explanations that help a model reason but, unlike the ALMANACS idiosyncratic questions, do not probe model understanding. We also observe that the success of an interpretability method has often been demonstrated qualitatively, for example by visually inspecting salience heatmaps for consistency with human intuition. Indeed, more recent efforts to systematically evaluate explanations for both text and images suggest they often fail to yield simulatability benefits \citep{hase2020evaluating, denain2022auditing, alqaraawi2020evaluating, chen2023models}. 



As with any benchmark, ALMANACS has its limitations. Because ALMANACS uses GPT-4 as an automated predictor, it remains unclear how its results will transfer to human studies. An interesting direction for future work would be to investigate this correspondence between automated and human predictors. %
Furthermore, simulatability is not the only desirable property for explanations. It would be interesting for future work to measure other properties such as minimality, i.e. the absence of extraneous information \citep{wang2023interpretability}.


Our results indicate that developing an explanation method that aids simulatability in ALMANACS remains an open challenge. If the field of explainability is not yet up to this challenge, then it could be valuable for future work to develop an easier version of the ALMANACS benchmark. Ideally, the key properties of ALMANACS could be preserved while using simpler tasks that are closer to past tasks in which explanations have been successful.
