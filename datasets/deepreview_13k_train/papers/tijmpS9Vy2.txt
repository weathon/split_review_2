\title{BAMDP Shaping: a Unified Theoretical Framework for Intrinsic Motivation and Reward Shaping}

\begin{abstract}
  Intrinsic motivation (IM) and reward shaping are common methods for guiding the exploration of reinforcement learning (RL) agents by adding pseudo-rewards. Designing these rewards is challenging, however, and they can counter-intuitively harm performance. To address this, we characterize them as reward shaping in Bayes-Adaptive Markov Decision Processes (BAMDPs), which formalizes the value of exploration by formulating the RL process as updating a prior over possible MDPs through experience. RL algorithms can be viewed as BAMDP policies; instead of attempting to find optimal algorithms by solving BAMDPs directly, we use it at a theoretical framework for understanding how pseudo-rewards guide suboptimal algorithms. By decomposing BAMDP state value into the value of the information collected plus the prior value of the physical state, we show how psuedo-rewards can help by compensating for RL algorithms' misestimation of these two terms, yielding a new typology of IM and reward shaping approaches. We carefully extend the potential-based shaping theorem~\citep{ng1999policy} to BAMDPs to prove that when pseudo-rewards are BAMDP Potential-based shaping Functions~(BAMPFs), they preserve optimal, or approximately optimal, behavior of RL algorithms; otherwise, they can corrupt even optimal learners. We finally give guidance on how to design or convert existing pseudo-rewards to BAMPFs by expressing assumptions about the environment as potential functions on BAMDP states. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



RL algorithms are known to struggle when rewards are sparse. A common solution is intrinsic motivation (IM) or reward shaping, which guides RL agents by adding pseudo-rewards to the true rewards~\citep{schmidhuber1991possibility,dorigo1994robot,barto2004intrinsically}. Intrinsic rewards may depend on the entire history while shaping rewards depend only on the action and previous and current MDP state. A wide variety of pseudo-rewards have been proposed and used successfully with scalable deep RL algorithms in complex environments ~\citep{pathak2017curiosity,berseth2019smirl,hafner2023mastering}. However, designing them is challenging, and they can affect performance in counter-intuitive ways leading to degenerate behaviors~\citep{taiga2021bonus,Clark_Amodei}. For instance, IM that rewards accurate prediction of the next percept may cause an agent to sit forever in front of a blank wall, while favoring "surprise"---states where the next percept is hard to predict---causes an agent to get stuck watching a "noisy TV" that randomly flips channels, rather than encouraging exploration as one might expect \citep{burda2018exploration}. 

These sorts of problems can be avoided by analyzing and designing pseudo-rewards within a theoretical framework for how a rational RL agent {\em should} behave. Such a framework already exists---the Bayes-Adaptive MDP (BAMDP)~\citep{bellman1959adaptive,martin1967bayesian}, a generalization of Bayesian bandits~\cite{gittins1979bandit}. In a BAMDP, the agent starts out not knowing which MDP it is operating in and learns more through observation. BAMDP states consist of the cumulative information observed from the actual MDP, i.e., the entire history of states, actions, and rewards, up to and including the current physical state. An RL algorithm can be viewed as a {\em policy} mapping the BAMDP state to an action that updates it \citep{duff2002optimal}. An optimal RL algorithm maximizes the expected BAMDP return, i.e., the expected return while exploring and learning (note that optimal RL algorithms do not necessarily converge to an optimal policy for the actual MDP~\cite{Bradt+al:1956}). Within this framework, we can understand both reward shaping and IM as \textit{BAMDP reward shaping}, i.e., a function of the action and previous and current BAMDP states. We show that this perspective yields both theoretical and practical implications.

First, we decompose the BAMDP state value into the value of the information collected (VOI) and the value of the physical MDP state under the prior, which we call the value of opportunity (VOO). We show that the base objective of widely-used deep RL algorithms neglects the VOI, explaining the need for many IM terms, e.g., entropy bonus~\cite{szepesvari2010algorithms} or information gain~\citep{houthooft2016vime}, that attempt to add some approximation of VOI back into the objective. These terms can fail to help when the intrinsic reward does not align well enough with VOI---for instance, observing the noisy TV yields high prediction error but no valuable information. Other kinds of pseudo-rewards try to compensate for an implied prior in the initialization of the RL algorithm that misestimates VOO. For example, goal proximity~\citep{colombetti1996robot} and subtask completion rewards~\citep{ng1999policy} compensate for overly pessimistic VOO estimates while surprise minimization~\citep{berseth2019smirl} and information cost~\citep{eysenbach2021robust} compensate for overly optimistic VOO estimates. This yields a new, principled typology of psuedo-rewards based on their roles in correcting different types of misestimated value. 

We can also design pseudo-rewards that never lead to unintended side-effects for sufficiently capable RL algorithms, by extending the potential-based shaping theorem~\citep{ng1999policy} from MDPs to BAMDPs. Potential-based reward shaping functions (PBSFs) take the form $\gamma\phi(s')-\phi(s)$ for any function $\phi$ (see, e.g., Fig. \ref{fig:PBS}). Adding a PBSF to any MDP preserves its optimal policy $\pi^*$. Thus, an RL algorithm that is (say) guaranteed to converge to an optimal policy for MDP $M$ will still converge to an optimal policy for $M$ after PBSF rewards are added. We extend this theory to BAMDPs to show that IM and reward shaping terms that are PBSFs in the BAMDP (BAMPFs)---which may not be valid PBSFs in the underlying MDP (e.g., Fig.~\ref{fig:BAMPF})---always preserve optimality of RL algorithms, while any other form of pseudo-reward can mislead even optimal algorithms. Thus, any process, e.g., meta-RL, that generates increasingly optimal RL algorithms with BAMPF-shaping will also generate increasingly optimal algorithms for the underlying problem.

We finally combine these results to offer guidance on how to design IM and reward shaping functions as optimality-preserving BAMPFs, by leveraging knowledge about the environment to design potential functions that compensate for the RL algorithm's misestimated BAMDP value components.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Markov Decision Processes and Reinforcement Learning Algorithms} \textbf{Markov decision processes} (MDPs) are defined by tuple $M=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},R,T,T_0,\gamma)$ with $\mathcal{S}$ a set of states, $\mathcal{A}$ a set of actions, $T(s'|s,a)$ and $R(r|s,a,s')$ the transition and reward probability functions with expected reward $R(s,a,s')$, $T_0(s_0)$ an initial state distribution, and $\gamma$ a discount factor (when it is not critical, we write $R$ without $s'$ for brevity). MDP policies map from current states to distributions over next actions: $\pi(a | s)$. The return is defined as the discounted sum of rewards $G=\sum_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tr_{t+1}$, and the value and action-value functions under policy $\pi$ are $V^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}_{M,\pi}[\sum_{t\eq 0}^\infty\gamma^tR(s_t,a_t)|s_0\eq s]$ and $Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{M,\pi}[\sum_{t\eq 0}^\infty\gamma^tR(s_t,a_t)|s_0\eq s,a_0\eq a]$ respectively. An optimal policy $\pi^*$ maximizes the expected return and corresponding value functions: $Q^*(s,a)=\max_\pi Q^\pi(s,a)$.

\textbf{Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms}, denoted by $\alg: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{H} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$, where $\mathcal{H}$ is the set of histories $h_t=s_0a_0r_1s_1...a_{t-1}r_ts_t$, learn to maximize return in an MDP by repeatedly updating some kind of internal state (for example, an estimated Q-function) while selecting actions and receiving observations. We measure performance in MDP $M$ by expected return \textit{while learning}: $\mathcal{J}_M(\alg)=\mathbb{E}_{M,\alg}[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t R(s_t,a_t)]$.\footnote{We can convert settings with episodic environments or train/test regimes to infinite-horizon MDPs by augmenting the state space, e.g., with within-episode step indices or train/test indicators.} The objective is to maximize the expected performance in MDPs sampled from prior $\pdist$: $\mathcal{J}(\alg)=\mathbb{E}_{\pdist}[\mathcal{J}_M(\alg)]$ \citep{singh2009rewards}, where $\pdist$ represents the distribution of MDPs that the algorithm will encounter in practice, e.g., MiniGrid mazes~\citep{MinigridMiniworld23} or Atari games.

\subsection{Intrinsic Motivation and Reward Shaping} 
\textit{Intrinsic motivation} (IM) is a method for guiding RL algorithms~\citep{barto2013intrinsic} by adding pseudo-rewards to the original reward at each step, generating composite reward signal: $r'_t= r_t + F(h_t)$, where $F$ denotes the IM function which can depend on the entire history $h_t$ (and thus also the algorithm's internal state or beliefs). \textit{Reward shaping functions} are restricted to the form $F(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})$, resulting in shaped reward function $R'(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})$ (whereas IM does not generally lead to a valid MDP reward function of this form).
The optimal policy for the shaped MDP, i.e. maximizing the composite return, is generally not optimal for the original MDP. \textit{Potential-based shaping functions} (PBSFs) take the form $F(s_t,a_t,s_{t+1})=\gamma\phi(s_{t+1})-\phi(s_t)$ and preserve optimal policies in all MDPs~\citep{ng1999policy}.

\subsection{Formulation of RL Problems as BAMDPs}\label{subsection:definition}
We formulate RL problems as BAMDPs, in which the optimal policy is the Bayes-optimal RL algorithm for the problem. Our conventions are inspired by \citet{zintgraf2019varibad} and \citet{guez2012efficient}. 
For clarity of exposition, we assume all possible MDPs in $p(M)$ share the same $\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\gamma$, so only $R,T,T_0$ are initially uncertain.\footnote{This formulation can be extended to POMDPs and for distributions over $\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\gamma$ without any conceptual changes---the agent receives observations $o_t$, and expectations are calculated over additional variables as needed.}
$\podist$ is the posterior after updating $\pdist$ on the evidence in history $h_t \eq s_0a_0r_1s_1...a_{t-1}r_ts_t$ using Bayes' rule, i.e., $\podist \propto p(h_t|M)\pdist$. We use overlines, e.g., $\Me$, to denote the BAMDP version of any object. 

 A BAMDP is a tuple $\Me=(\Sbar,\mathcal{A},\Rbar,\Tbar,\Tbar_0,\gamma)$ where:
\begin{itemize}%[noitemsep,nolistsep]
\item $\Sbar$ is an augmented state space $\mathcal{S}\times \mathcal{H}$, so $\se=\langle s,h\rangle$. This encapsulates all the information $\alg$ could use when choosing an action---though typically it maintains a lossy memory of $h$.
\item $\mathcal{A}$ and $\gamma$ are shared with the underlying MDPs, although internally $\alg$ may sample its actions from the MDP policy that it learnt: $\pi_t=\alg(\se_t)$, so that $\alg(a|\se_t)=\pi_t(a|s_t)$.
\item $\Rbar(\se_t,a)=\mathbb{E}_{\podist}[R(s_t,a)]$, the expected reward under the current posterior.

\item 
$\Tbar(\se_{t+1} |\se_t,a_t)=\mathbb{E}_{\podist}[T(s_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)R(r_{t+1}|s_t,a_t)\mathbbm{1}[h_{t+1}\eq h_ta_tr_{t+1}s_{t+1}]]$
\item 
$\Tbar_0(\langle s_0,h_0\rangle) = \mathbb{E}_{\pdist}[T_0(s)]\mathbbm{1}[h_0=s_0]$. 
\end{itemize}

We illustrate the basic concepts of BAMDPs using the {\em caterpillar problem} shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:transgraph}. In this problem, $p(M)$ represents how butterflies usually lay eggs on the best food source in the area, but 10\% of the time a more rewarding source is nearby; upon hatching on the weed, the caterpillar must decide whether exploring the neighboring bush is worth the energy and opportunity cost. The bush's reward varying across possible MDPs manifests as stochastic BAMDP dynamics when the caterpillar first observes it (e.g., taking \textit{stay} from the state labelled $\se_A$). After observing the reward, $\podist$ collapses to the underlying $M$ and all dynamics become deterministic (e.g., at the last step in the trajectories labelled A and B it had collapsed to $M_1$ and $M_2$, respectively).

The value of an RL algorithm is its value as a policy in the BAMDP, or, more explicitly, its expected BAMDP return is its expected return while learning in initially unknown MDP $M\sim \pdist$:
\begin{eqnarray*}
    \mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}] &=& 
    \mathbb{E}_{\Tbar_0}\left[\eV^\alg(\se_0)\right]
    =\mathbb{E}_{\Tbar_0,\Tbar}\left[\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^t\Rbar(\se_t,\alg(\se_t))\right]\\
    &=&\mathbb{E}_{\pdist}\left[\mathbb{E}_{M}\left[\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^\infty\gamma^tR(s_t,\alg(\se_t))\right]\right]
    =\mathbb{E}_\pdist\left[\mathcal{J}_M(\alg)\right]=\mathcal{J}(\alg).
\end{eqnarray*}

Thus, the optimal policy for the BAMDP, $\alg^*$ maximizing $\mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}]$, explores optimally for the problem, i.e., it is the Bayes-optimal RL algorithm with respect to prior $p(M)$. 

Since $\mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}]$ is calculated over future beliefs, $\alg^*$ must \textit{plan through its own learning}. E.g., in Fig.~\ref{fig:transgraph} with large enough $\gamma$, the optimal caterpillar would deem $s_b$ worth exploring because it can stay if it finds food (trajectory A), otherwise it will learn that $s_b$ is empty, and use this knowledge to return to $s_w$ and never go back.

Note that, since $\alg^*$ explores only when exploration is expected to increase return, \textbf{exploring enough to find and settle on an optimal MDP policy $\pi^*$ is not generally Bayes-optimal}.
E.g., in Fig.~\ref{fig:transgraph} with small enough $\gamma$, immediate expected reward dominates $\mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}]$, so $\alg^*$ stays at $s_w$ (trajectory C) rather than risking delaying its reward by checking $s_b$.}
Thus, as noted in the introduction, optimal RL algorithms do not necessarily converge to optimal policies for the underlying MDP. Conversely, RL algorithms that {\em do} have this property---which is typically considered an absolute requirement in theoretical RL papers---may well be over-exploring.


\section{Pseudo-rewards Correct BAMDP Value Misestimation}


We first use our framework to explain how IM and reward shaping can help RL algorithms by influencing their value estimates to align with the Bayes-Optimal BAMDP value.

\subsection{The Relationship Between Value Misestimation and Regret}\label{subsection:residual}
RL algorithms typically act, implicitly or explicitly, to maximize a value prediction estimated from the history, which we denote by $\mathsmaller{\hat{\eQ}}(\se_t,a)$. This estimate is rarely equal to the true Bayes-Optimal value $\eQ^*$, because it is generally difficult to initialize the algorithm with the correct prior and intractable to plan through the space of future beliefs. Applying the performance difference lemma~\citep{kakade2002approximately} at the BAMDP level tells us that algorithmic regret is directly related to how these estimates differ from the Bayes-Optimal value over their trajectory:

\begin{lemma}\label{thm:regret_bound}
    The Bayesian regret\citep{ghavamzadeh2015bayesian} of algorithms acting on estimate $\mathsmaller{\hat{\eQ}}(\se_t,a)$ can be expressed as:
    \begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{\alg^*}[\bar{G}]-\mathbb{E}_{\alg}[\bar{G}] = \mathbb{E}_{\alg}\left[\sum\nolimits_t \gamma^t \left(\eV^*(\se_t)-\eQ^*\left(\se_t,\argmax\nolimits_a \mathsmaller{\hat{\eQ}}(\se_t,a)\right)\right)\right],
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
Thus, we can improve RL algorithms by \textit{aligning} their value estimates $\mathsmaller{\hat{\eQ}}$ more with the Bayes-Optimal BAMDP value $\eQ^*$ such that they select higher value actions, even if we cannot calculate $\eQ^*$ itself.

\subsection{BAMDP Value Decomposition}\label{subsection:voivoo}
We can now explain the role of pseudo-rewards as correcting RL algorithms' value estimates towards the optimal BAMDP value. IM terms that reward novel observations or diverse actions encode the value of the information in the BAMDP state $h_t$; these are popular because the value of gaining information is ignored by commonly used RL algorithms (see section \ref{section:1step}). On the other hand, even if the agent would reach a state in which no valuable information had been collected, i.e., $\eV^*(\langle s_{t+1},h_{t+1}\rangle)=\eV^*(\langle s_{t+1},h_0\rangle)$, it could misestimate the value there due to incorrect \textit{initial} beliefs. Many other pseudorewards compensate for this type of misestimated value, typically when there is significant prior knowledge about how to maximize rewards, e.g., when playing football it is advantageous for your team to have possession of the ball. This is helpful to express as a pseudo-reward because it can be difficult to program such prior knowledge into non-tabular (e.g., deep RL) algorithms before they begin learning. We decompose the BAMDP value function into these two values, which we call the Value of Information and Value of Opportunity, respectively.

\begin{definition}[Value of Information]
The Value of Information (VOI) from state $\se_t$ is the increase in $\alg^*$'s expected return from $s_t$ due to the information in $h_t$ compared to its initial beliefs:
\begin{equation}
     \eV^*_I(\langle s_t,h_t\rangle)= \eV^*(\langle s_t,h_t\rangle)-\eV^*(\langle s_t,h_0\rangle).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
E.g, after watching the Noisy TV $h_t$ contains more information than $h_0$, but that information wouldn't help $\alg^*$ get higher return, so $\eV^*_I$ would be zero. Meanwhile, discovering that a maze section contains no reward helps $\alg^*$ focus its search on a smaller remaining area, so $\eV^*_I$ would be positive. 

\begin{definition}[Value of Opportunity]
The Value of Opportunity (VOO) to $\alg$ from state $\se_t$ is the expected optimal value of state $s_t$ without having learnt anything, i.e.:
\begin{equation}
    \eV^*_O(\langle s_t,h_t\rangle)=\eV^*(\langle s_t,h_0\rangle).
\end{equation}
\end{definition}
E.g., if there is always a reward at a known goal state $s_g$, then $s_t$ that are fewer steps from it have higher VOO. But RL agents with misspecified priors may underestimate the value of $s_t$ before first discovering the reward at $s_g$. Conversely, if walking near a cliff edge has a high chance of injury, then $s_t$ there have lower VOO, but RL agents may overestimate their value before the first fall. Attractive or repulsive $\VoO^*$ signal can correct RL algorithms that under or overestimate VOO, respectively.

\begin{lemma}[BAMDP Value Decomposition]\label{theorem_decomposition}
The optimal BAMDP value can be decomposed into the Value of Information and the Value of Opportunity:
 $
   \eV^*(\se_t)=\eV^*_I(\se_t)+\eV^*_0(\se_t).
$
\end{lemma}

We can now categorize IM and reward shaping terms by which of these components they signal (see Table~\ref{table:voivoo}); note that some signal the value itself, and others the change in value from the previous state to the next. We can understand the failure of pseudo-rewards to effectively guide RL algorithms as a result of them aligning insufficiently well with the true BAMDP value. This provides us with some guidance on how to design pseudo-rewards, but we defer that discussion to section~\ref{section:designing_bampfs}.

\subsection{Certainty-Equivalent RL Algorithms Underestimate the Value of Information}\label{section:1step}
Section \ref{subsection:voivoo} showed how we can understand many IM terms as encouraging and directing exploration through encoding the value of the resulting information. This type of IM is so popular because many commonly used RL algorithms under-estimate the value of information; we now introduce a model of these algorithms to formalize this within the BAMDP framework. Many RL algorithms, from policy-based methods like policy gradient \citep{sutton1999policy,schulman2017proximal} to value-based methods like Q-Learning \citep{watkins1989learning,mnih2015human}, avoid the intractability of belief-space planning by acting as if beliefs are fixed. 
We formalize this objective with the \Greedy{}\footnote{The idea of \textit{certainty equivalent} solutions goes back to \citet{simon1956dynamic} and was also described by \citet{duff2002optimal} as the \textit{best reactive policy} with respect to $\alg^\gsup$'s beliefs} 
RL algorithm $\alg^\gsup$.

\begin{definition}[\Greedy{} RL Algorithm]
    At each step, the \Greedy{} Algorithm $\alg^\gsup$ follows the MDP policy maximizing its estimated expected return under current beliefs:\footnote{Note that this is focused on exploitation- other than IM, ad-hoc methods such as epsilon-greedy or Boltzmann exploration may also be used to encourage exploration~\citep{kaelbling1996reinforcement}.}
\begin{equation}\label{greedy_lifelong}
         \alg^\gsup(\langle s_t,h_t \rangle ) \in \argmax\nolimits_\pi \mathbb{E}_{b^\gsup(h_t)}[\tV^\pi(s_t)],
\end{equation}
where $b^\gsup(\cdot)$ denotes how $\alg^\gsup$ interprets its experience, which could be anything from a distribution over world models maintained by updating a prior, to a point estimate of $Q^*$ maintained by training a randomly initialized neural net on batches sampled from $h_t$ \citep{mnih2015human}. For ease of notation we use $\pi_t$ and $a_t$ interchangeably, since $\pi_t$ is only used at step $t$ to output $a_t$. 
\end{definition} 
For example, policy gradient algorithms like Reinforce sample actions from an MDP policy $\pi_\theta$, i.e.,
$
    \alg^{\text{Reinforce}}(\langle s_t,h_t \rangle ) = \pi_\theta(s_t),
$
where $\pi_\theta$ is learnt by gradient updates towards maximizing the expected returns $\mathcal{R}(\tau)$ of the trajectories $\tau$ that it generates, i.e. $J(\theta)=E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[\mathcal{R}(\tau)]$. The algorithm estimates $J(\theta)$ from environment interactions so far, i.e.\ $h_t$,
so $\hat{J}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{b^\gsup(h_t)}[E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[\mathcal{R}(\tau)]]$ 
where $b^\gsup(h_t)$ is concentrated on a point estimate of $J(\theta)$. If, as a model, we assume that policy gradient were to maximize $J(\theta)$ between each interaction, then we find that it matches the behavior of $\alg^\gsup$:
\begin{equation}
    \argmax\nolimits_{\theta}\hat{J}(\theta)=\argmax\nolimits_\theta \mathbb{E}_{b^\gsup(h_t)}[E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[\mathcal{R}(\tau)]]=\argmax\nolimits_\pi \mathbb{E}_{b^\gsup(h_t)}[V^\pi(s)].
\end{equation}

The \Greedy{} algorithm effectively estimates the Q value as:
\begin{equation}
    \qhat(\se_t,a) = \max\nolimits_\pi \mathbb{E}_{b^\gsup(h_t)}[R(s_t,a)+\gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1})].
\end{equation}
Comparing this to the optimal value $ \eQ^*(\se_t,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\podist}[R(s_t,a)+\gamma \eV^*(\langle s_{t+1},h_{t+1}\rangle)]$, we see that, even assuming accurate beliefs $b^c(h_t)=\podist$, the \Greedy{} algorithm still misses value from the ability to learn from the information in $h$, because with $V^\pi(s_{t+1})$ it assumes it would still follow the same $\pi$ from step $t+1$, when in reality it would have updated it with the latest observation. \section{Potential-Based Shaping in the BAMDP}

Section \ref{section:value_correction} explained how IM and reward shaping help suboptimal RL algorithms; however, when following a process to develop increasingly optimal algorithms (either manually as a field, or automatically, e.g., with meta-RL) we also care about what forms of pseudo-reward preserve optimality. To investigate this, we now extend existing theory on MDP potential-based shaping preserving optimal policies, to BAMDP potential-based shaping and optimal RL algorithms. Note that extending the original proofs is non-trivial, because, unlike regular MDPs, rewards influence the BAMDP state.

\begin{definition}(BAMPF)
    Let any $\Sbar,\mathcal{A},\gamma$ be given. Function $\Fshape$ is a \textbf{BAM}DP \textbf{P}otential-Based Shaping \textbf{F}unction if there exists a real-valued function $\phi$ such that for all realizable $h_t$,
    \begin{equation}
    \Fshape(h_t)=\gamma\phi(h_t)-\phi(h_{t-1}),
    \end{equation}
    where $h_{t-1}$ is the first $t-1$ timesteps of $h_t$. See Fig. \ref{fig:BAMPF} for an example of a simple BAMPF based on the size of the set of MDP states visited in $h$. 
\end{definition}

Observe that IM functions $\Fshape(h_t)$ are valid BAMDP shaping functions because they depend on only the BAMDP state, but they are not necessarily BAMPFs. 

\subsection{A Motivating Example}
IM terms based on increase in experience can be valid BAMPFs, e.g. \textit{information gain} \citep{houthooft2016vime} can easily be recast as a BAMPF with $\phi(h)=-H(\hat{p}(T|h))$, i.e., the certainty of the algorithm's posterior belief over the MDP dynamics~$\hat{p}(T)$ after updating on $h$:
$
    \Fshape(h_t) = H(\hat{p}(T|h_{t-1}))-\gamma H(\hat{p}(T|h_t))
$
Meanwhile, \textit{prediction error}  \citep{pathak2017curiosity,burda2018exploration} based on the error in predicting features of the latest state given the experiences so far, i.e., $\Fshape(h_t)=||\hat{f}(s_t|h_{t-1})-f(s_t)||^2$, is not a valid BAMPF, because it cannot be expressed as a difference between potentials at successive histories.

Note that both types of IM can \textit{sometimes} be MDP potential-based shaping, e.g, there may eventually be enough information in $h$ that they become constant, making them trivial PBSFs in future episodes.\footnote{In fact, it is common to decay IM to 0 during training to avoid affecting the optimal policy at convergence.} But even if they do converge, we have no guarantees about how long that would take or how catastrophically they might impact the RL agent's behavior before convergence. 

BAMPF theory goes beyond PBS theory; Theorem \ref{thm:maintheorem} tells us that we can always use information gain to guide our RL algorithms while preserving optimal behavior, whereas prediction error can distract even optimal algorithms from maximizing the true return. E.g., in the Noisy TV problem, even Bayes-optimal algorithms would watch TV forever if the shaped return from unpredictably flipping channels was higher than that from exploring the more predictable corridors of the maze to reach the extrinsic reward. Concretely, if we use BAMPF pseudo-rewards then:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,nolistsep]
    \item As we develop increasingly Bayes-optimal RL algorithms, they will also be increasingly optimal with respect to the true rewards.
    \item Lifelong learning agents that learn to act more Bayes-optimally for the composite rewards over time will also act more Bayes-optimally for the underlying environment.
    \item We could shape the objective of meta-RL systems to help them discover better RL algorithms more quickly, without making them eventually converge to suboptimal algorithms.\footnote{In this case, the learnt $\alg$ would not even need to observe the pseudo-rewards itself, e.g., information gain can encourage a metalearner to generate $\alg$ that inherently take more information-gathering actions, since the metalearner observes the shaped return and updates $\alg$ to maximize it.}
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Bayes-Optimal RL Algorithms are Invariant to BAMPFs}
We model the effect of pseudo-reward function $\Fshape(h_t)$ as producing \textit{shaped BAMDP} $\Me'$ with shaped reward $\Rbar'(\se_t,a,\se_{t+1})=\Rbar(\se_t,a)+\Fshape(h_{t+1})$, while $\Sbar$ and $\Tbar$ are unchanged, i.e., $h_t$ still only contains the underlying rewards. This reflects the fact that $\Fshape(h_t)$ is fully known from the start, so it should not be treated as information or influence the posterior. We model the pseudo-rewards as being added inside the RL algorithm, rather than entering through $h_t$ in $\se_t$. An optimal algorithm for $\Me'$ maximizes the expected shaped return from $\Rbar'$, i.e., $\alg^{*\prime}=\argmax_\alg \mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}']$.

We now state our main theorem that BAMPFs always preserve Bayes-Optimality. 
\begin{restatable}[BAMDP Potential-Based Shaping Theorem]{theorem}{fta}\label{thm:maintheorem}
    For a pseudo-reward function to guarantee that the optimal algorithm for any shaped BAMDP is optimal for the original BAMDP, i.e., Bayes-optimal for the underlying RL problem, it is necessary and sufficient for it to be a BAMPF.
\end{restatable}
\begin{proofsketch}
     For sufficiency, we show that the $\phi$ form a telescoping sum, reducing to a constant. For necessity, we construct a BAMDP such that when $\Fshape-(\gamma\phi'-\phi)$ is nonzero, different actions maximize the composite and extrinsic returns.  See \ref{subsection:pbsproofs} for full proofs (note that extending the original proofs is non-trivial, because unlike regular MDPs, rewards are part of the BAMDP state).
\end{proofsketch}
Thus, if we develop RL algorithms that are optimal with BAMPF shaping, they will still maximize the true, underlying expected return. 
Meanwhile, if we use IM that is not a BAMPF, there will be settings where  $\alg^{*\prime}$ behaves suboptimally for the underlying problem. 

\subsection{Near Optimal Algorithms are Nearly Invariant to BAMPFs}
Bayes-optimality is generally intractable, but we can extend our results to approximate algorithms. 

\begin{restatable}{corollary}{eps}
\label{thm:epsilon}
     With BAMPF shaping, a near-optimal algorithm for $\Me'$ will also be near-optimal when applied to $\Me$, i.e.,
     $
        \mathbb{E}_{\alg^{*\prime}}[\bar{G'}]-\mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G'}] < \epsilon \iff \mathbb{E}_{\alg^*}[\bar{G}]-\mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}] < \epsilon.
    $
\end{restatable}
\begin{proofsketch}
    Because BAMPF shaping shifts BAMDP returns by a constant, lower shaped return $\mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G'}]$ corresponds directly to $\mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}]$ decreasing by the same amount. See \ref{proof:eps} for the full proof.
\end{proofsketch}

Another form of approximate optimality is the maximization of return over a finite horizon $k$. We can also upper-bound the regret as a function of $k$.

\begin{definition}[k-Step learning-aware]
We define an RL algorithm as k-step learning-aware for a BAMDP if it maximizes the expected k-step return in it:
\begin{equation}
    \alg_k^{*} \in \argmax_\alg \mathbb{E}_{\Tbar_0,\Tbar}\left[\sum\nolimits_{t=0}^k\gamma^t\Rbar(\se_t,\alg(\se_t))\right]. %= \argmax_\alg \mathbb{E}_\alg[\bar{G}_k]  
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\begin{restatable}{corollary}{ks}\label{thm:kstep}
If $\Fshape$ is a BAMPF with potential function of maximum magnitude $\phi_{\max}$, and the extrinsic reward has maximum magnitude $R_{\max}$, then the k-step learning-aware algorithm for the shaped BAMDP, $\alg_k^{*\prime}$, has regret bounded by $2\gamma^k(\phi_{\max}+R_{\max}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma})$ in the underlying BAMDP, i.e.,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{\alg^*}[\bar{G}]-\mathbb{E}_{\alg_k^{*\prime}}[\bar{G}] \leq 2\gamma^k\left(\phi_{\max}+R_{\max}\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right).
\end{equation}
\end{restatable}
\begin{proofsketch}
    The telescoping $\phi$ summed over horizon $k$ leaves a trailing term of $\gamma^k\phi_k$ from the last step, which allows us to bound $\alg_k^{*\prime}$'s k-step regret compared to $\alg_k^*$ in terms of $\phi_\max$. The regret of $\alg_k^*$ compared to the fully Bayes-optimal algorithm $\alg^*$ is bounded by the worst-case regret after step $k$, giving us a bound in terms of $R_\max$. See \ref{lemma:kstep} for the full proof. 
\end{proofsketch}
\subsection{Designing Intrinsic Motivation and Reward Shaping as BAMPFS}\label{section:designing_bampfs}
We now synthesize the results from the previous sections to provide practical guidance for designing effective pseudo-rewards as BAMPFs. We showed in section~\ref{subsection:voivoo} that pseudo-rewards can help by compensating for components of BAMDP value that the RL agent misestimates; to achieve this in the form of a BAMPF, simply expresswrite the missing value as a potential function on the BAMDP state $\phi(h_t)$. A BAMPF based on those potentials encourages suboptimal RL agents to go to higher potential BAMDP states, while preserving the performance of near-optimal agents. 

It is also straightforward to convert existing pseudoreward terms to BAMPFs. E.g., to convert VOI terms $\phi(h_t)$ should capture the total expected value of information in $h_t$; e.g., \textit{Entropy bonus} expresses the value in the information from trying a wide spread of actions from each state, which can be converted to $\phi(h_t)=H(a|s)$ with $H$ calculated from the frequencies in $h_t$. For VOO terms, $\phi(h_t)$ should capture the agent's missing prior knowledge of the value at $s_t$ encoded by the pseudo-reward; e.g., \textit{Negative Surprise} corrects algorithms lacking the prior knowledge that unpredictable states have lower value because the environment is dangerous, so $\phi(h_t)$ could be $-||\hat{f}(s_t|h_{t-1})-f(s_t)||^2$, or \textit{Subgoal Reaching} signals the underestimated value of completing subgoals necessary for reaching the final reward, with $\phi(h_t)$ equal to the number of subgoals completed at $s_t$. 


\section{Related Work}


\textbf{Theory of Intrinsic Motivation} \citet{oudeyer2007intrinsic} categorize
IM as knowledge-based, competence-based or morphological, and evaluate them by their exploration (leading to exploratory and investigative behavior) and organization (leading to structured and organized behavior) potentials, which are similar to the effects of signalling $\VoO$ and $\VoI$.
\citet{singh2009rewards} propose an evolutionary framework for rewards as maximizing expected fitness over a distribution of environments, concluding that optimal IM depends on regularities across the distribution and properties of the learning agent.  
\citet{aubret2023information} propose an information-theoretic taxonomy of IM, but this only captures IM terms signalling value of information, and they do not consider the prior distribution and so cannot explain how IM should be designed for a given domain. 

\textbf{Extensions of Potential-Based Shaping} \citet{devlin2012dynamic} and \citet{forbes2024potential} extend PBS theory to potential functions that vary over time, including functions of the history, but this is still at the level of the optimal MDP policy rather than the optimal RL algorithm.
\citet{eck2016potential} extend PBSFs to POMDP planning, defining potential functions over POMDP belief states
and categorizing them as Domain-dependent and Domain-independent, which share similarities to $\VoO$ and $\VoI$. \citet{kim2015reward} propose BAMDP potential-based shaping specifically for a model-based Bayesian RL method, although they do not prove that the PBS theorem still holds for BAMDPs (which is non-trivial, because unlike regular MDPs, the rewards influence the BAMDP state) and do not consider the broader implications for developing IM for any RL algorithm.

\textbf{Value of Information} The classical notion of the value of information originates in decision theory \citep{howard1966information}. Early work in metareasoning for tree search considers the utility of the information resulting from a computation~\citep{russell1989optimal,russell1991principles}. \citet{dearden1998bayesian} first applied the concept to RL, approximating it to select Bayes-optimal actions.
\citet{chalkiadakis2003coordination} viewed BAMDP Q values as
including the value of information defined as the value of the change in beliefs quantified by its impact on future decisions, but they do not derive an expression for it.




\section{Limitations}

Although the \Greedy{} algorithm is a model for many widely used RL algorithms, particularly in deep RL, to which intrinsic motivation is often applied, we do not formally analyze how other types of RL algorithms misestimate value in the BAMDP. The \Greedy{} algorithm also only models the high-level objective that these algorithms optimize, and does not capture effects that may arise at the implementation level. (Note that our BAMDP potential-based shaping result holds for any type of RL algorithm.) Moreover, the optimal BAMDP value, and the two components we decompose it into are, in general, intractible. Thus, we only provide general principles for designing IM using assumptions about the problem to signal these components, rather than computing the optimal IM exactly. Our BAMPF results only guarantee that BAMPF IM won't significantly mislead approximately optimal algorithms, but cannot guarantee it will never cause bad behaviors in highly suboptimal algorithms. But if exact computation of the optimal fool-proof IM for any algorithm were possible, then we would also be able to compute the Bayes-optimal algorithm directly and IM would be unnecessary.


\section{Discussion}

By formulating RL problems as BAMDPs, we can formalize how intrinsic motivation and reward shaping guide RL algorithms by correcting their misestimated BAMDP value towards the Bayes-optimal value, which we decompose into the value of information and the value of opportunity. This allows us to characterize the roles of many existing IM and reward shaping terms and explain their failure modes, providing principles for tailoring pseudo-rewards to the properties of the environments and algorithms they're applied to. We also extend potential-based shaping theory to the BAMDP level to show that pseudo-rewards in the form of BAMDP potential-based shaping functions always preserves the optimality of RL algorithms, while non-BAMPF pseudo-rewards can mislead even optimal or near-optimal algorithms, which has implications whenever we use them to kick-start meta-RL, lifelong learning, or any process where we develop increasingly optimal RL algorithms. We finally describe how to design helpful BAMPFs based on potential functions that measure the misestimated components of BAMDP value: if the agent is not collecting sufficient information, $\phi$ should compensate for VOI underestimation, and if it is not initialized with important prior knowledge, $\phi$ should correct for the misestimated VOO. We hope this framework will aid in the design of useful and reliable pseudo-reward terms; we are particularly excited about the prospect of our results inspiring the use of BAMPFs for guiding meta-RL and lifelong RL while preserving optimality.
