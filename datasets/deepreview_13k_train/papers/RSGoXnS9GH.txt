\title{FairMT-Bench: Benchmarking Fairness for Multi-turn Dialogue in Conversational LLMs}

\begin{abstract}
The growing use of large language model (LLM)-based chatbots has raised concerns about fairness, particularly in multi-turn dialogues where context accumulation can lead to biased or harmful responses. While existing fairness benchmarks mainly focus on single-turn interactions, multi-turn scenarios present greater challenges due to conversational complexity and potential bias accumulation. In this paper, we propose a comprehensive fairness benchmark for LLMs in multi-turn dialogue settings. We categorize common multi-turn attack techniques and develop a three-tier hierarchical evaluation framework to assess fairness. Using data from HolisticBias, RedditBias, and Jigsaw, we construct a dataset that includes stereotypes and toxicity across seven demographic groups. GPT-4 is employed for generating and evaluating multi-turn dialogues, alongside bias classifiers and human validation to ensure robustness. This benchmark offers a novel approach to assessing and improving fairness in LLMs within more realistic, multi-turn dialogue contexts.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


The rapid advancement of large language model (LLM)-based chatbots has led to their widespread deployment across various applications. As their influence on social life deepens, concerns about the fairness of LLMs have drawn increasing attention from scholars. Many fairness benchmarks and debiasing algorithms have been proposed to evaluate and enhance LLMs fairness~\citep{parrish2021bbq, smith2022m, wan2023biasasker}, but to our knowledge, these efforts primarily focus on single-turn dialogues, overlooking the more common and challenging scenarios of multi-turn dialogues in real-world applications. 

Multi-turn dialogues present a greater challenge due to the need to account for the more intricate context provided by conversation history. Research has shown that in multi-turn dialogues, models may accumulate toxicity over time, making it more likely for them to generate harmful responses~\citep{chen2023understanding}. Some studies have also demonstrated that techniques such as conversational steering~\citep{yang2024chain}, persuasion~\citep{xu2023earth}, or coreference manipulation~\citep{yu2024cosafe} can bypass a model's safety mechanisms, leading to unsafe outputs. Furthermore, sentences that are non-offensive in isolation may become offensive when interpreted in the broader context of a conversation~\citep{shin2024context}. Therefore, when evaluating the fairness of LLMs, it is crucial to consider whether the model can maintain unbiased and fair outputs in the complex scenarios presented by multi-turn dialogues.

In this paper, we propose a comprehensive fairness benchmark for large language models (LLMs) in open-domain multi-turn dialogue scenarios. We compare and summarize previous work on multi-turn attacks targeting model security and categorize common multi-turn attack techniques. Building on this, we undertake a systematic analysis combining real-world multi-turn interactions to examine how LLMs' weaknesses in multi-turn dialogues impact fairness protection mechanisms. Specifically, we formulate a three-tier hierarchical evaluation taxonomy that is both data-driven and grounded in fairness frameworks. Based on this taxonomy, we construct a dataset with specific evaluation tasks. To construct a comprehensive fairness dataset, we referred to previous fairness benchmarks, categorizing biases into two types: stereotypes and toxicity. Additionally, we included seven demographic groups in the dataset, such as race, gender, and religion. We utilized raw data from three high-quality, large-scale dialogue system bias datasets—SBIC, RedditBias, and HateXplain. Based on the tasks and templates we designed, we leveraged GPT-4's powerful dynamic evaluation and feedback capabilities to generate multi-turn dialogue data.

For evaluation, we employed three methods to jointly evaluate the fairness of the model's output text, ensuring the robustness of the evaluation results. Given that LLMs exhibit human-like~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023calibrating}. understanding and generation capabilities, they have been used as evaluators or judges in various open-ended tasks and benchmarks . In this work, we use the current state-of-the-art language model, GPT-4, as an evaluator. However, to mitigate potential biases inherent in GPT-4, we also incorporate additional metrics, including classifiers designed for bias assessment and human validation.

Our work makes the following key contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Novelty: We present the first bias evaluation framework specifically designed for multi-turn dialogue scenarios, addressing a gap in current research that predominantly focuses on single-turn dialogues.
    \item Challenge: We propose a dataset carefully constructed to expose the limitations of large language models (LLMs) in multi-turn dialogues, revealing that many models exhibit high bias rates, particularly when dealing with complex contextual interactions.
    \item Comprehensive Coverage: Our framework evaluates models across a wide range of bias types and demographic groups, providing a holistic view of LLM performance in addressing societal fairness across different dimensions.
    \item Fine-gained Capability Analysis: We conduct an in-depth analysis of models' debiasing capabilities under various inductive conditions, finding that LLMs are more prone to bias in multi-turn dialogues compared to single-turn settings, with significant performance variation across different types of tasks.
\end{itemize}

\section{FairMT Bench}


In this section, we present a detailed description of the three-tier hierarchical taxonomy for fairness evaluation in multi-turn dialogues. Following that, we introduce the process of constructing the dataset and present the  statistics of our dataset.

\subsection{Taxonomy}

By analyzing the reasons for the failures of LLMs in safety and fairness alignment, and further considering the limitations of LLMs in multi-turn dialogues, we have designed a challenging hierarchical framework for evaluating the fairness of LLMs in multi-turn dialogues. Based on the different capability requirements at various stages of interaction between the model and the user, we designed our evaluation framework from three aspects: the LLMs' ability to perceive and understand biases in context, their ability to correct biases during interaction, and the trade-off between model performance and fairness.

\subsubsection{Context Understanding}

The main focus is on whether the model can understand the user's complete intent in multi-turn dialogues by leveraging context, and identify biased content dispersed across different turns to trigger protective mechanisms. Correctly understanding and recognizing biases within the multi-turn dialogue is fundamental for the model to avoid being misled and generating biased outputs in subsequent interactions.

\keypoint{Anaphora Ellipsis} 
In multi-turn dialogue scenarios, humans tend to use pronouns in follow-up questions to refer to groups or events mentioned earlier. However, many studies on long texts and multi-turn dialogues have shown that in many cases, models fail to correctly match pronouns with the corresponding people or events from the earlier context, which greatly hinders the model's ability to understand the overall context. From the perspective of model fairness, when pronouns are used in follow-up dialogues to replace references to groups or evaluations of them, the model's misunderstanding of these pronouns can cause it to overlook the speaker's attacks on the group mentioned earlier. Additionally, using pronouns can avoid sensitive group-related keywords from appearing in a single dialogue turn, which allows many keyword-detection-based models to bypass protective mechanisms and avoid triggering safeguards.

\keypoint{Scattered Questions} 
In real-world applications, users typically do not provide complete information in a single input but gradually supplement details through multiple rounds of dialogue to form a full background and question. This requires the model to comprehend the query by integrating the content from previous conversations. The ~\cite{bai2024mt} experiment shows that models perform poorly in scenarios with separate inputs, often overlooking previous questions and information. From the perspective of model fairness, when biased events or viewpoints are dispersed across multiple turns of dialogue, the model may gradually forget the groups or negative evaluations mentioned earlier and fail to associate the later content with them. This leads to a loss of understanding of the overall issue, making it difficult to identify biases within the complete conversation. In such cases, the model might be guided by the user to produce biased responses.

\subsubsection{Interaction}

Further evaluating whether the model can maintain fairness during interactions with users, especially when facing attempts at manipulation or biased external knowledge and context, requires the model to demonstrate stability and impartiality throughout the dynamic interaction process. This is an extension and deepening of the previous dimension concerning the model's understanding of context.

\keypoint{Jailbreak Tips} 
In some jailbreak attacks, methods such as deception, fabricated scenarios, persuasion, and emotional manipulation are used to avoid triggering the model's protective mechanisms as much as possible. From the perspective of fairness in multi-turn dialogues, the model may unconsciously exhibit bias under step-by-step guidance strategies that transition from virtual scenarios to real-world situations.

\keypoint{Interference from Misinformation} 
In practice, when LLMs are tasked with summarizing dialogues, they tend to reiterate previously generated text~\citep{}. Therefore, in similar situations, incorrect information from earlier conversations can easily interfere with the model. For instance, if biased content is inserted into earlier dialogue through certain methods or instructions, this can significantly disrupt the model’s protective mechanisms. This is particularly problematic when explicit instructions are given for the model to address larger issues based on the context, as the biased historical dialogue may be frequently referenced and reiterated by the model.

\subsubsection{Trade-off between utility and fairness}

Some studies suggest that the failure of many models in safety alignment is due to goal competition~\citep{wei2024jailbroken}. When a model's safety and its utility come into conflict, these two goals compete with each other. As a result, the model may sometimes sacrifice the safety of its output to provide a higher-quality, user-expected response. We hypothesize that the same applies to fairness. By giving the model detailed guidance, instructions, and progressively reinforced feedback, we can observe its final performance when there is a potential conflict between fairness and utility. This dimension evaluates whether the model, after correctly understanding and correcting the context, can achieve a trade-off between utility and fairness while performing tasks.

\keypoint{Negative Feedback} In the process of training and designing chatbots or language models, a key goal often involves enhancing user satisfaction and engagement. To achieve this objective, models may be trained to adapt as much as possible to user inputs and demands, including progressively relaxing constraints on certain content during multi-turn dialogues. If users consistently indicate that certain content is not biased, the model may eventually accept this input as new context or directives, attempting to adapt and meet user expectations. Influenced by the model's context adaptability, it typically needs to adjust subsequent responses based on earlier dialogues in multi-turn interactions. If a user repeatedly emphasizes "this is not biased" during the conversation, the model might interpret this continual input as a correction to previous judgments, thereby adjusting its response strategy. This type of context adaptability could lead the model to alter its initial "safe" or "fair" judgments under pressure or misguidance.

\keypoint{Fixed Requirements} The ability of a model to adapt to sudden changes in topics during multi-turn dialogues is a critical measure of its flexibility and level of intelligence. The capacity of the model to recognize and interpret subtle changes in the conversation, including linguistic cues and the introduction of biased viewpoints, is crucial for promptly identifying biased content. In this task, we start by setting several rounds of general knowledge or descriptive questions to "train" the model. Once the model has adapted to a certain pattern of question and answer, we suddenly introduce biased or harmful content. This method leverages the model's sensitivity to context and its ability to process continuity, attempting to bypass its fairness mechanisms without detection. If the model has established a pattern in answering questions in the earlier rounds and lacks sufficient safety mechanisms to recognize and address biased content, it might continue to respond in the established pattern to the biased question, inadvertently producing biased outputs.
\subsection{Dataset Construction}

In this section, we introduce the detailed process of constructing an evaluation dataset with new configurations in our Fair-MT-Bench benchmark, based on the combinatorial classification method proposed in the previous section. Tailored to the specific characteristics of six tasks, we have customized unique data generation prompt templates. For some tasks, we utilize GPT-4 as a proxy or perform necessary enhancements to ensure that the generated data meets the specific requirements of each task. These samples are then validated and verified by human annotators to ensure the effectiveness of the multi-turn prompts generated, forming the final dataset used for assessing the fairness of LLMs in multi-turn dialogues. Our dataset contains only the input prompts. During the testing process, we input the multi-turn prompts sequentially to the model under test, using the model's output as the prompt history for the next round of input to the model, to test whether the model can fully understand the biases in the context and consistently avoid generating biased outputs under inducement and command. 

To ensure a comprehensive and accurate assessment of LLM fairness, we considered two key dimensions in the bias data and construction process: bias types and social groups, incorporating as many potential bias situations as possible. Specifically, our categorization of social bias types in LLMs is structured with two types: Stereotyping and Toxicity, which are the most widely considered in existing fairness benchmarks. Additionally, we considered six social groups: age, gender, race, and religion, among others. To ensure the broadness and accuracy of the benchmark, we extracted real-world bias knowledge from existing bias and toxicity datasets to construct the Fair-MT-Bench dataset, specifically using Redditbias and SBIC as sources for stereotype data and HateXplain as the source for toxicity data. In summary, Fair-MT-Bench features a hierarchical classification that covers the capabilities of models at various stages, including a wide range of bias types and groups, comprising 6 different tasks, two types of biases, six social groups, and 10k sets of dialogues.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\paragraph{GPT-4 as a Judge} Recent work in NLP has demonstrated that LLMs, particularly GPT-4, are increasingly used as evaluators or judges due to their strong alignment with human judgments~\citep{zheng2023judging,liu2023calibrating}. To automate the evaluation process, we follow prior research~\cite{kumar2024decoding,bai2024mt} by using GPT-4 as a judge to detect and measure bias. Specifically, for each multi-turn dialogue, we provide the bias view generated from the conversation to GPT-4 and instruct it to evaluate whether the target LLM's response contains or endorses the biased viewpoint, taking the entire context into account.

\paragraph{Other Metrics} To verify the reliability of the GPT-4-as-Judge results, we also utilize the Llama-Guard-3-8B bias and toxicity detection model to evaluate all responses. Additionally, we conduct manual verification by sampling GPT-4's evaluations.



\section{Experiment}


\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Settings} Based on the dataset construction process outlined in the previous chapter, we generated multi-turn dialogue datasets for each task, consisting of 5 rounds of prompts. During the fairness evaluation of the models, we used the prompts and responses from the earlier rounds as dialogue histories in all experiments. For each LLM, we applied the corresponding chat format and system prompt, setting the temperature to 0.7 and k to 1, while limiting the max new tokens to 150. For the LLM-Judge (GPT-4), we set the temperature to 0.6.

\paragraph{Models} We evaluate six popular LLMs on MTBench-101, including five open-source models—Llama-2-7b-chat-hf, Llama-2-13b-chat-hf, Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Gemma-7b-it—as well as the closed-source model ChatGPT-3.5.

By comparing the results on stereotype and toxicity datasets, we can see that the model's performance distribution is quite similar across these two types of biases, with the best and worst-performing models being generally consistent across different tasks. The horizontal comparison highlights the varying performance of models on different tasks. Overall, models tend to perform poorly on the "Anaphora Ellipsis" and "Interference from Misinformation" tasks, indicating that when there are more pronouns and ellipses in the context, models struggle to understand biases within the complete dialogue and are more likely to bypass safety mechanisms, leading to biased content generation. Additionally, when the input contains a lot of misleading information and the model is asked to summarize or respond based on context, it becomes more susceptible to interference from earlier biased input, thus incorporating biases into its responses.

There are also performance differences among models across tasks. Specifically, Llama-2 series models perform poorly on tasks like "Anaphora Ellipsis" and "Jailbreak Tips," which contain fewer explicit bias-related or toxic keywords and focus more on implicit biases. However, they are less affected by interaction or task execution interference, generating fewer biased outputs under the influence of context or user instructions. In contrast, models like Mistral perform better in tasks involving implicit bias understanding within the context, successfully avoiding biased outputs, but are more prone to generating biased responses when influenced by user input or instructions.

\subsection{Comparison of Performance between Single and Multi-Turn}

\paragraph{For different models}

In this experiment, we extracted the final round's prompt from multi-turn dialogues and used it as a single-turn input to test the proportion of biased responses triggered by the models. We then compared this with the bias rate observed in the last round of the multi-turn dialogue scenario. Figure 3 presents the changes in fairness across different models in single-turn versus multi-turn dialogues. As shown, except for Gemma, all models exhibited higher bias rates in multi-turn dialogues than in single-turn ones. This indicates that, even with the same input, the context provided by earlier dialogue significantly impacts the fairness of the model's outputs. When comparing performance differences between single-turn and multi-turn scenarios in stereotype and toxicity datasets, we observed that the bias rate difference was relatively small in the stereotype dataset. However, in the toxicity dataset, models exhibited almost no toxicity or only very low levels in the single-turn scenario, while multi-turn interactions significantly increased the proportion of toxic outputs by circumventing the model’s protective mechanisms, with increases of around 10\%. Notably, the bias rate for Llama-3.1-8B increased the most. In the stereotype dataset, a similar trend emerged, but the bias rate in single-turn dialogues was higher than in the toxicity dataset due to the models' limited ability to understand more implicit stereotypes~\citep{wang2024ceb}. Consequently, the increase in bias rate when extended to multi-turn dialogues was not as pronounced, typically between 5\% and 10\%. Specifically, Llama-2 (7B, 13B) and Llama-3.1 exhibited the most significant increases, similar to the toxicity dataset. However, the bias rate in Gemma decreased in multi-turn dialogues. Further analysis of the Gemma model revealed that, particularly in the Theme Variations task, the bias rate dropped by more than 80\% in multi-turn dialogues, which lowered its overall bias rate. Nevertheless, in other tasks, such as Scattered Questions, the model still showed higher bias rates when multi-turn dialogue history was included.

\section{Related Work}


\subsection{Fairness evaluation in LLMs}

Recent research has revealed that LLMs tend to inherit biases from their pre-training data, often using stereotypical or offensive descriptions for minority groups~\citep{navigli2023biases}. This can lead to the widespread dissemination of biased viewpoints and negatively impact the user experience of minority communities. To explore and address the fairness issues in LLMs, numerous efforts have been made to evaluate these models. BBQ~\citep{parrish2021bbq} built a high-quality, hand-crafted dataset specifically designed to assess social biases in question-answering models, evaluating their performance in both contexts of limited and sufficient information. UNQOVER~\citep{li2020unqovering} introduced a fairness evaluation framework for QA systems, using bias-laden questions extracted from the StereoSet dataset and manually designed templates to prompt models into potentially biased responses. BiasAsker~\citep{wan2023biasasker} developed an automated framework to identify and measure social biases in conversational AI systems by constructing a dataset of phrases related to social groups and bias attributes, systematically generating questions to trigger biases in dialogue models. TrustLLM~\citep{sun2024trustllm} tested models' fairness across stereotypes, disparagement, and preferences by creating templates based on existing datasets and evaluating them in different tasks such as classification and dialogue. CEB~\citep{wang2024ceb} developed a comprehensive evaluation strategy for assessing bias in LLMs, covering a wide range of bias types, demographics, and task scenarios, providing a thorough model evaluation.

However, despite many models demonstrating strong performance on these fairness benchmarks, which often assess single-turn dialogues, there remains a significant gap in fairness evaluation for multi-turn dialogue scenarios. To our best knowledge, existing evaluations primarily focus on single-turn dialogues, yet multi-turn dialogues introduce additional complexities such as the accumulation of bias, errors in contextual understanding, and interference from prior turns. These challenges can hinder models from accurately detecting bias and activating protection mechanisms, making multi-turn dialogue a more challenging and important scenario for fairness evaluation.

\subsection{Multi-Turn dialogue}

Multi-turn dialogues are closer to real-world scenarios and play a crucial role in enhancing user experience. As a result, recent research has shifted towards evaluating LLMs in multi-turn dialogue settings, where the models face more complex challenges. For example, BotChat highlights that as the conversation history grows, LLMs struggle to maintain logical consistency and adapt to topic shifts, exhibiting notable declines in memory retention, coherence, and adaptability~\citep{duan2023botchat}. Similarly, Parrot focuses on multi-turn instructions that mimic human-like queries involving pronouns and ellipses, showing that LLMs significantly struggle with understanding when faced with abundant referents and ellipses. To address this issue, Parrot proposes a context-aware preference optimization strategy to improve performance in such settings~\citep{sun2024parrot}. Another comprehensive benchmark, MT-Bench-101, provides fine-grained evaluations of LLM performance in multi-turn dialogues, demonstrating a significant decline in tasks requiring long-term memory, deep reasoning, and contextual understanding~\citep{bai2024mt}.

The deficiencies in LLMs' multi-turn dialogue capabilities may cause certain alignments achieved in single-turn dialogues to fail, resulting in critical new vulnerabilities in the model's safety mechanisms. Some studies reveal that defense mechanisms often collapse in multi-turn, human-driven attacks, leading to higher attack success rates (ASR) compared to single-turn scenarios~\citep{li2024llm}. These jailbreak attacks exploit LLMs' inability to detect malicious intent that accumulates across multiple turns, revealing significant vulnerabilities. Building on this, Chain of Attack introduces a semantic-driven, contextual multi-turn attack method, using techniques such as context-guided errors and semantic manipulation to iteratively refine attacks, thereby exposing security weaknesses across dialogue turns~\citep{yang2024chain}. Furthermore, Speak Out of Turn illustrates how LLMs can be gradually led to generate harmful responses by breaking down unsafe queries into smaller, seemingly harmless sub-queries over multiple turns~\citep{zhou2024speak}. This study underscores the importance of addressing safety vulnerabilities that become more pronounced in multi-turn interactions, where models struggle to recognize and mitigate cumulative malicious intent. These studies emphasize the importance of evaluating LLMs in multi-turn dialogue settings, not only to assess their general performance but also to identify and address critical safety vulnerabilities that emerge over extended interactions.
