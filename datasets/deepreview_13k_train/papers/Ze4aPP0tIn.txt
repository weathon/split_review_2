\title{Step-by-Step Reasoning for Math Problems  via Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo}

\begin{abstract}

Augmenting the multi-step reasoning abilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been a persistent challenge. Recently, verification has shown promise in improving solution consistency by evaluating generated outputs. However, current verification approaches suffer from sampling inefficiencies, requiring a large number of samples to achieve satisfactory performance. Additionally, training an effective verifier often depends on extensive process supervision, which is costly to acquire. In this paper, we address these limitations by introducing a novel verification method based on Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC). TSMC sequentially refines its sampling effort to focus exploration on promising candidates, resulting in more efficient generation of high-quality solutions. We apply TSMC to LLMs by estimating the expected future rewards at partial solutions. This approach results in a more straightforward training target that eliminates the need for step-wise human annotations. We empirically demonstrate the advantages of our method across multiple math benchmarks, and also validate our theoretical analysis of both our approach and existing verification methods.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


In recent years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved significant breakthroughs across various domains \citep{park2023generative, kaddour2023challenges, song2306restgpt, li2023m, wang2023voyager, chen2023towards, zheng2023judging, wang2023large}. However, their performance in multi-step reasoning tasks, such as solving complex mathematical or coding problems, remains notably constrained \citep{lightman2024lets, huang2023large}. A key challenge arises from the high sensitivity of these tasks to individual errors at each step of reasoning. Autoregressive LLMs, in particular, struggle with maintaining consistency throughout the reasoning process, leading to solutions that are prone to mistakes or logical inconsistencies \citep{shen-etal-2021-generate-rank, cobbe2021training}.



Verification \citep{cobbe2021training, uesato2022solving, lightman2024lets} has emerged as an effective strategy to mitigate these issues. In a typical verification process, multiple solutions  are sampled from the generator (the LLM), and an external verifier evaluates each of these solutions. The verification outcomes are then used to adjust the weight of each solution in determining the final answer. Since verification is generally simpler than  generation, it tends to achieve higher accuracy and consistency compared to the methods that rely solely on the generator, such as majority voting \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}. There are two primary types of verifiers: the Outcome Reward Model (ORM) \citep{cobbe2021training} and the Process Reward Model (PRM) \citep{uesato2022solving}. The ORM evaluates the fully generated solution with a single scalar output representing the confidence score, and its training is straightforward, using outcome supervision based on comparing generated answers with ground truth. In contrast, the PRM focuses on providing rewards at each step of the reasoning process, giving more detailed feedback on the intermediate steps. Although empirical evidence suggests that PRM outperforms  ORM \citep{lightman2024lets}, there exists no simple metric in evaluating the correctness of each step and efficiently collecting process supervision for such intermediate steps remains a great challenge.
Despite being promising, existing verification methods are still limited in the following two areas:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
 \item \textbf{(Problem I) Low sampling efficiency:}  Current verification methods only evaluate fully generated solutions, without refining their quality during the generation process. Sampling efforts would  be wasted on partial solutions that are clearly incorrect. As a result, a large number of samples are needed to obtain even one correct solution, making the process inefficient and resource-intensive.

  \item \textbf{(Problem II) Difficulty in obtaining process supervision:} Training powerful verifiers like the PRM requires detailed step-wise supervision. Existing approaches either rely on human  effort \citep{uesato2022solving, lightman2024lets}  or tree search \citep{wang2023math, luo2024improvemathematicalreasoninglanguage} for intermediate step annotations. However, both approaches are inefficient and lack scalability, limiting their practical application for large-scale tasks.
\end{itemize}
To address these two significant problems in existing verification methods, we propose a novel approach based on \textit{Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC)} \citep{doucet2001sequential, Moral2006SMC, Briers2009Smooth, chopin2020introduction}. TSMC is a significant advancement in the \textit{Importance Sampling (IS)} technology. Building on the foundation of Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC), TSMC is intended to enhance sampling efficiency of IS in the high-dimensional space. It employs a series of intermediate target distributions at each resampling step, which are defined through twist functions. This function strategically guides the samples towards the high-density region in the target distribution. By retaining the most promising samples, TSMC effectively reduces the variance in estimated quantities and boosts the efficiency of the sampling process. %TSMC has proven valuable in approximating sequences of probability distributions in Bayesian filtering for complex, high-dimensional models. %Its utility spans various domains such as finance, engineering, and statistical physics.

Notably, the application of TSMC to improve the verification method in LLMs has not been explored previously, making our study the first of its kind in this area. 
Our approach is inspired by the realization that existing verification methods employing reward-weighted majority voting \citep{li-etal-2023-making} essentially performs IS, where the sampling efficiency  deteriorates as the disparity between the proposal distribution (which generates potential solutions) and the target distribution (concentrated around correct solutions) widens. % We attribute Problem I—low sampling efficiency—to the large variance in IS when the proposal differs significantly from the target. In tasks involving multi-step reasoning, even small  mismatches at each step can accumulate into a significant discrepancy between two distributions.
We identify Problem I—low sampling efficiency—as a consequence of high variance in IS when there is a substantial deviation between the proposal and target distributions. Multi-step reasoning, even minor discrepancies at each step can cumulate into a substantial mismatch between the two distributions.
We therefore apply TSMC to improve the sampling efficiency of verification by focusing the sampling effort on promising partial solutions during the intermediate decoding process. %We sequentially resamples the partial solutions to approximate the intermediate targets, which are defined through the twist functions. 
We have shown the optimal twist functions in our case, which is used to guide the sampling of TSMC, is proportional to expected future rewards, also known as the value function. The value function could be simply learnt through a neural regressor on the data independently sampled from the generator. This simplifies the training target by eliminating the need for human annotations or tree search. %To our knowledge, this work represents the first exploration of TSMC in multi-step reasoning by LLMs for inference-time verification-based improvements. 
We also highlight the relationship between TSMC and the PRM in existing verification methods, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of bias and variance.
We compare our proposed method with baseline approaches on two math benchmarks: GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021training}  and MATH \citep{MATH},  utilizing fine-tuned models from Llemma-7B \citep{azerbayev2023llemma} and DeepSeek-7B \citep{deepseek-math} as the generators. Our results indicate that TSMC consistently improves both the quality of the generated solutions and the overall verification performance. Additionally, we empirically validate the theoretical advantage of TSMC as an unbiased estimator with reduced variance, further highlighting its effectiveness.

\section{Preliminaries}


\subsection{LLMs for Math}
\label{subsec:verification}
Following \citet{lightman2024lets}, we fix the generator without further fine-tuning via reinforcement learning. For a problem statement $\mathbf{x}_0$, a (tokenized) candidate solution can be sampled from the generator, denoted as $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\sim p(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_0)$. For simplicity, we always assume the dependence on $\mathbf{x}_0$ and no longer explicitly write it out in the following text. 
The solution is assumed to be decomposable as $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{x}_{T}]$, where $\mathbf{x}_i$ is a variable-length reasoning step. By default, the LLM generates all steps in an autoregressive manner, i.e., $\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim p(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$. We represent the extracted answer from the solution as $a=\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, and its correctness as $\phi(a)$, which is $1$ if it is correct (matched with the ground-truth answer) and $0$ otherwise.
The primary methods for solving math problems with LLMs include majority voting \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} and verification \citep{cobbe2021training, uesato2022solving, lightman2024lets}.

\textbf{Majority Voting.} Majority voting independently samples $N$ (tokenized) candidate solutions $\{\mathbf{x}^i_{1:T}\}_{i=1}^N$ from the generator.  It selects the final answer as the one with the most votes, i.e.,  $a^*=\arg\max_a \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(a_i=a)$, where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function.

\paragraph{Verification.} Verification introduces an external verifier $r(\cdot)$ to evaluate the $N$ solutions produced by the LLM generator. %generated in the majority voting method. 
Existing methods can be roughly  divided into two kinds: %categories based on the type of verifiers used: 
the outcome reward model (ORM) family and the process reward model (PRM) family. ORM directly evaluates the confidence score for each full solution as $s=r_{ORM}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, while PRM instead aggregates the confidence scores of sub-sequences as %decomposes the solution confidence score 
 $s=r_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\texttt{Aggr}(\{ r_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})\}_{t=1}^T)$. Here, $r_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ corresponds to the  process reward, and $\texttt{Aggr}(\cdot)$ is the aggregation function such as the minimum or product:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:aggr}
 \texttt{min}=\min\{r_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}\}_{t=1}^T),\qquad \texttt{prod}=\prod_{t=1}^Tr_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}).
\end{equation}
The final answer could either be selected from the solution with the highest score $a^*=\arg\max_{a^i} s^i$ (best-of-$N$), or the answer with the highest total weight $a^*=\arg\max_{a} \sum_{i=1}^Ns^i\mathbb{I}(a_i=a)$ (weighted majority voting) \citep{li-etal-2023-making}. In this work, we mainly develop our method on top of the weighted majority voting due to its empirical better performance \citep{sun2024easy}.

\subsection{Importance Sampling and Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo}
\label{subsec:smc}

\paragraph{Importance Sampling.} Consider a target distribution $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\frac{\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{Z^{\sigma}}$, where $\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\geq0$ is the unnormalized probability density and $Z^{\sigma}=\int_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T}}\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})d\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$ is the normalizing factor, typically intractable. %In Bayesian inference, we are usually interested in estimating the expectation of a function $h(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ under the target distribution. However, it could be hard to directly sample from 
For a given function $h(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, it %becomes 
could be difficult to estimate %difficult to approximate 
its expectation under
$\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ via directly sampling. Importance sampling (IS) \citep{robert2004monte} instead introduces a proposal distribution $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ and provides an estimator of the expectation as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:is_expectation}
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[h(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})]=\frac{1}{Z^{\sigma}}\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[\frac{\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}h(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})]=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[\frac{\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}h(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})]}{\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[\frac{\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}]}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\frac{\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}$ is known as the \textit{importance weight} $w(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$. Using some  $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ that is easy to sample from, we can leverage Equation \ref{eq:is_expectation} to estimate the expectation via the Monte Carlo method,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:is_monte}
\mathbb{E}_{\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[h(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})]\approx\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{w(\mathbf{x}^i_{1:T})}{ \sum_{j=1}^Nw(\mathbf{x}^j_{1:T})} h(\mathbf{x}^i_{1:T}), \qquad \mathbf{x}^{i}_{1:T} \sim q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}).
\end{equation}
\iffalse
Using the same technique, we could also approximate the samples from the target distribution by sampling from a categorical distribution with the self-normalized importance weights,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:is_sample}
    \mathbf{x}_{1:T}^{\sigma} \leftarrow  \mathbf{x}_{1:T}^{\omega}, \quad \omega\sim \texttt{Cat}(\{\frac{w({\mathbf{x}^i_{1:T})}}{\sum_{j=1}^Nw({\mathbf{x}^j_{1:T}})}\}_{i=1}^N).
\end{equation}
\fi
Although ideally a zero variance of the importance weight could be achieved when $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, such a case rarely holds in practice. Remarkably, the distribution mismatches at each step are accumulated as the generation proceeds, leading to an exponentially increasing variance with respect to $T$ \citep{doucet2009tutorial}. Such a limitation makes IS inefficient in the high-dimensional space since extensive sampling is needed to reduce the variance.

\paragraph{Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo.}
Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC) enhances the sampling efficiency of IS by modifying the marginal distribution of the proposal, $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$, to a more informative intermediate distribution, $\pi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$. The aim is to ensure that partial sequences from $\pi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$ are more likely to result in higher-quality samples in the final target distribution $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$. Here, $\{\pi_t\}_{t=1}^T$ is known as the (twisted) intermediate targets where $\pi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{t} (\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{Z_t^{\pi}}$ and the final target is aligned with $\tilde{\pi}_T\equiv\tilde{\sigma}$. In standard Sequential Monte Carlo, $\pi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$ is typically the marginal of the target distribution $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, to ensure that at each time step, the marginal distribution matches the target. However, if our primary interest is only the final target $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, we are free to design $\{\pi_t\}_{t=1}^{T-1}$ on the specific problem at hand, leading to the flexibility of the TSMC method.

TSMC operates recursively, alternating between generation and resampling. At each step, TSMC takes the input of $N$ partial sequences, $\{\mathbf{x}^i_{1:t-1}\}_{i=1}^N$, following the distribution $\pi_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$, and extends  these sequences by sampling the next step from the proposal, i.e., $\mathbf{x}_{t}\sim q(\cdot|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$. It computes the \textit{incremental importance weight} for each sequence as 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:weights}
    w_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})=\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})\tilde{\pi}_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})}.
\end{equation}
These weights are used to approximate the distribution $\pi_{t}$ by resampling the partial sequences from a categorical distribution with  the self-normalized weights:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:smc_sample}
    \mathbf{x}_{1:t}^{i} \leftarrow  \mathbf{x}_{1:t}^{\omega_i}, \qquad \omega^i\sim \texttt{Cat}(\{\frac{w_t({\mathbf{x}^i_{1:t})}}{\sum_{j=1}^Nw_t({\mathbf{x}^j_{1:t}})})\}_{i=1}^N), \qquad i=1,\cdots,N.
\end{equation}
This new set of $N$ sequences would serve as the input to the next step of TSMC. With informative intermediate targets, the resampling step could promptly discard the sequences with a low potential in the target distribution and avoid a large variance in the importance weights. More importantly, since $\pi_T(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ is matched with the target $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, TSMC always yields an unbiased estimator of $\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T}}\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}) h(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ regardless of the twist functions \citep{moral2004feynman}.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Existing Verification Methods are Performing Importance Sampling}
The motivation of our method is based on the observation that existing verification methods are essentially performing IS. To see this, compare the normalized voting weight  of each answer in majority voting and weighted majority voting when $N$ is large 
\begin{align}
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{I}(a_i=a)}{N}&=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[\mathbb{I}(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=a)]&\text{(majority voting)}\\ 
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^N s_i\mathbb{I}(a_i=a)}{N} & =\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}[r(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\mathbb{I}(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=a)] & \text{(weighted majority voting)}
\end{align}
It can be seen that the weighting process actually introduces a factor $r(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ with a similar role of the importance weight in Equation \ref{eq:is_expectation}. In particular, we can let
$\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})r(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$
and treat weighted majority voting as IS to estimate the answer voting weight
\begin{equation}
w(a)=\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{1:T}}\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\mathbb{I}(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=a).
\end{equation}
However, as described in Section \ref{subsec:smc}, the importance weight in IS suffers from a large variance in the high-dimensional space, and so do the estimation objectives according to Proposition \ref{prop:var}.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{var}
\label{prop:var}For IS  with the  target $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ and proposal $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, up to a constant $C$ independent of $q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$, the following identity in the variance holds for the set of all  answers $\mathcal{A}$:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}} \mathbb{V}_q[\frac{\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\mathbb{I}(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=a)}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}] =\mathbb{V}_q[\frac{\tilde{\sigma}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}]+C.
\end{equation}
\end{restatable}
 This issue also accounts for Problem I, i.e., plenty of samples are needed to reduce the variance of the estimator. Therefore, we aim to address this problem via TSMC, which provides the unbiased estimator of $w(a)$, but with less  variance. We visualize the comparison between existing IS-based verification and our TSMC-based verification in Figure \ref{fig:overview}.

\subsection{Verification via TSMC}
\label{subsec:veri_smc}
The optimal reward model in our problem is simply the correctness function of each solution, i.e.,  $r^*(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$. In the following section, we fix our target distribution as  
\begin{equation}
\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))}{Z^{\sigma}},
\end{equation}
since it corresponds to the actual target distribution  we try to sample from.

To utilize TSMC for verification, we still need to decide the proposal distribution $q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ and intermediate targets $\{\pi_t\}_{t=1}^{T-1}$. Following \citet{zhao2024probabilistic}, we define the intermediate targets through the twist functions $\{\psi_t\}_{t=1}^{T-1}$ where $\psi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\geq 0$ are functions to be optimized
\begin{equation}
    \pi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}) = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\psi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{Z^{\pi}_t}.
\end{equation}
Let $\psi_0(\mathbf{x}_0)\equiv1$ and $\psi_T(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})\equiv \phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$, the incremental importance weight is given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:actual_weights}
w_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})\psi_{t}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})\psi_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})}.
\end{equation}
\citet{zhao2024probabilistic} have shown that the optimal proposal and intermediate targets correspond to 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:optim}
    q_t^*(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1}) = \frac{\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})}
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
    \pi_t^*(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}) = \sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{t+1:T}}\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ stands for the target marginal distribution. However, it is hard to directly apply these optimal choices to our case. We outline the reason and our approach as follows.
\paragraph{Proposal.} There are two challenges in preventing us from using the optimal proposal. First, the combinatorial nature of the step $\mathbf{x}_t$, which consists of multiple tokens, makes $q_t^*(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ generally intractable. Moreover, even if we can  approximate it via finetuning, we still encounter the second challenge as the large variance in $q_t^*(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ caused by  the high dimensionality of $\mathbf{x}_{t}$. This would result in the \textit{weight degeneracy} issue \citep{naesseth2019elements} of  TSMC, where the incremental importance weights would be dominated by a single sample, resulting in a poor diversity of solutions after resampling. We therefore simply let $q(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})=p(\mathbf{x}_{t}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ for the ease of sampling and weight degeneracy would be alleviated when $q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ cancels in Equation \ref{eq:actual_weights}.

\paragraph{Intermediate targets.} The intermediate targets in Equation \ref{eq:optim} is no longer optimal when we use $p(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ as our proposal. However, it is also hard to solve the globally optimal intermediate targets for an arbitrary proposal. We instead seek to  sequentially derive the locally optimal twists in a greedy manner. Since our ultimate goal is to estimate the answer weights $w(a)$, we start by looking for the optimal intermediate target $\pi^*_{T-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T-1})$ in minimizing the variance of the incremental importance weight in the last TSMC step.
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{intermediate}
\label{prop:optimal_target}
    Given an (intermediate) target   $\pi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$ and the proposal $q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$, the optimal  $\pi_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})$ in minimizing the variance of the incremental importance weight corresponds to 
    \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optimal_target}
        \pi^q_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})\propto \sqrt{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_t}\frac{\pi_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})^2}{q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})}}.
    \end{equation}
\end{restatable}
 Taking $t=T$ and $q=p$ implies $\pi_{T-1}^p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T-1})\propto p(\mathbf{x}_{1:T-1})\sqrt{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{T}}p(\mathbf{x}_T|\mathbf{x}_{1:T-1})\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))}$. Here we apply the fact that $\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))^2=\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$ as it is binary. If we fix the intermediate target as the choice above, we could further propagate the derivation to previous steps by recursively applying Proposition \ref{prop:optimal_target}, getting the locally optimal  intermediate targets for $t<T$ as
\begin{equation}
    \pi_{t}^p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\propto p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\sqrt{\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{t+1:T}}p(\mathbf{x}_{t+1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))}.
\end{equation}
 In particular, $\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{t+1:T}}p(\mathbf{x}_{t+1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$  actually represents the value function $V^p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$ in reinforcement learning \citep{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow}. Hence, the locally optimal twists are given by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:twist}
    \psi^p_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t}) \propto \sqrt{V^p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Connection with the PRM}
\label{subsec:connection}
 Based on our above choices, the incremental importance weights in Equation \ref{eq:actual_weights} becomes
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:optimal_weigths}
    w^p_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})=\frac{\psi^p_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{\psi^p_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})}\propto\sqrt{\frac{V^p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{V^p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})}}.
\end{equation}
The incremental importance weight could also be treated as a measurement of the step quality, similar to the process reward in the PRM. To further augment this connection, note that
\begin{equation}
\prod_{t=1}^Tw^p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})=\prod_{t=1}^T\frac{\psi^p_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{\psi^p_{t-1}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})}=\frac{\psi^p_T(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})}{\psi^p_{0}(\mathbf{x}_{0})}=\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})),
\end{equation}
which is in the same format as the PRM with \texttt{prod} aggregation. The key observation here is that TSMC always yields an unbiased estimator of the importance weight $\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$ when there is no estimation error of $V^p$. We continue to compare this estimator to some existing PRMs.
\paragraph{The PRM learnt through automatic supervision.} 
This class of PRMs \citep{wang2023making, luo2024improvemathematicalreasoninglanguage} computes the process reward by evaluating the value function at each partial solution with respect to a roll-out policy $\mu$. The solution confidence score will be computed as
\begin{equation}
    r_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T})=\texttt{Aggr}(\{r(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})\}_{t=1}^T) =\texttt{Aggr}(\{V^{\mu}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\}_{t=1}^T). 
\end{equation}
But such an estimator is always biased no matter \texttt{min} or \texttt{prod} is used for aggregation.
\paragraph{The PRM learnt through human supervision.} The human supervision is generated through the logical sense of the step correctness. 
\begin{restatable}{proposition}{prm}
    The ground-truth PRM over the step correctness corresponds to
\begin{equation}
r_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_{1:t-1})=\mathbb{I}(\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})>0).
\end{equation}
\end{restatable}
Therefore, the solution confidence score of this PRM is always an unbiased estimator of $\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$ for both \texttt{min} and \texttt{prod} aggregation. However, using $\mathbb{I}(\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})>0)$ for the intermediate target, as tried by \citet{uesato2022solving}, does not effectively reduce the sampling variance or improve the verification performance since it ignores the likelihood of the proposal $p(\mathbf{x}_{t+1:T}|\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$.

\subsection{Value Function Estimation}
The  approximation of $\{\psi_t^p\}_{t=1}^{T-1}$ and $r^*$ can be consolidated into a single learning task: estimating the value function $V^p$. We therefore use a single neural model parameterized by $\theta$ for approximation.  Estimating the value function through independently sampled data from the policy (generator) is a well-studied topic \citep{bertsekas2012dynamic}. It therefore eliminates the need for explicit process supervision during training, as outlined in Problem II.

In this paper, we adopt the Contrastive Twist Learning (CTL) method developed by \citet{zhao2024probabilistic}. Directly approximating $\pi_t^p$ would be hard, so we still approximate  the target marginal $\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})$ to learn the value function and take the square root of the value function during the inference time.  Let $V^{\theta}$ be our approximation of the value function $V^p$, and define the  intermediate target  $\pi_t^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})=\frac{p(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}{Z_t^{\pi}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}$. CTL minimizes the KL divergence between the target marginal distributions and the  intermediate targets, i.e., 
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta}L_{CTL}(\theta)=\min_{\theta}\sum_{t=1}^T D_{KL}(\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})\| \pi^{\theta}_t(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})),  
\end{equation}
whose gradient at $t$-th step can be derived as 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{\sigma(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}[\nabla_{\theta}\log V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_t^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})}[\nabla_{\theta}\log V^{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:t})].
\end{equation}
We approximate the gradient in the first term via rejection sampling while the gradient in the second term via importance sampling, as done in Equation \ref{eq:is_monte}.




\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiment}
\subsection{Experimental Setup} 
  

\paragraph{Datasets.} Building on prior work \citep{uesato2022solving, lightman2024lets, wang2023making}, we assess our TSMC method using two widely used math datasets: GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021training} and MATH \citep{MATH}. For GSM8K, we evaluate model performance across all testing instances. Whereas for math, we follow \citet{lightman2024lets} to select a representative subset of 500 testing instances, referred to as ``MATH500" in the following text.

\paragraph{Generators.} We fine-tune our solution generators using two different pretrained LLMs,  Llemma-7B \citep{azerbayev2023llemma} and DeepSeek-7B \citep{deepseek-math}. Following \citet{sun2024easy}, we use the filtered PRM800K  \citep{lightman2024lets} as the supervised fine-tuning dataset.

\paragraph{Baselines.} We compare our method to both the non-verification method using majority voting (MV) \citep{wang2023selfconsistency} and the verification methods using weighted majority voting (WMV). For verification methods, we utilize various types of verifiers, including the ORM, the PRM  trained with human supervision on PRM800K \citep{lightman2024lets}) and the PRM trained with automatic supervision on MATH-SHEPHERD \citep{wang2023making}. We employ  \texttt{min} for aggregation on both PRMs. We keep the architecture and pretrained weights of all the verifiers, including the reward model and the parameterized value function, the same as the generator across all settings. The problem solving rate (in \%) is used as the metric for comparison. 

\paragraph{TSMC details.} Our TSMC is applied on the step level. We implement a warm-up stage that skips resampling in the initial stage, setting this threshold at 50 tokens across all experiments. A maximum of five resampling steps is allowed to reduce the latency. For sequences that terminate early, we assign an incremental importance weight of 1 during the remaining resampling steps. We employ stratified sampling \citep{kitagawa1996stratified} for resampling to reduce the variance. Instead of resampling across the full batch of $N$ solutions, we perform resampling over a mini-batch with $M$ samples. The batch size $M$ is fixed as $80$ (the maximum number of sequences fit into our GPUs) by default. %but it could be further tuned to improve the performance as shown in Section \ref{sub_sec:sensitivity}.

\subsection{Impact of the Bias in the Estimator}
TSMC is characterized by its unbiased estimation of the importance weight, which is  $\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$ in our task. However, since the training error is unavoidable in practice, it remains unclear whether such kind of unbiased estimation at the theoretical optimal case is useful. We thus look into this problem by comparing different biased and unbiased estimators analyzed in Section \ref{subsec:connection}.

We consider both the PRM predicting the step correctness, PRM (PRM800K), and the PRM predicting the value function, including PRM (SHEPHERD) and the value function estimated in TSMC. Beyond the $\texttt{min}$ and $\texttt{prod}$ aggregations, we also consider an unbiased ORM-like strategy that only uses the process reward at the last step as the solution score:
\begin{equation}
       \texttt{last}=r_{PRM}(\mathbf{x}_T|\mathbf{x}_{1:T-1}).
\end{equation}
Exceptionally, we evaluate the value function of TSMC on the data generated by TSMC, so we use the last incremental importance weight for \texttt{last} in this scenario as we do in our original TSMC method. This is equivalent to the product of incremental importance weights, which is also an unbiased estimator. 
We compare all estimators in Figure \ref{fig:bias}.

The trend is highly consistent when the value function is used as the process reward, as in PRM (SHEPHERD) and Value (TSMC). The \texttt{prod} strategy shows an overall inferior performance across all settings. Since the step value could be small, the product of step values is highly biased towards the solutions with fewer steps. In contrast, \texttt{min} could overcome such a bias as its value is insensitive to the number of steps. It also shows a clear advantage in comparison to \texttt{prod}, in line with the choice of \citep{wang2023making}. However, its performance is still consistently worse than the unbiased estimator using \texttt{last}. A different pattern shows up in PRM (PRM800K), where the step correctness is used as the process reward. The \texttt{min} strategy still achieves the overall best result, but \texttt{prod} is also comparably good. We find no advantage of \texttt{last} in this case as all three estimators are unbiased. Basically, the advantage of PRM against ORM holds only when both of them are unbiased estimators of $\phi(\texttt{Ans}(\mathbf{x}_{1:T}))$. When the PRM is biased, there is no clear guarantee of a better performance against the ORM, which is always an unbiased estimator. While TSMC assimilates the strengths of the unbiased estimation from the ORM and the intermedidate step modeling from the PRM, leading to the best performance.

\label{sub_sec:sensitivity}
In above experiments, we use the maximum number of samples that can fit into the memory of our GPUs as the batch size $M$ of TSMC. In practice, a smaller memory and or a larger model would lead to a smaller TSMC batch size. We thus try to investigate the effect of $M$ on the TSMC performance by varying $M$ over the values in $\{10,20,40,80\}$. The comparative results are shown in Figure \ref{fig:smc_n}. It can be seen that smaller $M$ achieves comparable and even better results. The advantage of TSMC still clearly holds when $M=10$, which is a batch size that can be fit into most GPUs.


\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Verification for reasoning.}
Verification has proven to be an effective approach for enhancing the multi-step reasoning ability of LLMs. Two widely adopted verification methods are the Outcome Reward Model (ORM) \citep{cobbe2021training} and the Process Reward Model (PRM) \citep{uesato2022solving}. While empirical evidence suggests that PRM outperforms ORM \citep{lightman2024lets}, training the PRM presents a significant challenge due to the need for process supervision, which is often difficult to obtain. Recent research has therefore increasingly focused on automatic supervision  to train PRMs more efficiently \citep{wang2023math, luo2024improvemathematicalreasoninglanguage, wang2024multistepproblemsolvingverifier}. %In this work, we have proposed a new way to evaluate  and apply the process reward via TSMC.
\paragraph{(Twisted) Sequential Monte Carlo.}
Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) is a generic statistical inference approach that has been widely applied across various domains, including signal processing \citep{doucet2009tutorial, godsill2004monte}, financial econometrics \citep{JOHANNES20101, creal2012economics}, and robotics \citep{michael2002fastslam, bailey2006simul, thrun2005probabilistic}.  Recently, SMC has been integrated with neural models to enhance sequential generative models, such as diffusion models \citep{trippe2023diffusion, wu2023fine} and  LLMs \citep{lew2023sequentialmontecarlosteering, zhao2024probabilistic}. The most relevant work to ours is \citet{zhao2024probabilistic}, which presents a general framework for controlled text generation using Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC). Our work primarily focuses on multi-step reasoning, one of the most challenging areas for LLMs. Additionally, we are the first to bridge TSMC with the predominant verification methods, offering a novel theoretical perspective for explainability.



\section{Conclusion \& Limitation}

In this paper, we introduce a novel verification method for multi-step reasoning using Twisted Sequential Monte Carlo (TSMC). Our approach sequentially approximates intermediate targets, enhancing the reasoning process of large language models and improving both solution quality and sampling efficiency. By incorporating step-wise guidance without human supervision for training, our method provides a scalable framework for various multi-step reasoning tasks.

Although promising, our method also introduces the additional latency in the inference time due to variable step lengths. A potential optimization involves blockwise resampling over a fixed number of tokens. Future work could also explore the impact of TSMC batch size and refine algorithmic design for further efficiency gains.
