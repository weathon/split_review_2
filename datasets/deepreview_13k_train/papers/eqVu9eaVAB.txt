\title{What Matters in Hierarchical Search for Combinatorial Reasoning Problems?}

\begin{abstract}
Efficiently tackling combinatorial reasoning problems, particularly the notorious NP-hard tasks, remains a significant challenge for AI research. Recent efforts have sought to enhance planning by incorporating hierarchical high-level search strategies, known as subgoal methods. While promising, their performance against traditional low-level planners is inconsistent, raising questions about their application contexts. In this study, we conduct an in-depth exploration of subgoal-planning methods for combinatorial reasoning. We identify the attributes pivotal for leveraging the advantages of high-level search: \emph{hard-to-learn value functions, complex action spaces, presence of dead ends in the environment, or using data collected from diverse experts}. We propose a consistent evaluation methodology to achieve meaningful comparisons between methods and reevaluate the \mbox{state-of-the-art} algorithms. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Many real-world tasks involve underlying abstract structures that capture their complexity. For example, robotic navigation can be efficiently modeled as a graph search problem, enabling optimal path planning and execution within complex environments \cite{lavalle2006planning, choset2005principles}.  Similarly, sequence alignment in bioinformatics can be effectively modeled as a graph of alignment positions and pathways, facilitating the identification of similarity regions \cite{needleman1970general, smith1981identification}. Hence, addressing these problems in discrete environments, which demand intricate reasoning and include numerous NP-hard problems, is crucial for advancing AI \cite{bengio_combinatorial_optimization}. %\frb{advancing AI imo is still cliche!}
Tackling these challenges requires efficient planning strategies \cite{DBLP:conf/nips/BruckG87}. A recent, promising approach to planning uses subgoals to decompose complex tasks into sequences of simpler steps, drawing inspiration from how humans plan their actions \cite{Hull1932, Fishbach2005, Kool2014}. Hierarchical planning was successfully applied to tasks with high combinatorial complexity by Subgoal Search (kSubS) \cite{ksubs}, followed by other algorithms, including Adaptive Subgoal Search (AdaSubS) \cite{ada}, Hierarchical Imitation Planning with Search (HIPS) \cite{hips}, and HIPS-$\varepsilon$ \cite{hipseps}. These methods take high-level steps, which are commonly believed to scale better to longer horizons \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2401-02644, DBLP:conf/nips/LeeKJK22}, bypass errors in value approximations \cite{ksubs} and reduce complexity by decomposing the problem into subtasks \cite{DBLP:journals/ai/SuttonPS99, ada}.

\emph{While subgoal methods have demonstrated strong results in the literature, our findings reveal that classical, low-level search algorithms can perform at least equally well in certain domains}. Thus, it remains an open question in which settings subgoal-based methods should be applied to leverage their advantages.

In this work, we present a comprehensive study exploring how the choice of environments and training data impacts subgoal-based methods for combinatorial reasoning problems. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic study to investigate this relationship. We identify key attributes that enable leveraging the advantage of high-level search: \emph{hard-to-learn value functions}, \emph{complex action spaces}, \emph{the presence of dead ends in the environment}, and \emph{the use of data collected from diverse experts}. Additionally, our experiments reveal several evaluation pitfalls that are easy to overlook but can lead to misleading conclusions. To address these issues, we propose a consistent evaluation methodology that allows meaningful comparisons between various methods.

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep, topsep=0pt]
    \item We identify the key properties of environments and datasets that impact the performance of subgoal-based methods. 
    \item We analyze the suitability of Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) algorithms for combinatorial reasoning and list the necessary attributes.
    \item We highlight critical points for reporting reliable results in this domain, such as reporting success rates for the entire search budget.
\end{itemize}






\section{Related Work}

Classical reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) \cite{schulman2017proximal} and Deep Q-Network (DQN) \cite{Mnih2015}, are designed to solve problems modeled as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) \cite{DBLP:journals/ai/SuttonPS99}. Recently, there has been a surge in research leveraging large datasets of demonstrations \cite{walke2023bridgedata, open_x_embodiment_rt_x_2023, grauman2022ego4d, Dosovitskiy17} to learn and solve complex real-world decision-making tasks using Imitation Learning (IL) techniques. These methods, including Behavioral Cloning (BC) \cite{DBLP:books/lib/SuttonB98, DBLP:conf/icml/SammutHKM92, walke2023bridgedata}, Inverse Reinforcement Learning \cite{baker2009action}, and DAgger \cite{dagger}, aim to mimic expert actions provided in datasets.
\textbf{Subgoal-based methods} decompose complex tasks into manageable subgoals, streamlining the solution of complex MDPs. HIRO~\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/NachumGLL18} leverages offline data by relabeling past transitions. HAC~\cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/LevyKPS19} learns a hierarchy of high-level policies using hindsight~\cite{her}. Graph-based search methods, such as SoRB~\cite{sorb} or DHRL~\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/LeeKJK22}, construct a high-level graph of the state space, enabling fast searches for arbitrary goals. Hierarchical Diffuser~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2401-02644} generates action plans at various timescales using a diffusion model~\cite{DBLP:conf/iclr/WangHZ23}. HIPS~\cite{hips} employs Policy-guided Heuristic Search \cite{phs} to navigate between subgoals generated by VQ-VAE models~\cite{DBLP:conf/nips/OordVK17}. HIPS-$\epsilon$~\cite{hipseps} combines high-level and low-level planning to ensure completeness of the search.

Recently, kSubS \cite{ksubs} was introduced as a hierarchical search algorithm for combinatorial domains. To solve the given task, it iteratively constructs a search tree consisting of achievable subgoals. In each iteration, the $k$-subgoal generator predicts states that are $k$ steps closer to the solution, after which the low-level policy computes paths to these subgoals. Nodes are expanded based on a best-first strategy guided by the value function predictions. All components of kSubS are trained on offline datasets using imitation learning.
AdaSubS~\cite{ada} builds on kSubS by adding multiple subgoal generators trained to output subgoals at various distances. During the planning phase, AdaSubS prioritizes long-distance subgoal generators, retracting to shorter if the search becomes locally stuck. This way, AdaSubS dynamically adjusts its planning strategy based on the difficulty of the current search region. 
\textbf{Classical search algorithms} are widely used to solve hard decision-making problems~\cite{silver_mcts, silver_mcts_2, agnostelli, alphafold, alphago, alphatensor, DBLP:journals/jacic/ChienSTCRCDMTSB05}. Best-First Search (BestFS) prioritizes states with the highest predicted values, often using policy to select actions based on top-ranked choices or cumulative confidence thresholds~\cite{feng2022left}. A* improves upon BestFS by combining value estimates with distances from the root state, enabling more efficient exploration. MCTS \cite{mcts} builds an asymmetric tree to estimate action values through random simulations, precisely choosing the most promising action. Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) \cite{mcts} builds an asymmetric tree to estimate action values through random simulations, selecting the most promising actions precisely. Combining MCTS with deep neural networks has led to breakthroughs like AlphaGo and AlphaZero~\cite{alphago, alphazero}.






\section{Preliminaries}


\subsection{Combinatorial environments and algorithmic desiderata }\label{sec:preliminaries_environments}

The abstract structure of real-world tasks captures the essence of reasoning. Therefore, our study targets solving discrete combinatorial reasoning environments, a category that includes many NP-hard problems, such as the Traveling Salesman Problem \cite{applegate2006traveling}, the Knapsack Problem \cite{kellerer2004knapsack}, the Rubik's Cube \cite{singmaster1981notes}, or the Sudoku \cite{yato2003complexity}. Specifically, in our experiments, we use the \textit{Rubik's Cube, Sokoban, N-Puzzle, and Inequality Theorem Proving (INT)} \cite{int}. These are classical benchmarks, explored in various planning works \cite{agnostelli, ksubs}, and are known to be NP-hard \cite{Demain, pspace_soko, npuzzle}.

The combinatorial reasoning environments are typically characterized by an enormous space of possible configurations, making learning without priors or handcrafted dense rewards prohibitively challenging\footnote{For instance, we trained a PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} agent to solve the Rubik's Cube, but, unsurprisingly, it failed to make any progress due to never reaching the goal in the haystack of $4.3\times 10^{19}$ states, hence never observing a positive reward. This would be the case for any algorithm that learns goal-reaching.} Similarly, the space complexity usually limits the number of configurations that can be observed in training. Hence, the solver cannot expect that it will eventually visit a closely located state again, which is essential for methods like \cite{sorb, DBLP:conf/nips/HuangLS19, DBLP:conf/nips/LeeKJK22}. Therefore it follows that the following conditions are necessary for an algorithm to be efficient in solving combinatorial problems:

\begin{enumerate}%[leftmargin=10pt, itemsep=1pt, topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Learning from offline demonstrations} Finding a solution through exploration is prohibitively hard in combinatorial environments. Thus, the algorithm has to be able to learn from demonstrations.%\kucil{2105: AlphaZero learns on "combinatorial hard env" but does not learn from offline data}\michalz{It doesn't solve goal-reaching problems per se}.
    \item \textbf{Generalization} The algorithm will typically visit a given state only once, so it needs to generalize well across the state space. %\piotrm{21.05 How much this is differenlty from many other ML problems?}\michalz{Not much, but it excludes e.g. memory graph-based searches from consideration.}
    \item \textbf{Planning} The algorithm needs a planning module as it cannot scale in solving NP-hard problems \cite{DBLP:conf/nips/BruckG87} within a fixed computation budget.% \kucil{2105: problems mentioned earlier}\michalz{I'm not sure which, AlphaZero does use planning :)}
\end{enumerate}

Standard hierarchical methods have not been specifically designed for combinatorial problems and, thus, fail to meet the necessary conditions for effective application. For instance, \cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2401-02644, DBLP:journals/tnn/YangMJA18} require continuous state or action space, \cite{DBLP:conf/icml/GhavamzadehM03} learns from online interactions, \cite{sorb, DBLP:conf/nips/HuangLS19, DBLP:conf/nips/LeeKJK22} assume a good coverage of the whole state space, and \cite{DBLP:conf/nips/NachumGLL18, DBLP:conf/iclr/LevyKPS19} do not use planning to determine actions. %\kucil{2105: Case of AlphaZero}\michalz{We may argue that AlphaZero was not applied to goal-reaching (chess, go, SC are 2-pl 0-sum which is a different setting), but then AlphaZero "can be made" applicable to our problems, but then we should include it in our study, but then our MCTS is kind of that, but then its results are weaker than we should expect, etc... \subsection{Subgoal methods} \label{sec:preliminaries_subgoal_methods}
Subgoal methods perform planning with high-level steps composed of multiple actions.
This high-level guidance may take various forms. We consider subgoal methods characterized by the following components:

\begin{itemize}%[leftmargin=10pt, itemsep=1pt, topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Subgoal generator} that, given a state, outputs subgoals to achieve. That may be a future state, a class of desired outcomes, or any other form of high-level guidance.
    \item \textbf{Low-level policy} that  determines low-level paths between subgoals. It may be a trained goal-reaching policy \cite{ksubs, ada}, a local search \cite{ksubs, hips}, a stored path from previous episodes \cite{sorb, DBLP:conf/nips/LeeKJK22}.%, or any other function that determines low-level paths between subgoals.
    \item \textbf{Planner} that determines the order of generating subgoals. Usually, standard  planners, e.g., BestFS \cite{ksubs}, PHS \cite{hips}, or their modified forms \cite{ada}, are repurposed for this goal.
    \item \textbf{Value function} that estimates the distance to the solution.
\end{itemize}

In this work, we will study kSubS and AdaSubS as subgoal methods suitable for combinatorial problems. For reporting, we use the notation \textit{kSubS-$n$} to indicate that the subgoal generator was trained for distance $n$. Similarly, \textit{AdaSubS-$a$+$b$+$c$} indicates using a set of generators trained for distances $a$, $b$, and $c$.

We compare the performance of subgoal search methods with three widely used low-level algorithms: BestFS, A*, and MCTS.


\section{Analysis}

In this work, we aim to establish a solid empirical foundation for developing subgoal-based search algorithms tailored to combinatorial reasoning problems. To this end, we pose the following fundamental questions:

\begin{enumerate}%[leftmargin=20pt, itemsep=1pt, topsep=0pt]
\item[Q1.] Are hierarchical search algorithms indeed proficient in solving combinatorial problems?
\item[Q2.] In which environments do hierarchical search algorithms offer an advantage over traditional low-level search strategies? Can we identify specific properties that make an environment amenable to high-level search?
\item[Q3.] How should we select the baselines for a fair comparison? What common mistakes should be avoided when reporting experimental results?
\end{enumerate}

To address these questions, we extensively analyze the algorithms and combinatorial reasoning environments. Each experiment is described starting with its motivation, followed by the presentation of our experimental results, and concluding with concise takeaways.}
\paragraph{Experimental setup} For each experiment, we generate a dataset of approximately $5\times 10^5$ solving trajectories. We train the components for each method through imitation learning. Whenever possible, components are shared between algorithms; for instance, all tested algorithms use the same value function. For clarity, we present only the best-performing variants of each method.

Our primary metric, \textit{success rate}, is the percentage of solved instances calculated across a set of $500$ instances in a given environment. The success rate depends on the \textit{search budget}, which is the total number of states visited during the search, including both the subgoals and the low-level steps.

The costs associated with this metric arise from two main sources: generating subgoals and filling low-level paths, each requiring network calls. Therefore, we argue that reporting all search nodes is essential. For instance, a generator might produce a subgoal that is 10 steps ahead of the current state, but reaching this subgoal could be highly costly due to the associated reaching expenses. However, it would be hidden by the other metric only for subgoal methods. A further discussion of this decision is presented in Section \ref{sec:analysis_budget}.
\subsection{Rubik's Cube}

\subsubsection{Verifying experimental outcomes}\label{sec:rubik_replicate}

\paragraph{Motivation} Learning to solve the Rubik's Cube is hard, even for humans. \cite{ksubs, ada} report that both kSubS and AdaSubS can solve it, while the low-level BestFS struggles. We want to verify these claims and understand the reasons behind them.

\paragraph{Experimental Results}
Following \cite{ada}, each method was trained on trajectories obtained by shuffling the cube with $20$ random moves and reversing it. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:rubik_replicate}, every tested method solves the environment nearly perfectly. AdaSubS is the most effective when using smaller search budgets, while BestFS takes the lead for larger budgets. A* performs similarly to BestFS, but MCTS is not effective. Thus, using subgoals in this setting is not helpful.
This contrasts with the results presented in \cite{ksubs, ada}. Firstly, the low-level algorithms successfully solve the task. Secondly, each subgoal method requires about three times smaller computational budget than reported in \citep{ada}.

For this study, we reimplemented and trained the components from scratch, with some technical modifications, such as model size, architecture, and hyperparameters, and using up-to-date libraries. That may explain the substantial difference in performance.

\takeaways{Low-level search algorithms are competitive with subgoal methods for solving the Rubik's Cube}{}

\subsubsection{Learning from diverse experts}\label{sec:rubik_moe}
\paragraph{Motivation}
Subgoal methods are considered more effective for long-horizon planning problems~\cite{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2401-02644} and are believed to reduce noise in training data \cite{ksubs}. To verify these properties, we trained the algorithms to solve the Rubik's Cube using a dataset composed of various sources: human-oriented \textit{Beginner} and \textit{CFOP}, computational \textit{Kociemba}, and the random solutions used in Section \ref{sec:rubik_replicate}. The first two solvers solve the cube layer by layer; hence, their trajectories are long and composed of high-level subroutines. 

\paragraph{Experimental Results}
When training on data from diverse experts, the subgoal methods (particularly AdaSubS) outperform the low-level search by a wide margin (see Figure \ref{fig:rubik_moe}). Notably, despite using strongly suboptimal and diverse data, AdaSubS and kSubS can solve the most challenging instances within an even smaller budget than in the previous experiment. On the other hand, all low-level methods perform much worse than in the previous experiment. Additionally, we observe that given lengthy demonstrations, increasing the subgoal distance for kSubS improves performance, unlike in Section \ref{sec:rubik_replicate} with short trajectories.
We attribute the observed performance gap to the noise in the value function estimates. Specifically, it fails to consistently assign low values to fully scrambled cubes and nearby states. This issue arises because both \textit{Beginner} and \textit{CFOP} yield solutions that are even five times longer than those of the other solvers. Subgoal algorithms are, to some degree, robust to this noise, as claimed by \cite{ksubs}. By taking longer steps, they can leap over the faulty value function. 
\takeaways{Subgoal methods successfully leverage diverse, long demonstrations, while low-level search performs better on short, homogeneous trajectories.}{}

\subsubsection{Structure in demonstrations}\label{sec:rubik_beginner}

\paragraph{Motivation} While Section \ref{sec:rubik_moe} showcases an advantage of subgoal methods, it is still unclear which property of the environment contributes the most to it. We conjecture that the key factor is the diversity of training data, which makes the value estimation problem hard. To verify this, we use training data collected from a single algorithmic solver -- \textit{Beginner}. 
Unlike in the previous experiment, no other expert is used. 
Therefore, the training data, although not optimal, becomes unambiguous.

\paragraph{Experimental Results}

After removing the variance from demonstrations by switching to \textit{Beginner}, the performance of AdaSubS and BestFS becomes nearly identical, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:rubik_beginner}. Similarly, the performance of A* closely matches that of kSubS. MCTS shows significant improvement over previous experiments, though it still lags behind other methods. Longer subgoals continue to outperform shorter ones, but extending them beyond a distance of four offers no additional benefits. Furthermore, a comparison with the results in Section \ref{sec:rubik_moe} reveals that the performance of subgoal methods remains consistent whether using single or multiple sources of demonstrations.

Importantly, we found that in order to achieve good performance, BestFS requires careful tuning. It achieves its best results when, at each expansion, we select actions that collectively exceed $70\%$ confidence. Adopting the approach recommended by \cite{ksubs, ada} of selecting a fixed number of actions leads to highly suboptimal outcomes, as demonstrated by the poorly performing agent in Figure \ref{fig:rubik_beginner}, \textit{BestFS-top-2}.
\takeaways{Given homogeneous data, low-level search algorithms can leverage the long, structured demonstrations equally well as subgoal methods. 
}{}

\subsubsection{Robustness to noisy value function}\label{sec:noisy_value}
\paragraph{Motivation} The advantage of subgoal methods observed in Section \ref{sec:rubik_moe} does not primarily come from long or structured demonstrations. However, high-level algorithms show surprisingly consistent performance regardless of the training data, as demonstrated in Sections \mbox{\ref{sec:rubik_replicate}-\ref{sec:rubik_beginner}}. That observation hints that the subgoal methods are resilient to noise in the training data. To verify that proposition, we take the components trained as in Section \ref{sec:rubik_replicate} and add artificial Gaussian noise to each output of the value function.

\paragraph{Experimental Results}
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:rubik_noise}, while the standard BestFS can solve nearly all instances, its performance heavily depends on the quality of the learned value function. In a similar way, the noisy values strongly impact the performance of A* and MCTS. However, kSubS is much more resilient when using subgoals longer than a single step. Even with completely random values ($\sigma=100$), it successfully solves about $40\%$ instances. Moreover, AdaSubS is barely affected by the noise at all.
That confirms the claim of \cite{ksubs} that subgoals successfully leap over value noise. 

As noted in \cite{ada}, the search performed by the subgoal methods is strongly guided by the subgoal generators, which considerably reduces the reliance on the value function.


\takeaways{Subgoal methods successfully leap over the noise. Thus, they should be used when estimating the value is hard, for instance, when learning from diverse and suboptimal experts.}{}

\subsection{INT}

\subsubsection{Scaling to complex mathematical reasoning}

\paragraph{Motivation} We aim to explore whether subgoal methods can more effectively scale to complex real-world applications than low-level methods, as suggested in \cite{ksubs, ada}. To this end, we consider the INT environment \cite{int}, which involves solving inequalities. It is the most challenging among the environments studied in \cite{ksubs, ada, hips, hipseps}, both in terms of observations and actions complexity. 

\paragraph{Experimental Results}
Our experiments show a clear advantage of subgoal methods compared to low-level search on INT, as shown in  Figure \ref{fig:int_replicate}. We note that we use the proofs length of $25$, which is a more challenging setup than typically used.

\takeaways{Subgoal methods significantly outperform low-level search strategies in the context of mathematical reasoning. 
}{}

\subsubsection{Complex action space}
\paragraph{Motivation} We conjecture that the result of the previous experiment is due to the high complexity of the action space compared to the state space. This suggests that state-based subgoals may be more effective in such settings than action-based policy guidance. To further investigate this in a controlled setting, we inflate the action space of the Rubik's Cube by a factor of $100$ by creating $100$ copies of each original action.

\paragraph{Experimental Results}

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:rubik_actions_x100}, the low-level search methods stuck below $30\%$ success rate, while the subgoal methods perform comparably to the standard cube case from Section \ref{sec:rubik_replicate}. We could expect that since, given so many similar actions, the low-level search nearly trivializes. Although the subgoal methods also use a low-level policy for connecting the subgoals, it always follows a single path towards the subgoal, so it is barely affected.

\takeaways{When facing a problem with a complex action space, subgoal methods should outperform low-level search.}{}

\subsection{Sokoban}\label{sec:sokoban_reporting}

\subsubsection{Importance of budget measures}\label{sec:analysis_budget} 

\paragraph{Motivation} Given that Sokoban is PSPACE-complete \cite{pspace_soko}, it presents a challenging testbed for AI planning algorithms. \cite{ada} reports that AdaSubS outperforms BestFS in Sokoban. They also report that kSubS surpasses BestFS only, given a relatively high search budget. Some works use the number of high-level nodes visited during the search as the search budget \cite{hips}. This contrasts with the total number of visited nodes (the main metric in this paper). We aim to show how this difference affects the interpretation of the results and propose recommendations for reporting the search budget. For these experiments, each method was trained on a dataset containing trajectories collected by an external RL agent.
 
\paragraph{Experimental Results}

Figure \ref{fig:budget} shows that reporting only the high-level nodes excessively enhances subgoal methods' scores. AdaSubS and kSubS seem absolutely superior to BestFS despite the last two algorithms visiting a similar number of states during the search. Hence, that way of reporting the results would lead to erroneous conclusions about the strong primacy of subgoal methods, and we recommend using the total number of visited states as the search budget. 

\takeaways{
When reporting the search budget, it is crucial to count all visited states, not only the high-level nodes.}{}

\subsubsection{Dead-ends}
\paragraph{Motivation} We call a state a dead-end if it is impossible to reach the goal from it. They pose an additional challenge as an algorithm must quickly recognize dead-ends to abandon the search in hopeless state-space regions. We investigate how the subgoal and low-level methods handle dead-ends.
\paragraph{Experimental Results}
Table \ref{tab:deadends} indicates that subgoal methods, especially those involving longer subgoals, notably reduce the likelihood of stacking in dead-ends. We conjecture that this effectiveness stems mainly from relying less on value function guidance than low-level methods, as detailed in Section \ref{sec:noisy_value} and supported by \cite{feng2022left}.

\takeaways{Subgoal methods are better in avoiding dead-ends than low-level search.}{}

\subsection{N-Puzzle}

 
\paragraph{Motivation} The N-puzzle, also known as the Sliding Tile Puzzle, is a classic benchmark in the search literature \cite{agnostelli, hips, DBLP:journals/ai/Korf85} and is known to be NP-hard \cite{npuzzle}. It was used to evaluate subgoal methods in \cite{hips, hipseps}. Despite its large state space, the N-puzzle is highly structured, allowing for solving very simple suboptimal strategies. We aim to determine whether subgoal techniques offer an advantage over low-level search methods in this environment.

A simple policy trained with behavioral cloning (BC) perfectly solves the environment, even without using search, see Figure \ref{fig:n_puzzle}. This indicates that learning from structured demonstrations is relatively straightforward. Notably, AdaSubS surpassed BC, indicating that this subgoal approach improved the cloned sub-optimal behavior. Furthermore, kSubS, BestFS, and A* perform similarly to the BC policy, showcasing no computational waste. 

\takeaways{Due to its structure, the N-Puzzle environment is not a good benchmark for testing the performance of advanced search algorithms.}{}




\section{Open questions and future directions}


While we identified several features that facilitate the performance of subgoal methods, that list is not exhaustive. Thus, it is essential to study this topic further, expand the analysis to more subgoal-based and low-level algorithms, and include even more types of environments. Additionally, our work provides mostly experimental validation of the claims. Finding theoretical foundations for the observed properties would also be a valuable direction.



\section{Conclusions}

\label{sec:conclusions}


We conducted a thorough comparison of subgoal-based and low-level search algorithms for combinatorial reasoning tasks. Our experiments indicate that hierarchical approaches excel in environments where value estimation is challenging and learned estimates face significant uncertainty, particularly when learning from various suboptimal experts. Furthermore, subgoal methods demonstrate better scalability in complex action spaces and are more effective at avoiding dead ends than low-level methods. Thus, in environments characterized by those properties, it is advisable to consider subgoal-based methods.

According to our experiments, the best-performing low-level search was usually BestFS with a confidence threshold. Although it is rather sensitive to the threshold value, which has to be optimized for each domain separately, we advocate using it as a standard baseline for further research in hierarchical search.

Finally, we identified a few easy-to-overlook mistakes in reporting the results that may lead to misleading conclusions. Most importantly, the reported search budget of the hierarchical methods must include all the visited states and not only the high-level nodes as used in some prior works.
