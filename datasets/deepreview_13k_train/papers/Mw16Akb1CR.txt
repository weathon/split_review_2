\title{Branches: A Fast Dynamic Programming and Branch & Bound Algorithm for Optimal Decision Trees}

\begin{abstract}
  Decision Tree (DT) Learning is a fundamental problem in Interpretable Machine Learning, yet it poses a formidable optimisation challenge. Despite numerous efforts dating back to the early 1990's, practical algorithms have only recently emerged, primarily leveraging Dynamic Programming (DP) and Branch \& Bound (B\&B) techniques. These methods fall into two categories: algorithms like DL8.5, MurTree and STreeD utilise an efficient DP strategy but lack effective bounds for pruning the search space; while algorithms like OSDT and GOSDT employ more efficient pruning bounds but at the expense of a less refined DP strategy. We introduce \method, a new algorithm that combines the strengths of both approaches. Using DP and B\&B with a novel analytical bound for efficient pruning, \method~offers both speed and sparsity optimisation. Unlike other methods, it also handles non-binary features. Theoretical analysis shows its lower complexity compared to existing methods, and empirical results confirm that \method~outperforms the state-of-the-art in speed, iterations, and optimality.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\label{sec:introduction}

Black-box models are ill-suited for contexts where decisions carry substantial ramifications. In healthcare, for instance, an erroneous negative diagnosis prediction could delay crucial treatment, leading to severe outcomes for patients. Likewise, in the criminal justice system, black-box models may obscure biases associated with factors such as race or gender, potentially resulting in unjust and discriminatory rulings. These considerations underscore the importance of adopting interpretable models in sensitive domains.

Decision Trees (DTs) are valued for their ability to generate simple decision rules from data, making them highly interpretable models. Unfortunately, DT optimization poses a significant challenge due to its NP-completeness, as established by \cite{laurent1976constructing}. Consequently, heuristic methods, such as ID3~\citep{quinlan1986induction}, C4.5~\citep{quinlan2014c4} and CART~\citep{breiman1984classification}, have been favoured historically. These methods construct DTs greedily by maximising some local purity metric for each chosen split, however, while they are fast and scalable, their greedy nature often leads to suboptimal and overly complex DTs, detracting from their interpretability. 

This suboptimality issue spurred researchers into investigating alternatives since the early 1990's, these alternatives are mainly based on \textit{Mathematical Programming}, they range from \textit{Continuous Optimisation}~\citep{bennett1996optimal, norouzi2015efficient, blanquero2021optimal} to \textit{Mixed Integer Programming (MIP)}~\citep{bertsimas2017optimal, verwer2019learning, gunluk2021optimal} and \textit{Satisfiability (SAT)}~\citep{bessiere2009minimising, narodytska2018learning, hu2020learning} and Constraint Programming (CP)~\citep{verhaeghe2020learning}. However, solving these Mathematical Programs scales poorly with large datasets and many features. Moreover, these approaches often fix the DT structure and only optimise the internal splits and leaf predictions, which is significantly less challenging than optimising both accuracy and DT structure (sparsity). Nonetheless, breakthroughs based on Dynamic Programming (DP) and Branch \& Bound (B\&B) have emerged recently \citep{hu2019optimal, aglin2020learning, lin2020generalized, demirovic2022murtree, mctavish2022fast, van2024necessary} and they provided the first practical algorithms for DT optimisation. These methods fall into two categories, algorithms like DL8.5, MurTree and STreeD operate at the level of the nodes, and consequently have an efficient DP strategy. However, they lack effective bounds pruning the search space. On the other hand, methods like OSDT and GOSDT operate at the level of DTs, this confers them better pruning bounds but at the expense of a less refined DP strategy.
In this work, we bridge the gap between the two categories. Our new DP and B\&B algorithm, \method, utilises an efficient DP strategy similarly to DL8.5, and employs a novel and more efficient analytical pruning bound than OSDT's and GOSDT's, called Purification Bound. For a comprehensive presentation of our approach, we frame it within a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework \citep{sutton2018reinforcement}, capitalizing on its convenient terminology for defining our recursive DP strategy. We analyze \method's computational complexity and demonstrate its superiority over existing literature. Furthermore,  we extensively compare \method~with the state-of-the-art. \method~not only achieves faster optimal convergence in most cases, it also always terminates in fewer iterations, thus validating our theoretical analysis. Our contributions are summarized below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We derive a novel analytical bound to prune the search space effectively.
    \item We develop \method~within a RL framework, its search strategy utilises DP and B\&B with our novel pruning bound, called Purification bound.
    \item \method~is not exclusively applicable to binary features.
    \item We analyse \method's computational complexity and show its superiority compared to the complexity bounds derived in the literature.
    \item We show that \method~outperforms state of the art methods on various real-world datasets with regard to optimal convergence, speed and number of iterations.
\end{itemize}



\section{Related work}


To seek optimal DTs, a significant body of literature was devoted to the \textit{Mathematical Programming} approach. We first review these approaches before delving into DP and B\&B methods. Early approaches tackled the problem within a \textit{Continuous Optimization} framework. \cite{bennett1992decision, bennett1994global, bennett1996optimal} formulated a Multi-Linear Program to optimize a non-linear and non-convex objective function over a polyhedral region. \cite{norouzi2015efficient} derived a smooth convex-concave upper bound on the empirical loss, which serves as a surrogate objective amenable to minimization via Stochastic Gradient Descent. In a recent development, \cite{blanquero2021optimal} introduced soft (randomized) decision rules at internal nodes and formulated a Non-Linear Program for which they minimise the expected misclassification cost. However, except for \citep{bennett1996optimal}, the solvers employed by these methods are locally optimal. Furthermore, \textit{Continuous Optimization} lacks the flexibility needed to model univariate Decision Trees (DTs), where each internal split tests only one feature. These DTs are of particular interest because they display better interpretability than multi-variate DTs. To address this limitation, a \textit{Mixed Integer Programming (MIP)} framework was rather considered in a multitude of research papers~\citep{bertsimas2017optimal, verwer2017learning, verwer2019learning, zhu2020scalable, gunluk2021optimal, boutilier2022shattering, aghaei2024strong}, and alternatively, some studies have explored the \textit{Satisfiability (SAT)} framework \citep{bessiere2009minimising, narodytska2018learning, avellaneda2020efficient, hu2020learning}. Despite the rich literature of \textit{Mathematical Programming} approaches, they suffer from serious limitations. The number of variables involved in the Mathematical Programs increases with the size of the dataset and the number of features, slowing down the solvers and severely limiting scalability. In addition, these methods often fix a DT structure a priori and only optimise its internal splits and leaf predictions. While this simplifies the problem, it misses the true optimal DT unless the optimal structure has been fixed in advance, which is highly unlikely. And finally, \textit{SAT} methods seek DTs that perfectly classify the dataset, as such, they are especially prone to overtraining.

In the last five years, DP and B\&B offered the first practical algorithms for Optimal DTs, and as such, triggered a paradigm shift from \textit{Mathematical Programming}. The first of these algorithms is OSDT~\citep{hu2019optimal}, it seeks to minimise a regularised misclassification error objective with a penalty on the number of leaves. To achieve this, OSDT employs a series of analytical bounds to prune the space of DTs (its search space). OSDT was followed shortly after by GOSDT~\citep{lin2020generalized} to generalise the approach to other objective functions. In contrast, DL8.5~\citep{aglin2020learning} is a fundamentally different approach, it is based on ideas from the earlier DL8 algorithm~\citep{nijssen2007mining, nijssen2010optimal}. DL8 operates on a lattice of itemsets as its search space, from which it mines the optimal DT, this is fundamentally distinct from the search space of DTs employed by OSDT and GOSDT. However, DL8 is a purely DP algorithm, and as such it is computationally and memory costly. DL8.5 addressed this issue by incorporating B\&B to DL8, which offers higher speed and better scalability, albeit without addressing sparsity (DL8.5 fixes a maximum depth but does not actively minimise the depth or the number of leaves). Additionally, DL8.5's B\&B strategy is based on the best solution found so far rather than more sophisticated analytical bounds, hindering its pruning capacity of the search space. Meanwhile, OSDT and GOSDT solve for sparsity but are comparatively slower due to their less refined DP strategy. Our work is motivated by this landscape, aiming to leverage the speed and scalability of methods like DL8.5 while addressing sparsity concerns and improving on the pruning efficiency of OSDT and GOSDT.

Additional recent advancements in the field include MurTree~\citep{demirovic2022murtree}, which enhances DL8.5 with similarity bounds and a tailored method for handling DTs of depth 2. \cite{mctavish2022fast} introduce a guessing strategy to navigate the search space, seeking solutions with performance akin to a reference ensemble model. \cite{demirovic2023blossom} improve on DL8.5 via a different search strategy that prioritizes expanding non-terminal branches before optimising grown branches, thus inducing an anytime algorithm called Blossom. \cite{van2024necessary} investigate separable objectives and constraints and introduce a generalised DP framework called STreeD.



\section{Problem formulation}


We consider classification problems with categorical features $X = \left( X^{\left( 1\right)}, \ldots, X^{\left( q\right)}\right)$ and class variable $Y \in \{ 1, \ldots, K\}$ such that:
\[
\forall{i \in \{ 1, \ldots q\}}: X^{\left( i\right)} \in \{ 1, \ldots, C_i\}, \; C_i \ge 2
\]
where $q \ge 2, K \ge 2$. We are provided with a dataset $\mathcal{D} = \{ \left( X_m, Y_m\right)\}_{m=1}^n$ of $n \ge 1$ examples. In the following sections, we define the notions of branches and sub-DTs that are key to our formulation.

\subsection{Branches}

A branch $l$ is a conjunction of clauses on the features of the following form:
\[
l = \bigwedge_{v=1}^{\splits\left( l\right)}\ind\Big\{ X^{\left( i_v\right)} = j_v\Big\}
\]
such that $\forall{v \in \{ 1, \ldots, \splits\left( l\right)\}}: i_v \in \{ 1, \ldots, q\},\, j_v \in \{ 1, \ldots, C_{i_v}\}$ and:
\begin{align*}
    \forall{v, v' \in \{ 1, \ldots \splits\left( l\right)\}}: v \neq v' \implies i_v \neq i_{v'} 
\end{align*}
This condition ensures that no feature is used in more than one clause within $l$. We refer to these clauses as rules or splits. $\splits\left( l\right)$ is the number of splits in $l$.

For any datum $X$, the valuation of $l$ for $X$ is denoted $l\left( X\right) \in \{ 0, 1\}$ and defined as follows:
\[
l\left( X\right) = 1 \iff \bigwedge_{v=1}^{\splits\left( l\right)}\ind\Big\{ X^{\left( i_v\right)} = j_v\Big\} = 1
\]
When $l\left( X\right) = 1$, we say that $X$ is in $l$ or that $l$ contains $X$. The branch containing all possible data is called the root and denoted $\Omega$. Since the valuation of $l$ for any datum remains invariant when reordering its splits, we represent $l$ uniquely by ordering its splits from the smallest feature index to the highest, i.e. we impose $1 \le i_1 < \ldots < i_{\splits\left( l\right)} \le q$. This unique representation is at the core of our DP memoisation.

In the following, we define the notion of splitting a branch. Let $i \in \{ 1, \ldots, q\} \setminus \{ i_1, \ldots, i_{\splits\left( l\right)}\}$ be an unused feature in the splits of $l$. We define the children of $l$ that stem from splitting $l$ with respect to $i$ as the set $\children\left( l, i\right) = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{C_i}\}$ where:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:children}
    \forall{j \in \{ 1, \ldots, C_i\}}: l_j = l \wedge \ind\Big\{ X^{\left( i\right)} = j\Big\}
\end{equation}
The dataset $\mathcal{D}$ provides an empirical distribution of the data. The probability that a datum is in $l$ is:
\[
\prob\left[ l\left( X\right) = 1\right] = \frac{n\left( l\right)}{n}
\]
where $n\left( l\right) = \sum_{m=1}^n l\left( X_m\right)$ is the number of data in $l$. Likewise, we want to define the probability that a datum is in $l$ and correctly classified. For this purpose, we define the predicted class in $l$ as:
\[
k^*\left( l\right) = \mathrm{Argmax}_{1 \le k \le K} \{ n_k\left( l\right)\}
\]
Where $n_k\left( l\right) = \sum_{m=1}^n l\left( X_m\right)\ind\{ Y_m = k\}$ is the number data in $l$ that are of class $k$. $k^*\left( l\right)$ is the majority class in $l$. Then the probability that a datum is in $l$ and correctly classified is:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:accuracy-branch}
    \objective\left( l\right) = \prob\left[ l\left( X\right) = 1, Y = k^*\left( l\right)\right] = \frac{n_{k^*\left( l\right)}\left( l\right)}{n}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Decision Trees}

Let $l$ be a branch, a sub-DT rooted in $l$ is a collection of branches $T = \big\{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\big\}$ that stem from a series of successive splittings of $l$, its children and so on. $T$ partitions $l$ in the following sense:
\[
\begin{cases}
    l = \bigvee_{u=1}^{|T|}l_u\\
    \forall{u, u' \in \{ 1, \ldots, |T|\}}: u \neq u' \implies l_u \wedge l_{u'} = 0
\end{cases}
\]
We denote $\splits\left( T\right)$ the number of splitting steps it took to construct $T$ from $l$. For any datum $X$ in $l$, $T$ predicts the majority class of the branch $l_u$ containing $X$:
\[
T\left( X\right) = \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}l_u\left( X\right)k^*\left( l_u\right)
\]
Now we can define the proportion of data in $\mathcal{D}$ that is in $l$ and is correctly classified by $T$:
\[
\objective\left( T\right) = \prob\left[ l\left( X\right) = 1, T\left( X\right) = Y\right] = \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\objective\left( l_u\right)
\]
The additivity property is due to $\{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$ forming a partition of $l$. A DT is a sub-DT that is rooted in $\Omega$. Let $T$ be a DT, since $\Omega\left( X\right) = 1$ for any datum $X$, then:
\[
\objective\left( T\right) = \prob\left[ \Omega\left( X\right) = 1, T\left( X\right) = Y\right] = \prob\left[ T\left( X\right) = Y\right]
\]
which is the accuracy of $T$. Maximising accuracy is not a suitable objective, it overlooks sparsity. To incorporate sparsity, we rather consider the following regularised objective:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:objective}
    \objectiver\left( T\right) = -\lambda\splits\left( T\right) + \objective\left( T\right)
\end{equation}
with $\lambda \in \left[ 0, 1\right]$ a penalty parameter penalising DTs with too many splits. This objective is employed by CART during the pruning phase, it was also considered by \cite{bertsimas2017optimal} and recently by \cite{chaouki24a}. \cite{hu2019optimal, lin2020generalized} use a slightly different version, where the total number of leaves is penalised instead.

\subsection{Markov Decision Process (MDP)}
\label{sec:mdp}

To frame the problem within a Reinforcement Learning framework, we define the following MDP.

\textbf{State space:} The set of all possible sub-DTs. A state with only one branch $T = \{ l\}$ is called a unit-state. To make the notation lighter, we just denote it $l$. There are special types of states called absorbing states. A state is absorbing if all actions transition back to it and yield $0$ reward. The initial state is always the root $\Omega$.

\textbf{Action space:} At every state $T$, we denote $\action\left( T\right)$ the set of permissible actions at $T$. We first define this set of actions for unit-states, then we generalise it to all states. Let $l = \bigwedge_{v=1}^{\splits\left( l\right)}\ind\{ X^{\left( i_v\right)} = j_v\}$ be a unit-state, there are two types of actions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The terminal action $\overline{a}$. It transitions from $l$ to an absorbing state $\overline{l}$. We denote $l \xlongrightarrow{\overline{a}} \overline{l}$.
    \item Split actions. The set of possible split actions at $l$ is $\{ 1, \ldots, q\} \setminus \{ i_1, \ldots, i_{\splits\left( l\right)}\}$ the set of unused features by $l$. Let $i$ be a split action, taking $i$ transitions $l$ to state $T = \children\left( l, i\right)$, defined in \cref{eq:children}. We denote the transition with $l \xlongrightarrow{i} T = \children\left( l, i\right)$.
\end{itemize}
Thus $\action\left( l\right) = \{ \overline{a}\} \cup \{ 1, \ldots, q\} \setminus \{ i_1, \ldots, i_{\splits\left( l\right)}\}$. When $\splits\left( l\right) = q$, then $\action\left( l\right) = \{ \overline{a}\}$ and we can only transition to $\overline{l}$. We can now generalise the set of permissible actions to any state $T = \{ l_1, \ldots l_{|T|}\}$ as $\action\left( T\right) = \action\left( l_1\right)\times \ldots \times \action\left( l_{|T|}\right)$. Taking action $a = \left( a_1, \ldots, a_{|T|}\right) \in \action\left( T\right)$ in $T$ is equivalent to taking each action $a_u$ in $l_u$ for $1 \le u \le |T|$, thus performing the transition:
\[
T \xlongrightarrow{a} T' = \bigcup_{u=1}^{|T|}T'_u, \; \; \forall{u \in \{ 1, \ldots, |T|\}}: l_u \xlongrightarrow{a_u} T'_u
\]

\textbf{Reward function:} For any state $T$ and action $a \in \action\left( T\right)$, $r\left( T, a\right)$ is the reward of taking action $a$ in $T$. Similarly to the definition of the actions, we first define the reward for unit-states and then we generalise it to all states. Let $l$ be a unit-state and $a \in \action\left( l\right)$ then we have:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $a$ is a split action, then $r\left( l, a\right) = -\lambda$ regardless of $l$ (except if $l$ is absorbing).
    \item If $a = \overline{a}$, then $r\left( l, \overline{a}\right) = \objective\left( l\right)$ as per \cref{eq:accuracy-branch}.
    \item If $l = \overline{l}$, i.e. $l$ is an absorbing state, then $r\left( \overline{l}, a\right) = 0$.
\end{itemize}
For any state $T = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$ and action $a = \left( a_1, \ldots, a_{|T|}\right) \in \action\left( T\right)$, we define the reward as:
\[
r\left( T, a\right) = \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}r\left( l_u, a_u\right)
\]
A policy $\pi$ maps each state $T$ to one of its actions $\pi\left( T\right) \in \action\left( T\right)$. From a state $T$, the return of policy $\pi$ is defined as the cumulative reward of following $\pi$ starting from $T$:
\[
\rf^\pi\left( T\right) = \sum_{t=0}^\infty r\left( T_t, \pi\left( T_t\right)\right)
\]
where $T_0 = T$ and $\forall{t \ge 0}: T_{t} \xlongrightarrow{\pi\left( T_{t}\right)} T_{t+1}$. Each policy is evaluated by its return from the initial state $\Omega$, our objective is to find the optimal policy as we shall justify shortly. First, we need to ensure that there are no divergence issues related to the infinite sum in the definition of the return.
\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:tree-policy}
    Let $\pi$ be a policy, $l$ a unit-state and consider $T_0 = l$ and $\forall{t \ge 0}: T_{t} \xlongrightarrow{\pi\left( T_{t}\right)} T_{t+1}$. Then, there exists $\tau \ge 0$ such that for any $t \ge \tau$, $T_t = \{ \overline{l_1}, \ldots, \overline{l_{|T_\tau|}}\}$ is an absorbing state. In which case we call $T_l^{\pi} = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T_\tau|}\}$ the sub-DT of $\pi$ rooted in $l$. If $l=\Omega$ we abbreviate the notation $T_\Omega^{\pi} \equiv T^\pi$ and call $T^\pi$ the DT of $\pi$.
\end{proposition}
\cref{prop:tree-policy} states that all policies eventually arrive in an absorbing state after a finite number of steps, regardless of where they start. Therefore all policies have finite returns. Now let us justify why we seek the optimal policy.
\begin{proposition}
    \label{prop:return-objective}
    For any policy $\pi$ and unit-state $l$, the return of $\pi$ from $l$ satisfies:
    \[
    \rf^\pi\left( l\right) = \objectiver\left( T_l^\pi\right) = -\lambda\splits\left( T_l^\pi\right) + \objective\left( T_l^\pi\right)
    \]
    In particular $\rf^\pi\left( \Omega\right) = \objectiver\left( T^\pi\right) = -\lambda\splits\left( T^\pi\right) + \objective\left( T^\pi\right)$.
\end{proposition}
\cref{prop:return-objective} links the return of a policy to the regularised accuracy of its sub-DT. On the other hand, since any DT $T$ is constructed with successive splittings starting from $\Omega$, there always exists a policy $\pi$ such that $T^\pi = T$, and therefore $\rf^\pi\left( \Omega\right) = \objectiver\left( T\right)$. This result provides the equivalence between finding the optimal DT and the optimal policy:
\[
T^* = \mathrm{Argmax}_T\{ \objectiver\left( T\right)\}, \; \pi^* = \mathrm{Argmax}_\pi \{ \rf^\pi\left( \Omega\right)\}
\]
in which case the optimal DT is the DT of $\pi^*$, i.e. $T^* = T^{\pi^*}$. To conclude this section, our objective is now is to find $\pi^*$ and then deduce $T^*$ as $T^{\pi^*}$. We abbreviate the notation $\rf^{\pi^*} \equiv \rf^*$.



\section{The algorithm: \method}


\method~is a Value Iteration algorithm~\citep{sutton2018reinforcement} that is enhanced with a structured B\&B search. To describe this algorithm, it is convenient to further introduce the state-action return quantity. For any policy $\pi$, state $T$ and action $a \in \action\left( T\right)$, let $T \xlongrightarrow{a} T_1$ and $\forall{t \ge 1}: T_t \xlongrightarrow{\pi\left( T_t\right)} T_{t+1}$.
Then the state-action return $\qf^\pi\left( T, a\right)$ is the cumulative reward of taking action $a$ first, then following $\pi$:
\[
\qf^{\pi}\left( T, a\right) = r\left( T, a\right) + \sum_{t=1}^\infty r\left( T_t, \pi\left( T_t\right)\right) = r\left( T, a\right) + \rf^\pi\left( T_1\right)
\]
Given the optimal state-action returns $\qf^{*}\left( T, a\right) = \qf^{\pi^*}\left( T, a\right)$, we can deduce the optimal policy:
\[
\pi^*\left( T\right) =\mathrm{Argmax}_{a \in \action\left( T\right)}\qf^{*}\left( T, a\right)
\]
In the next section, we show how \method~estimates these optimal state-action returns.

\subsection{Estimating the optimal state-action returns $\qf^*\left( l, a\right)$}

Let $l$ be a non-absorbing unit-state and $a \in \action\left( l\right)$, we denote $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ the estimate of $\qf^*\left( l, a\right)$. For the terminal action $\overline{a}$, $\qf^*\left( l, \overline{a}\right)$ is directly accessible from the data via:
\begin{equation}
    \qf\left( l, \overline{a}\right) = \qf^*\left( l, \overline{a}\right) = r\left( l, \overline{a}\right) = \objective\left( l\right) = \frac{n_{k^*\left( l\right)}\left( l\right)}{n} \label{eq:state-action-estimate-terminal}
\end{equation}
For a split action $a \in \action\left( l\right)\setminus \{ \overline{a}\}$, such that $l \xlongrightarrow{a} T = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$, $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ is defined according to the Bellman Optimality Equations below.
\begin{proposition}[Bellman Equations]
    \label{prop:bellman}
    Let $l$ be a non-absorbing unit-state, $a \in \action\left( l\right) \setminus \{ \overline{a}\}$ a split action such that $l \xlongrightarrow{a} T = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$. Then we have:
    \begin{align}
        \qf^*\left( l, a\right) &= -\lambda + \rf^*\left( T\right) =  -\lambda + \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\rf^*\left( l_u\right)\nonumber \\
        \forall{u \in \{ 1, \ldots, |T|\}}: \rf^*\left( l_u\right) &= \qf^*\left( l_u, \pi^*\left( l_u\right)\right) = \max_{a \in \action\left( l_u\right)}\qf^*\left( l_u, a\right) \nonumber
    \end{align}
\end{proposition}
\cref{prop:bellman} suggests the following recursive definitions of the estimates:
\begin{align}
    \qf\left( l, a\right) &= -\lambda + \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\rf\left( l_u\right) \label{eq:state-action-estimate}\\
    \forall{u \in \{ 1, \ldots, |T|\}}: \rf\left( l_u\right) &= \max_{a \in \action\left( l_u\right)}\qf\left( l_u, a\right)\label{eq:state-estimate}
\end{align}
The estimate $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ in \cref{eq:state-action-estimate} can only be calculated if the estimates $\rf\left( l_u\right)$ in \cref{eq:state-estimate} are available. Otherwise we initialise $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ with \cref{eq:upper-bound-state-action-return} according to \cref{prop:branches-bound}.
\begin{proposition}[Purification Bound]
    \label{prop:branches-bound}
    For any non-absorbing unit-state $l$ and split action $a \in \action\left( l\right)\setminus \{ \overline{a}\}$, we define the Purification Bound estimates:
    \begin{align}
        \qf\left( l, a\right) &= -\lambda + \prob\left[ l\left( X\right) = 1\right] = -\lambda + \frac{n\left( l\right)}{n} \label{eq:upper-bound-state-action-return}\\
        \rf\left( l\right) &= \max\{ \objective\left( l\right), -\lambda + \prob\left[ l\left( X\right) = 1\right]\} = \max\Bigg\{ \frac{n_{k^*\left( l\right)}\left( l\right)}{n}, -\lambda + \frac{n\left( l\right)}{n}\Bigg\} \label{eq:upper-bound-state-return}
    \end{align}
    Then the estimates $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ and $\rf\left( l\right)$ are upper bounds on $\qf^*\left( l, a\right)$ and $\rf^*\left( l\right)$ respectively.
\end{proposition}
In the following, we provide an intuition behind the Purification Bound. If the split action $a$ yields $l \xlongrightarrow{a} T = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$ such that all the data in the resulting children branches $l_u$ are correctly classified (in which case, the branches $l_u$ are called pure), then:
\[
\qf^*\left( l, a\right) = -\lambda + \objective\left( T\right) = -\lambda + \prob\left[ T\left( X\right) = Y, l\left( X\right) = 1\right] = -\lambda + \prob\left[ l\left( X\right) = 1\right]
\]
Thus the bound \cref{eq:upper-bound-state-action-return} coincides exactly with the optimal state-action value of an action that \textit{purifies} $l$ (if it exists), hence the name \textit{Purification Bound}.

Now we can straightforwardly define $\qf\left( T, a\right)$ for any state $T$. Consider a state $T = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$ and an action $a = \left( a_1, \ldots, a_{|T|}\right) \in \action\left( T\right)$ such that:
\[
\begin{cases}
    T \xlongrightarrow{a} T' =  \bigcup_{u=1}^{|T|} T'_u\\
    \forall{u \in \{ 1, \ldots, |T|\}}: l_u \xlongrightarrow{a_u} T'_u
\end{cases}
\]
Then we have the following:
\begin{align*}
    \qf^*\left( T, a\right) &= r\left( T, a\right) + \rf^*\left( T'\right)\\
    &= \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}r\left( l_u, a_u\right) + \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\rf^*\left( T'_u\right) = \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\left( r\left( l_u, a_u\right) + \rf^*\left( T'_u\right)\right) = \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\qf^*\left( l_u, a_u\right)
\end{align*}
Therefore, this suggests defining the estimate $\qf\left( T, a\right)$ directly with:
\begin{equation}
    \qf\left( T, a\right) = \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\qf\left( l_u, a_u\right)
    \label{eq:state-action-estimate-general}
\end{equation}
\paragraph{Summary:} For any unit-state $l$, the estimate $\qf\left( l, \overline{a}\right)$ for the terminal action $\overline{a}$ is known in advance and calculated with \cref{eq:state-action-estimate-terminal}. For any split action $a \in \action\left( l\right)\setminus \{ \overline{a}\}$, $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ is calculated with \cref{eq:state-action-estimate} when estimates for the children are available, otherwise it is initialised with \cref{eq:upper-bound-state-action-return}. For a general state $T$, the estimate is deduced straightforwardly via \cref{eq:state-action-estimate-general}.

\subsection{The Search strategy}
\label{sec:branches-strategy}

Initially, all the non-terminal unit-states $l$ are labelled as unvisited and incomplete, which means that $\rf^*\left( l\right)$ are still unknown. The absorbing states are labelled as complete on the other hand. Moreover, the state-action pairs $\left( l, a\right)$ (for non-absorbing unit-states $l$) are also labelled as incomplete since we do not know $\qf^*\left( l, a\right)$ either. We initialise an empty memo where the encountered state values estimates $\rf\left( l\right)$ are stored. Each iteration of \method~follows the Value Iteration pipeline below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Selection:} Initialise an empty list \textit{path}. Starting from the root $l = \Omega$, choose the action maximising the optimal state-action value estimate:
    \[
    a = \mathrm{Argmax}_{a' \in \action\left( l\right)} \qf\left( l, a'\right)
    \]
    Append $\left( l, a\right)$ to \textit{path} and transition $l \xlongrightarrow{a} T = \{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$. Choose an incomplete unit-state $l_u \in T$ and make it the current state $l = l_u$, \textit{this choice can be arbitrary or according to some heuristic}. Repeat this process until reaching an unvisited or absorbing unit-state $l$. Note that the \textit{path} list does not include this final state $l$.
    \item \textbf{Expansion:} If $l$ is absorbing, then we move to the Backpropagation step. Otherwise we estimate $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ for all $a \in \action\left( l\right)$ as explained below.
    
    For the terminal action, we set $\qf\left( l, \overline{a}\right) = \objective\left( l\right)$ as per \cref{eq:state-action-estimate-terminal} and we label $\left( l, \overline{a}\right)$ as complete. For any split action $a \in \action\left( l\right) \setminus \{ \overline{a}\}$, let $l \xlongrightarrow{a} T=\{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$. We calculate $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ according to \cref{eq:state-action-estimate}:
    \begin{align*}
        \qf\left( l, a\right) = -\lambda + \sum_{u=1}^{|T|}\rf\left( l_u\right)
    \end{align*}
    where for each $l_u \in T$, $\rf\left( l_u\right)$ is retrieved from the memo in case $l_u$ is labelled as visited, otherwise $\rf\left( l_u\right)$ is initialised with \cref{eq:upper-bound-state-return}:
    \[
    \rf\left( l_u\right) = \max\Bigg\{ \frac{n_{k^*\left( l_u\right)}\left( l_u\right)}{n}, -\lambda + \frac{n\left( l_u\right)}{n}\Bigg\}
    \]
    and we store $\rf\left( l_u\right)$ in the memo. If all children $l_u$ are complete, then we label $\left( l, a\right)$ as complete and we have $\qf\left( l, a\right) = \qf^*\left( l, a\right)$. Once we have calculated $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ for all actions $a \in \action\left( l\right)$, we deduce the state value estimate of $l$ as follows:
    \[
    \rf\left( l\right) = \max_{a \in \action\left( l\right)}\qf\left( l, a\right)
    \]
    If $a^* = \mathrm{Argmax}_{a \in \action\left( l\right)}\qf\left( l, a\right)$ is such that $\left( l, a^*\right)$ is complete, then we label $l$ as complete and we have $\rf^*\left( l\right) = \rf\left( l\right) = \qf\left( l, a^*\right) = \qf^*\left( l, a^*\right)$.
    \item \textbf{Backpropagation:} Update $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ and $\rf\left( l\right)$ for all $\left( l, a\right)$ in \textit{path} via Backward recursion. For $j=length(path)-1, \ldots, 0$, let $\left( l, a\right) = path\left[ j\right]$ with $l \xlongrightarrow{a} T=\{ l_1, \ldots, l_{|T|}\}$, then we update $\qf\left( l, a\right)$ and $\rf\left( l\right)$ with \cref{eq:state-action-estimate} and \cref{eq:state-estimate} respectively.
    We update $\rf\left( l\right)$ in the memo. If all children $l_u$ are complete, we label $\left( l, a\right)$ as complete. If $a^* = \mathrm{Argmax}_{a \in \action\left( l\right)}\qf\left( l, a\right)$ is such that $\left( l, a^*\right)$ is complete, then we label $l$ as complete.
\end{itemize}
\method~terminates when the root $\Omega$ is complete. 



\section{Theoretical Analysis}

\label{sec:branches-theory}

In this section, we prove the optimality of \method~in \cref{thm:branches-optimality} and we analyse its computational complexity in \cref{thm:branches-complexity-dependent} and \cref{cor:branches-complexity-independent}. 
\begin{theorem}[Optimality of \method]
    \label{thm:branches-optimality}
    When \method~terminates, the optimal policy is the greedy policy with respect to the estimated state-action values $\qf\left( l, a\right)$, which means that for any state $T$:
    \[
    \pi^*\left( T\right) =\mathrm{Argmax}_{a \in \action\left( T\right)}\qf\left( T, a\right)
    \]
\end{theorem}
To the best of our knowledge, \cite{hu2019optimal} are the only authors providing a complexity analysis of their algorithm in the DP and B\&B literature of optimal DTs. \cite[Theorem E.2]{hu2019optimal} derives an upper bound on the total number of DT evaluations performed by OSDT. There is an inaccuracy in the result, the sum should be up to the maximum depth of the optimal DT rather than the maximum number of its leaves. We provide a corrected version and discuss it in \cref{thm:osdt-complexity}. To compare the computational complexities of OSDT and \method, we analyse the number of branch evaluations, i.e. calculations of $\objective\left( l\right)$, performed by \method~to reach termination.
\begin{theorem}[Problem-dependent complexity of \method]
    \label{thm:branches-complexity-dependent}
    Let $\Gamma\left( q, C, \lambda\right)$ denote the total number of branch evaluations performed by \method~for an instance of the classification problem with $q \ge 2$ features, $0 < \lambda \le 1$ the penalty parameter, and $C \ge 2$ the number of categories per feature. Then, $\Gamma\left( q, C, \lambda\right)$ satisfies the following bound:
    \[
    \Gamma\left( q, C, \lambda\right) \le \sum_{h = 0}^\kappa \left( q - h\right)C^{h+1}\binom{q}{h}; \; \kappa = \min\Bigg\{ \floor*{\splits\left( T^*\right) - 1 + \frac{1 - \objective\left( T^*\right)}{\lambda}}, q \Bigg\}
    \]
\end{theorem}
\begin{corollary}[Problem-independent complexity of \method]
    \label{cor:branches-complexity-independent}
    Let $\Gamma\left( q, \lambda, C\right)$ be defined as in \cref{thm:branches-complexity-dependent}, then it satisfies:
    \[
    \Gamma\left( q, C, \lambda\right) \le \sum_{h = 0}^\kappa \left( q-h\right)C^{h+1}\binom{q}{h}; \; \kappa = \min\Bigg\{ \floor*{\frac{1}{K\lambda}}-1, q \Bigg\}
    \]
\end{corollary}

It is difficult to analytically compare the the bound in \cref{cor:branches-complexity-independent} with the bound in \citep[Theorem E.2]{hu2019optimal}. For this reason, we compare them numerically on some reasonable instances of the problem. \cref{tab:branches-complexities} clearly shows the vast computational gains that \method~offers over OSDT. This claim is further validated in our experiments. We note however, that the immense numbers upper bounding the complexity of OSDT \textit{do not reflect OSDT's true complexity} but rather that the bound is too loose. Indeed, the reasoning behind \citep[Theorem E.2]{hu2019optimal} pertains to counting all the possible DTs which depths do not exceed the maximum depth of the optimal DT, it does not analyse OSDT's pruning capacity. In fact, it is unclear how such analysis could be performed with OSDT's pruning bounds. On the other hand, the Purification bound we provide in \cref{prop:branches-bound} offers a natural pruning strategy that allows for such analysis.

We compare \method~with the state of the art based on the following metrics: \textbf{optimal convergence}, \textbf{execution time} and \textbf{number of iterations}. We provide the source code of our implementation in the supplementary material.

We employ 11 datasets from the UCI repository, which we chose because of their frequent use in benchmarking optimal Decision Tree algorithms. For each dataset, different types of encodings are considered: Suffix -l indicates a One-Hot Encoding where the last category of each feature is dropped, likewise -f drops the first category, -o is for an Ordinal Encoding. We chose different encodings because they yield problems with varying degrees of difficulty. Moreover, the state of the art algorithms exclusively consider binary features, thus necessitating a preliminary binary encoding. This seemingly benign detail can significantly harm performance by introducing unnecessary splits as we explain in \cref{sec:branches-ordinal-vs-binary}. \method~can sidestep this issue since it is directly applicable to an Ordinal Encoding of the data. We set a time limit of 5 minutes for all experiments. \cref{tab:datasets} summarises the characteristics of the datasets we consider.

\cref{tab:branches-experiments-gosdt} shows that \method~outperforms OSDT, PyGOSDT and GOSDT on almost all the experiments, with GOSDT being the most competitive method. We especially notice the large computational gains achieved by applying \method~to the datasets in their original form through Ordinal Encoding. On the monk datasets, while both GOSDT and \method~are always optimal, \method~is faster, sometimes significantly. On the other hand, OSDT and PyGOSDT are only optimal for monk1-l, and they are prohibitively slow. There are a few datasets where \method~does not perform the best. On Mushroom, OSDT and PyGOSDT surprisingly outperform both \method~and GOSDT; on tic-tac-toe, car-eval and balance, GOSDT and \method~terminate but GOSDT is faster. However, we suspect that this is mainly due to GOSDT's optimised C++ implementation, which confers it an advantage over \method's Python implementation. In fact, the difference in speed between these programming languages is very evident from the large gap in execution times between GOSDT and PyGOSDT. We note however, that \method~always converges in fewer iterations than GOSDT, around 10 times less in many cases. This corroborates our complexity analysis in \cref{sec:branches-theory}, indicating that our Purification bound improves the pruning efficiency of the search algorithms. A future C++ implementation of \method~will further improve \method's scalability, especially since it is amenable to true parallel computing. Indeed, the Algorithmic steps Selection, Expansion and Backpropagation can all be run through parallel synchronous threads. Unfortunately, Python is limited in its parallel computing capacity, it does not permit multithreading, and its multiprocessing module requires copying the data, which greatly slows down the algorithm and loses all the computational benefits of parallel computing.

In \cref{tab:branches-experiments-dl85}, for a fair comparison, since \method~and GOSDT do not constrain the depth of the searched solutions, we impose a similar condition on the DL8.5, MurTree and STreeD. In the implementation of STreeD (as of version v1.3.1), a maximum depth lower than $20$ has to be specified. For this reason, we set the maximum depth to $20$, we further impose a maximum number of nodes of $80$ to avoid memory issues. \cref{tab:branches-experiments-dl85} shows that only \method~and STreeD truly solve for sparsity, which means that upon terminating they return the optimal solution with respect to $\objectiver$. 
To see this, note the many cases where MurTree terminates, i.e. it does not reach $TO$, while returning a suboptimal solution. This happens for monk1-f, monk2-l, monk2-f, monk3-l, monk3-f, kr-vs-kp, zoo, and balance. 
As for DL8.5, it was evident that it does not solve for sparsity since it does not optimise the regularised objective $\objectiver$. 
The second take-away from \cref{tab:branches-experiments-dl85} is that \method~outperforms STreeD on almost all the datasets except mushroom. Moreover, due to its heuristic nature, CART never achieves optimality in these experiments.



\section{Conclusion, limitations and future work}

\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced \method, a new DP and B\&B algorithm for constructing optimal Decision Trees. \method~leverages the Purification bound in \cref{prop:branches-bound} to prune the search space more effectively than state of the art algorithms. We analysed \method's complexity and proved that our complexity bound is significantly smaller than those provided in the literature. This claim was further substantiated through our experiments, we showed that \method~always achieves optimal convergence in fewer iterations than GOSDT, leading to faster execution times in most cases. Moreover, unlike the state of the art, \method~is not limited to datasets with binary features, which yields substantial gain in performance compared to cases with binary encoded data.

For now \method~is limited to categorical features. In fact, all the cited optimal DT methods were developed for categorical features and are applied to numerical features through discretisation. Furthermore, \method~is currently implemented in Python, which hinders its execution times sometimes compared to the C++ implementations. The large gap in performance between PyGOSDT and GOSDT motivates a future implementation of \method~in C++. Since \method~far outperforms the existing Python methods and is even competitive and often better than GOSDT, we believe that a future C++ implementation of \method~will yield further great improvements over the state of the are, especially in scalability.
