\title{PolyPythias: Stability and Outliers across Fifty Language Model Pre-Training Runs}

\begin{abstract}
Understanding the stability of language model pre-training and its effects on downstream performance is still understudied. 
Prior work shows that the training process can yield significantly different results in response to slight variations in initial conditions, e.g., the random seed.
Crucially, resources to study pre-training stability in language models are still lacking, especially for decoder-only models.
We introduce the PolyPythias, a set of 45 new training runs for the Pythia model suite: 9 new seeds across 5 model sizes, from 14M to 410M parameters, resulting in about 7k new checkpoints that we release.
Using these new 45 training runs, in addition to the 5 already available, we study the effects of different initial conditions determined by the seed---i.e., parameters' initialisation and data order---on (i) downstream performance, (ii) learned linguistic representations, and (iii) emergence of training phases.
In addition to common scaling behaviours, our analyses generally reveal highly consistent training dynamics across both model sizes and initial conditions.
Additionally, the new seeds for each model allow us to identify outlier training runs and delineate their characteristics.
Our findings show the potential of using these methods to predict training stability.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


Training deep learning models, including contemporary language models (LMs), is an inherently stochastic process in which randomness factors (Pecher et al., 2024) such as the initialisation of model parameters and data shuffling play a crucial role (Pham et al., 2020; Gundersen et al., 2023). Prior work on LM adaptation (Dodge et al., 2020; McCoy et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2021; Mosbach et al., 2021; inter alia) and pre-training (D’Amour et al., 2022; Madaan et al., 2024; Alzahrani et al., 2024; inter alia) shows that even slight variations in these randomness factors can lead to substantially different outcomes. Specifically, training multiple times using the same implementation, hardware, dataset, and hyperparameters can, nonetheless, lead to large deviations in the final performance. Thus, conclusions drawn from a particular training run cannot easily be generalised to the underlying model class (Sellam et al., 2022; Goldfarb-Tarrant et al., 2024). Stability to randomness factors is still underexplored in LM pre-training, especially for recent decoder-only architectures (e.g., Radford et al., 2019). Moreover, studying learning dynamics while ensuring coverage across these randomness factors is increasingly compute-intensive due to the size of contemporary LMs and datasets.  

In this work, we define stability as the change in a metric of interest (e.g., validation loss) caused by small-scale changes in randomness factors and quantify it using the standard deviation of that metric (see Du & Nguyen, 2023 for other approaches to quantify stability). To provide a basis for analysing the stability of LMs to randomness factors (e.g., their training dynamics or final performance) without incurring the costs to train contemporary LMs, we introduce the PolyPythias: an extension of the Pythia model suite (Biderman et al., 2023) trained on the Pile dataset (Gao et al., 2020), for which we release 9 new training runs for 5 model sizes, from 14M up to 410M parameters. These new 45 training runs—in addition to the 5 already available in the suite—cover approximately 7k checkpoints across pre-training, and enable us to analyse training stability with respect to model size, parameter initialisation, and data order as quantified by metrics along the entire model training pipeline: downstream performance and consistency of predictions (§3), shifts in linguistic representations (§4), and dynamics of the model parameters and training phases (§5).  

The study of the PolyPythias allows us to identify which training dynamics are stable across model sizes and randomness factors and investigate which characteristics indicate unstable (or outlier) training runs. Furthermore, it enables us to shed more light on the findings from prior research into training dynamics. Specifically, our results $(\S6)$ show that language modelling is largely stable and follows predictable scaling laws with respect to downstream performance. Across training, we identify consistent learning phases: an initial learning phase between steps $10^{3}–10^{4}$ and a critical learning phase between steps $10^{4}–10^{5}$ . Using training maps constructed from statistics of the model parameters, we identify the characteristics and early signals indicating when a training run begins diverging from these common dynamics. Finally, measuring training dynamics throughout the model allows us to not just identify these commonalities across training, but also enables us to shed more light on the findings from prior research into training dynamics.  

Additional Related Work and Limitations. Prior work that released multi-seed model suites includes Sellam et al. (2022) who released the MultiBERTs, a set of 25 BERT-base (final) checkpoints trained with similar hyper-parameters to the original encoder-only BERT architecture (Devlin et al., 2019) but with different random seeds. However, only 28 intermediate checkpoints are available for 5 of the runs, and the release is limited to encoder-only models in a single size. Karamcheti et al. (2021) introduced 10 GPT-2 (124M and 355M parameters; Radford et al., 2019) training runs, each with 600 intermediate checkpoints, but still limited to two model sizes. More recently, Madaan et al. (2024) trained a suite of 10 Llama-2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023) models initialised with different random seeds on 210B tokens, analysing 21 intermediate checkpoints which, however, remain publicly unavailable. PolyPythia compares favourably to these suites by spanning 5 model sizes with 154 checkpoints per run, using 10 seeds per model, for a total of almost 7k checkpoints trained on publicly available data. A limitation of our suite is that it spans model sizes up to 410M parameters. While our suite is capped at 410M parameters, this choice reflects computational constraints, prioritising seed exploration and checkpoint granularity over scaling up model size. Our aim is to provide an additional resource for researchers unable to train even 410M parameter models from scratch, thus enabling them to study training stability across model sizes. To further this aim, we investigate whether the training dynamics of smaller models can serve as reliable indicators of the dynamics in their larger counterparts.  

In the following sections, we describe the PolyPythias release (§2) and how we use the multiple training runs per model size to study the stability of models across various aspects of the model training pipeline $(\S3{-}\S5)$ . We conclude by combining the insights from the individual analyses $(\S6)$ to make high-level observations about LM stability with respect to randomness factors.  



\section{2 EXTENDING THE PYTHIA SUITE: POLYPYTHIAS RELEASE DESCRIPTION  }


The Pythia model suite (Biderman et al., 2023)—with its open data and weights for multiple model sizes, intermediate checkpoints, and detailed reporting of the training configurations—allows researchers to study the learning dynamics of realistic LMs without the need to train them from scratch. Since its release, the suite has been extensively used to study, e.g., LMs’ learning dynamics (Michaelov & Bergen, 2023; Arnold et al., 2024), memorisation patterns (Biderman et al., 2024a; Lesci et al., 2024), and biases (Hu et al., 2024). The suite is composed of 10 models ranging in size from 14M to 12B parameters trained on the Pile dataset (Gao et al., 2020; Biderman et al., 2022), a 300B-token curated collection of English documents.1 All models are trained using the same data. Specifically, the dataset is shuffled and “packed” into sequences of $2{,}049^{2}$ tokens. Training was performed using a cosine learning rate schedule with warm-up, and using a batch size of 1,024 sequences, resulting in exactly 143k optimisation steps. In the original Pythia suite, for each model size, a single training run is available and consists of 154 checkpoints: at initialisation (step 0), log-spaced up to step 1k (steps $1,2,...,512)$ , and every 1k steps afterwards (steps 1k-143k).  

We consider models with 14M, 31M, 70M, 160M, and 410M parameters. For each model size, we release3 a set of 9 additional training runs resulting in about 7k new checkpoints. Each training run uses the same hyperparameters, codebase, data, and hardware as Biderman et al. (2023) but varies the seeds for parameter initialisation and batch composition.4 We use the standard (i.e., non-deduplicated) version of the Pile and release5the pre-tokenised and pre-shuffled datasets corresponding to the different seeds to facilitate future work on the effect of data order (e.g., memorisation).  



\section{3 DOWNSTREAM PERFORMANCE BENCHMARKS  }


We start our analysis by asking a key question, especially relevant for practitioners: “How stable is model performance and behaviour on downstream tasks to randomness factors?”. We first study how performance varies across seeds for a given model size. Then, we study how models’ predictions and learned biases vary throughout the training process and across seeds.  



\section{3.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP  }


Following Biderman et al. (2023), we measure accuracy on a set of multiple-choice tasks: ARC (Easy), ARC (Challenge), LAMBADA, Logiqa, Piqa, SciQ, WinoGrande, and WSC. We also measure how predictions agree across seeds and throughout training by computing the Cohen’s $\kappa$ on the individual multiple-choice answers, where $\kappa=1$ means perfect agreement while $\kappa\!=\!0$ denotes agreement at the chance level. Specifically, we define the inter-seed agreement as the Cohen’s $\kappa$ between the predictions of a model trained with a particular seed and the same model trained with seed 0. Additionally, we define self-consistency as the Cohen’s $\kappa$ between the predictions of a model at the last checkpoint and any previous one. Further, we measure a model’s gender bias using the BLiMP (Gender Agreement), CrowS-Pairs (Gender), Simple Co-occurrence Bias benchmarks for which we report, respectively, accuracy, proportions of times the stereotypical answer is chosen or male is preferred.  

We perform the evaluation using the Language Model Evaluation Harness framework6 (Gao et al., 2024; Biderman et al., 2024b). To limit computational costs while being able to track model behaviour across training, for each size and seed, we evaluate performance on a subset of the available checkpoints. Specifically, we use checkpoints at (log-spaced) steps $0,1,2,...,512,1$ k, and from step 3k onwards we choose every $\mathrm{10k}$ -th step up to 143k, the final checkpoint.  



\section{3.2 RESULTS  }


We show the accuracy, inter-seed agreement, and self-consistency (median and interquartile range across seeds) for the ARC (Easy) and SciQ in Fig. 1. We report these metrics for all considered datasets in App. C. We show the gender bias results in Fig. 2.  

Accuracy. We find (unsurprisingly) that the larger models consistently outperform their smaller counterparts (Fig. 1, top row), except for the more challenging tasks for which all models perform as good as random (see ARC (Challenge), Logiqa, WSC, WinoGrande in App. C, Fig. 6), a result consistent with Biderman et al., 2023. Performance improves most after step $\bar{10}^{3}$ . However, it drops between step $10^{4}$ and $10^{5}$ steps for all but 410M, especially for the smallest models. This finding aligns with prior work showing that smaller LMs suffer from “saturation”, i.e., a drop in performance at a later stage of the training process (Michaelov & Bergen, 2023; Godey et al., 2024).  

Inter-Seed Agreement and Self-Consistency. For a more fine-grained analysis of how much the model behaviour changes across seeds, we compare the inter-seed agreement with the self-consistency in Fig. 1 (middle and bottom rows, respectively). Inter-seed agreement peaks at $\kappa\approx0.7$ around step $\bar{1}0^{3}$ before converging towards a “moderate agreement”—i.e., $\kappa\approx0.5$ —around step $10^{4}$ and remaining at this level until the end of training. Self-consistency steadily increases to “moderate agreement”—i.e., $\kappa\approx0.5.$ —up to step $10^{3}$ , after which it plateaus up to step $10^{4}$ , before continuing to increase until the end of training as the models settle on their final answers.

Is there anything special about step $\mathbf{10^{3}\?}$ A qualitative analysis of model predictions around step $10^{3}$ reveals that models begin generating non-random answers and accuracy begins to increase at this stage. This result holds across multiple tasks and seeds. Also, it is consistent with observations in Jumelet et al. (2024) who study adjective order preferences in Pythia models and find that these preferences are acquired around step $\mathrm{1k-2k}$ . However, this observation alone does not explain why inter-seed agreement rises to such a degree near this point. While we are uncertain as to the exact cause of this phenomenon, prior work has found that models may not always follow a human interpretation of task instructions (Webson & Pavlick, 2022; Webson et al., 2023), or may resort to simplistic heuristics or biases such as defaulting to the first answer choice where applicable (Pezeshkpour & Hruschka, 2024; Wang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024). We believe this question of what models learn at the point of “emergence” (Srivastava et al., 2023; Schaeffer et al., 2023, inter alia) when beginning to be capable of solving a given task remains to be studied further in the literature and hope that PolyPythias can help this study in future work.  

Finding outlier seeds based on Accuracy and Validation Loss. Zooming in Fig. 1, we see that per-size accuracy is generally consistent across seeds, with two exceptions. For a given model size, we can identify outlier seeds as follows.7 First, we consider the accuracy of the last model checkpoints on the ARC (Easy), LAMBADA, Piqa, and SciQ tasks, for which all models perform better than random. Second, for each task and model size, we standardise accuracy to have mean zero and standard deviation of one by subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation computed across seeds. Finally, we define a region of $\pm\,2$ standard deviations from the mean of a model on that task and consider “outliers” those model-seed combinations that fall outside of this region. We (remarkably) find only two such combinations: 410M seed 4 and 3, which fall outside the region on all tasks considered. Indeed, it is exactly for these seeds that we observe “loss spikes” (see App. A). We will further explore these outlier seeds using other metrics in $\S5$ .  

Gender Bias. We find distinct phases in the development of gender, both grammatical and bias, in Fig. 2. Specifically, the models start to learn gender agreement between step $\mathrm{\overline{{10^{2}}}}$ and $10^{3}$ (see BLiMP (Gender Agreement)), which coincides with a sudden shift to a strong bias for using “male identifier words” (see Simple Co-occurrence Bias). Around this step, but less sharply, we also see an increase in gender bias measured by the CrowS-Pairs (Gender) benchmark, which measures more semantically diverse stereotypes than simple co-occurrence statistics. We posit that the large variance observed for the bias measures reflects the poor reliability (e.g., due to small benchmark size, poor quality test items) rather than actual bias differences (see Van der Wal et al., 2024).

\section{4 REPRESENTATIONAL STABILITY OF LINGUISTIC INFORMATION  }


We now focus on the step before output generation and analyse the models’ intermediate latent representations. When training models with different seeds, the resulting model parameters vary, leading to distinct representations given the same inputs. We study representational (in)stability by applying the information-theoretic probing approach proposed in Müller-Eberstein et al. (2023) to several linguistic tasks $(\S4.1)$ . For each model, checkpoint, and seed, we train a probe—i.e., a classification layer, $\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times|\mathcal{D}|}$ , that maps from model representations to classes $y\in\mathcal{V}$ —as follows: First, given an input-output pair, $(x,y)\overset{}{\in}\mathcal{D}$ , we collect the model representations, $\mathbf{h}_{l}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , for each token $x$ in the input sequence $\textbf{\em x}$ at each layer $l$ . Then, we aggregate per-layer representations into a global representation for that token using a learned weighting scheme $\dot{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}$ , obtaining: $\begin{array}{r}{\mathbf{h}(x)\!=\!\sum_{l=1}^{L}\alpha_{l}\,\mathbf{h}_{l}(x)}\end{array}$ . This global representation is finally passed as input to the linear probe which outputs the label for that token. The weighting scheme and the linear probe are jointly learned by optimising the minimum description length (MDL) loss:  

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\sf M D L}(\theta)=-\operatorname*{\mathbb{E}}_{\theta\sim p(\theta)}\left[\sum_{({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})\in\mathcal{D}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathfrak{y}}}\overbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\sf C E}\left(\theta^{\top}\mathbf{h}({\boldsymbol{x}}),{\boldsymbol{y}}\right)}^{\mathrm{Token-level\,loss}}\right)\right]+\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p(\theta)\|q(\theta)\bigr)
$$  

where $\mathcal{L}_{\tt C E}$ is the cross-entropy loss. Probes trained on the cross-entropy loss alone can learn to correctly map random representations to labels (Voita & Titov, 2020). Thus, $\mathcal{L}_{\sf M D L}$ includes a KL-divergence term constraining the probe’s distribution $p(\theta)$ close to a sparsity-inducing prior $q(\theta)$ .  

Using the task-specific probes per each model, seed, and checkpoint, we study representational (in)stability through the following metrics. First, we use the probe’s macro-F1 score to measure the information content of a model’s latent representations. Second, we note that eq. (1) corresponds to the MDL of the data and the probe in nats, i.e., its codelength. To measure representational efficiency, we define the codelength ratio between random vs. the probe’s representations where values close to 0 indicate high representational efficiency. Finally, we measure the representational shift between two consecutive checkpoints using the principal subspace angles (SSAs; Knyazev & Argentati, 2002). Given two probes trained on the representations from consecutive checkpoints, SSAs return an angle between $0^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$ where lower values represent more similar representations.  



\section{4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP  }


We replicate the setup of Müller-Eberstein et al. (2023), which allows us to compare their result for encoder-only LMs to ours for decoder-only LMs. We consider seven linguistically-motivated token-classification tasks: coreference resolution (Coref; Pradhan et al., 2013), dependency parsing (Dep; Silveira et al., 2014), named entity recognition (NER; Pradhan et al., 2013), part-of-speech tagging (PoS; Pradhan et al., 2013), semantic tagging (SemTag; Abzianidze et al., 2017), sentiment analysis (Senti; Socher et al., 2013), and topic classification (Topic; Lang, 1995).

\section{4.2 RESULTS  }


Results are summarised in Fig. 3, which shows the macro-F1, Codelength Ratio, and SSAs (rows) for each task (colour) averaged across seeds (line) and the respective standard deviation (shaded area) for each model size (columns) and checkpoint ( $\bf\Tilde{x}$ -axis). We discuss these results and explore their correlation across different model sizes below.  

Information Content. The macro-F1 scores mirror (unsurprisingly) the scaling behaviour observed for the ARC (Easy) and SciQ tasks $(\S3)$ , where scores consistently improve with model size across both lower-level syntactic tasks (e.g., PoS, Dep) and higher-level semantic ones (e.g., NER, Senti). In other words, larger models return representations with higher information content. While final values differ across model sizes, their trajectory is consistent: mirroring accuracy scores in Fig. 1 (top row), we observe initial improvements around step $10^{3}$ and a rapid increase in macro-F1 up to step $10^{4}$ , after which performance for most tasks remains stable, except for the smaller model sizes.  

Representational Efficiency. Like macro-F1, the Codelength Ratio starts improving around step $10^{3}$ and reaches its stable value around step $10^{4}$ . The final Codelength Ratio for each task depends on the model size, with a distinction between models below and above 100M parameters. Specifically, we observe similar final values for syntactic tasks (e.g., PoS, Dep, Coref) across model sizes, while larger models achieve a $5\mathrm{-10\%}$ abs. lower Codelength Ratio on semantic tasks (e.g., NER, Senti).  

Representational Shift. The SSAs follow (remarkably) similar trajectories across model sizes. For each task and model size, SSA increases up to step $10^{3}$ , then plateaus until step $10^{4}$ , and finally decreases until the end of training to a value around $20^{\circ}$ . In other words, model representations after step $10^{4}$ tend to become more stable, matching the reduced rate of change in macro-F1 and Codelength Ratio. The magnitude of changes in SSA does not directly correlate with macro-F1 or Codelength Ratio. Specifically, larger changes in SSA early on have positive effects, while smaller changes later on can negatively affect these metrics (e.g., 14M).  

Correlation across model sizes. We investigate the similarity of the trajectories of representational stability metrics across model sizes. A metric’s trajectory for a model size is based on the concatenation of its values for all tasks across all checkpoints. We first compute the Pearson correlation $\boldsymbol{r}_{i,j}$ between a metric’s trajectory for each pair of model sizes $\langle i,j\rangle\in\bar{\mathcal{M}}\times\mathcal{M}$ . To obtain the average $\overline{r}$ across all pairs of model sizes, we use the Fisher transformation over correlation coefficients (Fisher, 1970): $\begin{array}{r}{\overline{{\boldsymbol{r}}}\!=\!\operatorname{tanh}\left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|^{2}}\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}}\operatorname{arctanh}\left(\boldsymbol{r}_{i,j}\right)\right)}\end{array}$ . Finally, we average the resulting $\overline{r}$ ’s across seeds in the same way to obtain per metric average correlations. We find that macro-F1, Codelength Ratio, and SSA are highly correlated across model sizes with an average $\overline{r}$ of 0.99 for macro-F1, 0.98 for Codelength Ratio, and 0.94 for SSA (all with a $p$ -value smaller than 0.001). This indicates that representational stability of smaller models is strongly indicative of that of larger models.  



\section{5 TRAINING PHASES AND OUTLIERS  }


After investigating the dynamics of higher-level constructs like performance and intermediate representations, we now study the dynamics of the most atomic model components: its parameters. Specifically, we use (statistics of the) model parameters to construct the training map of a training run (i.e., sequence of checkpoints) using the approach of Hu et al. (2023). A training map assigns each checkpoint $t$ to a latent state $s\in S$ by fitting a Hidden Markov Model (HMM; Baum & Petrie, 1966) on a vector of statistics $\boldsymbol{z}_{t}=(z_{1},z_{2},...)$ derived from the model parameters (e.g., $L_{2}$ -norm). Using the HMM, we assign each checkpoint to a state, obtaining a sequence $\{s_{t}\}_{1:T}$ of states $s\in S$ corresponding to the training map of that run. Once we obtain the training maps, to compare different training trajectories and study their relationship to final model performance, we represent each training map as a bag-of-states: a vector counting the number of times a model visits each state. In this section, we use training maps to (i) visualise training phases, (ii) find which properties of the parameter drive phase changes, and (iii) identify outlier training runs.

Predicting Training Maps “zero-shot” across model sizes. We are interested in assessing whether information from the training maps of smaller models can help us predict the performance of a larger model. For each model size (apart from 410M), we fit an HMM. Then, using only the information from the size-specific HMM, we construct the training map for the 410M model by assigning each checkpoint to a state using its statistics. In this sense, we “zero-shot” predict the training map of a larger model from a smaller one. Finally, we use the bag-of-state representation of the zero-shot predicted training map for 410M to predict the final performance of the model, i.e. the average z-score, using a linear regression. In Table 2, we find that forming the bag-of-states using all checkpoints allows us to (i) predict the average z-score successfully with $R^{2}>0.{\bar{9}}$ for all models, apart from 14M; (ii) predict which seeds will underperform: specifically, seed 3 and 4.We leave the investigation of which properties can be predicted from the bag-of-states of early checkpoint metrics as future work.

\section{6 DISCUSSION  }


Our experiments on downstream performance (§3), intermediate representations (§4), and model parameters (§5) allow us to examine the stability of training and find outlier runs using different methods across the model training pipeline. In this section, we analyse commonalities across the resulting metrics to identify broader characteristics of LM pre-training dynamics.  

Language modelling is largely stable. Generally, we observe LM pre-training dynamics to follow consistent trajectories. Across seeds and model sizes, downstream performance and representational efficiency consistently increase during pre-training, and training maps are linear (except for the outliers). Furthermore, model scaling laws seem to hold across seeds, not only for downstream performance but also for information content and representational efficiency of the latent representations. Similarly, both at the performance and representational level, we observe the effect of “saturation” (Michaelov & Bergen, 2023; Godey et al., 2024) in smaller models.  

Linguistic information is encoded in the initial learning phase $\mathbf{[10^{3}-10^{4}}$ steps). Across all experiments, metrics start moving away from the initial random baseline around step $10^{3}$ (2B tokens circa) and reach their convergence level around step $10^{4}$ (20B tokens circa). In this initial phase, representational shift peaks and linguistic information begins to be encoded into the models’ latent representations. Through the lens of multiple metrics, we can analyse model behaviour in this phase in detail. Specifically, while the amount and the efficiency with which linguistic information is encoded in model representations have already increased substantially at step $1\bar{0}^{3}$ , the model does not yet generate coherent outputs, as indicated by low performance on linguistic acceptability benchmarks like BLiMP (Gender Agreement). Simultaneously, self-consistency is low while inter-seed agreement is high, which we hypothesise to be an artefact of all models initially choosing an incorrect baseline answer. In terms of training maps, this phase corresponds to the $\dot{\textcircled{0}}\to\textcircled{1}$ transition, which occurs consistently in this initial training phase for all model sizes (except for the 410M outliers).  

Most improvements happen in the “critical” learning phase $\mathbf{10^{4}{-10^{5}}}$ steps). In the range of $10^{3}$ to $10^{\bar{4}}$ steps, most learning occurs, as measured by all of our metrics. Performance increases the most, and linguistic information content and representational efficiency converge to close-to-final values. At the same time, representational shift begins to decrease from its peak, showing how the information encoded in the model begins to stabilise. This is reflected in terms of performance by the simultaneous increase in self-consistency. The fact that inter-seed agreement decreases before remaining relatively constant until the end indicates that models settle on their final answers after this stage. In the training maps, this phase corresponds to the $\textcircled{1}\to\textcircled{2}$ and $\textcircled{2}\to\textcircled{3}$ transitions that occur before step $10^{5}$ . The fact that our outlier runs (410M, seeds 3 and 4) exit state $\circled{2}$ prematurely indicates that this phase is important for the LM’s downstream performance. Furthermore, we note that these learning phases are (remarkably) similar and occur at the same time during pre-training as for encoder-only models (Müller-Eberstein et al., 2023). Also, they follow similar trajectories as recurrent LSTM architectures (Saphra & Lopez, 2019). We leave the exploration of which modelling or data decisions may result in these learning phases for future work. Finally, we observe that all benchmarks still improve past the optimal token count (e.g., 8.2B tokens, or around $\mathrm{4k}$ steps for 410M) predicted by the Chinchilla scaling law (Hoffmann et al., 2022).  

Training Maps describe outlier seeds. Using the downstream performance results and training loss $\S3$ , and training maps $\S5$ , we identify outlier seeds and explain how the model parameters change in the unstable pre-training regime. Moreover, we find that it is possible to predict training maps in a “zero-shot” fashion (e.g., using HMM trained on smaller models to predict the training map of larger ones) suggesting that statistics of the parameters of smaller models are informative of their larger counterparts. We leave the exploration of this aspect as future work.  



\section{7 CONCLUSIONS  }


In this work, we present the PolyPythias, a multi-seed extension of the Pythia model suite (Biderman et al., 2023)—45 additional pre-training runs for a total of 10 seeds across 5 model sizes—to facilitate training dynamics research. Our experiments using this new resource show its usefulness in studying both stability and outliers across different metrics in the language modelling pipeline.  

Future work. We hope the PolyPythia suite will facilitate the study of the potential causes of suboptimal training dynamics and when these can be detected during training: e.g., whether a model will diverge in loss because of observed numerical instabilities, loss spikes, etc. (Zoph et al., 2022; Zhai et al., 2023; Chowdhery et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2023; Chung et al., 2024, inter alia) as we observed for some seeds in 410M.8,9 Moreover, the availability of multiple training runs with different data orders allows for studying their effect on memorization (e.g., Lesci et al., 2024; Biderman et al., 2024a) or its relationship to the emergence of learned phenomena (e.g., Jumelet et al., 2024; Biderman et al., 2023; van der Wal et al., 2022). Lastly, the PolyPythia suite can also be used for assessing the (test-retest) reliability of benchmark evaluations and model interventions (Van der Wal et al., 2024; Madaan et al., 2024; Sellam et al., 2022), e.g., for measuring and mitigating social biases.
