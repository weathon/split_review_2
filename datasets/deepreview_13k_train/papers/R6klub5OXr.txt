\title{An Extensive Analysis on the Underlying Premises Behind Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithm Design}

\begin{abstract}
The progress in reinforcement learning algorithm development is at one of its highest points starting from the initial study that enabled sequential decision making from high-dimensional observations. Currently, deep reinforcement learning research has had quite recent breakthroughs from learning without the presence of rewards to learning functioning policies without even knowing the rules of the game. In our paper we focus on the underlying premises that are actively used in deep reinforcement learning algorithm development. We theoretically demonstrate that the performance profiles of the algorithms developed for the data-abundant regime do not transfer to the data-limited regime monotonically. We conduct large-scale experiments in the Arcade Learning Environment and our results demonstrate that the baseline algorithms perform significantly better in the data-limited regime compared to the set of algorithms that were initially designed and compared in the data-abundant regime.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


Reinforcement learning research achieved high acceleration upon the proposal of the initial study on approximating the state-action value function via deep neural networks (Mnih et al., 2015). Following this initial study several different highly successful deep reinforcement learning algorithms have been proposed (Hasselt et al., 2016b; Wang et al., 2016; Hessel et al., 2018; 2021) from focusing on different architectural ideas to employing estimators targeting overestimation, all of which were designed and tested in the high-data regime (i.e. two hundred million frame training).  

An alternative recent line of research with an extensive amount of publications focused on pushing the performance bounds of deep reinforcement learning policies in the low-data regime (Yarats et al., 2021; Ye et al., 2021; Kaiser et al., 2020; van Hasselt et al., 2019; Kielak, 2019) (i.e. with one hundred thousand environment interaction training). Several different unique ideas in current reinforcement learning research, from model-based reinforcement learning to increasing sample efficiency with observation regularization, gained acceleration in several research directions based on policy performance comparisons demonstrated in the Arcade Learning Environment 100K benchmark. In this paper, we demonstrate that there is a significant overlooked underlying premise driving this line of research without being explicitly discussed: that the performance profiles of deep reinforcement learning algorithms have a monotonic relationship with different sample-complexity regions. This implicit assumption, that is commonly shared amongst a large collection of low-data regime studies, carries a significant importance due to the fact that these studies shape future research directions with incorrect reasoning while influencing the overall research efforts put in for particular research ideas for several years following. Thus, in our paper we target these underlying premises and aim to answer the following questions:  

Hence, to be able to answer the questions raised above in our paper we focus on sample complexity in deep reinforcement learning and make the following contributions:  

• We provide theoretical foundation on the non-transferability of the performance proflies of deep reinforcement learning algorithms designed for the high-data regime to the low-data regime.   
• We theoretically demonstrate that the performance proflie has a non-monotonic relationship with the asymptotic sample complexity and the low-data sample complexity region. Furthermore, regarding the central focus of the large scale implicit assumption instances, we prove that the sample complexity of the algorithm family that learns the state-action value distribution is higher than the sample complexity of baseline deep $\mathcal{Q}$ -network algorithms.   
• We conduct large scale extensive experiments for a variety of deep reinforcement learning baseline algorithms in both the low-data regime and the high-data regime Arcade Learning Environment benchmark.   
• We highlight that recent algorithms proposed and evaluated in the Arcade Learning Environment 100K benchmark are significantly affected by the implicit assumption on the relationship between performance profiles and sample complexity.  



\section{2 BACKGROUND AND PRELIMINARIES  }


The reinforcement learning problem is formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) represented as a tuple $\langle S,A,\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma,\rho_{0}\rangle$ where $S$ represents the state space, $A$ represents the set of actions, $\mathcal{P}:S\times A\rightarrow\Delta(S)$ represents the transition probability kernel that maps a state and an action pair to a distribution on states, $\mathcal{R}:S\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ represents the reward function, and $\gamma\in(0,1]$ represents the discount factor. The aim in reinforcement learning is to learn an optimal policy $\pi(s,a)$ that outputs the probability of taking action $a$ in state $s$ , $\pi:S\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ that will maximize expected cumulative discounted rewards $\begin{array}{r}{R=\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi(s_{t},\cdot),s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})}\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})}\end{array}$ . This objective is achieved by constructing a state-action value function that learns for each state-action pair the expected cumulative discounted rewards that will be obtained if action $a\in A$ is executed in state $s\in S$ .  

$$
\mathcal{Q}(s_{t},a_{t})=\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})+\gamma\sum_{s_{t}}\mathcal{P}(s_{t+1}|s_{t},a_{t})\mathcal{V}(s_{t+1}).
$$  

In settings where the state space and/or action space is large enough that the state-action value function $\mathcal{Q}(s,a)$ cannot be held in a tabular form, a function approximator is used. Thus, for deep reinforcement learning the $\mathcal{Q}$ -function is approximated via deep neural networks.  

$$
\mathfrak{h}_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\alpha(\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})+\gamma\mathcal{Q}(s_{t+1},\mathrm{arg}\operatorname*{max}_{a}\mathcal{Q}(s_{t+1},a;\theta_{t});\theta_{t})-\mathcal{Q}(s_{t},a_{t};\theta_{t}))\nabla_{\theta_{t}}\mathcal{Q}(s_{t},a_{t};\theta_{t}).
$$  

Dueling Architecture: At the end of convolutional layers for a given deep $\mathcal{Q}$ -Network, the dueling architecture outputs two streams of fully connected layers for both estimating the state values $\mathcal{V}(s)$ and the advantage $\mathcal{A}(s,a)$ for each action in a given state $s$ , $A(s,a)=\mathcal{Q}(s,\bar{a})-\operatorname*{max}_{a}\mathcal{Q}(s,a)$ . In particular, the last layer of the dueling architecture contains the forward mapping  

$$
\mathcal{Q}(s,a;\theta,\alpha,\beta)=\mathcal{V}(s;\theta,\beta)+\left(\mathcal{A}(s,a;\theta,\alpha)-\operatorname*{max}_{a^{\prime}\in A}\mathcal{A}(s,a^{\prime};\theta,\alpha)\right)
$$  

where $\theta$ represents the parameters of the convolutional layers and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ represent the parameters of the fully connected layers outputting the advantage and state value estimates respectively.  

Learning the State-Action Value Distribution: The initial algorithm proposed to learn the stateaction value distribution is C51. The projected Bellman update for the $i^{\mathrm{th}}$ atom is computed as  

$$
(\Phi T\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t}))_{i}=\sum_{j}^{N-1}\big[1-\frac{|[\mathcal{T}z_{j}]_{v_{\mathrm{min}}}^{v_{\mathrm{max}}}-z_{i}|}{\Delta z}\big]_{0}^{1}\tau_{j}(s_{t+1},\underset{a\in A}{\operatorname*{max}}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}(s_{t+1},a))
$$  

where $\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}\big(s_{t},a_{t}\big)$ is the value distribution, $z_{i}=v_{\operatorname*{min}}+i\Delta z:0\leq i<N$ represents the set of atoms in categorical learning, and the atom probabilities are learnt as a parametric model  

$$
\tau_{i}(s_{t},\underset{a\in A}{\operatorname*{max}}\,\mathbb{E}\mathcal{Z}_{\theta}(s_{t},a))=\frac{e^{\theta_{i}(s_{t},a_{t})}}{\sum_{j}e^{\theta_{j}(s_{t},a_{t})}}\,\,,\,\,\Delta z:=\frac{v_{\operatorname*{max}}-v_{\operatorname*{min}}}{\mathcal{N}-1}
$$  

Following this baseline algorithm the $\mathcal{Q}\sf R D\mathcal{Q}\sf N$ algorithm is proposed to learn the quantile projection of the state-action value distribution  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{T\mathcal{Z}(s_{t},a_{t})=\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})+\gamma\mathcal{Z}(s_{t+1},\underset{a\in A}{\arg\operatorname*{max}}\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{Z}(s_{t+1},a_{t+1})}[z])}\end{array}
$$  

with $s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})$ where $\mathcal{Z}\;\in\;Z$ represents the quantile distribution of an arbitrary value function. Following this study the $\mathtt{I}\mathcal{Q}\Nu$ algorithm is proposed (i.e. implicit quantile networks) to learn the full quantile function instead of learning a discrete set of quantiles as in the $\mathcal{Q}\sf R D\mathcal{Q}\sf N$ algorithm. The $\mathtt{I}\mathcal{Q}\Nu$ algorithm objective is to minimize the loss function  

$$
\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K^{\prime}}\rho_{\delta}(\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})+\gamma\mathcal{Z}_{\delta_{j}^{\prime}}(s_{t+1},\underset{a\in A}{\arg\operatorname*{max}}\,\mathcal{Q}_{\beta}(s_{t},a_{t}))-\mathcal{Z}_{\delta_{i}}(s_{t},a_{t}))
$$  

where $\rho_{\delta}$ represents the Huber quantile regression loss, and $\begin{array}{r}{\mathcal{Q}_{\beta}\,=\,\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{-1}(\delta)d\beta(\delta)}\end{array}$ . Note that $\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{Z}}^{-1}(\delta)$ is the quantile function of the random variable $\mathcal{Z}$ at $\delta\in[0,1]$ .  



\section{3 LOW-DATA REGIME VERSUS ASYMPTOTIC PERFORMANCE  }


The high-level message of our empirical results is that comparing the asymptotic performance of two reinforcement learning algorithms does not necessarily give useful information on their relative performance in the low-data regime. In this section we provide mathematical motivation for this claim in the setting of optimizing non-stationary policies (i.e. rewards and transitions can vary with each step in an episode) in undiscounted, finite-horizon MDPs with linear function approximation. This is a setting that has seen recent progress in provable regret bounds with function approximation (Zanette et al., 2020). In particular, a finite horizon MDP is represented as a tuple $\langle S,A,{\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{R}},{\mathcal{H}}\rangle$ where $S$ is the set of states, and $A$ represents the set of actions. For each timestep $t\in[\mathcal{H}]=\{1,\ldots,\mathcal{H}\}$ , state $s$ and action $a$ the transition probability kernel $\mathcal{P}_{t}\big(s_{t+1}\big|s_{t},a_{t}\big)$ gives the probability distribution over the next state, and the reward $\mathcal{R}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})$ gives the immediate rewards. A non-stationary policy $\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{\mathcal{H}})$ induces a state-action value function given by  

$$
\mathcal{Q}_{t}^{\pi}(s_{t},a_{t})=\mathcal{R}_{t}(s_{t},a_{t},s_{t+1})+\mathbb{E}_{s_{t}\sim\mathcal{P}_{t}(s_{t+1}\mid s_{t},a_{t}),a_{t}\sim\pi}\left[\sum_{h=t+1}^{\mathcal{H}}\mathcal{R}_{t}(s_{h},\pi_{h}(s_{h}),s_{h+1})\bigg|s_{t},a_{t}\right]
$$  

where we let $\pi(s)$ be the action taken by the policy $\pi$ in state $s$ , and the corresponding value function $\mathcal{V}_{t}^{\pi}(s_{t})=\mathcal{Q}_{t}\big(s_{t},\pi(s_{t})\big)$ . The optimal non-stationary policy $\pi^{*}$ has value function $\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}(s_{t})=\mathcal{V}_{t}^{\pi^{*}}(s_{t})$ satisfying $\begin{array}{r}{\mathcal{V}_{t}^{*}(s_{t})=\operatorname*{sup}_{\pi}\mathcal{V}_{t}^{\pi}(s_{t})}\end{array}$ . The objective is to learn a sequence of non-stationary policies $\pi^{k}$ for $k\in\{1,\ldots,\kappa\}$ while interacting with an unknown MDP in order to minimize the regret, which is measured asymptotically over $\kappa$ episodes of length $\mathcal{H}$  

$$
\operatorname{REGRET}({\mathcal{K}})=\sum_{k=1}^{\mathcal{K}}\left(\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*}(s_{1}^{k})-\mathcal{V}_{1}^{\pi^{k}}(s_{1}^{k})\right)
$$  

where $s_{1}^{k}\in S$ is the starting state of the $k$ -th episode. In words, regret sums up the gap between the expected rewards obtained by the sequence of learned policies $\bar{\pi}^{k}$ and those obtained by $\pi^{*}$ when learning for $\kappa$ episodes. In the linear function approximation setting there is a feature map $\phi_{t}:S\times A\breve{\to}\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}$ for each $t\in[\mathcal{H}]$ that sends a state-action pair $(s,a)$ to the $d_{t}$ -dimensional vector $\phi_{t}(s,a)$ . Then, the state-action value function $\mathcal{Q}_{t}\big(s_{t},a_{t}\big)$ is parameterized by a vector $\theta_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{t}}$ so that $\mathcal{Q}_{t}(\theta_{t})(s_{t},a_{t})=\phi_{t}(s,a)^{\top}\theta_{t}$ . Recent theoretical work in this setting gives an algorithm along with a lower bound that matches the regret achieved by the algorithm up to logarithmic factors.  

Theorem 3.1 (Zanette et al. (2020)). Under appropriate normalization assumptions there is an algorithm that learns a sequence of policies $\pi^{k}$ achieving regret  

$$
\mathsf{R E G R E T}(K)=\tilde{O}\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{H}}d_{t}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}}+\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{H}}\sqrt{d_{t}}\mathcal{Z}\mathcal{K}\right),
$$  

where $\mathcal{T}$ is the intrinsic Bellman error. Furthermore, this regret bound is optimal for this setting up to logarithmic factors in $d_{t},\kappa$ and $\mathcal{H}$ whenever $\begin{array}{r}{\mathcal{K}=\Omega((\sum_{t=1}^{\mathcal{H}}d_{t})^{2})}\end{array}$ , in the sense that for any level of intrinsic Bellman error $\mathcal{T}$ and sequence of feature dimensions $\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}$ , there exists a class of MDPs $\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z},\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H})$ where any algorithm achieves at least as much regret on at least one MDP in the class.  

The class of MDPs $\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z},\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H})$ constructed in Theorem 3.1 additionally satisfies the following properties. First, every MDP in $\bar{\cup}_{\mathcal{Z},\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}}\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z},\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H})$ has the same transitions (up to renaming of states and actions). Second, for each fixed value of the intrinsic Bellman error $\mathcal{T}$ and the dimensions $\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}$ , every MDP in $\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{Z},\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H})$ utilizes the same feature map $\phi_{t}(s_{t},a_{t})$ . Thus one can view the class $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{T},\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H})$ as encoding one "underlying" true environment (defined by the transitions), with varying values of $\mathcal{T}$ and $\{d_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}$ corresponding to varying levels of function approximation accuracy, and model capacity for the underlying environment. For simplicity of notation we will focus on the setting where $d_{t}\,=\,d$ for all $t\in\{\bar{1},\cdot\cdot\cdot H\}$ and write $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z},d)$ for the class of MDPs constructed in Theorem 3.1 for this setting. Utilizing this point of view, we can then prove the following proposition on the relationship between the performance in the asymptotic and low-data regimes.  

Theorem 3.2. For any $\epsilon>0$ , let $d_{\alpha}$ be any feature dimension, and let $d_{\beta}=d_{\alpha}^{1-\epsilon/2}$ . Then there exist thresholds $K_{l o w}<K_{h i g h}$ and intrinsic Bellman error levels $\mathcal{T}_{\beta}>\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}$ such that  

1. There is an algorithm achieving regret REGRET $l o w(\boldsymbol{\kappa})$ when $\kappa<\kappa_{l o w}$ for all MDPs in $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{\beta},d_{\beta})$ . However, every algorithm has regret at least $\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\beta}^{\epsilon/2}\mathrm{REGRET}_{l o w}(K)\right)$ when $\kappa<\kappa_{l o w}$ on some MDP $M\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha})$ .   
2. There is an algorithm achieving regret REGRET $_{h i g h}({\mathcal{K}})$ when $\kappa>\kappa_{h i g h}$ for all MDPs in $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha})$ . However, every algorithm has regret at least $\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}\mathrm{REGRET}_{h i g h}(K)\right)$ on some MDP $M\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{\beta},d_{\beta})$ when $\kappa>\kappa_{h i g h}$ .  

Proof. Let $\epsilon>0$ and consider $\begin{array}{r}{d_{\beta}=d_{\alpha}^{1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}},\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}=\frac{1}{d_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}\sqrt{d_{\beta}}},\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{d_{\alpha}^{\frac{1}{2}+2\epsilon}},K_{\mathrm{low}}=d_{\alpha}^{2+\epsilon},K_{\mathrm{high}}=d_{\alpha}^{2+4\epsilon}}\end{array}$ We begin with the proof of part 1. Therefore, for $\kappa<\kappa_{\mathrm{low}}$ ,  

$$
\sqrt{d_{\beta}}\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}K=d_{\alpha}^{-\epsilon}\mathcal{K}<d_{\alpha}^{1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}}=d_{\beta}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}}.
$$  

Therefore, by Theorem 3.1 there exists an algorithm achieving regret  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{\mathsf{R E G R E T}_{\mathrm{low}}(K)=\tilde{O}\left(\mathcal{H}d_{\beta}\sqrt{K}+\mathcal{H}\sqrt{d_{\beta}}\mathbb{Z}_{\beta}K\right)=\widetilde{O}\left(d_{\beta}\sqrt{K}\right)}\end{array}
$$  

in every MDP $M\,\in\,\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{\beta},d_{\beta})$ . Further, since ${\cal{K}}_{\mathrm{low}}\,=\,d_{\alpha}^{2+\epsilon}\,>\,\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\alpha}^{2}\right)$ , the lower bound from Theorem 3.1 applies to the class of MDPs $\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha})$ for all $K\in\left[\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\alpha}^{2}\right),\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{low}}\right]$ . In particular, every algorithm receives regret at least  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{\mathrm{REGRET}(K)=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{H}d_{\alpha}\sqrt{K}+\mathcal{H}\sqrt{d_{\alpha}}\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}K\right)>\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{H}d_{\beta}^{\frac{1}{1-\epsilon/2}}\sqrt{K}\right)>\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{H}d_{\beta}^{\frac{\epsilon/2}{1-\epsilon/2}}d_{\beta}\sqrt{K}\right)}\\ {>\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\beta}^{\epsilon/2}\mathrm{REGRET}_{\mathrm{low}}(K)\right).}\end{array}
$$  

For part 2, note that for $\kappa>\kappa_{\mathrm{high}}$ we have both  

$$
\sqrt{d_{\alpha}}\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}K=d_{\alpha}^{-2\epsilon}K>d_{\alpha}^{-2\epsilon}\sqrt{K\cdot K_{\mathrm{high}}}>d_{\alpha}\sqrt{K}
$$  

and  

$$
\sqrt{d_{\beta}}\bar{Z}_{\beta}K>d_{\alpha}^{-\epsilon}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}\cdot\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{low}}}=d_{\alpha}^{1+\epsilon}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}}>d_{\beta}\sqrt{\mathcal{K}}.
$$  

Therefore again by Theorem 3.1 that for $\kappa>\kappa_{\mathrm{low}}$ there exists an algorithm achieving regret  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{\mathrm{REGRET}_{\mathrm{high}}(K)=\tilde{O}\left(\mathcal{H}d_{\alpha}\sqrt{K}+\mathcal{H}\sqrt{d_{\alpha}}\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}K\right)=\tilde{O}\left(\mathcal{H}\sqrt{d_{\alpha}}\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}K\right).}\end{array}
$$  

for every MDP $M\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha},d_{\alpha})$ . However, by the lower bound in Theorem 3.1, for $\kappa>\kappa_{\mathrm{low}}$ every algorithm receives regret at least  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{\mathsf{R E G R E T}(K)=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{H}d_{\beta}\sqrt{K}+\mathcal{H}\sqrt{d_{\beta}}\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}K\right)>\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{H}\sqrt{d_{\beta}}\mathcal{Z}_{\beta}K\right)}\\ &{\qquad\qquad=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(\mathcal{H}d_{\alpha}^{-\epsilon}K\right)=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}\mathcal{H}d_{\alpha}^{-2\epsilon}K\right)=\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}\mathcal{H}\sqrt{d_{\alpha}}\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha}K\right)}\\ &{\qquad\qquad>\widetilde{\Omega}\left(d_{\alpha}^{\epsilon}\mathrm{REGRET}_{\mathrm{high}}(K)\right)}\end{array}
$$  

Theorem 3.2 shows that in the linear function approximation setting, there is a provable tradeoff between performance in the low-data regime (i.e. $\kappa<\kappa_{\mathrm{low}})$ and the high-data regime (i.e. $\kappa>{\cal K}_{\mathrm{high}})$ . In particular, in the low-data regime lower capacity function approximation (lower feature dimension $d_{\beta}$ ) with larger approximation error (larger intrinsic Bellman error $\mathcal{T}_{\beta}$ ) can provably outperform larger capacity models (feature dimension $d_{\alpha}$ ) with smaller approximation error (intrinsic Bellman error $\mathcal{T}_{\alpha}$ ). Furthermore, the relative performance is reversed in the high-data regime $\kappa\,>\,{\cal K}_{\mathrm{high}}$ . Thus, comparisons between algorithms in the asymptotic/high-data regime are not informative when trying to understand algorithm performance with limited data.  



\section{4 LOWER BOUNDS FOR LEARNING THE STATE-ACTION VALUE DISTRIBUTION  }


The instances of the implicit assumption that the performance proflie of an algorithm in the high-data regime will translate to the low-data regime monotonically appear in almost all of the studies conducted in the low-data regime. In particular, we see that when this line of work was being conducted the best performing algorithm in the high-data regime was based on learning the state action value distribution. Hence, there are many cases in the literature (e.g. DRQ, OTR, DER, CURL, SimPLE, Efficient-Zero) where all the newly proposed algorithms in the low-data regime are being compared to an algorithm that learns the state-action value distribution, under the implicit assumption that the algorithm that learns the state-action value distribution must achieve the current best performance in the low-data regime. The large scale experiments provided in Section 5 demonstrate the impact of this implicit assumption in the low-data regime deep reinforcement learning algorithm design. In particular, the results reported in Section 5 prove that the performance proflie of an algorithm in the high-data regime does not monotonically transfer to the low-data regime. Due to this extensive focus throughout the literature on low-data regime comparisons to algorithms that learn the state action value distribution, we provide additional theoretical justification for the empirically observed sample complexity results in the low to high-data regime in deep reinforcement learning.  

To obtain theoretical insight into the larger sample complexity exhibited by learning the state-action value distribution we consider the fundamental comparison between learning the distribution of a random variable $\mathcal{X}$ versus only learning the mean $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}]$ . In the base algorithm that learns the state-action value distribution the goal is to learn a distribution over state-action values that has finite support. It is well-known that learning a discrete distribution to error $\epsilon$ in total variation distance requires more samples than estimating the mean to within error epsilon (see Proposition A.1). Although this fact implies that learning the state-action value distribution has an intrinsically higher sample complexity than that of standard $\mathcal{Q}$ -learning, it does not provide insights into the comparison of an error of $\epsilon$ in the mean with an error of $\epsilon$ in total variation distance. Hence, the following proposition demonstrates a precise justification of the comparison: whenever there are two different actions where the true mean state-action values are within $\epsilon$ , an approximation error of $\epsilon$ in total variation distance for the state-action value distribution of one of the actions is sufficient to reverse the order of the means.  

Proposition 4.1. Fix a state s and consider two actions $a,a^{\prime}$ . Let $\mathcal{X}(s,a)$ be the random variable distributed as the true state-action value distribution of $(s,a)$ , and $\mathcal{X}(s,a^{\prime})$ be the same for $(s,a^{\prime})$ . Suppose that $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(s,a^{\prime})]+\epsilon$ . Then there is a random variable $\boldsymbol{\wp}$ such that $d_{T V}(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{X}(s,a))\leq\epsilon$ and $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(s,a^{\prime})]\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}]$ .  

Proof. Let $\tau^{*}\in\mathbb{R}$ be the infimum $\tau^{*}=\operatorname*{inf}\left\{\tau\in\mathbb{R}\ |\ \mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)\geq\tau]=\epsilon\right\}$ i.e. $\tau^{*}$ is the first point in $\mathbb{R}$ such that $\mathcal{X}(s,a)$ takes values at least $\tau^{*}$ with probability exactly $\epsilon$ . Next let the random variable $\boldsymbol{\wp}$ be defined by the following process. First, sample the random variable $\mathcal{X}(s,a)$ . If $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}(s,a)\geq\boldsymbol{\tau}^{*}$ , then output $\tau^{*}-1$ . Otherwise, output the sampled value of $\mathcal{X}(s,a)$ . Observe that the probability distributions of $\boldsymbol{\wp}$ and $\mathcal{X}(s,a)$ are identical except at the point $\tau^{*}-1$ and on the interval $\left[\tau^{*},\infty\right)$ . Let $\mu$ be the Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}$ . By construction of $\boldsymbol{\wp}$ the total variation distance is given by  

$$
\begin{array}{l}{{\displaystyle{\cal I}_{T V}(y,\mathcal{X})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl|\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)=z]-\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{Y}=z]\bigr|\,d\mu(z)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl|\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)=\tau^{*}-1]-\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{Y}=\tau^{*}-1]\bigr|}}\\ {\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\,\frac{1}{2}\int_{\{\tau^{*},\infty\}}\bigl|\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)=z]-\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{Y}=z]\bigr|\,d\mu(z)=\frac{\epsilon}{2}+\frac{\epsilon}{2}=\epsilon.}\end{array}
$$  

Next note that the expectation of $\boldsymbol{\wp}$ is given by  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Y}]=\epsilon(\tau^{*}-1)+\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,\tau^{*}]}z\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)=z]\,d\mu(z)}\\ &{\qquad=\epsilon(\tau^{*}-1)+\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}z\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)=z]\,d\mu(z)-\displaystyle\int_{(\tau^{*},\infty]}z\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)=z]\,d\mu(z)}\\ &{\qquad\leq\epsilon(\tau^{*}-1)+\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)]-\epsilon\tau^{*}=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{X}(s,a)]-\epsilon}\end{array}
$$  

where the inequality follows from the fact that $\mathcal{X}$ takes values larger than $\tau^{*}$ with probability $\epsilon$ .  

To summarize, Proposition 4.1 shows that, in the case where the mean state-action values are within $\epsilon$ , unless the state-action value distribution is learned to within total-variation distance $\epsilon$ , the incorrect action may be selected by the policy that learns the state-action value distribution. Therefore, it is natural to compare the sample complexity of learning the state-action value distribution to within total-variation distance $\epsilon$ with the sample complexity of simply learning the mean to within error $\epsilon$ as is done in Proposition A.1.  



\section{4.1 SAMPLE COMPLEXITY WITH UNKNOWN SUPPORT  }


The setting considered in Proposition A.1 most readily applies to the base algorithm that learns the state-action value distribution C51, which attempts to directly learn a discrete distribution with known support in order to approximate the state-action value distribution. However, further advances in learning the state-action value distribution including $\mathcal{Q}\sf R D\mathcal{Q}\sf N$ and IQN do away with the assumption that the support of the distribution is known. This allows a more flexible representation in order to more accurately represent the true distribution on state-action values, but, as we will show, potentially leads to a further increase in the sample complexity. The $\mathcal{Q}\sf R D\mathcal{Q}\sf N$ algorithm models the distribution of state-action values as a uniform mixture of $\mathcal{N}$ Dirac deltas on the reals i.e. $\begin{array}{r}{\mathcal{Z}(s,a)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\theta_{i}(s,a)}}\end{array}$ , where $\theta_{i}(s,a)\in\mathbb{R}$ is a parametric model.  

Proposition 4.2. Let $\mathcal{N}>\mathcal{M}\,\geq\,2,$ , $\begin{array}{r}{\epsilon>\frac{\mathcal{M}}{4\mathcal{N}}}\end{array}$ , and $\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}$ for $i\in[N]$ . The number of samples required to learn a distribution of the form $\begin{array}{r}{\dot{\mathcal{Z}}=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{\mathcal{N}}\delta_{\theta_{i}}}\end{array}$ to within total variation distance $\epsilon$ is $\Omega\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)$ .  

 Depending on the choice of parameters, the lower bound in Proposition 4.2 can be significantly larger than that of Proposition A.1. For example if the desired approximation error is $\begin{array}{r}{\epsilon=\frac{1}{8}}\end{array}$ one can take $\begin{array}{r}{\mathcal{M}=\frac{\mathcal{N}}{2}}\end{array}$ . In this case if the value of $k$ in Proposition A.1 satisfies $k=o(\mathcal{N})$ , then the sample complexity in Proposition 4.2 is asymptotically larger than that of Proposition A.1.  



\section{5 LARGE SCALE EXPERIMENTAL INVESTIGATION  }


The experiments are conducted in the Arcade Learning Environment (ALE) (Bellemare et al., 2013). The Double $\mathcal{Q}$ -learning algorithm is trained via Double Deep $\mathcal{Q}$ -Network (Hasselt et al., 2016a) initially proposed by van Hasselt (2010). All of the results are reported with the standard error of the mean in all of the tables and figures in the paper. As discussed in Section 4.1, more complex representations for broader classes of distributions come at the cost of a higher sample complexity required for learning. One intriguing fact is that the original SimPLE paper provides a comparison in the low-data regime of their proposed algorithm with the Rainbow algorithm which is essentially an algorithm that is designed in the high-data region by having the implicit assumption that the state-of-the art performance proflie must transfer monotonically to the low-data region. These instances of implicit assumptions also occur in $\mathrm{DR}\mathcal{Q}^{\mathrm{ICLR}}$ , CURL, SPR and Efficient-Zero even when comparisons are made for more advanced algorithms such as MuZero. Note that these implicit assumptions give faulty signals on why and what makes these algorithms work when designed for the low-data regime, and hence affect future research directions while misdirecting research efforts from ideas that could have worked in the algorithm design process.  



\section{6 CONCLUSION  }


In this paper we aimed to answer the following questions: (i) Do the performance profiles of deep reinforcement learning algorithms designed for certain data regimes translate monotonically to a different sample complexity region?, and (ii) What is the underlying theoretical relationship between the performance profiles and sample complexity regimes? To be able to answer these questions we provide theoretical investigation on the sample complexity of the baseline deep reinforcement learning algorithms. We conduct extensive experiments both in the low-data region 100K Arcade Learning Environment and high-data regime baseline 200 million frame training. Our results demonstrate that the performance profiles of deep reinforcement learning algorithms do not have a monotonic relationship across sample complexity regimes. The underlying premise of the monotonic relationship of the performance characteristics and the sample complexity regions that exists in many recent state-of-the-art studies has been assumed incorrectly. Thus, our results demonstrate that several baseline $\mathcal{Q}$ algorithms are almost as high performing as recent variant algorithms that have been proposed and shown as the state-of-the-art.
