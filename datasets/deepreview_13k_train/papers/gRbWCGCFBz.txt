\title{ToolBridge: An Open-Source Dataset to Equip LLMs with External Tool Capabilities}

\begin{abstract}
  Through the integration of external tools, large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4o and Llama 3.1 significantly expand their functional capabilities, evolving from elementary conversational agents to general-purpose assistants. 
  We argue that the primary drivers of these advancements are the quality and diversity of the training data.
  However, the existing LLMs with external tool integration provide only limited transparency regarding their datasets and data collection methods, which has led to the initiation of this research.
  Specifically, in this paper, our objective is to elucidate the detailed process involved in constructing datasets that empower LLMs to effectively learn how to utilize external tools and make this information available to the public through the introduction of ToolBridge.
  ToolBridge proposes to employ a collection of general open-access datasets as its raw dataset pool and applies a series of strategies to identify appropriate data entries from the pool for external tool API insertions.
  By supervised fine-tuning on these curated data entries, LLMs can invoke external tools in appropriate contexts to boost their predictive accuracy, particularly for basic functions including data processing, numerical computation, and factual retrieval.
  Our experiments rigorously isolates model architectures and training configurations, focusing exclusively on the role of data.
  The experimental results indicate that LLMs trained on ToolBridge demonstrate consistent performance improvements on both standard benchmarks and custom evaluation datasets.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing, excelling in tasks including question answering, summarization, and text generation \cite{jiang2023mistral, gunter2024apple, team2024gemma}.
Despite the impressive achievements of LLMs, they persistently underperform in fundamental areas, such as arithmetic or factual lookup, where introducing external tools like calculators and search engines can effectively provide solutions \cite{cobbe2021training,thoppilan2022lamda,schick2024toolformer,shen2024hugginggpt,gao2023pal,achiam2023gpt,zhao2024let}.

Consequently, there has been a rise in research efforts aimed at equipping LLMs with the ability to leverage external tools. 
For example, Llama 3.1 \cite{dubey2024llama} is trained to utilize Brave Search, Python interpreter and Wolfram Alpha API by using synthetic data combined with human-annotated data. 
Toolformer \cite{schick2024toolformer} adopts a self-supervised training strategy to equip LLMs with the capability to leverage diverse tools effectively.
However, these existing LLMs tend to either only open-source their trained models and code or restrict access to the models through usage interfaces \cite{achiam2023gpt, dubey2024llama, gunter2024apple}.
The data underlying the training of these models is commonly withheld from public disclosure, enveloped in secrecy.

The widespread availability of pre-trained models stands in stark contrast to the limited transparency surrounding their training datasets, which impedes the community’s progress in advancing discoveries in this area.
This emphasizes the critical need to explore ways to create high-quality, publicly available benchmark datasets for training LLMs to utilize external tools to facilitate their reasoning process.

In response to the challenges discussed, this paper proposes a pipeline for the large-scale creation of datasets designed to train LLMs in leveraging external tools. 
Specifically, we begin by aggregating a substantial collection of open-source datasets used for LLM supervised fine-tuning (SFT) from the community, which circumvents proprietary concerns (\emph{e.g.}, copyright issues).
Upon establishing this dataset pool, we propose a systematic approach to assist in the identification of valuable data entries and convert them into a standardized format.
Finally, valuable data entries filtering is conducted to further boost the quality of these converted data entries and ToolBridge is constructed.
As demonstrated in Figure \ref{fig.datasamples}, we showcase the data entries from ToolBridge, which are intended to guide LLMs in understanding how to incorporate external tools in appropriate contexts, thereby improving the accuracy and reliability of their outputs.
For instance, the third example in Figure \ref{fig.datasamples} serves to instruct LLMs in leveraging the \emph{requests} module to gather factual information from web sources. 
Moreover, LLMs can utilize the fourth example to understand how \emph{sympy} library can be applied to solve linear equation of one variable.

In summary, the contributions of this paper are as follows,

\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a pipeline capable of producing large volumes of entries for training LLMs to incorporate various external tools.
    The collection of over 178,000 yielded data entries, named ToolBridge, will be open-sourced to the community, marking a significant advancement in the transparency and accessibility of the data for training the large language models to leverage external tools.
    
    \item Our experimental results indicate that LLMs supervised fine-tuned on our ToolBridge can achieve consistent performance improvements on several standard benchmarks.
    
    \item We also propose to curate a set of data entries to examine the abilities of LLMs in numerical calculation, data processing and factual retrieval before and after supervised fine-tuning on ToolBridge.
    Experimental outcomes reaffirm the effectiveness of ToolBridge.
\end{itemize}

This is, to our knowledge, the first research in the domain of equipping LLMs with external tools that open-sources the training data.
We anticipate that ToolBridge will enable the community to further investigate the ability of LLMs to use external tools, thereby advancing LLMs from basic conversational models to versatile general-purpose assistants.


\section{Related Work}


\noindent \textbf{Tool Use for LLMs.} Enabling LLMs to use tools like search engines and code interpreters significantly broadens the range of tasks they can address and improves their accuracy in tasks involving data processing, numerical computation and factual retrieval \cite{nakano2021webgpt,schick2022peer,gao2023pal,achiam2023gpt,dubey2024llama,schick2024toolformer}.
For instance, Komeili et al. \cite{komeili2021internet} proposed to enable the models to use a search engine by learning to generate an internet search query based on the context, and then condition its final response on the search results.
Cobbe et al. \cite{cobbe2021training} facilitated LLMs' usage of a calculator during testing by training LLMs with calculation annotations injected into the training set. 
Thoppilan et al. \cite{thoppilan2022lamda} assisted LLMs in invoking external tools from a toolset, comprising an information retrieval system, a calculator, and a translator, by training them to produce a special string \emph{TS}.
Gao et al. \cite{gao2023pal} proposed adopting LLMs to interpret natural language problems and generate programs as intermediate reasoning steps, while delegating the solution process to a runtime environment like a Python interpreter.
HuggingGPT \cite{shen2024hugginggpt} demonstrated how LLMs can act as controllers to manage and integrate various AI models across different domains and modalities.
Toolformer \cite{schick2024toolformer} allowed LLMs to learn how to employ external tools through a self-supervised learning approach.
Of late, GPT-4o \cite{achiam2023gpt}, Llama 3.1 \cite{dubey2024llama} and Apple LLMs \cite{gunter2024apple} and several other works \cite{zhao2024let} further strengthened LLMs' ability to use external tools through improvements in models, training data, and other areas.

Although these works are highly commendable, they rarely open-source the data required to train their models, which is essential for the community to further research and refine their contributions.
This paper presents a pipeline targeted at yielding data entries for training models in external tool utilization, along with open-sourcing all data entries produced using this methodology.
This open access facilitates the development of more effective and efficient algorithms for the next generation of LLMs integrated with external tool functionalities.

\noindent \textbf{Tool Use Benchmark Datasets.}
Previous research on benchmark datasets associate with tool use primarily focused on evaluating the ability of models to utilize external tools \cite{srinivasan2023nexusraven,zhuang2023toolqa, patil2023gorilla,xu2023tool,qin2023toolllm}.
Specifically, the datasets proposed in Kasai et al. \cite{kasai2024realtime} and Chen et al. \cite{chen2021dataset} were used to investigate how text retrievers improve the performance of LLMs on timely information.
ToolQA \cite{zhuang2023toolqa} proposed to develop tool-based question-answer pairs to assess LLMs' capability in arriving at accurate solutions. 
Huang et al. \cite{huang2023metatool} suggested setting various tasks to evaluate the proficiency of LLMs in tool usage awareness and the ability to select appropriate tools.
Xu et al. \cite{xu2023tool} developed a benchmark focused on tool manipulation, featuring a range of software tools applicable to real-world scenarios.
Given the scarcity of open-source large-scale datasets for equipping LLMs with external tool usage capabilities, we present ToolBridge which is a dataset of more than 178,000 data entries to support LLMs in effectively learning how to utilize external tools.

\subsection{Dataset Pool Construction}

Our research starts with a comprehensive review of the data accessible in the community for the purpose of supervised fine-tuning (SFT).
Table \ref{tab.datapool} summarizes the results.
Owing to the diverse range of teams contributing the SFT datasets, there is significant heterogeneity in their formats, which poses challenges for effective model training.
Hence, we first reformat all candidate datasets into a standardized ChatML format for further processing:

\begin{center}
\begin{BVerbatim}
entry = [{"role": "user", "content": "..."}, 
         {"role": "assistant", "content": "..."}, 
         ...].
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{center}

After reformatting all the datasets, we construct the dataset pool as $\mathcal{P}=\{(\mathcal{D}_i, W_i, Q_i) ~|~ i \ge 0 \}$, where $\mathcal{D}_i$ denotes one candidate dataset, 
$W_i$ measures the proportion of valuable entries for each candidate dataset, and $Q_i$ serves as a metric for assessing the data quality of each candidate dataset.

Practically, to obtain $W_i$, we first perform random sampling on $\mathcal{D}_i$ to produce a subset $\mathcal{S}_i$.
Llama3 \cite{touvron2023llama} is then applied to judge the appropriateness of each data entry in $\mathcal{S}_i$ for external tool invocation to enhance reasoning,
llama3prompts}.
In generally, if a data entry is deemed suitable for invoking external tools to help LLMs' reasoning process, we label it as a valuable data entry.
At last, we determine $W_i$ as the ratio between the number of valuable data entries and the total number of data entries in $\mathcal{S}_i$.

Additionally, we observe that some candidate datasets within the dataset pool are partially sourced from the Internet via web scraping,
resulting in the presence of meaningless HTML tags and other irrelevant content.
Thus, we incorporate $Q_i$ as an additional metric to evaluate the data quality of each candidate dataset.
To compute $Q_i$, we randomly sample $N$ data entries from $\mathcal{D}_i$ and conduct a manual review to identify any presence of irrelevant characters or content.
$Q_i$ is then derived as the fraction of data entries devoid of irrelevant content over the total number of sampled entries $N$.

\subsection{Valuable Data Entries Selection} \label{sec.vdes}

Upon constructing the dataset pool $\mathcal{P}$, due to the large scale of candidate data entries, we propose to select 10 million data entries from $\mathcal{P}$ for further processing according to the dataset attributes $W_i$ and $Q_i$.
Particularly, we first arrange $\mathcal{D}_i$ in descending order based on the value of $Q_i \times W_i$.
Then, the data entries are selected from the top-ranked datasets sequentially until the overall volume reaches 10 million entries.

\subsection{Valuable Data Entries Conversion}

Following valuable data entries selection, we further convert the selected data entries, allowing the language models to learn how to invoke external tools effectively within the proper context to support their reasoning process.
In particular, we draw on previous approaches~\cite{schick2024toolformer,dubey2024llama,thoppilan2022lamda} by embedding special characters in each selected entry to enable the external tool invocation, 
and LLMs are employed to pinpoint the appropriate context for calling external tool APIs.

As illustrated in the examples in Figure \ref{fig.datasamples}, \verb|<python>| and \verb|</python>| are represented as a pair of special tokens.
While the content enclosed by the special tokens specifies the Python invocation for external tool use.
During the construction of ToolBridge, we predominantly utilize GPT-4o-mini to insert the special tokens in the correct context within each data entry identified in Section \ref{sec.vdes}, as well as to create the associated code for invoking the tools.
To facilitate the return of tool execution results, we examine the code format utilized by GPT-4o when calling Python API as part of its reasoning process while answering user queries.
It is observed that the final result is always printed at the end of the code.
gpt40miniprompts}). 

Following GPT-4o-mini's processing of $\mathcal{V}$, we extract the code segments between \verb|<python>| and \verb|</python>|, execute them, and finally insert the captured output after corresponding \verb|</python>|,
where the output will be wrapped within another pair of special tokens, denoted as \verb|<result>| and \verb|</result>|.
To summarize, the tool invocation in ToolBridge can be formatted as,

\begin{center}
\begin{BVerbatim}
<python>generated code</python><result>captured output</result>.
\end{BVerbatim}
\end{center}

During the reasoning process of the models, it is sufficient to check for the presence of the pre-defined special tokens \verb|<python>| and \verb|</python>| during generation.
If identified, Python interpreter will be adopted to execute the enclosed code between the special tokens, and the executed result is wrapped in \verb|<result>| and \verb|</result>| to condition further text generation.
Algorithm \ref{alg.processst} describes the primary inference process of LLMs post-SFT on the ToolBridge dataset.

\begin{algorithm} [h]
\caption{Process Special Tokens During Inference} \label{alg.processst}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Python interpreter and $inputs$.
\Ensure Execute the code enclosed by \texttt{<python>} and \texttt{</python>}, and use the captured output as a condition for the subsequent text generation.

\State Initialize an empty list $outputs$
\State Initialize $condition \gets \text{None}$
\While{$outputs$ is empty \textbf{or} $outputs[-1] \neq \texttt{<|end\_of\_text|>}$}
    \State $output \gets \text{LLM}(inputs)$
    \If {$output = \texttt{<python>}$}
        \State $start \gets \text{length of } outputs$ + \text{length of \texttt{<python>}}
    \ElsIf {$output = \texttt{</python>}$}
        \State Extract substring $code \gets outputs[start:]$
        \State $condition \gets \text{ExecutePython}(code)$
    \EndIf
    \State Append $output$ to both $inputs$ and $outputs$
    \If {$condition$ is not \texttt{None}}
        \State Append $condition$ to both $inputs$ and $outputs$
        \State Reset $condition \gets \text{None}$
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State Post-process and return $outputs$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Data Entries Filtering by Consistency Validation}

In practice, we observe that LLMs trained on $\mathcal{C}$ do not always base their subsequent content on the results produced by the yielded Python code during inference.
So, we conduct a re-evaluation of the data entries within $\mathcal{C}$ and observe that the execution results from the code generated by GPT-4o-mini also does not always align with the ensuing text, which explains LLMs' sporadic inconsistencies between tool execution results and further contents during inference.

To alleviate the issues above, we propose to filter the data entries in $\mathcal{C}$ where the tool execution results are inconsistent with the following text,
which is achieved by checking if the execution results are included in the subsequent text in our implementation.
Upon completing filtering, the open-source dataset ToolBridge is finally constructed.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\noindent \textbf{Baseline Models.} 
Our experiments involve four baseline models: the base model of Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B.
Also, we remove all the external tool invocation sections in each data entry in ToolBridge (denote as ToolBridge$^{\S}$) and report the results of Llama2-7B and Llma3-8B SFT on ToolBridge$^{\S}$ as two additional baseline models.

\noindent \textbf{Benchmark Datasets.}
The standard benchmark datasets used in our experiments include GSM 8k \cite{cobbe2021training}, GSM Plus \cite{li2024gsm}, MathBench \cite{liu2024mathbench} and Stanford WebQA \cite{berant-etal-2013-semantic}.
For GSM 8k and GSM Plus, we conduct performance evaluation on their respective test sets under few-shot setting, where we leverage a fixed CoT-n-shot prompt template, as outlined in \cite{li2024gsm}.
For MathBench, we report results on MathBench-A, where we transform the multiple-choice questions in the College, High and Middle categories into a question-and-answer format for CoT-n-shot evaluation.
To differentiate from original MathBench, we refer to this adjusted dataset as MathBench$^*$.

We also design two custom datasets, named RandomQA and FACT, to evaluate the capabilities of the language models in data processing, numerical computation and factual retrieval.
Section \ref{sec.rcb} elaborates on the specifics of both datasets.

\noindent \textbf{SFT Settings.}
All the models in our experiments are trained with the open-source TRL library from Hugging Face \cite{vonwerra2022trl}.
The LoRA module \cite{hu2021lora} is used to perform SFT on the base model of Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B utilizing ToolBridge$^{\S}$ or ToolBridge, with a LoRA rank of 16.
Model training is conducted on 64 $\times$ MI200 64GB GPUs, with each processing a batch size of 2 (\emph{i.e.}, total batch size is 128).
AdamW is employed to optimize the parameters of LoRA, with a cosine learning rate scheduler, configuring the initial lr at 3e-5 and the total training epoch at 3.

\noindent \textbf{Inference Settings.}
The primary process of model inference is described in Algorithm \ref{alg.processst}.
Moreover, to handle potential tool call failures during inference, we propose to eliminate failed tool calls from the current output before conditioning the generation of further text.
All trained models are evaluated on 16 $\times$ MI200 64 GB GPUs, with the max new tokens set to 512 and the temperature set to zero.

Upon obtaining these candidate question-answer pairs, we continuously draw random data entries from them and manually check their correctness until 200 correct data entries are verified.
By repeating the above process three times, we collect three FACT datasets, each with a size of 200 data entries, termed FACT-200-Batch1, FACT-200-Batch2 and FACT-200-Batch3.
Table \ref{tab.factzeroshot} provides a comparison of LLMs' performance on the three datasets before and after undergoing SFT on ToolBridge, which shows that LLMs demonstrates enhanced abilities in retrieving factual content.

Besides, one should be aware that relying on external tools to improve the fact retrieval performance of the models can be inconsistent.
During the course of our experiments, we uncover the following deficiencies,

\begin{itemize}
    \item Using external tools like the \emph{requests} module to fetch information from external websites can lead to potentially unreliable information and LLMs thus yield incorrect results. 
    A solution to address this issue is to implement a curated list of trustworthy domains, restricting LLMs from accessing websites beyond this approved whitelist.
    Additionally, further refining the training data entries to exclude unreliable access links is essential.

    \item The content scraped from web pages may be overly lengthy, hindering the ability of the large language models to efficiently extract useful information from the scraped contents.
   
    \item When LLMs access some external websites, they may attempt to download files from those sites, which poses potential security risks in real-world applications.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, we recommend that ToolBridge is only used for academic research, with controlled measures in place when it attempts to access external websites.

\section{Conclusion}


In this paper, we seek to shed light on the process of constructing datasets for training LLMs in the use of external tools.
Specifically, we propose a pipeline to build the ToolBridge dataset, where the foundation of ToolBridge lies in the raw data entries for SFT, sourced from the community.
The selection, conversion, and filtering of these raw data entries are crucial steps in developing high-quality training data for equipping LLMs with external tool abilities.
Our experiments demonstrate that SFT on ToolBridge enables LLMs to effectively learn how to utilize external tools to aid in reasoning process, particularly in the areas including data processing, numerical computation and factual retrieval.
We will make the data generation pipeline for training LLMs in tool use publicly available, along with ToolBridge, for the benefit of the community in advancing research on enabling LLMs to learn how to use external tools.
