\title{Automatic Combination of Sample Selection Strategies for Few-Shot Learning}

\begin{abstract}
In few-shot learning, such as meta-learning, few-shot fine-tuning or in-context learning, the limited number of samples used to train a model have a significant impact on the overall success. Although a large number of sample selection strategies exist, their impact on the performance of few-shot learning is not extensively known, as most of them have been so far evaluated in typical supervised settings only. In this paper, we thoroughly investigate the impact of 20 sample selection strategies on the performance of 5 few-shot learning approaches over 8 image and 6 text datasets. In addition, we propose a new method for automatic combination of sample selection strategies (ACSESS) that leverages the strengths and complementary information of the individual strategies. The experimental results show that our method consistently outperforms the individual selection strategies, as well as the recently proposed method for selecting support examples for in-context learning. We also show a strong modality, dataset and approach dependence for the majority of strategies as well as their dependence on the number of shots -- demonstrating that the sample selection strategies play a significant role for lower number of shots, but regresses to random selection at higher number of shots.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Many domains are characterised by a labelled data scarcity due to data collection and annotation costs and privacy, making the use of typical deep learning unfeasible. To address these problems, few-shot learning aims to train or adapt machine learning models to new tasks using only few labelled samples per class~\cite{song2023survey}.

As the number of utilised labelled samples is small, the role of individual samples is all the more important. Based on the choice of samples for in-context learning, the observed performance can vary between state-of-the-art and random predictions, indicating a strong sensitivity to the sample selection~\cite{pecher2023effects, koksal2022meal, zhang-etal-2022-active}. Even though a lot of research is dedicated to sample selection, such as active learning~\cite{ren2021survey} or core-set selection~\cite{guo2022deepcore}, the most common approach in few-shot learning is a simple random selection. The exception is in-context learning, where a large number of sample selection strategies have been proposed recently, selecting samples according to their properties such as similarity, diversity, informativeness or quality~\cite{li-qiu-2023-finding, zhang-etal-2022-active, chang-jia-2023-data}. 

However, the impact of sample selection strategies on in-context learning or gradient-based few-shot learning remains largely under-explored. Therefore, the goal in this paper is to \textbf{investigate the impact of the sample selection strategies on few-shot image and text classification tasks across a wide range of datasets}.

In addition, as the different strategies mostly focus on selecting samples based on a single property (e.g., how often the samples are forgotten~\cite{toneva2018empirical}, how easy they are to learn~\cite{swayamdipta-etal-2020-dataset, zhang-plank-2021-cartography-active} or how informative they are~\cite{ye-etal-2023-complementary, liu2023towards}), we ask \textbf{whether the selection based on their combination would not lead to better results}. To answer this question and to leverage the strengths of different sample selection strategies, we propose \textbf{ACSESS}, an effective method for \textbf{A}utomatic \textbf{C}ombination of \textbf{S}ampl\textbf{E} \textbf{S}election \textbf{S}trategies. The proposed method first identifies a subset of relevant strategies that can improve the overall success of few-shot learning. Afterwards, the identified strategies are combined together using weighting based on their expected contribution to find samples that can provide the most benefit. We summarise our contributions as follows\footnote{To support replicability and extension of our results, we openly publish the source code of our experiments at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ACSESS}}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We evaluate the impact of 20 sample selection strategies on 5 few-shot learning approaches, including meta-learning, few-shot fine-tuning and in-context learning, across 8 image and 6 text datasets. Experimental results show the selection strategies often lead to non-negligible improvements in performance (up to $2$ percentage points for gradient few-shot learning or $3$ percentage points for in-context learning), while showing a strong approach and dataset dependence.
    \item We propose a method for automatic combination of sample selection strategies to leverage their strengths and identify samples with complementary properties required for the success of few-shot learning. Experimental results show the method consistently leads to performance improvement (up to $3$ percentage points for gradient few-shot learning or $5$ percentage points for in-context learning), outperforming all investigated strategies, including a recent in-context specific strategy LENS~\cite{li-qiu-2023-finding}.
    \item We further analyse how the impact of sample selection changes when increasing the number of shots. We find following key insights: 1) sample selection has significantly higher impact on lower number of shots; 2) at higher number of shots (30-40 on average), the impact of sample selection is negligible as all strategies regress to random selection; and 3) after a certain point, the boost in performance from increasing the number of shots is negligible (50-shot for gradient few-shot learning; 20-shot for in-context learning with strong dependence on input text size and model context size).
\end{itemize}



\section{Related Work}


A lot of focus is dedicated to sample selection: 1) to reduce the computation costs without the loss of performance, by distilling dataset to lower number of samples~\cite{yu2023dataset}; 2) selecting a core set of a dataset with similar properties and same performance as the full set~\cite{guo2022deepcore}; or 3) to reduce annotation costs using active learning~\cite{ren2021survey}. A specific research focus is dedicated to selecting good performing samples for in-context learning, as the overall performance was found to be sensitive to this choice, leading to large variability in results~\cite{pecher2023effects, koksal2022meal, zhang-etal-2022-active}. However, the adoption of sample selection in few-shot learning is limited to active learning strategies and in-context learning.

The sample selection strategies can be divided into two groups. The first, more prevalent, \textbf{uses heuristics and unsupervised metrics} to rate and select samples according to specific property that serves as estimate of their potential to increase the model's performance. The most popular approach is selecting samples based on the \textbf{similarity} to the test sample for in-context learning. The methods differ in employed: 1) similarity measure, such as cosine/Euclidean distance~\cite{liu-etal-2022-makes, an-etal-2023-skill, zemlyanskiy-etal-2022-generate, gupta-etal-2022-retronlu, pasupat-etal-2021-controllable}, BM-25~\cite{wang-etal-2022-training, nashid2023retrieval} or their combination~\cite{agrawal-etal-2023-context, gao-etal-2021-making}; and 2) representation, such as simple TF-IDF~\cite{agrawal-etal-2023-context, wang-etal-2022-training, zemlyanskiy-etal-2022-generate}, word embeddings from basic or further fine-tuned transformers~\cite{liu-etal-2022-makes, gao-etal-2021-making, gupta-etal-2022-retronlu, pasupat-etal-2021-controllable}, or representation generated by prompting a large language model~\cite{an-etal-2023-skill, nashid2023retrieval}.

Besides similarity, the samples are often selected based on their \textbf{informativeness} or the \textbf{uncertainty}. The active learning strategies are most common~\cite{koksal2022meal, schroder-etal-2022-revisiting, margatina-etal-2021-active, park2022active, mavromatis2023examples}, but also other approaches are utilised, such as using the bias of the samples~\cite{ma2023fairness}. Other methods define a notion of \textbf{quality} for each sample, either by prompting a large language model to rate the samples~\cite{shin-etal-2021-constrained} or observing how the inclusion or removal of the sample affects the performance~\cite{maronikolakis-etal-2023-improving, nguyen2023context}.

When considering \textbf{representativeness}, the samples are selected using core-set methods that are designed to find a subset of samples with properties that are representative of the full training set~\cite{guo2022deepcore, killamsetty2021glister, mirzasoleiman2020coresets, paul2021grand, coleman2020Selection}. \textbf{Learnability} metric either determines how easy it is to learn the specific sample~\cite{swayamdipta-etal-2020-dataset, zhang-plank-2021-cartography-active}, or how often the sample is forgotten after being learned~\cite{toneva2018empirical}.  

Finally, some approaches \textbf{balance multiple properties} at the same time. Often, the similarity of samples is balanced with their diversity~\cite{wu2023selfadaptive, qin2023context} or the informativeness of the samples is balanced with their representativeness~\cite{su2022selective, levy-etal-2023-diverse, ye-etal-2023-complementary, liu2023towards}. In specific cases, the search is done in two step fashion, such as finding set of informative samples and then using diversity guided search to improve this set~\cite{li-qiu-2023-finding}.

The second set of approaches \textbf{leverages training to select the samples}. One possibility is to train a sample retriever, such as a separate scoring model~\cite{rubin-etal-2022-learning, luo2023dr, li-etal-2023-unified, wang2023learning, aimen2023leveraging} or using determinantal point process~\cite{yang-etal-2023-representative, ye2023compositional}. Another set of approaches leverages reinforcement learning to select good samples~\cite{zhang-etal-2022-active, shum-etal-2023-automatic, scarlatos2023reticl}. Finally, the Datamodels approach trains a linear model to predict the performance gain of a set of samples to select the subset that would lead to the highest possible performance increase~\cite{ilyas2022datamodels, chang-jia-2023-data, jundi-lapesa-2022-translate, vilar-etal-2023-prompting}.

In this work, we investigate the impact of the existing strategies on few-shot setting. In contrast to~\citep{li-qiu-2023-finding}, which represents a work closest to ours, we explore multiple few-shot learning approaches not only in-context learning. Our proposed method is inspired by the Datamodels approach~\cite{ilyas2022datamodels}, but performs the selection at the level of strategies instead of samples.%, reducing the computational costs.

In this work, we thoroughly investigate the impact of strategies that consider 3 groups of properties: 1) \textit{informativeness}, or how informative the samples are for the model; 2) \textit{representativeness}, or how well the subset of samples represents the full dataset; and 3) \textit{learnability}, or how easy it is to obtain or retain the information contained in the samples. We specifically focus on strategies that perform the selection by assigning a score to each sample that is used to sort the samples and choose the best. This includes active learning strategies that measure the uncertainty of the model, core-set selection strategies that find subset representative of the full-set and other strategies that use training dynamics.

\subsection{ACSESS: Automatic Combination of Strategies}

Even though the different selection strategies can identify samples with specific single property, the use of these identified samples may not have a significant impact on the performance when working in a few-shot setting. For example, an informative sample that is a hard to learn or is often forgotten, may not contribute as much, leading to low impact on the performance. Instead, we propose to select examples that are characterised by a balance of complementary properties, even when they do not represent the best sample of any given property (e.g., a sample that may be less informative, but is more easily learned or retained). To accomplish this, we propose \textbf{ACSESS}, an effective method for \textbf{A}utomatic \textbf{C}ombination of \textbf{S}ampl\textbf{E} \textbf{S}election \textbf{S}trategies, composed of two stages: 1) identification of a subset of well-performing and relevant strategies that can improve the overall success of few-shot learning; and then 2) combination of the identified strategies using a weighted scheme based on their expected contribution. The final set of samples is selected by sorting the samples according to their final score and selecting the ones with the highest score.

\paragraph{Identifying the Subset of Relevant Strategies}

In the first phase, the aim is to identify relevant strategies that, when combined, can select high quality samples based on their different properties. Even though the most optimal combination would be achieved by searching through all the combinations of strategies, such search is infeasible as it requires extensive computation costs. Instead, we use three separate methods for selecting strategies, inspired by feature selection in traditional machine learning and the Datamodels approach~\cite{ilyas2022datamodels}, specifically: 1) \textit{forward} strategy selection; 2) \textit{backward} strategy selection; and 3) \textit{datamodels} strategy selection. The final subset of selection strategies is composed of the ones that are identified by each of the methods as most impactful, to minimise the effect of potential drawbacks of the individual method.

\textbf{Forward Selection} iteratively adds to the subset the selection strategy that results in the highest performance increase (similarly to forward feature selection). Starting with an empty subset, the method alternates between two steps. First, for each strategy not included in the subset we determine the increase in performance the strategy would yield if added to the subset (evaluating the performance of samples selected by the combination of already selected subset and the potential new strategy and comparing to old subset). Afterwards, the strategy that provides the highest positive increase in performance is added to the subset and the first step is repeated. If no added strategy leads to increase of the overall performance, the process ends, resulting in the subset of relevant selection strategies. 

\textbf{Backward Selection} iteratively removes strategies from the subset until the performance no longer increases (similarly to backward feature selection). The method works the same way as the forward selection, but starting with all selection strategies and removing them from the subset based on the performance increase such removal leads to.

\textbf{Datamodels Selection} draws inspiration from the Datamodels~\cite{ilyas2022datamodels, chang-jia-2023-data}, but works on the level of the strategies instead of samples. First, a set of 150 random combinations of strategies is created and evaluated. The random search is constrained to include each strategy at least 5 times to ensure sufficient coverage of each strategy. Afterwards, these combinations are used to create a simple regression dataset, with the difference in performance to the baseline selection (the classic few-shot learning selection) as label, and the multi-label presence vector of strategies as features. The dataset is used to train a regularised linear regression, LASSO~\cite{tibshirani1996regression}, which zeros out the weights for as many strategies as possible. The selection strategies with positive weights in the model represent the subset of relevant strategies.

\paragraph{Combining the Identified Strategies}
After the relevant selection strategies are identified, they are combined using a weighting scheme. Each sample is assigned an importance score by each of the identified strategies. As the different strategies may assign scores of different magnitudes, all scores are normalised to zero-one interval. The final sample score is obtained as a weighted average of its assigned scores and the samples with highest score is selected. 

In this paper, we evaluate three separate weighting schemes. First, \textbf{uniform} combination, assigns the same weight to each of the identified strategies. This represents the simplified version of the ACSESS method, potentially resulting in lower performance impact, but also with lower computation costs as no further optimisation is required. This weighting is also used for forward and backward selection. Second, \textbf{weighted} combination, assigns a different weight to each strategy, prioritising specific sample properties. The final trained weights of the \textit{Datamodels} linear model are used as the dataset and model specific weights for the different strategies. As the weighting scheme is dataset and model specific, it should lead to the highest performance impact, at the cost of requiring more extensive computation. Finally, the \textbf{weighted combination with random selection} introduces a random element to the \textit{weighted} combination, as random selection was identified as a strong baseline that can improve active learning performance~\cite{park2022active}. Each sample is assigned additional uniformly randomised score which is included in the combination. The weight of this score is determined by the \textit{Datamodels} by including random selection as additional strategy, due to its impact being dataset and model dependent.



\section{Experiments}



\textbf{Datasets.} The experiments are conducted on image and text classification datasets composed of different tasks with different number of classes. For the image datasets, we use 8 randomly chosen datasets from the \textbf{Meta-Album}~\cite{meta-album-2022}, specifically \textbf{LR\_AM.DOG} and \textbf{LR\_AM.AWA} for animal classification, \textbf{VCL.APL} for plane classification, \textbf{HUM\_ACT.ACT\_410} for classification of human actions, \textbf{MNF.TEX\_DTD} for classification of texture, \textbf{MCR.PRT} and \textbf{MCR.PNU} for classification of microscope images, and \textbf{PLT.FLW} for flower classification. In addition, we use 6 widely-adopted text datasets: \textbf{20 News Group}~\cite{LANG1995331} and \textbf{News Category}~\cite{misra2022news} for news category classification and \textbf{ATIS}~\cite{hemphill-etal-1990-atis}, \textbf{Facebook}~\cite{schuster-etal-2019-cross-lingual}, \textbf{HWU-64}~\cite{liu2021benchmarking} and \textbf{SNIPS}~\cite{coucke2018snips} for intent classification.

\textbf{Few-shot Learning Approaches.} The evaluation is done in a \textbf{5-way 5-shot setting} using the gradient few-shot learning on both the image and text datasets and the in-context learning on the text datasets. For gradient few-shot learning, we use meta-learning approaches, specifically \textbf{Prototypical Networks}~\cite{snell2017prototypical} and \textbf{MAML}~\cite{finn2017model}, and \textbf{Few-Shot Fine-Tuning}~\cite{chen2019closer}. For in-context learning, we use the \textbf{Mistral-7B}~\cite{jiang2023mistral} and \textbf{Zephyr-7B}~\cite{tunstall2023zephyr} models.% large language models with 4 bit quantisation.

\textbf{Baselines.} The selection strategies are compared with 2 baselines: 1) \textbf{Classic selection}, where a new set of samples is randomly selected for each new task, potentially covering the whole datasets when evaluating on enough tasks; and 2) \textbf{Random selection}, where only a single set of 5 samples per class is randomly selected at the start and used for every single single task, representing a more realistic setting with limited budget. In-context learning is also compared with the recently proposed \textbf{LENS}~\cite{li-qiu-2023-finding} strategy that is in-context learning specific. Each selection strategy is run over 10 random seeds to determine their sensitivity to the effects of randomness (e.g., randomly initialised seeds). In addition, each experiment is repeated 5 times for different data splits and an average accuracy over these runs is reported (if not specified otherwise).

In this section, our goal is to answer the following research question: \emph{\textbf{RQ1:} What is the impact of single-property selection strategies on the few-shot learning?} The objective is to analyse the performance increase of the different selection strategies across various datasets and few-shot learning approaches. We present the results separately for image and text datasets, as well as for the gradient few-shot learning and in-context learning, as we observed a strong dependence of the strategy benefit on the modality and few-shot learning approach used. 

\textbf{Majority of selection strategies fail to outperform the Classic selection in case of gradient few-shot learning}. In most cases, the strategies lead to performance on par or even lower than the Classic selection (for example hard samples on image datasets or diverse samples on text datasets). Only specific strategies consistently lead to better performance (e.g., easy to learn samples on image datasets, or Glister on text datasets). As such, the random sample selection represents a strong baseline for the gradient few-shot learning.

\textbf{Sample selection is more beneficial for in-context learning.} Larger number of strategies leads to increase in performance, while only few strategies, such as the popular selection based on similarity or diversity, under-perform the Classic selection. At the same time, the increase in performance is higher than with gradient few-shot learning, although with a larger variance in results. In contrast to gradient few-shot learning, the hard to learn samples provide the most benefit for in-context learning. The reason may be that hard to learn samples provide a lot of information beneficial for the classification, which cannot be easily leveraged in the gradient few-shot learning due to the samples being hard to learn. However, as there is no explicit ``learning" in the in-context learning use of large language models, such samples can be more easily leveraged.
\textbf{Learnability property is a strong indicator of quality for few-shot learning}. The strategies that choose based on how easy it is to learn the samples (Cartography) or how often they are forgotten (Forgetting) consistently lead to overall highest increase in performance. The observed improvement is on average as high as $1$ percentage point for gradient few-shot learning and $1.5$ percentage points for in-context learning.


\subsection{ACSESS Results and Characteristics}

\begin{figure}[tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\linewidth]{figures/combination-lens-diff.png}
    \caption{Comparison of the LENS method \cite{li-qiu-2023-finding} to our proposed automatic combination of selection strategies (ACSESS).}
    \label{fig:combination-lens-diff}
\end{figure}

In this section, we answer the following research question: \emph{\textbf{RQ2:} How does the ACSESS method perform in comparison to the single-property strategies?} Besides analysing the benefit and comparing with other strategies, we analyse the influence of the identified strategies and of the different weighting schemes.%  We aim to identify the selected strategies, analyse the influence of various strategy selection approaches and weighting schemes.

\textbf{The ACSESS method consistently leads to performance increase across all few-shot learning approaches.} Our proposed method outperforms all the investigated single-property strategies, leading to an average performance increase of up to $1.8$ percentage points for gradient few-shot learning and $2.5$ percentage points for in-context learning. Comparing with the in-context specific strategy LENS~\cite{li-qiu-2023-finding} (see Figure~\ref{fig:combination-lens-diff} for comparison), which also outperforms all single-property strategies, we observe the increase of ACSESS is statistically more significant (p-value of $0.0002$ using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test).

\textbf{The ACSESS method identifies samples with complementary properties, with higher priority on learnability.} The identified selection strategies that are used in the combination are different for the different data modalities (image vs. text) and the type of few-shot learning (gradient vs. in-context learning). For the image datasets, the combination of easy to learn samples (Cartography) that are located on the decision boundary (Margin) provides the most benefit. For the gradient few-shot learning approaches on text datasets, the combination of easy and ambiguous samples (Cartography) that are least often forgotten (Forgetting) on the decision boundary (Margin) that provide additional informativeness from the Graph-Cut strategy provides the most benefit. In case of in-context learning, the combination of hard to learn samples (Cartography) that are least often forgotten (Forgetting) on the decision boundary (Margin) and with highest Entropy provide the most benefit. Further analysing the identified strategies and their weights, we find the ACSESS method gives higher importance to samples that are \textit{learnable}, lower importance to \textit{informative} samples (but with multiple sources for both), while the strategies that focus on \textit{representativeness} are not included at all.

\textbf{The dataset and approach specific weighting leads to the best performance}. The only exception is the Few-Shot Fine-Tuning approach, where the uniform weighting shows the most benefit. At the same time, the weighting strategy that incorporates the random selection often leads to worse performance and higher variance in the results. Even though the uniform combination is computationally less expensive than the weighted combination, its performance increase is only slightly lower (average difference of $0.10-0.25$). As such, the \textbf{uniform weighting represents a good trade-off} between the performance increase and the computation costs, making it in general a better choice.


\subsection{Effects of Variable Number of Shots}

\begin{figure*}[tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/change-by-shots.png}
    \caption{Effect of the number of shots on the benefit of selection strategies, comparing ACSESS and random selection, aggregated over datasets. The benefit is calculated as a difference to the classic selection at 5-shots. The benefit of sample selection is more significant at lower number of shots. At larger number of shots, the benefit and the performance boost from further increase of shots becomes negligible.}% with the performance remaining constant or even decreasing in the case of in-context learning.}
    \label{fig:change-by-shots}
\end{figure*}
In this section, we answer the following research question: \emph{\textbf{RQ3:} How does the benefit of sample selection change based on the number of shots?} Our aim is to investigate how the benefit of sample selection strategies changes as we increase or decrease the number of shots that we select. In addition, we investigate whether the few-shot learning approaches themselves benefit from larger number of shots. The results of this evaluation are included in Figure~\ref{fig:change-by-shots}, comparing the benefit of the ACSESS strategy and Random selection over the main experimental setup (5-shot setting).

\textbf{The selection strategies provide more benefit on lower number of shots.} On the 1-shot setting, the difference in accuracy between the Random strategy and the ACSESS strategy can be as high as $5$, $7$ or $4$ percentage points respectively for the few-shot learning on image data and text data, and in-context learning on text data. Increasing the number of selected shots leads to diminished impact, as the ACSESS strategy regresses to random selection when selecting more than 30-40 shots. This comparison further reinforces our findings, as we find that the difference to random selection is statistically significantly higher in the 5-shot (p-value of $1.93e-07$ using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test).
\textbf{Impact of selection strategies and further increase of the number of shots is negligible after certain point.} For the gradient few-shot learning, the performance after approximately 50 shots remains more or less constant. For the in-context learning, the performance even decreases significantly after 20-25 shots, achieving similar performance to the 1-shot setting when using 50-shots. The main reason for this drop-off may be the limited context size of the large language models, making the model degenerate to random guessing as the cross-over is text length dependent. As such, the focus on selecting high quality samples is paramount when only few labelled samples are available (e.g., working with limited labelling budget), while the random selection can be safely used with enough labels. Furthermore, labelling more samples and using them as additional shots for few-shot learning may not lead to better performance.

\subsection{Additional Insights to Sample Selection Strategies}

\begin{figure*}[tbh]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/aggregated-std.png}
    \caption{Standard deviation introduced by multiple runs of the different selection strategies. The results for Prototypical Networks, MAML and Few-Shot Fine-Tuning are aggregated over both the image and text datasets, while the results for Mistral and Zephyr are only from text datasets. The ACSESS method shows lower sensitivity to repeated runs compared to the majority of the strategies included in the combination.}
    \label{fig:strategy-deviation}
\end{figure*}

In this section, we provide additional insights from ablation studies.

\textbf{Specific strategies show sensitivity to the effects of random seed.} Changing the random seed for specific strategies may often lead to different set of selected samples and different performance impact. The only exception are some of the core set selection strategies (such as Glister or the Graph-Cut), which consistently lead to the selection of the same set of samples. On the other hand, strategies with random initial condition, such as the similarity selection or some of the active learning strategies, are more sensitive to the effects of the random seed. \textbf{The ACSESS method shows lower sensitivity to repeated runs} (with the exception of weighting with random element) \textbf{but still may lead to different sets of samples based on the random seed}.

\textbf{Strong dataset dependence for selection strategies.} The strategy that performs well on one dataset (such as DeepFool on the HUM\_ACT.ACT\_410 dataset) may perform quite poorly on other datasets (such as MCR.PRT or MCR.PNU). Such strategies cannot be used on any dataset without further evaluation. \textbf{Only few strategies perform consistently on the majority of the datasets}, such as Herding showing poor performance on all image datasets, or Cartography showing good performance across all datasets and models. We \textbf{do not observe a significant dataset dependence of the ACSESS method}, as it identifies the well-performing strategies and leverages their strengths for the specific dataset. 

\textbf{Strong model dependence and sensitivity to selected samples.} The strategy performing well for one model (GraNd on MCR.PNU for MAML, or KCenter on 20 News for Zephyr) may result in a decrease of performance for other models (GraNd for Few-Shot Fine-Tuning, or KCenter for Mistral on the same setting). Overall, the selected samples resulting from different strategy or from a repeated run of the same strategy \textbf{affect the Few-Shot Fine-Tuning less}, leading to smaller benefit. At the same time, the \textbf{MAML} method and the \textbf{in-context learning} models are \textbf{more affected by the selected samples}, leading to more varied results and often higher performance increase. Such sensitivity to sample selection was observed in previous works as well~\cite{pecher2023effects, koksal2022meal, zhang-etal-2022-active, li-qiu-2023-finding}. Curiously, Prototypical Networks gain quite large performance increase from sample selection, but show low sensitivity to the selected samples and variance from repeated runs of sample selection. As such, \textbf{sample selection is more important for MAML and In-Context Learning and less important for Few-Shot Fine-Tuning.}




\section{Conclusion}


In this paper, we investigate the impact of larger number of different sample selection strategies on the success of few-shot learning. To leverage strengths of these strategies, we propose the ACSESS method for automatic combination of the sample selection strategies to select samples with complementary properties. The experimental results show that although the individual sample selection strategies may not always be beneficial, their combination using our proposed method consistently leads to better performance. Curiously, we observe that the \textit{learnability} of the samples is more important for few-shot learning than their \textit{informativeness} or \textit{representativeness}. Finally, analysing the behaviour of the sample selection in regards to the number of selected shots, we find that the sample selection provides the most benefit on low number of shots, while at the higher number of shots all strategies regress to random guessing. In addition, after certain point, increasing the number of available samples no longer leads to improvement in performance of few-shot learning, but may lead to decrease in performance in some cases, especially for in-context learning.
