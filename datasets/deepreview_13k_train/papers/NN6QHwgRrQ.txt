\title{MAP: Multi-Human-Value Alignment Palette}

\begin{abstract}

Ensuring that generative AI systems align with human values is essential but challenging, especially when considering multiple human values and their potential trade-offs. Since human values can be personalized and dynamically change over time, the desirable levels of value alignment vary across different ethnic groups, industry sectors, and user cohorts. Within existing frameworks, it is hard to define human values and align AI systems accordingly across different directions simultaneously, such as harmlessness, helpfulness, and positiveness. To address this, we develop a novel, first-principle approach called Multi-Human-Value Alignment Palette (MAP), which navigates the alignment across multiple human values in a structured and reliable way. MAP formulates the alignment problem as an optimization task with user-defined constraints, which define human value targets. It can be efficiently solved via a primal-dual approach, which determines whether a user-defined alignment target is achievable and how to achieve it. We conduct a detailed theoretical analysis of MAP by quantifying the trade-offs between values, the sensitivity to constraints, the fundamental connection between multi-value alignment and sequential alignment, and proving that linear weighted rewards are sufficient for multi-value alignment. Extensive experiments demonstrate MAP's ability to align multiple values in a principled manner while delivering strong empirical performance across various tasks.



\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have highlighted the critical need for aligning AI systems with human values, a concept known as \textit{human value alignment}~\citep{griffith2013policy,arumugam2019deep,gabriel2020artificial}.
The alignment can serve the purpose of generating outcomes that are better suited for human ethics~\citep{griffith2013policy}, personalized needs~\citep{kirk2024benefits}, or reduced harmful content~\citep{bai2022training}.
This alignment has traditionally been pursued by adjusting AI behavior to adhere to specific attributes via preference datasets or reward functions. This process involves finetuning the original model according to the optimization problem:
{
\begin{align}
    \max_{\q \in \mathcal{P}} \E_{x \sim \D, y \sim \qx}\biggl\{ \r(x, y) -\beta \Dkl{\q(\cdot \mid x)}{\px} \biggr\}. \label{eq_RLHF}
\end{align}
}
Here, $\mathcal{P}$ denotes the class of all distributions, $\p$ is the distribution that represents the generative model to align, $\q$ is the distribution that represents the aligned model, $\r$ is a reward function that quantifies the preference level of any given pair of prompt $x$ and generation $y$, $D_{\KL}$ measures the KL-divergence, and $\beta > 0$ is a regularization hyperparameter. 
This formulation has deep conceptual roots in the Bayesian decision theoretic framework~\citep{bissiri2016general}. Specifically, if we consider $x$ as observed data and $y$ as a parameter $\theta$, the problem (\ref{eq_RLHF}) can be expressed as $\E_{\theta \sim \q(\cdot)} \bigl\{ \log p(x \mid \theta) - \Dkl{\q(\cdot)}{\p(\cdot)} \bigr\}$. This formulation yields the solution $p_x(\theta) \propto \p(\theta) p(x \mid \theta)$, which is precisely Bayes' Rule.
However, while this formulation provides an elegant interpretation of how AI models can be adjusted to reflect new information or preferences, it may not fully capture the complexity required when aligning AI systems to multiple, potentially conflicting human values. For example, a healthcare-related large language model must deliver information that is not only precise but also easy-to-understand and harmless~\citep{gebreab2024llm}. Similarly, a customer service chatbot optimized for efficiency may compromise on politeness or empathy~\citep{kolasani2023optimizing}. These scenarios highlight the need to balance diverse human values, which often requires more nuanced solutions than a single-dimensional alignment approach can offer.

\textbf{Related work. }
Centered around the formulation (\ref{eq_RLHF}), a standard approach is the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)~\citep{griffith2013policy,arumugam2019deep,bai2022training,dai2024safe} that first trains a reward model $\r$ based on pairwise comparison or ranking data to serve  as a proxy for human preferences, and then use reinforcement learning with the reward model to address the optimization problem (\ref{eq_RLHF}). An alternative method known as direct preference optimization (DPO)~\citep{rafailov2023direct} directly optimizes an empirical risk based on the Bradley-Terry loss~\citep{bradley1952rank} applied to the preference data, using an implicit reward in the form of $ r(x,y) \de \beta \log (\q(y \mid x)/\p(y \mid x))$. 
To address the alignment of multiple human values, recent work on aligning foundation models have used multi-objective reinforcement learning (MORL) algorithms~\citep{barrett2008learning,li2020deep,wu2024fine}. Existing studies focus on approximating trade-offs among values utilizing a linear scalarization method that combines either various reward functions or data sources in both RLHF and DPO approaches~\citep{bai2022training,rame2023rewarded,dai2024safe}. Alternatively, some studies utilize specific choices of combination weights to manage trade-offs~\citep{dognin2024contextual}. A recent technique named ``rewarded soup'' was introduced to efficiently compute the aligned models under linear scalarization~\citep{rame2023rewarded}. It first separately fine-tunes multiple models, each with a particular reward function, and subsequently aggregate these networks by linearly interpolating their weights. This method aims to approximate the ensemble of fine-tuned models that would otherwise result from optimizing a single reward composed of various linear combinations of the individual reward functions, namely $\r \de \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i r_i$ with diverse randomly generated $\bm \lambda = [\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_m]$. A similar idea was applied to the DPO method~\citep{zhou2023beyond}, where the DPO-aligned models under individual values are linearly interpolated to numerically approximate the Pareto Frontier.

\textbf{Challenges.} 
Despite significant progress in the domain, aligning AI models to multiple values simultaneously presents several unresolved challenges.
First of all, as demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig_pareto_demo}, aligning with one value, such as helpfulness, harmlessness, or humor, could inadvertently diminish another. 
These observations lead us to pose a critical question: \textit{How can we quantify and achieve the concurrent enhancement of multiple human values without compromise?}

Even if we can try all possible combinations, there is no theoretical justification that linearly combining individual reward functions is sufficient to obtain the Pareto Frontier. The DPO method, while simplifying the alignment process through a direct empirical risk optimization, still does not address the issue of integrating multiple data sources, which have their underpinning still at the problem (\ref{eq_RLHF}). \textbf{Contributions. } 
We introduce the Multi-Human-Value Alignment Palette (MAP), a principled approach designed to rigorously aligning multi-dimensional values with a provable guarantee. Similar to an artist's color palette, MAP enables the intricate blending of multiple human values to ``paint'' the AI's behavior with a broad spectrum of preference shades. In Figure~\ref{fig_hist_llama2chat_80}, we illustrate how MAP allows users to precisely customize and control the level of improvement for all values in an interpretable manner. 
The proposed MAP introduces several technical innovations and contributions:

$\bullet$ Formulation. We propose a novel problem formulation that allows one to align multiple human values using user-defined constraints, which we term ``value palettes.'' Each palette acts as a constraint that represents a preferred level of alignment, allowing us to ``MAP'' from any targeted value levels specified by the user to a particular reward function for (\ref{eq_RLHF}). This precise one-to-one mapping ensures exact adjustments to model behavior.
$\bullet$ Theory. We provide theoretical analysis within the MAP framework quantifies the representation of the solution, its sensitivity to changes in the value palette, and its feasible operational range. This leads to a deeper understanding of the inherent trade-offs among various values. Furthermore, we investigate the range of realizable value levels and demonstrate that a linear combination of individual reward functions, is sufficient to reach the Pareto Frontier. We also establish a crucial link between multi-value alignment and sequential alignment, showing that cyclically updating each value multiple times achieves equivalent results to a single execution of MAP.

$\bullet$ Computational methodology. Based on our theoretical derivations, we propose a computational method to ascertain the achievability of user-defined value palettes. The proposed method utilizes a primal-dual approach to efficiently solve the optimization problem for a feasible choice within the palette. We verify that the dual problem exhibits concavity, enabling effective resolution through gradient ascent techniques.  Notably, MAP is agnostic to the stages of model alignment, whether at the decoding or training stage, the model architecture, and the specific definitions of values used. Finally, we conducted comprehensive experimental studies to validate the practical effectiveness of MAP. 


\section{MAP: Multi-Human-Value Alignment Palette}

 
\subsection{Problem Formulation} 

The formulation in (\ref{eq_RLHF}) can be seen as maximizing the expected reward while imposing a regularization to minimize unnecessary deviations from the original model. This insight leads us to define a value alignment through a statistical functional constraint:
\begin{align}
    \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \qx} r(x,y) \geq c ,\label{eq_3}
\end{align}
which is interpreted as \textit{the expected rewards, or realized levels, under a value preference must be at least $c$}.
Likewise, to align $m \geq 1$ value preferences, we introduce the following \textbf{MAP problem}:
{
\begin{align}
    &\min_{\q \in \mathcal{P}} \E_{x \sim \D, y \sim \qx}\Dkl{\qx}{\px} %\nonumber \\ %\label{eq_MAP} \\
    \, \st \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \qx}r_i(x,y) \geq c_i, \, \forall i =1,\ldots, m. \label{eq_MAP_b}
\end{align}
}
We denote $\bm c \de [c_1,\ldots,c_m]^\T$ as the \textit{value palette}. With a solution $\q$, we refer to $\E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \qx} (\bm r(x,y) \de [r_1(x,y), \ldots, r_m(x,y)]^\T)$ as the \textit{realized value levels}.
We write $\bm u \geq \bm v$ if the two vectors are of the same size and $u_i \geq v_i$ for each entry~$i$.

\begin{theorem}[Representation of MAP solution]\label{thm_MAP_solution}
    The solution to the MAP problem (\ref{eq_MAP_b}) is
    \begin{align}
        \q_{\bm \lambda}(y \mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x,\lambda)} \p(y \mid x) e^{\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y)} ,\label{eq_5}
    \end{align}
    where $\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y) = \sum_{i=1}^m \lambda_i  r_i(x,y)$, for some $\bm \lambda \geq \bm 0$. 
    Moreover, assuming that $\bm r(x,y)$ is not trivially a constant on the support set of $x,y$, the above $\bm \lambda$ is the unique solution to the problem:
    \begin{align}
        \max_{\bm \lambda \geq \bm 0} g(\bm \lambda) \de - \log Z(\bm \lambda) + \bm \lambda^\T \bm c ,  
        \label{eq_dual}
    \end{align}
    where $Z(\bm \lambda) \de \E_{x \sim \D, y \sim \px} e^{\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y)}$, and $g$ is strictly concave. As a result, we can treat $\bm \lambda$ in (\ref{eq_5}) as an implicit function of $\bm c$ and denote it as 
    $ %\begin{align}
         \bm \lambda = \bm \lambda (\bm c) \de \argmax_{\bm \lambda \geq \bm 0} g(\bm \lambda) . %\label{eq_20}
    $ %\end{align}
\end{theorem}

\begin{remark}[Interpretation of $\bm \lambda$]
    Theorem~\ref{thm_MAP_solution} establishes a one-to-one correspondence between the vectors $\bm c$ and $\bm \lambda$.
    The proof to the first part of Theorem~\ref{thm_MAP_solution} uses the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions, and the second part is obtained by showing that (\ref{eq_dual}) is the dual problem of (\ref{eq_MAP_b}). From a decision theoretic view, the decision of $\lambda$ is based on trading off the utility term $\bm \lambda^\T \bm c$ and the ``risk'' term $-\log Z(\bm \lambda)$. The latter term can be seen as a form of risk aversion, because maximizing it would penalize decisions that place a disproportionate weight on less likely, albeit highly desirable, outcomes.
    Practically, the expectation $\E_{x \sim \D, y \sim \px}$ can be easily approximated using a sample average from a dataset generated under $\p$, allowing the dual problem (\ref{eq_dual}) to be numerically solved. This computational aspect is further explored in Section~\ref{subsec_dual}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Choice of Value Palette $\bm{c}$] \label{remark_palette}
We outline interpretable methods for selecting the value palette $\bm{c}$.
The first method is the \textit{quantile-based approach}, which utilizes statistical distributions of reward outputs. For any reward function, input prompts $x \in \D$, and model-generated outputs $y \sim \px$, the mapping 
$ %\begin{align}
      (x, y) \mapsto \bm{r}(x,y)   
$ %\end{align}
defines a random variable, denoted by $\mathcal{R}(\D,\p)$. The expectation of $\mathcal{R}(\D,\p)$ is the realized value under the model $\p$. Users can set each component of $\bm{c}$ to correspond to specific quantiles of $\mathcal{R}(\D,\p)$. For example, setting $c$ to the 80\% quantile means the targeted expectation of $\mathcal{R}(\D,\q)$ for the aligned model $\q$ is positioned within the upper 20\% of expected outcomes. This distribution can be Monte Carlo approximated using data generation from $\p$.
The second method is the \textit{classifier-based approach}, which leverages a pretrained classifier. Suppose $r$ is a reward function representing the log probability of an attribute (e.g., harmlessness). Users can enhance this attribute by increasing its likelihood in the aligned model $\q$, such as by setting
$ %\begin{align}
    \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \qx}r(x,y) \geq \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \px}r(x,y) + \log(1.2) .
$ %\end{align}
This is a straightforward mechanism for users to make practical, user-defined enhancements to the model's output characteristics.
It is important to note that not all choices of $\bm{c}$ lead to a feasible MAP problem. We will revisit this issue and propose an algorithmic approach that auto-suggests a suitable value palette for users based on feasibility in Section~\ref{subsec_dual}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Source of Reward Functions]\label{remark_reward}

We discuss common methods for obtaining reward functions $r_i$:

\textbf{(i) From pretrained classifiers}: Each reward function associated with a preference class $v_i$ is defined as:
\begin{align}
    r_i(x,y) \de \log p(v_i \mid x,y) \in (-\infty, 0] \label{eq_reward1},
\end{align}
where $p(v_i \mid x,y)$ denotes the probability that a sentence $(x,y)$ belongs to the preference class $v_i$. For instance, for $v_i$ representing ``harmlessness,'' $p(v_i \mid x,y)$ might be sourced from a classifier trained to detect if text is harmless. Assuming conditional independence of $v_i$ given $(x,y)$ and $\lambda_i = 1$ for all $i$, the solution for $q$ in (\ref{eq_5}) reduces to:
\begin{align}
    \q(y \mid x) \propto \p(y \mid x) \prod_{i=1}^m p(v_i \mid x,y) \propto p(y \mid x, v_1,\ldots,v_m).
\end{align}
In this configuration, the generative model $\q$ functions as a ``controlled generator,'' producing text conditioned on specified human values. For more refined applications, the model may employ  $p(v_i \mid x,y)^{\lambda_i}$, allowing for the adjustment of preferences through the tempering of likelihoods. From a modeling perspective, the choice of weights $\lambda_i$ could reflect some suspicion of mis-specification of models or hyperparameters~\citep{holmes2017assigning,ding2018model,zhang2023information}.

\textbf{(ii) From pairwise comparison datasets}: Rewards can also be derived from standard functions learned explicitly through pairwise or ranking comparisons, a method commonly employed in the RLHF literature.

\textbf{(iii) From language processing metrics}: Metrics such as coherence, diversity, and perplexity are used to construct rewards that reflect various aspects of language quality and effectiveness. These metrics help in refining the model to produce more desirable text characteristics. 
\end{remark}


\subsection{Realizable Value Levels of the MAP problem and Pareto Frontier} \label{subsec_realizable}

We first show that the MAP problem can be written as the original alignment problem (\ref{eq_RLHF}) with a particular reward function that is simply a linear combination of individual rewards. 

\begin{theorem}[Solution of MAP]\label{thm_MAP_equal_RLHF}
     The solution to the MAP problem (\ref{eq_MAP_b}) is the same of the problem (\ref{eq_RLHF}) with $\r(x, y) \de \beta \cdot \lambda(\bm c)^\T \bm r(x,y)$, where $\bm \lambda(\bm c)$ was introduced in Theorem~\ref{thm_MAP_solution}. 
\end{theorem}
This prompts the natural question of whether MAP limits the breadth of realizable value levels compared to those achievable under (\ref{eq_RLHF}) with arbitrarily chosen $R$. Next, we will show that actually the realizable value levels of (\ref{eq_RLHF}) and the MAP problem in (\ref{eq_MAP_b}) are the same.

Given the reference distribution $\p$ and any specific reward functions $\r$, the solution $\q$, if feasible, depends solely on $\r$. To illustrate this dependency, we denote $\q$ as $\q_{\r}$. Let $\mathfrak{F}_{\RLHF}(\p)$ represent the range of $\r$ that admits feasible solutions to the RLHF problem, essentially those with valid probability densities. The realizable value levels under the RLHF problem are defined as:
{
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{V}_{\RLHF}(\p) \de \biggl\{ \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \q_{\r}(\cdot \mid x)} \bm r(x,y) : \,  \r \in \mathfrak{F}_{\RLHF}(\p) \biggr\} .\nonumber
\end{align}
}
For multiple reward functions $r_1, \ldots, r_m$, we consider a specific class of $R$ comprising various non-negative linear combinations, and define their realizable value levels similarly:
{
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{V}_{\RLHF}(r_1, \ldots, r_m; \p) \de \biggl\{ \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \q_{\r}(\cdot \mid x)} \bm r(x,y) : \, \r \de \sum_{i=1}^m \rho_i r_i \in \mathfrak{F}_{\RLHF}(\p) , \, \rho_i \geq 0 \biggr\}. \nonumber
\end{align}
}
In the MAP problem, given $\p$ and $\bm r$, the solution $\q$, if feasible, depends only on the user-specified value palette $\bm c$. To emphasize this relationship, we denote $\q$ as $\q_{\bm c}$. Let $\C_{\MAP}(r_1, \ldots, r_m; \p)$ denote the range of $\bm c$ that admits feasible solutions to the MAP problem. We further consider the realized value levels of all feasible solutions under various $\bm c$, defined as:
{
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{V}_{\MAP}(r_1, \ldots, r_m; \p) \de \biggl\{ \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \q_{\bm c}(\cdot \mid x)} \bm r(x,y) : \, \bm c \in \C_{\MAP}(r_1, \ldots, r_m; \p) \biggr\} . \nonumber
\end{align}
}
\begin{theorem}[Equivalent realizable value levels] \label{thm_realizable}
    For any original generative model $\p$, we have 
    \begin{align}
        \mathcal{V}_{\MAP}(r_1, \ldots, r_m; \p) 
        = \mathcal{V}_{\RLHF}(\p) 
        = \mathcal{V}_{\RLHF}(r_1, \ldots, r_m; \p) .
    \end{align} 
In particular, using linear combinations of individual reward functions is sufficient to cover the entire Pareto Frontier. 
\end{theorem} 
\begin{figure}[tb]
  \centering
\includegraphics[width=0.33\linewidth]{fig_realized_values.pdf} 
  \caption{
    { Illustration of Theorem~\ref{thm_realizable}.}
    }
  \label{fig_realized_values}
\end{figure}
Theorem~\ref{thm_realizable} establishes that the realizable value levels by MAP equate to those in the original alignment problem (\ref{eq_RLHF}) using a specific reward function -- a linear combination of individual rewards. It is crucial to note that the sets of solutions, namely $\q$, are not identical for both problems. The key insight is that the set of realizable value levels, which resides within a finite $m$-dimensional space, is mapped from the infinitely dimensional set of solutions $\q$ through a many-to-one mapping, as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig_realized_values}.




We denote all feasible value palettes $\C_{\MAP}(r_1, \ldots, r_m; \p)$ simply as $\C$. The following theorem explores the characteristics of this set.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm_pareto}
    The following properties of $\C$ hold. 

    (i)
    If $\bm c \in \C$, for any $\bm c'$ such that $\bm c' \leq \bm c $, we have $\bm c'\in \C$.

    (ii) 
    For any $\bm c'$ such that $\bm c' \leq \bm c_0 \de \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \px}\bm r(x,y)$, the value level of the original model, we have the corresponding $\bm \lambda(\bm c') = \bm 0$.

    (iii)
    Let $B_i \de \esssup_{x \sim \D, y \sim \px} r_i(x,y)$, where the essential supreme is under the original model $\p$. Then, $\C \subseteq (-\infty, B_1] \times \cdots \times (-\infty, B_m]$. In other words, a feasible solution does not exist if $c_i > B_i$ for some $i$.
    (iv) 
    Let
    $%\begin{align}
        \q_{\bm \lambda}(y \mid x) \de \p(y \mid x) e^{\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y)}/ Z(\bm \lambda, x) 
    $ %\end{align}
    be the $\bm \lambda$-adjusted distribution of $\p$, where 
    $ %\begin{align}
        Z(\bm \lambda, x) \de \int_{y'} \p(y' \mid x) e^{\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y')} dy' 
    $ %\end{align}
    is the normalizing constant. 
    Treating the optimal solution $\bm \lambda=\bm \lambda(\bm c)$ as a function of $\bm c$, we have $\frac{d \bm \lambda(\bm c)}{\bm c} = - V^{-1}$, where $V \de \V_{\bm \lambda}(\bm r(x,y))$ is the covariance matrix of $\bm r(x,y)$ under the distribution $x \sim \D, y \mid x \sim \q_{\bm \lambda}(\cdot \mid x)$.

\end{theorem}

The result in (iv) can be used to check if a small increase in $\bm c$, e.g., changing $c_1$ to $c_1+\delta$ for a small $\delta>0$, will cause $\bm \lambda$ to change in a way that remains feasible.

In this section, we introduced a practical framework to solve MAP problem defined in  (\ref{eq_MAP_b}), illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig_schema} and Algorithm~\ref{algo_MAP}. 

Once a user defines a value palette according to Remark~\ref{remark_palette}), in Step~2, we derive $\bm \lambda$ from $\bm c$ as per Theorem~\ref{thm_MAP_solution}. We then approximate Problem (\ref{eq_dual}) as follows:
{
    \begin{align}
        \max_{\bm \lambda \geq \bm 0} g(\bm \lambda) \de - \log \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n e^{\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x_j,y_j)} + \bm \lambda^\T \bm c, 
        \label{eq_dual_approx}
    \end{align}
}
where the dataset $\{(x_j,y_j)_{j=1}^n\}$ serves as a finite-sample approximation of the distribution $\p(x,y)$ with $y_1, \ldots, y_n$, generated conditional on respective prompts $x_1, \ldots, x_n$. 
It can be verified that both the original problem (\ref{eq_dual}) and its approximation (\ref{eq_dual_approx}) are concave, allowing for the use of gradient ascent to efficiently find the optimal $\bm \lambda$:
{
\begin{align}
    \bm \lambda 
    \leftarrow \bm \lambda + \alpha \frac{d }{d \bm \lambda} g(\bm \lambda) 
    = \bm \lambda + \alpha \bigl(  - \soft(\bm \lambda^\T \bm r_{1:n}) \cdot \bm r_{1:n}^\T  + \bm c \bigr) \label{eq_gd} 
\end{align}
}
where $\bm r_{1:n} \de [\bm r(x_1, y_1), \ldots, \bm r(x_n, y_n)]$. In practical implementations, we applied a change of variable $\bm \tau \de \log \bm \lambda$ to ensure the constraints $\bm \lambda \geq \bm 0$ are satisfied.
While users can select any value palette $\bm c \in \R^m$, not all choices are feasible. If the updates in (\ref{eq_gd}) do not converge, this indicates that the chosen $\bm c$ is infeasible. In such cases, MAP either prompts the user to adjust the value palette or automatically modifies $\bm c$ to ensure feasibility. For instance, if $\bm c$ is found to be infeasible, MAP could adjust it by:
    $ %\begin{align}
        \bm c' \de \bm c - \rho (\bm c - \bm c_0) 
    $ %\end{align}
using various $\rho$ values between 0 and 1, where $\bm c_0$ is the originally realized value level, until a feasible solution is achieved.

In Step~3, we implement $\q(y \mid x) \propto \p(y \mid x) e^{\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x, y)}$ through two practical approaches during experiments. The first, \textit{decoding-based alignment}, employs importance sampling for decoding from $\q$. Specifically, for any input $x$, we generate $k$ samples from $\p$, labeled $y_1, \ldots, y_k$, and select one via Multinomial sampling with weights proportional to $\exp(\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y_i))$ for each sample $i$. The second approach, \textit{finetuning-based alignment}, fine-tunes a model $\q_{w}$ with neural weights $w$ based on the reward function $(x,y) \mapsto \bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y)$. Compared with the first approach, the function space of $\q$ is restricted by the model architecture, and the learning of $w$ is computationally intensive. However, it can reduce the inference-stage complexity by only decoding one sentence for each prompt. 


\subsection{Simultaneous Versus Sequential or Greedy Alignment}

To align a model to a specified value palette $\bm c$, a natural baseline method is sequentially aligning individual values. That is, we sequentially update the model to align each value to an entry in $\bm c$ until all values have been addressed. This section elaborates on this method and compares it with the MAP procedure.

  

\textbf{Sequential alignment algorithm}.
Suppose we have aligned with one value palette $\bm c_{(\ell-1)}$ and then update with $\bm c_{(\ell)}$, $\ell=1,2,\ldots$
For notational convenience, let the initial $\bm c_{(0)}$ be the realized level of the original model $\p$. At the end of the $\ell$-th alignment, the aligned distribution can be expressed as $\q_{(\ell)}(y \mid x) \propto \p(y \mid x) \cdot \exp(\bm \lambda_{(\ell)}^\T \bm r(x,y))$ for some vector $\bm \lambda_{(\ell)}$. 
Given the value palettes $\{\bm c_{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1,2,\ldots}$, we recursively obtain $\bm \lambda_{(\ell)}$ from $\bm \lambda_{(\ell-1)}$. Like the problem (\ref{eq_dual}), the alignment objective at the beginning of the $\ell$-th alignment is:
{
    \begin{align}
        \max_{\bm \lambda \geq \bm 0} g_{(\ell)}(\bm \lambda) \de - \log \E_{x \sim \D, y \sim \q_{(\ell-1)}(\cdot \mid x)} e^{\bm \lambda^\T \bm r(x,y)} + \bm \lambda^\T \bm c_{(\ell)} .
        \label{eq_dual_iter}
    \end{align}
}
Like the problem (\ref{eq_dual_approx}), we can numerically solve (\ref{eq_dual_iter}) by addressing the problem:
{
    \begin{align}
        \max_{\bm \lambda \geq \bm 0} g_{(\ell)}(\bm \lambda) \de - \log \bigl(  \soft(\bm \lambda_{(\ell-1)}^\T \bm r_{1:n}) \cdot e^{\bm r_{1:n}^\T \bm \lambda} \bigr) + \bm \lambda^\T \bm  c_{(\ell)} .
        \label{eq_dual_iter_approx}
    \end{align}
}
\textbf{Connection between Sequential and Simultaneous Alignment}.
The problem (\ref{eq_dual_iter}) establishes a connection between the $(\ell-1)$-th and $\ell$-th alignment. Recall that $m$ denotes the number of values or the dimension of $\bm c$. In a canonical setting where one value is aligned at a time, define $\{\bm{c}_{(\ell)}\}_{\ell=1,2,\ldots}$ by:
$ %\begin{align}
    \bm{c}_{(\ell)} = [-\infty, \ldots, c_{\ell}, -\infty, \ldots], %\label{eq_60}
$ %\end{align}
for $\ell \leq m$, where the $\ell$-th element is the same as that of $\bm{c}$, and the others are trivially negative infinity.
For $\ell > m$, we can re-align the first value, and so on. Namely, we replace the above $c_{\ell}$ in $\bm{c}_{(\ell)}$ %(\ref{eq_60}) 
with $c_{\ell \mod m}$.
The following result shows this sequential, iterative alignment process converges to the joint alignment using MAP.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm_sequential_MAP}
    Let $\q_{(0)} \de \p, \, \q_{(1)}, \, \q_{(2)}, \ldots$ be the sequence of distributions obtained by sequentially aligning the original model
    according to the single-value MAP objective:
    \begin{align}
    &\min_{\q \in \mathcal{P}} \E_{x \sim \D, y \sim \qx}\Dkl{\qx}{\q_{(\ell-1)}(\cdot \mid x)}
    \, \st \E_{x \sim \D , y \sim \qx}r_{\ell'}(x,y) \geq c_{\ell'}, \nonumber
    \end{align}
    where $\ell' = (\ell \mod m)$. Assuming $\bm r(x,y)$ is not trivially a constant on the support set of $(x,y)$, this sequence weakly converges to $\q_{\bm \lambda(\bm c)}$, which is the solution to the MAP problem in (\ref{eq_5}).
\end{theorem}

The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm_sequential_MAP} demonstrates that the sequential alignment process fundamentally operates as a coordinate-wise gradient ascent algorithm. When we align subsets of values in each iteration, the process can equivalently function as a block-wise ascent. Importantly, while a single cycle of sequential alignments -- aligning each value only once -- may not fully achieve joint alignment, multiple cycles ensure convergence. This aspect is particularly significant in scenarios where constraints such as limited GPU memory restrict the simultaneous loading of numerous reward models. Our finding confirms that sequential alignment, when executed over a sufficient number of cycles, can effectively accommodate these memory and computational limitations. 
 



\section{Experimental study}





\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We generate prompts from two data sources: Anthropic harmless data~\citep{Anthropic_data}, which includes human requests delineated between the tags ``Human:'' and ``Assistant:'', and IMDB data~\citep{imdb_dataset} from which we retain movie reviews exceeding 30 characters in length. %Each selected text is then trimmed to a random length between 20 to 30 characters as prompt, and we aim to generate reviews.
For backbone models, we employ {OPT-1.3B}~\citep{zhang2022opt} and {Llama2-7B-chat}~\citep{touvron2023llama}, which have demonstrated robust language modeling capabilities in previous assessments.
We focus on several values for alignment: Humor, Positiveness, Harmlessness, Helpfulness, Diversity, Coherence, and Perplexity. The Humor reward is assessed using the logits from a humor detection model~\citep{humor_no_humor_2024}, while Positiveness uses a DistilBERT model trained on IMDB sentiment analysis~\citep{distilbert_imdb_2024}.  Harmlessness and Helpfulness are evaluated through two GPT-2 models equipped with a value head fine-tuned to predict these attributes~\citep{yang2024rewards}. Diversity is measured by the lexical variety within sentences, calculated through the proportion of unique $n$-grams ($n=2,3,4$) and their composite score~\citep{zhang2019bertscore}. Coherence is determined by the semantic similarity of sentences within a context, using a supervised SimCSE BERT-based model that captures sentence embeddings to assess textual coherence~\citep{gao2021simcse}.

\subsection{Comparison with Other Baseline Approaches} \label{subsec_exp_comparison}

To demonstrate the effectiveness of MAP in reliably aligning multiple values, we conduct an experiment showing that MAP can identify desirable outcomes with randomly sampled $\bm{\lambda}$. According to Theorem~\ref{thm_pareto}, we sample $\bm{\lambda}$ randomly from the range $(\bm{c_0}, \bm{B})$ and retain the feasible ones according to MAP's feasibility check. From these, we select $\bm{\lambda}$ vectors with a bounded $\ell_1$-norm less than 6. We compare this with a standard MORL approach where $\bm{\lambda}$ is randomly generated from $s \cdot \bm u$, where $s$ is uniformly sampled from $(0,6)$ and $\bm u$ is uniformly sampled from the probability simplex. For both approaches, we implement two alignment strategies: For the decoding-based approach (``MAP-D'', ``MORL-D''), we generate 16 candidates of $y$ for each prompt $x$ and select the final output using Multinomial sampling as described in Algorithm~\ref{algo_MAP} Step~3. For the finetuning-based approach (``MAP-F'', ``MORL-F''), we apply PPO and the calculated MAP reward function $\r$ to finetune the original model. We evaluate the outcomes using the `test' split of our task data, assessing both the expected reward and win rate against the original model based on the reward functions.

Additionally, we compare MAP with the DPO approach that uses various mixtures of preference data, and DPO-Soup, which applies linear interpolation of DPO-trained, value-specific models. The results, presented in Figure~\ref{fig_compare}, show that MAP generates alignment results that tend to fall into, or remain close to, the desirable regime, namely the upper-right quadrant relative to the original model. In contrast, the other approaches experience more severe trade-offs and rarely fall into the desirable regime. Table~\ref{tab_NE} quantifies this comparison by calculating the frequency of alignment results falling within the desirable regime, termed Navigation Efficiency (NE).

\section{Conclusion}



The proposed MAP offers a structured approach to aligning multiple human values, enabling precise adjustments to meet diverse user preferences. Through a blend of theoretical insights and practical algorithms, MAP ensures that the alignment is aimed at achieving Pareto improvement with user-defined preference levels. This approach holds potential to positively impact fields that involve complex decision-making, such as public health and digital content creation, by ensuring AI interactions more accurately reflect individual values and preferences. Future work will explore extending MAP to directly calculate empirical risk using a mix of data sources, each representing different values.
