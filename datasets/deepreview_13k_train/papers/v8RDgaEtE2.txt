\title{Regression Conformal Prediction under Bias}

\begin{abstract}
We will neither achieve perfect data collection, nor build flawless models in practice.
It is crucial to account for these imperfections when designing practical systems through uncertainty quantification (UQ). 
One such imperfection is bias, defined as the systematic deviation of a prediction from ground truth and is extremely prevalent in many practical applications.
Conformal prediction (CP) offers a powerful framework for generating calibrated prediction intervals with valid coverage, making it an effective tool for UQ. 
In this paper, we find that bias significantly inflates CP interval lengths computed using conventional symmetric adjustments and argue that the effect of bias can be mitigated by using asymmetric adjustments.
Under the split CP framework for regression, we present theoretical and empirical analyses for how prediction interval lengths with symmetric and asymmetric adjustments impact the ``tightness'' of the intervals. 
We prove 1) the upper bound of symmetrically adjusted lengths increases by $2|b|$ where $b$ is the bias, 2) asymmetrically adjusted interval lengths are not affected by bias, and 3) conditions when asymmetrically adjusted interval lengths are guaranteed to be smaller than those of symmetrically adjusted intervals.
We propose a simple algorithm to empirically determine a scalar value for bias, and show  evidence using synthetic data and real life scenarios, including when data is scarce and non-exchangeable.
Our results have important implications on how to design predictive systems to be more robust to bias.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




With the growing application of deep learning algorithms to high-impact applications such as healthcare, finance, and climate science, it is equally crucial to develop methods that can robustly quantify their uncertainties. This is particularly important since deep learning algorithms are known to yield confident yet incorrect prediction values~\citep{guo2017calibration,wang2023calibration,niculescu2005predicting}. Given a prediction by a learning algorithm on a fresh test example, uncertainty quantification methods typically aim to return a \emph{prediction set} with some guarantee that the true value lies within that set. In particular, a prediction set for a regression problem consists of an interval with lower and upper bounds~\citep{lei2018distribution, romano2019conformalized}. %Armed with these bounds, users can \GB{Matt fill in medical example maybe?}.

Conformal Prediction (CP) is a powerful family of uncertainty quantification methods that is \emph{distribution-agnostic}, i.e., makes no assumptions about the underlying data distribution, and generates prediction sets with guarantees of containing ground truth values with some probability~\citep{angelopoulos2021gentle,fontana2023conformal,shafer2008tutorial,papadopoulos2002inductive}. 
For example, split CP is based on collecting a separate (from training) \emph{calibration} dataset containing both ground truth and predicted values from the algorithm of interest. Then, given the algorithm's prediction on a test example, split CP computes a \emph{non-conformity} score quantifying how ``unusual'' new predictions will be with respect to the calibration dataset, and adjusts the prediction via the empirical quantile of the calibration scores to generate a prediction set.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{./figs/fig1_intuition.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Key Intuition.} Conformal prediction interval lengths computed using symmetric adjustments significantly increase with increasing bias, where bias is defined as the systematic deviation of a prediction from ground truth. On the other hand, those computed using asymmetric adjustments are not affected by bias. We aim to understand how bias impacts symmetrically and asymmetrically adjusted prediction interval lengths.}
    \label{fig:intuition}
\end{figure}

In practice, to minimize prediction uncertainty, we aim to obtain the tightest possible intervals that maintain valid coverage. Interval tightness (or, inversely, its length) depends on various factors: non-conformity scores, data distributions, and underlying models~\citep{kato2023review}. There has been much theoretical work analyzing aspects of interval length, including optimal efficiency~\citep{sesia2021conformal,vovk2016criteria,kiyani2024length,bai2022efficient}, expected set sizes~\citep{dhillon2024expected}, conditional and marginal set size differences~\citep{xu2023conformal}, and differences in set sizes between oracle and estimated prediction intervals~\citep{xu2023conformal,lei2018distribution}. Existing empirical work includes investigations into lengths under covariate shift~\citep{tibshirani2019conformal}, skewed distributions~\citep{vilfroy2024conformal}, and heteroskedasticity~\citep{lei2018distribution, romano2019conformalized}.

One important scenario, however, that has not been investigated is how CP fares for a learning algorithm that is \emph{biased}, i.e., produces predictions that systematically deviate from ground truth values. For example, we can define the bias $b$ of an algorithm as the mean difference between the expected values of its predictions $\hat Y$ with respect to ground truth $Y$ over the calibration set: 
\begin{equation}
    b(Y, \hat Y) =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\mathbb{E}[\hat Y_i]-Y_i)
    \label{eq:bias}
\end{equation}
Bias is a well-known issue plaguing machine learning models due to various factors such as skewed training distributions~\citep{nandy2022robust}, sensor drift~\citep{jing2013dimethyl,ying2007pvdf,piazzo2015drift}, concept drift~\citep{lu2018learning, lu2014concept,bayram2022concept}, attrition bias~\citep{lewin2018attrition}, and noisy labels~\citep{ding2022impact}. 
We find that large bias inflates the conventional symmetrically adjusted interval lengths (Fig.~\ref{fig:intuition}-left) because the intervals must be adjusted equally in both positive and negative directions.

In this paper, we argue that the effects of bias on CP interval lengths can be mitigated by computing intervals with asymmetric adjustments~\citep{linusson2014signed,romano2019conformalized} instead of conventional symmetric adjustments. Asymmetric adjustments allow lower and upper endpoints to be adjusted independently to account for directional bias, maintaining the guarantee that the resulting interval contains the ground truth with high probability (Fig.~\ref{fig:intuition}-right). While asymmetric adjustments have been theorized to yield longer interval lengths as a consequence of stronger guarantees~\citep{romano2019conformalized}, our work observes that with bias, that may not be the case. We expand the theoretical understanding of CP interval lengths for absolute residual ($L_1$) and quantile adjusted (i.e., Conformalized Quantile Regression or CQR~\citep{romano2019conformalized}) non-conformity scores by analyzing their behavior for symmetrically and asymmetrically adjusted interval lengths under prediction bias. Specifically, we prove the following:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The upper bound of symmetrically adjusted interval lengths increases by $2|b|$ (Thm.~\ref{thm:symL}), 
\item Asymmetrically adjusted interval lengths are not affected by bias (Thm.~\ref{thm:asymIndepBias}), and
\item Conditions when asymmetrically adjusted interval lengths are guaranteed to be smaller than those of symmetric adjustments (Cor.~\ref{cor:SymVSAsymLengths}).
\end{enumerate}
Our theoretical results are significant for several reasons. 
First, existing theory shows that while asymmetric adjustments give stronger coverage guarantees, they also result in slightly longer intervals~\citep{romano2019conformalized}. However, contrary to this theory, it has also been empirically observed that asymmetric adjustments yield tighter intervals than symmetric ones~\citep{linusson2014signed,wang2023conformal,cheung2024metric},
but the underlying reasons were unclear. Our work provides a theoretical answer to this phenomenon. Second, our theoretical results suggest that asymmetric adjustments are preferable in practice to symmetric ones when systematic biases are expected, e.g., with sensor drift.  However, if symmetric adjustments are desired (e.g., when error distributions are assumed to be symmetric), we also offer a method to achieve tighter symmetric intervals during calibration. 

We validate that our theoretical analyses align with synthetic and real prediction tasks.
Our synthetic experiments validate our theoretical analyses in the ideal setting when $n$ is large for no skew and skewed distributions.
Our real tasks validate our theoretical analyses in two settings: when $n$ is extremely low and time series.
Our real tasks deal with bias arising in different contexts: (1) when a medical imaging reconstruction algorithm systematically under- or over-estimates volumes of an anatomical region (computed tomography (CT) reconstruction), and (2) temporal ``drift'' of values over time (weather forecasting).
The contributions of this study give machine learning practitioners fundamental and practical insight into using CP under the common scenario of biased predictions. 


\section{Background: Split Conformal Prediction (CP)}

We focus on a ``split'' CP setup~\citep{papadopoulos2002inductive,lei2018distribution} in this work, but the same theoretical analysis can be applied to other CP forms and extensions~\cite{fontana2023conformal,barber2021predictive}.
In split CP, we assume a calibration dataset $D_C=\{(\hat Y_1, Y_1),...,(\hat Y_n, Y_n)\}$ and test point $\hat Y_{n+1}$, where $\hat Y_i$ and $Y_i$ represent the $i$-th prediction and ground truth values. The calibration data is separate from a training dataset used to train the ML algorithm of interest. The calibration dataset and test point are assumed to be exchangeable. The goal of CP is to construct a prediction interval $C(\hat Y_{n+1})=[L(\hat Y_{n+1}), U(\hat Y_{n+1})]$ for $\hat Y_{n+1}$, where $L(\hat Y_{n+1}),U(\hat Y_{n+1})\in \mathbb{R}$ are lower and upper bounds, such that $\mathbb{P}[Y_{n+1} \in C(\hat Y_{n+1})]\geq 1-\alpha$, for some user-specified mis-coverage rate $\alpha\in (0,1)$.
To compute symmetric intervals, we perform the following steps. 
First, for each data point in the calibration set $D_C$, we compute non-conformity scores $S=\{s_1,...,s_n\}$. 
Next, we compute the $(1-\alpha)$-th empirical quantile of the non-conformity scores $q=Q_{1-\hat \alpha}(S)$, where $\hat \alpha=\frac{\lfloor \alpha (n+1)\rfloor}{n+1}$ denotes the finite-sample adjusted mis-coverage rate.
Finally, we adjust the predictions of the test data using $q$ to achieve valid prediction sets. 
This algorithm provides marginal coverage: on average, the prediction sets contain ground truth $(1-\alpha)\%$ of the time. More rigorously, based on key CP results:
\begin{lemma}
    \label{lem:conformal}
    Let $(\hat Y_i, Y_i) \in \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}, i=1,...,n+1$ be exchangeable random variables. 
    Assume that a predictor $f$ has been trained on a proper training set independent of and exchangeable with these $n+1$ points.
    Consider a calibration set ${(\hat Y_i, Y_i)}_{i=1}^n$ and a fresh test point $\hat Y_{n+1}$.
    Let $s_i$ be a non-conformity score computed using the predictor $f$ for $i=1,...,n+1$.
    Let $q=Q_{1-\hat \alpha}(\{s_i\}_{i=1}^n)$ be the $\lceil(1-\alpha)(n+1)\rceil$-th smallest value of $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^n$ and $C(\hat Y_{n+1})=\{y \in \mathbb{R}: s_{n+1} \leq q\}$ be the prediction set for the test point $\hat Y_{n+1}$.
    Then, for any $\alpha \in (0,1)$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\mathbb{P}[Y_{n+1} \in C(\hat Y_{n+1})] \geq 1-\alpha$ and
        \item $\mathbb{P}[Y_{n+1} \in C(\hat{Y}_{n+1})] \leq 1-\alpha + \frac{1}{n+1}$ if random variables $Y_1,...,Y_{n+1}$ are almost surely distinct
    \end{enumerate}
    
    \noindent Proof: See variations in \cite{vovk2005algorithmic, lei2018distribution,tibshirani2019conformal,oliveira2024split}
\end{lemma}
Many non-conformity scores exist~\citep{kato2023review}, including absolute residuals ($L_1$)~\citep{papadopoulos2002inductive} and quantile-based (Conformalized Quantile Regression or CQR ~\citep{romano2019conformalized}) scores.

This can be extend to asymmetric adjustments~\citep{linusson2014signed,romano2019conformalized,cordier2023flexible} by computing $(1-\alpha_{lo})$-th and $(1-\alpha_{hi})$-th empirical quantiles of the conformity scores, where $\alpha_{lo}$ and $\alpha_{hi}$ are lower and upper mis-coverage rates.
In the asymmetric case, the empirical quantiles for the lower and higher mis-coverage rates $\alpha_{lo}$ and $\alpha_{hi}$ are given by $\hat \alpha_{lo}=\frac{\lfloor \alpha_{lo} (n+1)\rfloor}{n+1}$ and $\hat \alpha_{hi}=\frac{\lfloor \alpha_{hi}(n+1)\rfloor}{n+1}$.
The proof of validity relies on applying Lem. \ref{lem:conformal} twice ~\citep{linusson2014signed,romano2019conformalized}.
It is easy to see that when $\alpha_{lo}+\alpha_{hi}=\alpha$ the asymmetric case yields empirically larger coverage based on the finite sample adjustment.
The reason is due to the ``rounding effect'' of the ceiling function, which tends to push the empirical quantiles toward more extreme values (larger or smaller), especially when $n$ is small. Since the asymmetric case deals with two separate quantiles, this effect is compounded, leading to a prediction set that empirically offers larger coverage. However, as we will see, symmetrically adjusted interval lengths may not always be shorter than asymmetric ones in the presence of bias. 



\section{Theoretical Analysis}

\label{sec:theory}

We assume biased predictions $\hat Y_i^b = \hat Y_i^0 + b$ where $b\in \mathbb{R}$ is a constant (positive or negative) applied between all unbiased predicted values ($\hat Y^0$) and ground truth values ($Y$) from the calibration set.
For example, for prediction and ground truth distributions that are symmetric and centered around their means, we can use Eq.~\ref{eq:bias}. However, when the distributions are skewed, the mean may no longer be a good measure of central tendency~\citep{rousseeuw2011robust,huber2011robust}. 
In Sec.~\ref{subsec:biasEst}, we will use results from our theoretical analyses to estimate bias more accurately in these cases.

We consider symmetric non-conformity scores with canonical expression:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:canonical}
    s_i^b = \max(f_{lo}(\hat Y_i^b)-Y_i, Y_i-f_{hi}(\hat Y_i^b)),
\end{equation}
where $f_{lo}$ and $f_{hi}$ are the lower adjustment and upper adjustment functions that have linear properties: $f_{lo}(\hat Y_i^b)=f_{lo}(\hat Y_i^0)+b$ and $f_{hi}(\hat Y_i^b)=f_{hi}(\hat Y_i^0)+b$.
Eq.~\ref{eq:canonical} covers the conventional $L_1$ and CQR non-conformity scores. 
For the $L_1$ non-conformity score given by $s_i^b=|Y_i-\hat Y_i^b|$, $\hat Y_i^b$ represents a point estimate, and the score can be rewritten as $s_i^b=\max(\hat Y_i^b-Y_i, Y_i-\hat Y_i^b)$.
For the CQR non-conformity score given by $s_i^b=\max(Q_{\alpha_{lo}}(\hat Y_i^b)-Y_i, Y_i-Q_{1-\alpha_{hi}}(\hat Y_i^b))$, $\hat Y_i^b$ represents a set of samples $\hat Y_i^b=\{\hat Y_{ij}^b\}_{j=1}^{n_s}$.
The adjustment is given by $q^b=Q_{1-\hat \alpha}(\{s_i^b\}_{i=1}^n)$, the prediction interval is given by $C(\hat Y^b_{n+1})=[f_{lo}(\hat Y^b_{n+1})-q^b, f_{hi}(\hat Y^b_{n+1})+q^b]$, and the interval length is given by $L_{sym}(\hat Y^b_{n+1})=f_{hi}(\hat Y^b_{n+1})-f_{lo}(\hat Y^b_{n+1})+2q^b$.
This setup does not cover locally adaptive non-conformity scores~\citep{papadopoulos2008normalized,papadopoulos2011regression,lei2018distribution} and variations of CQR such as CQR-r and CQR-m non-conformity scores ~\citep{sesia2020comparison}. 
Using Eq. \ref{eq:canonical}, we first derive an upper bound for symmetrically adjusted interval lengths under bias (Thm. \ref{thm:symL}):
\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:symL}
   Given biased predictions for a fresh test point $\hat Y_{n+1}^b = \hat Y_{n+1}^0+b$, the upper bound on prediction interval lengths of non-conformity scores described in Eq. \ref{eq:canonical} is:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:symLengthNoBias}
        L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b) \leq L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)+2|b|,
    \end{equation}
    where $L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)$ and $L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)$ are the interval lengths computed using symmetric adjustments for predictions with and without bias.
    
    \noindent Proof: See App. \ref{app:canonicalSymLength}
\end{theorem}
We find the upper bounds of symmetrically adjusted interval lengths increase linearly with the magnitude of bias.
Next, we show 1) that asymmetric adjustments are not affected by bias and 2) conditions when using asymmetric adjustments produce shorter lengths than symmetric adjustments. To accomplish this, we introduce a similar canonical expression for asymmetric non-conformity scores:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:canonicalAsymNonConformity}
    (s_{i,lo}^b, s_{i,hi}^b) = (f_{lo}(\hat Y_i^b)-Y_i, Y_i-f_{hi}(\hat Y_i^b)),
\end{equation}
where $s_{i,lo}^b$ and $s_{i,hi}^b$ represent lower and upper non-conformity score adjustments when predictions are biased with $b$. The lower and upper asymmetric adjustments are computed by taking the $(1-\alpha_{lo})$-th and $(1-\alpha_{hi})$-th empirical quantile of the sets of non-conformity scores 
$q_{lo}^b=Q_{1-\hat \alpha_{lo}}(\{s_{i,lo}^b\}_{i=1}^n)$ and $q_{hi}^b=Q_{1-\hat \alpha_{hi}}(\{s_{i,hi}^b\}_{i=1}^n$). 
Thus, the prediction interval is $C(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)=[f_{lo}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)-q_{lo}^b, f_{hi}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)+q_{hi}^b]$.

Using this setup, we prove the following relationship for the length of a CP prediction interval using asymmetric non-conformity scores, under bias $b$:
\begin{theorem}
    \label{thm:asymIndepBias}
    Given biased predictions for a fresh test point $\hat Y_{n+1}^b = \hat Y_{n+1}^0+b$,
    the lengths for $L_1$ and CQR non-conformity scores computed using asymmetric adjustments are bias-independent:
    \begin{equation}
        L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)=L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)
    \end{equation}
    where $L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)$ and $L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)$ are the interval lengths computed using asymmetric adjustments for predictions with and without bias.
    
    \noindent Proof: See App. \ref{app:asymIndepBias}
\end{theorem}

\begin{algorithm}[t!]
\caption{Estimating bias by minimizing symmetrically-adjusted interval lengths; Example using gradient descent.}
\label{alg:cap}
\begin{algorithmic}
    \Require $\gamma$: learning rate, $\{ (\hat Y_i^b,Y_i )\}_{i=1}^n$: calibration dataset, $\tau$: tolerance, $L_{sym}(\hat Y_i^b)$: function that computes symmetrically adjusted interval lengths.
    \newline
    \State Initialize losses $l_{prev}$ and $l$ s.t. $l_{prev} \geq l$
    \State Initialize $b_{eff}$. E.g., $b_{eff} \gets \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\mathbb{E}[\hat Y_i^b]- Y_i)$
    \While{$|l_{prev}-l| \geq \tau$}
        \State $l_{prev} \gets l$
        \State $l \gets max(\{L_{sym}(\hat Y_i^b-b_{eff})\}_{i=1}^n)$
        \State $b_{eff} \gets b_{eff}-\gamma \nabla(l)$
    \EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We find that asymmetric adjustments are not affected at all by a constant bias $b$, which is a desirable property. However, recall that when predictions are unbiased, asymmetrically adjusted intervals tend to be longer than symmetric ones~\citep{romano2019conformalized}. This raises the question: at what level of bias does this behavior reverse? We derive conditions under which, in the presence of bias, asymmetrically adjusted intervals become shorter than symmetric ones:
\begin{corollary}
    \label{cor:SymVSAsymLengths}
    For $L_1$ and CQR non-conformity scores, asymmetric adjustments produce smaller interval lengths than symmetric adjustments under the following condition:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:SymVSAsymLengthCondPos}
        2|b| \geq L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)-L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0).
    \end{equation}
    where $b$ is the bias, $L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)$ and $L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)$ are lengths computed using symmetric and asymmetric adjustments for predictions without bias.

    \noindent Proof: The result is derived by using Thm. \ref{thm:asymIndepBias}, setting $L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b) \leq L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)$, substituting Eq. \ref{eq:symLengthNoBias}, and rearranging the inequality.
\end{corollary}

We find that when the difference in lengths for predictions without bias is greater than 2 times the magnitude of bias, the asymmetrically adjusted interval lengths will be guaranteed to be shorter than symmetric ones.

Our theoretical analyses (Thm.~\ref{thm:symL}, Thm.~\ref{thm:asymIndepBias} and Cor.~\ref{cor:SymVSAsymLengths}) provide important insights into how bias affects length and the conditions which asymmetric adjustments yield shorter lengths than and symmetric adjustments. In practice, when $\alpha_{lo}+\alpha_{hi}=\alpha$ and $n$ is large, the interval lengths under no bias are approximately equal $L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0) \approx L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^0)$, and the lengths for predictions with bias are shorter for asymmetric compared to symmetric adjustments $L_{asym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b) \leq L_{sym}(\hat Y_{n+1}^b)$ when $|b|>0$.

\subsection{Estimating bias}
\label{subsec:biasEst}
One unanswered question is how to empirically determine bias $b$ given data. To do so, we leverage Thm.~\ref{thm:symL} and adjust the predictions by a scalar value $b_{eff}$ to minimize the maximum symmetrically adjusted interval lengths:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:opt}
    b_{eff}=\argmin_C \bigg[\max(\{L_{sym}(\hat Y_i^b-C)\}_{i=1}^n)\bigg]
\end{equation}
$L_{sym}(\hat Y_i^b-b_{eff})$ achieves the minimum length for symmetric adjustments (when $b=0$).
$b_{eff}$ can be thought of as the ``debiasing'' constant for biased predictions $\hat Y^b$. We prove that the objective function in Eq.~\ref{eq:opt} reduces to minimizing a vertically and horizontally translated absolute value function (App.~\ref{app:conv}).
Therefore, the objective function is convex, and Alg.~\ref{alg:cap} converges using gradient descent and its variants.

We next evaluate Thm.~\ref{thm:symL} and \ref{thm:asymIndepBias} and Cor.~\ref{cor:SymVSAsymLengths} for $L_1$ and CQR non-conformity scores with synthetic and real-life experiments. 
For synthetic experiments, we assume normally distributed ground truth data, and simulate predictions by adding different types of noise. For real-life experiments, we consider two scenarios: when data is scarce (CT reconstructions for downstream radiotherapy planning using CQR) and when data is temporally varying (time series weather forecasting using $L_1$). The data distributions can be found in App.~\ref{app:dataDists}.

\subsection{Synthetic Data}
We first demonstrate the validity of the theoretical analysis Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} for CQR using Gaussian $N$ and Weibull $W$ distributions to simulate estimate, ground truth, and noise distributions. We used $N(10,5)$ to simulate a ground truth distribution. We added noise characterized by $W(1,0,5)$, $N(0,2)$, and $-W(1,-2,5)$ to the ground truth samples to simulate left-, no-, and right-skewing predictions.
We used 1000 calibration data points, 1000 test data points, and 1000 samples per data point to estimate the quantiles. We set $\alpha=0.1$ for symmetric adjustments, and $\alpha_{lo}=\alpha_{hi}=0.05$ for asymmetric adjustments. 
After determining $b_{eff}$ using Alg.~\ref{alg:cap}, we added a constant bias term from $-2$ to $2$ to the debiased predictions to examine the effect of biased predictions on lengths (Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic}).

Results in Fig.~\ref{fig:synthetic} confirm our theoretical analyses. We find that symmetrically adjusted interval lengths (red) are always upper bounded by the sum of length at $b=0$ and $2|b|$ (dashed black) holds true (Thm \ref{thm:symL}). 
We find that asymmetrically adjusted interval lengths (green) do not change under bias (Thm. \ref{thm:asymIndepBias}) and that they are always smaller than symmetrically adjusted lengths (red) when Cor. \ref{cor:SymVSAsymLengths} is true (dashed grey). 

\subsection{Real Data}
Next, we validate our theoretical analyses in two different real data scenarios: where upstream image reconstruction tasks may not fully capture spatial dependencies in downstream metrics (sparse-view computed tomography (CT) reconstruction), and where predictions ``drift'' from the ground truth over time (time series weather forecasting).
Through these two examples, we aim to show the validity and usefulness of our theoretical analysis from different perspectives.

\subsubsection{Limited Data}

In scenarios with limited imaging capabilities, such as low-resource clinics~\citep{aggarwal2023radiation,court2023addressing,kisling2018radiation}, reconstruction algorithms work with observations that do not contain complete information. For example, sparse cone-beam CT algorithms use limited ($<100$ instead of the standard $100$s) 2D X-ray observations to generate 3D CT scans~\citep{sun2023ct,ying2019x2ct,shen2019patient}. The observed information is insufficient to recover the true image with complete certainty, leading to potential biases such as systematically over- or under-estimating organ volumes. %One reason for this is that image reconstruction algorithms often perform pixel/voxel-wise optimization, while downstream tasks may take spatial correlations into account. For example, segmented organ volumes from reconstructed CT volumes could be systematically overestimated (Fig.~\ref{fig:seg}).

We simulate a medical imaging pipeline, where a patient is imaged using sparse-CT, an image reconstruction algorithm is applied to the projections, and the resulting volume is used for downstream radiotherapy planning (RT).
We use Neural Attenuation Fields (NAF)~\citep{zha2022naf}, a self-supervised image reconstruction algorithm.
We synthetically injected noise to the projections, reconstructed the volumes using different initializations of the reconstruction algorithm, and generated plans using the Radiation Planning Assistant (RPA, FDA 510(k) cleared)
\footnote{RPA is a web-based tool that combines organ segmentation algorithms and physics simulations for RT planning.}.
More details about our experimental setup can be found in App.~\ref{app:RTdetails}.

To validate our theoretical analysis in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} holds true even for extremely low $n$, we use 19 patients for calibration and 1 patient for testing. 
We generate 10 reconstructions per patient by perturbing acquisition angles, injecting noise into the projections, and using random initializations of NAF.
We perform leave-one-out cross-validation to examine each patient's interval lengths when calibrated with the rest of the patients. 
We use $\alpha=0.15$ for symmetric adjustments and $\alpha_{lo}=\alpha_{hi}=0.075$ for asymmetric adjustments, corresponding to $\hat \alpha=0.0567$ for the symmetric case and $\hat \alpha_{lo}=\hat \alpha_{hi}=0$ for the asymmetric case.
In the asymmetric case, this corresponds to an extreme case of taking the maximum and minimum non-conformity scores.

\subsubsection{Times series}

Weather forecasting is important for many aspects of daily life, from public safety to agriculture to disaster preparedness and response. We use the Yandex Weather Prediction dataset and the average pre-trained CatBoost model from ~\cite{angelopoulos2021gentle} to predict temperature changes. The temporal dependencies between points violate the exchangeability assumption. Therefore, we use weighted conformal prediction where we use a different adjustment 
for each new data point~\citep{tibshirani2019conformal}.
We use the $L_1$ non-conformity score and weight the data points in the window of size $K=1000$ equally. 
This setup effectively reduces to split CP applied each at time window and the theoretical analyses in Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} apply. 
The symmetric non-conformity score for time $t$ is given by $s^b_t=|\hat Y^b_t-Y^0_t|$.
The asymmetric non-conformity scores for time $t$ are given by
$s^b_{t,lo}=\hat Y^b_t-Y^0_t$ and $s^b_{t,hi}=Y^0_t-\hat Y^b_t$.
We set $\alpha=0.1$ and inject an increasing negative bias to the unbiased predicted values $\hat Y^b_t=\hat Y^0_t-(2\times 10^{-4})t$.
We plot temperature over time for predictions $\hat Y^b$ and ground truth $Y$ (Fig. \ref{fig:weather}A), 
coverage over time for weighted (symmetric and asymmetric adjustments) and naive (unweighted, symmetric adjustments) CP (Fig. \ref{fig:weather}B),
symmetrically (red) and asymmetrically (blue) adjusted intervals and where they overlap (purple) (Fig. \ref{fig:weather}C), and bias versus lengths for symmetric adjustments (red), lengths for asymmetric adjustments (blue), and upper bound lengths for symmetric adjustments from Thm. \ref{thm:symL} (purple).

We observe that symmetric and asymmetric adjustments produce valid coverage while naive approaches do not, confirming prior work \citep{angelopoulos2021gentle,barber2023conformal} (Fig. \ref{fig:weather}B).
We observe that asymmetric adjustments are independent of bias (Fig. \ref{fig:weather}D) yet still produce valid prediction intervals.
We observe that symmetrically adjusted interval lengths increase linearly with increasing bias, bounded by Thm.~\ref{thm:symL} (Fig. \ref{fig:weather}D).
Our results suggest that even if predictions drift ``far away'' from the ground truth values, asymmetrically adjusted intervals are still able to maintain the same tightness and validity of intervals as if the drift had never happened.


\section{Discussion and Conclusion}


We will never collect perfect data in practice, or build perfect predictive models that are robust over time. Therefore, it is integral to account for these imperfections when designing practical systems.
In this work, we argue that the effects of bias on CP prediction interval lengths can be mitigated by computing asymmetric adjustments as opposed to the conventional symmetric adjustments.
We prove the following for $L_1$ and CQR non-conformity scores.
In Thm. \ref{thm:symL} we showed that the upper bound of the prediction interval lengths with symmetric adjustments increases by $2|b|$. 
In Thm. \ref{thm:asymIndepBias}, we showed that prediction interval lengths with asymmetric adjustments are not affected by bias.
In Cor. \ref{cor:SymVSAsymLengths}, we showed the conditions when prediction interval lengths with asymmetric adjustments are guaranteed to be smaller than those of symmetric intervals.
We proposed an algorithm to empirically determine the bias and showed empirical evidence using synthetic and real-life data. Our results have have important implications on accounting for algorithmic bias, while also suggesting further areas of investigation:

\textbf{Stability of bias estimation for low $n$.}
Our work suggests that estimating bias based on symmetrically adjusted intervals is a straightforward, practical, and computationally efficient way to account for systematic errors in predictions. 
However, it is crucial to consider the impact of sample size on the reliability of these estimates when the calibration set is small~\citep{tversky1971belief}.
Small calibration sets can lead to noisy estimates of bias \citep{springate2012effect}. 
The challenges associated with limited data include under or overestimating the true bias, greater bias estimation variability, and being more susceptible to skewed, outliers, and random fluctuations.
We recommend considering the uncertainty in bias estimates when applying corrections and increasing sample size to improve the reliability of bias estimates where possible.

\textbf{More complex scores.} 
Our analysis reveals that while simple non-conformity scores, as presented in Eq.~\ref{eq:canonical} and \ref{eq:canonicalAsymNonConformity}, are tractable for theoretical guarantees, more complex non-conformity scores such as locally adaptive scores~\cite{papadopoulos2008normalized,papadopoulos2011regression,lei2018distribution} and CQR variants~\cite{sesia2020comparison} present challenges.
These challenges arise due to modeling bias as a globally applied additive constant. 
This simplification, although useful for theoretical and empirical analysis, may overlook that biases could exhibit more intricate patterns, possibly varying across the input space or depending on specific features.
For example, in time series data, we use a uniform weighting scheme that effectively reduces to split CP over each time window. 
Our results suggest that incorporating these techniques in more complex settings could reveal interesting behaviors and could help design more bias-robust scores.


\textbf{Covariate shift and Bias.}
Our work suggests correcting for bias using a globally applied constant to the predictions can significantly reduce the interval lengths.
However, our approach does not account for situations where the predicted distribution changes between calibration and test datasets.
Prior work on CP under covariate shift~\citep{tibshirani2019conformal} weighted predictions by a probability proportional to their likelihood ratio.
However, when calibration predictions are ``far away'' from the expected test predictions, the likelihood ratio may be very small or zero.
Thus, it is impossible to perform a covariate shift without significant overlap between the calibration and expected test predictions when the expected bias is large.
Our work suggests exploring both bias correction and covariate shift together could lead to tighter and more reliable prediction intervals for these situations.
