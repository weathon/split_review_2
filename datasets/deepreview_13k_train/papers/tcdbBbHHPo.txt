\title{Direct Alignment of Language Models via Quality-Aware Self-Refinement}

\begin{abstract}
Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has been commonly used to align the behaviors of Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences. Recently, a popular alternative is Direct Policy Optimization (DPO), which replaces an LLM-based reward model with the policy itself, thus obviating the need for extra memory and training time to learn the reward model. However, DPO does not consider the relative qualities of the positive and negative responses, and can lead to sub-optimal training outcomes. To alleviate this problem, we investigate the use of intrinsic knowledge within the on-the-fly fine-tuning LLM to obtain relative qualities and help to refine the loss function. Specifically, we leverage the knowledge of the LLM to design a refinement function to estimate the quality of both the positive and negative responses. We show that the constructed refinement function can help self-refine the loss function under mild assumptions. The refinement function is integrated into DPO and its variant Identity Policy Optimization (IPO). Experiments across various evaluators indicate that they can improve the performance of the fine-tuned models over DPO and IPO.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated significant capabilities across various natural language processing tasks \citep{radford2019language,zhang2022opt, touvron2023llama, achiam2023gpt}. Ensuring that these LLMs produce the desired responses and behaviors that are aligned with human preferences is crucial for safe and controllable AI systems \citep{ouyang2022training}. To achieve this, a popular method is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) \citep{christiano2017deep, achiam2023gpt, bai2022training}, which first trains a reward model using human-labeled response pairs, and then uses this to adjust the policy parameters of the LLM \citep{bai2022training, ouyang2022training}. However, the reward model is often constructed by another LLM, which requires further training and storage \citep{amini2024direct}.

To reduce the storage and training time of the reward model, a variety of methods have been proposed recently \citep{dong2023raft, yuan2023rrhf, amini2024direct}. In particular, a prominent solution is the Direct Policy Optimization (DPO) \citep{amini2024direct}, which replaces the reward model with the policy itself, thus obviating the need for an explicit reward model. Recently, numerous variants of DPO have also been developed \citep{wang2023beyond, azar2024general, amini2024direct, ethayarajh2024kto, song2024icdpo}.

The objective of DPO is to consistently increase the likelihood of human-preferred responses while reducing the likelihood of the undesired ones. However, this strategy does not consider the relative qualities of the positive and negative responses, and can lead to suboptimal training outcomes, particularly when the preferred responses are not substantially superior, or when the undesired responses are not adequately inferior \citep{amini2024direct, tunstall2023zephyr, cui2023ultrafeedback}. 

To alleviate this issue, Amini et al. \citep{amini2024direct} and Zhou et al. \citep{zhou2023beyond} propose the use of a score function to self-refine the objective. However, this approach requires the availability of an ideal reward or score function, which may not be always feasible. Similarly, Cui et al. \citep{cui2023ultrafeedback} and Tunstall et al. \cite{tunstall2023zephyr} employ GPT-4 \citep{achiam2023gpt} to select high-quality response pairs by scoring them. This method requires a strong LLM to effectively filter the dataset, which again may not always be practical. These considerations raise the question: \emph{Can we achieve this by using the inherent knowledge within the policy itself?}

Recently, self-alignment has attracted increasing attention due to its ability to leverage the inherent knowledge of LLMs to enhance alignment capabilities, obviating the necessity for additional human-annotated data \citep{munos2023nash, alami2024investigating, lee2024aligning, yuan2024self}. Inspired by this, we propose to utilize on-the-fly fine-tuning of an LLM's knowledge to help evaluate the quality of positive and negative responses. The underlying premise is that even relatively weak LLMs possess some ability to assess the quality of responses \citep{ji2023ai, ji2024aligner}. Consequently, our objective is to exploit this capability to more effectively evaluate response quality, thereby enhancing the efficiency and accuracy of the fine-tuned model.

In this paper, we investigate the use of intrinsic knowledge within the LLM to self-refine the loss function. In summary, the contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item We leverage the knowledge of the LLM to design a refinement function, which estimates the quality of positive and negative responses.
\item We demonstrate that the constructed refinement function can help self-refine the loss function under mild assumptions.
\item By utilizing the refinement function, we propose two novel approaches based on DPO and its variant Identity Policy Optimization (IPO)
\citep{azar2024general}.
\end{itemize}
Experimental results across various evaluators indicate that the proposed self-refined methods improve the performance of the fine-tuned models compared to their counterparts.



\section{Preliminaries}

\subsection{Classical RLHF with Bradley-Terry Reward Model}
Given a pre-trained large language model (LLM) $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ as initialization, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) \citep{christiano2017deep, achiam2023gpt, bai2022training} aims to learn an LLM $\pi$ that aligns with human values and preferences. Specifically, let $x$ be the query, $y$ be the output of $\pi$, and $r$ be a reward function that evaluates the performance of $y$ given $x$, RLHF tries to maximize $r$ while ensuring that the trained LLM $\pi$ does not deviate significantly from the pre-trained model $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This can be formulated as the following optimization problem \citep{ouyang2022training, nika2024reward, achiam2023gpt}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rl_obj}
\max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi(\cdot | x)}r(y | x) - \beta \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(y | x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y | x)],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(y | x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y | x)]$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between $\pi$ and $\pi_{\text{ref}}$, and $\beta$ is a constant.

Since $r$ is unknown, the user needs to provide a set of preferences $D \equiv \{(x_i, y^+_i, y^-_i)\}^N_{i=1}$, where $y^+_i$ (resp. $y^-_i$) is the positive (resp. negative) response for query $x_i$. A suitable model, typically another LLM (parameterized by $\omega$) \citep{lambert2022illustrating,achiam2023gpt}, then learns the reward function by maximizing the probability $p(y_i^+ \succ y_i^-|x)$ that $y_i^+$ is preferred over $y_i^-$ (denoted $y_i^+ \succ y_i^-$) \citep{ouyang2022training,amini2024direct,azar2024general}. Typically, this probability is defined by the Bradley-Terry preference model \citep{bradley1952rank, christiano2017deep} as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:bt}
p(y_i^+ \succ y_i^- | x_i) \equiv \sigma(r(y_i^+ | x_i) - r(y_i^- | x_i)),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function. The optimal $r$ can then be obtained by maximizing the log likelihood 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:train_reward}
\max_r \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim \mathcal{D}} \log 
[\sigma(r(y_i^+ | x_i) - r(y_i^- | x_i))].
\end{equation}
With the obtained $r$, we can then find $\pi$ by optimizing (\ref{eq:rl_obj}).

\subsection{Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)}
In RLHF, the reward model is represented by a LLM \citep{ouyang2022training}. This can be time- and memory-expensive. It is observed that the optimal $\pi$ in (\ref{eq:rl_obj}) indeed has the closed form \citep{rafailov2023direct}:
$\pi(y |x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y | x) \exp\left(\frac{r(y | x)}{\beta}\right),$ and so
\begin{equation} \label{eq:dpo}
r(y | x) = \beta \log \frac{\pi(y | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y | x)}+c(x),
\end{equation}
where $c(x) \equiv \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp \left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(y|x)\right)$, and $\mathcal{Y}$ is the set of responses. By plugging this into (\ref{eq:train_reward}), the optimal policy can be found directly by maximizing:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:obj_dro}
\max\nolimits_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim \mathcal{D}}\log\sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi(y^+ | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ | x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y^- | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- | x)} \right). 
\end{equation}

\subsection{Identity Policy Optimisation (IPO)}
DPO aims to maximize (\ref{eq:rl_obj}). However, our goal is to maximize the preference rather than the reward \citep{wirth2017survey}. Consequently, a better option is to maximize the preference probabilities $p(y \succ y' | x)$. To this end, IPO \citep{azar2024general} optimizes the following objective:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ipo_obj}
\max_{\pi} 
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y^+ \sim \pi(\cdot | x), y^- \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot
| x)} p(y^+ \succ y^- | x) -\beta
\mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(y^+ | x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- | x)].
\end{equation}

Similar to DPO, following \citep{azar2024general},  the optimal $\pi$ also has a closed-form solution:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:closed_form_ipo}
\pi(y^+ | x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- | x) \exp\left(\mathbb{E}_{y^+ \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot | x)}p(y^+ \succ y^- | x) /\beta \right).
\end{equation}

The loss for IPO is then defined as:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:ipo-loss}
\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim \mathcal{D}}\left[ \left(\log  \frac{\pi(y^+ | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ | x)} - \log \frac{\pi(y^- | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- | x)} - \frac{1}{2\beta}\right)^2\right].
\end{equation}

\subsection{Self-Alignment} 
The proposed method is also related to self-alignment~\citep{lee2024aligning,yuan2024self,madaan2024self}, which uses the LLM's own knowledge to improve the alignment. For example, self-judgement \citep{lee2024aligning,yuan2024self,sun2024principle,madaan2024self,zhao2023survey} uses the LLM as a judge to evaluate the generated answer. Self-improving \citep{munos2023nash,alami2024investigating,cheng2024self,chen2024self,calandriello2024human} improves LLM by playing game with another LLM opponent. Our work uses the self-alignment ability of LLM to construct a refinement function to adjust the quality of responses.



\section{Proposed Method}

\subsection{Limitation of Maximizing Bradley-Terry Preference} \label{sec:limitation}
Let $r^*$ and $r$ be the true and learned reward function respectively. For any tuple $(x, y^+, y^-) \in D$, the true reward of the positive response $y^+$ should exceed that of the negative response $y^-$, i.e., $y^+ \succ y^- \Leftrightarrow r^*(y^+|x) > r^*(y^-|x)$. Consider two tuples $(x_i, y_i^+, y_i^-)$ and $(x_j, y_j^+, y_j^-)$. When $r^*(y_i^+|x_i) - r^*(y_i^-|x_i)>r^*(y_j^+|x_j) - r^*(y_j^-|x_j)$, we prefer $p(y_i^+ \succ y_i^- | x_i) > p(y_j^+ \succ y_j^- | x_j)$, and so $\sigma(r(y_i^+|x_i) - r(y_i^-|x_i))> \sigma(r(y_j^+|x_j) - r(y_j^-|x_j))$ from (\ref{eq:bt}). In other words, the more informative tuple $(x_i, y_i^+, y_i^-)$ should be more important. However, RLHF simply maximizes (\ref{eq:train_reward}), which considers all tuples in the dataset $D$ equally.

\subsection{Refining the Reward Difference between Positive and Negative Responses}
\subsubsection{Intuition}
\label{sec:intuition}
To alleviate this problem, we propose adding a {\em refinement} function $\Delta( y^-, y^+;x):\mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ (where $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ are the sets of queries and responses, respectively) to adjust $r(y^+|x) - r(y^-|x)$, so that problem (\ref{eq:train_reward}) is modified to:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bt_obj2}
\max_\pi  \mathbb{E}_{(x, y^+, y^-) \sim \mathcal{D}} \log[\sigma(r(y^+|x) - r(y^-|x)-\lambda \Delta( y^-, y^+;x))],
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is a positive constant. Intuitively, when $\Delta( y^-, y^+;x)$ is large, $\sigma(r(y^+|x) - r(y^-|x)-\lambda \Delta( y^-, y^+;x))$ becomes small, and the optimization in (\ref{eq:bt_obj2}) will tend to enlarge the “distance” $r(y^+|x) - r(y^-|x)$ between the positive and negative responses. Thus, for two tuples $(x, y^+, y^-)$ and $(x, \ty^{+}, \ty^{-})$ corresponding to the same query $x$, we want to design a $\Delta$ such that when $\Delta( y^-, y^+;x) > \Delta( \ty^-, \ty^+;x)$, their true reward values satisfy: \[ r^*(y^+|x) - r^*(y^-|x) > r^*(\ty^{+}|x) - r^*(\ty^{-}|x). \] In other words, a larger difference \( r^*(y^+|x) - r^*(y^-|x)  \) in the true reward values between the positive and negative responses corresponds to a larger $\Delta(y^-, y^+;x)$, and vice versa. However, obviously the difficulty is that we do not have access to $r^*$. 

\subsubsection{Implementing $\Delta$ via Prompting}
First, we assume that the LLM is capable of learning a reward function that aligns with the true reward function. This premise is formalized as follows:
\begin{assumption} 
\label{assu:improve}
LLM $\pi$ can construct a reward model $r$ such that for any $(x,y^+,y^-)$ with $y^+\succ y^-$, the corresponding reward values satisfy $r(y^+|x)>r(y^-|x)$.
\end{assumption}

As $r^*$ is unknown, with a capable LLM, a natural idea is to use $r$ as a proxy of $r^*$. Recall from Section~\ref{sec:intuition} that a large $r^*(y^+|x) - r^*(y^-|x)$ should correspond to a large $\Delta( y^-, y^+;x)$. Using (\ref{eq:dpo}), one can define $\Delta$ as 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:naive}
\Delta_{\na} = r(y^+|x) - r(y^-|x) =\beta\left(\log\frac{\pi (y^{+} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{+} |x)} -\log \frac{\pi (y^{-} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{-} |x)}\right). 
\end{equation}
Substituting this into the DPO objective (\ref{eq:obj_dro}), we have
\begin{eqnarray*}
\lefteqn{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+} ,y^{-})\sim \mathcal{D}}
\log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi(y^+ | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ | x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y^- | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- | x)} - \lambda %\perp[
\Delta_{\na}( y^-,y^+;x)
\right)}\\
& = & \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+} ,y^{-}) \sim \mathcal{D}}
\log 
\sigma\left((\beta-\lambda\beta) \log \frac{\pi(y^+ | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ | x)} - (\beta-\lambda\beta) \log \frac{\pi(y^- | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- | x)}
\right). %\\
\end{eqnarray*}
However, this is the same as the original DPO objective except for a scaling of the regularization parameter $\beta$, and thus is not useful.

To alleviate this problem, our idea is to first improve the LLM performance by concatenating the query $x$ with a prompt $p$, as $p\oplus x$,
where $\oplus$ denotes concatenation. With (\ref{eq:dpo}) the reward with this prompt-augmented query is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:constructed_r}
r( y|p\oplus x) =\beta \log \frac{\pi (y|p\oplus x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y|p\oplus x)} +c(x).
\end{equation}

With a good prompt, this $r$ is expected to be a good proxy of $r^*$. Specifically, we use the following prompt $p$.
\begin{promptBox}
Please generate a response with a usefulness rating of 100 out of 100 for the following query. Note that the response should be harmless. The term 100 indicates the level of usefulness, where 100 is the maximum and 1 is the minimum. Query:
\end{promptBox}

We assume that adding this prompt does not change the preference between the positive and negative responses ($y^{+}$ and $y^{-}$). 
\begin{assumption} 
\label{assu:consistency} 
For a given query $x$, if $y^{+} \succ y^{-}$, we still have $y^{+}\succ y^{-}$ with the prompt-augmented query $p\oplus x$.
\end{assumption}

Analogous to (\ref{eq:naive}), we consider the following refinement function:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:delta}
 \Delta_\pi\left( y^-,y^+;x\right) \equiv \beta\log  \frac{\pi (y^+|p\oplus x)\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^- |p\oplus x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^+ |p\oplus x)\pi (y^- |p\oplus x)}.
\end{equation}

Note that we have added subscript $\pi$ to explicitly indicate the dependence of $\Delta$ on $\pi$. Obviously, when putting this $\Delta_\pi$ into the DPO objective (\ref{eq:obj_dro}), it does not suffer from the same problem as the $\Delta_{\na}$ discussed earlier.

The following Proposition shows that $\Delta_\pi$ in (\ref{eq:delta}) satisfies two important properties. First, $\Delta_\pi(y^-, y^+;x)$ can be measured relative to the optimal response $y^*$, which allows to represent $\Delta_\pi(y^-, y^+;x)$ in terms of $\Delta_\pi(y^-, y^*;x)$ and $\Delta_\pi(y^+, y^*;x)$. Second, the positive response (which has a higher true reward value) is ``closer" to $y^*$ than the negative response, and vice versa.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:displacement}
With Assumptions \ref{assu:improve} and \ref{assu:consistency}, we have
     (i) 
     $\Delta_\pi(y^-, y^+;x)= \Delta_\pi(y^-, y^*;x) - \Delta_\pi(y^+, y^*;x)$, where $y^*$ is the optimal $y$ for the given $x$;
     (ii)
    For any tuple $(x,y^+,y^-)$, $r^*(y^+|x) >r^*(y^-|x) \Leftrightarrow \Delta_\pi(y^+, y^*;x) < \Delta_\pi(y^-, y^*;x)$.
\end{proposition} 

The following Corollary shows that this $\Delta_\pi(y^-, y^+;x)$ satisfies the desired property in Section~\ref{sec:intuition}, namely that a larger difference \( r^*(y^+|x) - r^*(y^-|x)  \) in the true reward values between the positive and negative responses corresponds to a larger $\Delta_\pi(y^-, y^+;x)$.

\begin{corollary} 
\label{cor:relative_displacement}
For any $(x,y_i^+,y_i^-)$ and $(x,y_j^+,y_j^-)$, if $r^*(y_i^+|x)>r^*(y_j^+|x)$, and $r^*(y_i^-|x)<r^*(y_j^-|x)$ we have
$r^*(y_i^+|x) - r^*(y_i^-|x) > r^*(y_j^{+}|x) - r^*(y_j^{-}|x)
\Leftrightarrow 
\Delta_\pi(y_i^-, y_i^+;x)> \Delta_\pi(y_j^{-},y_j^{+};x)$.
\end{corollary} 

\subsection{Integration with DPO and IPO}
\subsubsection{Integration with DPO}
First, we show how to integrate the proposed refinement into DPO. Substituting (\ref{eq:dpo}) and (\ref{eq:delta}) into (\ref{eq:bt_obj2}), we obtain:
\begin{eqnarray}
\lefteqn{
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+} ,y^{-}) \sim \mathcal{D}}\log
\sigma(r(y^+|x) - r(y^-|x) - \lambda 
\Delta_\pi( y^-,y^+;x)
)} \nonumber\\
&=& \!\!\!\!\!\!
\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^{+} ,y^{-})\sim \mathcal{D}}
\log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi(y^+ | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^+ | x)} \! - \! \beta \log \frac{\pi(y^- | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^- | x)} - \lambda %\perp[
\Delta_\pi( y^-,y^+;x)
\right). \label{eq:Sr-DPO_obj}
\end{eqnarray}

Recall that $\Delta_\pi$ above depends on $\pi$. During learning, we use the stop-gradient operator\footnote{In other words, $\nabla_x\perp\!\![g(x)] \equiv 0$ and $\perp\!\![g(x)] \equiv g(x)$ for any differentiable $g$.} $\perp\!\![\cdot]$ on $\Delta_\pi$to prevent it from being changed. The whole procedure, which will be called Self-refined DPO (Sr-DPO), is shown in Algorithm \ref{alg:Sr-DPO}.
\SetKwComment{Comment}{/* }{ */}
\SetKwInput{KwInput}{Input}
\SetKwInput{KwOutput}{Output}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Self-refined Direct Policy Optimization (Sr-DPO).}
\label{alg:Sr-DPO}
\KwInput{Dataset $D =\{(x,y^+,y^-)\}$, a pre-trained LLM $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$, learning rate $\alpha$.}
{
\For{$t = 1,2,\dots, T$}{
sample a minibatch $B$ of size $N$ from $D$;\\
$\boldsymbol{\theta}_t\!\! = \!\! \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1}+ \alpha\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{1}{N}\sum^N_{i=1}\!\log\sigma\left(\beta\left(\log\frac{\pi (y_i^{+} |x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y_i^{+} |x_i)} \!- \! \log \frac{\pi (y_i^{-} |x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y_i^{-} |x_i)}\right) \! - \! \lambda \!\perp\![\Delta_\pi( y_i^-,y_i^+;x_i)] \right)$;
}
}
\Return $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}$.
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Integration with IPO }
For IPO, we first construct a variant of the IPO objective in (\ref{eq:ipo_obj}):
\begin{eqnarray}
\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\! & \max_\pi & \!\!\!\!\!\!\! \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},  y^{+} \sim \pi(\cdot | x), y^{-} \sim \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot | x)} p(y^{+} \succ y^{-} | x) 
\!\! + \!\! \lambda 
r (y^{+} | p\oplus x) \!\! - \!\! \beta \mathbb{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}[\pi(y^{+} | x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{-} | x)],
\label{eq:ipo_Cali}
\end{eqnarray}

which adds an extra $\lambda \mathbb{E}_{x\sim \mathcal{D} ,y^{+} \sim \pi (\cdot | x)} r (y^{+} | p\oplus x)$ term to maximize the expectation of reward $r (y^{+} | p\oplus x)$.

\begin{proposition} \label{prop:ipo_key}
The optimal policy $\pi$ in (\ref{eq:ipo_Cali}) satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ipo_eqv}
\log\frac{\pi (y^{+} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{+} |x)} -\log \frac{\pi (y^{-} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{-} |x)} =\frac{1}{2\beta } +\lambda \Delta_{\pi}( y^-,y^+;x).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Note that when $\beta = 0$, $\log\frac{\pi (y^{+} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{+} |x)} -\log \frac{\pi (y^{-} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{-} |x)}-\lambda\Delta_\pi( y^-,y^+;x)$ should be infinite. This implies maximizing $\log\frac{\pi (y^{+} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{+} |x)} -\log \frac{\pi (y^{-} |x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y^{-} |x)}-\lambda\Delta_\pi( y^-,y^+;x)$ w.r.t, $\pi$, which has a similar form to DPO. The procedure, which will be called Self-Refined IPO (Sr-IPO), is shown in Algorithm \ref{alg:Sr-IPO}. It uses stochastic gradient descent to minimize the difference between the LHS and RHS in (\ref{eq:ipo_eqv}).

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Self-refined Identity Policy Optimization (Sr-IPO).}
\label{alg:Sr-IPO}
\KwInput{Dataset $D =\{(x,y^+,y^-)\}$, a pre-trained LLM $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ with parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}_0$, learning rate $\alpha$.}
{
\For{$t = 1,2,\dots, T$}{
sample a minibatch $B$ of size $N$ from $D$;\\
$\boldsymbol{\theta}_t =\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} \!\!-\alpha\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \frac{1}{N}\sum^N_{i=1} \!\! \left[\left(\log\frac{\pi (y_i^{+} |x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y_i^{+} |x_i)} \! - \!\log \frac{\pi (y_i^{-} |x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y_i^{-} |x_i)}\right) \! - \! \lambda \perp\![\Delta_\pi( y_i^-,y_i^+;x_i)] -\frac{1}{2\beta }\right]^{2}$;
}
}
\Return $\boldsymbol{\theta}_{T}$.
\end{algorithm}

(i) MT-Bench \citep{zheng2023judging}, which is a multi-turn question set on writing, roleplay, extraction, reasoning, math, coding, knowledge I (STEM), and knowledge II (humanities/social science).
(ii) Vicuna-Bench \citep{vicuna2023}, which is a single-turn question set on writing, roleplay, generic, fermi, counterfactual, coding, math, and knowledge.

\textbf{Setup for MT-Bench and Vicuna-Bench.} 
As in \citep{rafailov2023direct}, we use Pythia 2.8B \citep{biderman2023pythia}, a pretrained LLM without supervised fine-tuning and RLHF, as the backbone model. Following \citep{amini2024direct}, we first conduct supervised fine tuning (SFT) using the HH-RLHF dataset \citep{bai2022training}, which is human preference data on helpfulness and harmlessness based on positive feedbacks. We then perform direct alignment using the HH-RLHF dataset on the SFT model. Finally, we follow \citep{rafailov2023direct} to use GPT-4~\cite{achiam2023gpt} as a judge to evaluate the testing performance of the direct alignment trained model. For performance evaluation, we use the win-rate, tie-rate, and lose rate as in \citep{wang2023beyond}.

\textbf{Setup for Open-LLM Leader-board.}
Following \citep{tunstall2023zephyr,chen2024self}, we use zephyr-7b-sft-full\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/alignment-handbook/zephyr-7b-sft-full}}, a supervised fine-tuned version of Mistral 7B \citep{jiang2023mistral}, as the basic model. We directly perform direct alignment using a large-scale diverse preference dataset Ultra-feedback \citep{cui2023ultrafeedback}. The fine-tuned model is then evaluated on the testing benchmarks via the platform~\citep{eval-harness}.

Following \citep{wang2023beyond}, we use three performance metrics: 

i) Average marginal: $\frac{1}{N}\sum^N_{i=1}\log \frac{\pi\left(y_i^{+} \mid x_i\right)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(y_i^{+} \mid x_i\right)}-\log \frac{\pi\left(y_i^{-} \mid x_i\right)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(y_i^{-} \mid x_i\right)}$, which measures the gap between positive and negative responses.

(ii) Accuracy: $\frac{1}{N}
\sum^N_{i=1}\mathbbm{1} \left(\log \frac{\pi(y_i^{+} \mid x_i)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{+} \mid x_i)}> \log \frac{\pi(y_i^{-} \mid x_i)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{-} \mid x_i)}\right)$, 
which measures the number of tuples with the reward of a positive response larger than that of the negative response.
Here,
$\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)$ returns 1 when the argument holds and 0 otherwise. 

(iii) Accuracy defined on the prompt-augmented tuples: 
$\frac{1}{N}\sum^N_{i=1}\mathbbm{1} \left(\log \frac{\pi(y_i^{+} \mid p\oplus x_i)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{+} \mid p\oplus x_i)}> \right.$ $\left.\log \frac{\pi(y_i^{-} \mid p\oplus x_i)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_i^{-} \mid 
p\oplus x_i)} \right)$,
which measures the accuracy for tuples with input augmented by the prompt.

\textbf{Baselines and Implmentation Details.}
We choose two widely-adopted direct alignment baselines: (i) DPO \citep{rafailov2023direct}, and (ii) IPO \citep{azar2024general}. Following \citep{rafailov2023direct}, $\beta$ is set to 0.1 for DPO and IPO. We also set $\beta=0.1$ for the proposed Sr-DPO and Sr-IPO for fair comparison. We select the optimal $\lambda$ from $\{0.1,0.3,0.5,1\}$ on the first 50 tuples from the HH-RLHF testing dataset. For both the HH-RLHF and UltraChat datasets, following \citep{rafailov2023direct}, the learning rate is $5 \times 10^{-7}$, the optimizer is RMSprop \citep{hinton2012neural}, the batch size is 64, and we also use gradient normalization to help training. The maximal input token size in training is 512. All experiments are performed on 8 A100 GPUs, and we use fully sharded data parallel~\citep{zhao2023pytorch} for distributed training.

\subsection{Correlation between Marginal and Score}
Recall that GPT-4 is used as the evaluator. For each instance $x_i$, we define the score difference between the corresponding positive $y_i^+$ and negative $y_i^-$ responses as $s^+(x_i) - s^-(x_i)$, where $s^+(x_i)$ (resp. $s^-(x_i)$) is GPT-4's evaluation score (0-5) of $y_i^+$ (resp. $y_i^-$). Inspired by \citep{kim2024prometheus}, which uses correlation to measure the agreements between GPT-4 and testing LLMs, here we compute the correlation between the marginal $\log \frac{\pi\left(y_i^{+} \mid x_i\right)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(y_i^{+} \mid x_i\right)} - \log \frac{\pi\left(y_i^{-} \mid x_i\right)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\left(y_i^{-} \mid x_i\right)}$ and score difference $s^+(x_i) - s^-(x_i)$ over 200 random tuples from the Ultrafeedback-binarized-preferences dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/argilla/ultrafeedback-binarized-preferences}} (which already contains $s^+(x)$'s and $s^-(x)$'s). In particular, we use (i) Pearson’s correlation~\citep{sedgwick2012pearson}, which measures linear relationships; (ii) Spearman’s rank correlation~\citep{sedgwick2014spearman}, which measures monotonic relationships; and (iii) Kendall’s Tau~\citep{noether1981kendall}, which evaluates the strength and direction of associations.

Table \ref{tbl:corr} shows the correlation. As can be seen, Sr-DPO (resp. Sr-IPO) exhibits higher correlation values compared to DPO (resp. IPO). This suggests that Sr-DPO and Sr-IPO can evaluate the qualities of the positive and negative responses more accurately.

\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we find that the widely adopted DPO method falls short by not accounting for the relative qualities of positive and negative samples, which can lead to sub-optimal training outcomes. To address this issue, we propose leveraging the intrinsic knowledge within LLMs to refine the loss function. Our main contributions are three-fold:
1) We utilize the knowledge of LLMs to create a refinement function that effectively estimates the quality of both positive and negative responses.
2) We demonstrate that under mild assumptions, the refinement function can enable the loss function to self-refine, leading to better alignment with human preferences.
3) Based on the refinement function, we develop two practical algorithms that enhance the training process.
Experimental results from various evaluators indicate that the proposed self-refined methods significantly improve the performance of fine-tuned models compared to existing approaches.

One limitation of our work is that we do not combine it with online policy-based direct alignment \citep{tang2024understanding}. We will investigate this in our future work.



\section{Broader Impacts}

Our work falls under one of the RLHF frameworks, and thus shares similar social impacts, both positive and negative. On the positive side, our method can help LLMs avoid generating harmful or unhelpful results by aligning with human feedback. However, conversely, our method also has the potential to enable LLMs to generate harmful or unhelpful results if it is used to align with malicious preferences.
