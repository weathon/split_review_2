\title{A few-shot Label Unlearning in Vertical Federated Learning}

\begin{abstract}
This paper addresses the critical challenge of unlearning in Vertical Federated Learning (VFL), an area that has received limited attention compared to horizontal federated learning. We introduce the first approach specifically designed to tackle label unlearning in VFL, focusing on scenarios where the active party aims to mitigate the risk of label leakage. Our method leverages a limited amount of labeled data, utilizing manifold mixup to augment the forward embedding of insufficient data, followed by gradient ascent on the augmented embeddings to erase label information from the models. This combination of augmentation and gradient ascent enables high unlearning effectiveness while maintaining efficiency, completing the unlearning procedure within seconds. Extensive experiments conducted on diverse datasets, including MNIST, CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and ModelNet, validate the efficacy and scalability of our approach. This work represents a significant advancement in federated learning, addressing the unique challenges of unlearning in VFL while preserving both privacy and computational efficiency.


\end{abstract}

Vertical Federated Learning (VFL) \citep{yang2019federated} allows multiple organizations to collaboratively utilize their private datasets in a privacy-preserving manner, even when they share some sample IDs but differ significantly in terms of features. In VFL, there are typically two types of parties: (i) the passive party, which holds the \textit{features}, and (ii) the active party, which possesses the \textit{labels}. VFL has seen widespread application, especially in sensitive domains like banking, healthcare, and e-commerce, where organizations benefit from joint modeling without exposing their raw data \citep{yang2019federated, li2020review}.

A fundamental requirement in Vertical Federated Learning (VFL) is the necessity for unlearning, which is driven by participants’ "right to be forgotten" as mandated by regulations such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)\footnote{\url{https://gdpr-info.eu/art-17-gdpr/}} and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)\footnote{\url{https://oag.ca.gov/privacy/ccpa}}. While unlearning has been explored in the context of Horizontal Federated Learning (HFL), there has been limited attention to its application in vertical settings. Existing studies on vertical federated unlearning \citep{zhang2023securecutfederatedgradientboosting, 10.1145/3657290, electronics12143182} primarily focus on the unlearning process for individual clients, often addressing the removal of all features from the passive party upon their exit. In contrast, this paper emphasizes the \textit{unlearning of labels}, which is a critical aspect in VFL, particularly in scenarios such as Credit Risk Assessment where the determination of a loan applicant's likelihood of default is essential. Moreover, the active party aims to eliminate label information not only from the active model but also from the passive models, as the passive models may retain label information \citep{fu2022label}.

A significant challenge in directly applying traditional machine unlearning methods, such as retraining \citep{bourtoule2020machineunlearning,foster2023fastmachineunlearningretraining} or Boundary unlearning \citep{chen2023boundaryunlearning}, in this context pose a \textit{risk of leaking unlearned labels} during the unlearning process. Typically, the active party, which retains the labels, must either inform the passive party about the samples that require unlearning or transfer the gradients associated with the unlearned label. This practice may inadvertently expose sensitive label information to the passive party (see Figure \ref{fig:1} and Sect. \ref{sec:leakage-lab}). 

To address this challenge, we propose a few-shot unlearning method that effectively erases labels from both the active model and passive model in vertical federated learning by leveraging a limited amount of private data (see Sect. \ref{sec:method}). Specifically, our method employs manifold mixup \citep{verma2019manifold} to augment the forward embeddings of each passive party. The active party then performs gradient ascent on the mixed embeddings to unlearn the active model and subsequently transfers the inverse gradients to the passive party to facilitate the unlearning of the passive model independently. This approach offers three key advantages: first, it necessitates only labels from a small amount of private data, significantly reducing the risk of label privacy leakage; second, by utilizing the manifold mixup technique, it enhances unlearning effectiveness with minimal data; and third, it is highly efficient, completing the unlearning process within seconds. The primary contributions of this work are as follows: 
\begin{enumerate} 
\item To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to address the unlearning of labels in vertical federated learning.

\item We systematically elucidate the label privacy leakage that may occur when directly applying traditional machine unlearning methods in Sect. \ref{sec:leakage-lab}.

\item We propose a few-shot label unlearning method that effectively erases labels from both the active and passive models in vertical federated learning, utilizing a limited amount of private data. Moreover, this approach leverages only a small number of data to mitigate the risk of label privacy leakage while employing manifold mixup to enhance unlearning effectiveness.

\item We conduct extensive experiments on multiple benchmark datasets, including MNIST, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and ModelNet, demonstrating that our method rapidly and effectively unlearns target labels compared to other machine unlearning methods.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Works}

\label{gen_inst}

\paragraph{Machine Unlearning \& Horizontal Federated Unlearning.} Machine unlearning was initially introduced by \citep{7163042} to selectively remove some data from model without retrain the model from scratch \citep{garg2020formalizingdatadeletioncontext, Chen_2021}. Machine unlearning can be categorized into exact unlearning and approximate unlearning. Exact unlearning methods such as SISA \citep{bourtoule2020machineunlearning} and ARCANE \citep{ijcai2022p556} split data into sections and train sub-models for each data section and merge all sub-models. During unlearning, retrain the affected data section and merge all sub-models again. In approximate unlearning, techniques such as fine tuning\citep{golatkar2020eternalsunshinespotlessnet,jia2024modelsparsitysimplifymachine} (fine tune with $\mathcal{D}_{r}$), random label\citep{graves2020amnesiacmachinelearning,chen2023boundaryunlearning} (fine tune with incorrect random label of $\mathcal{D}_{u}$), noise introducing\citep{Tarun_2024,huang2021unlearnableexamplesmakingpersonal}, gradient ascent\citep{goel2023adversarialevaluationsinexactmachine,choi2023machineunlearningbenchmarksforgetting,abbasi2023covarnavmachineunlearningmodel,hoang2023learnunlearndeepneural,golatkar2020eternalsunshinespotlessnet}(maximise loss associate with $\mathcal{D}_{u}$), knowledge distillation\citep{chundawat2023badteachinginduceforgetting,zhang2023machineunlearningmethodologybase,kurmanji2023unboundedmachineunlearning} (train a student model) and weights scrubbing \citep{golatkar2020eternalsunshinespotlessnet, golatkar2020forgettingoutsideboxscrubbing, golatkar2021mixedprivacyforgettingdeepnetworks, guo2023certifieddataremovalmachine, foster2023fastmachineunlearningretraining} (discarding heavily influenced weights) are used. 

Most of the existing research works in federated unlearning are horizontal federated unlearning \citep{wu2022federatedunlearningknowledgedistillation, gu2024ferrarifederatedfeatureunlearning, zhao2024surveyfederatedunlearningtaxonomy, romandini2024federatedunlearningsurveymethods, liu2024surveyfederatedunlearningchallenges,10189868, 10229075, ye2023heterogeneousdecentralizedmachineunlearning, gao2022verifiverifiablefederatedunlearning, cao2022fedrecoverrecoveringpoisoningattacks, yuan2022federatedunlearningondevicerecommendation, alam2023ridtrailremotelyerasing, li2023subspacebasedfederatedunlearning, halimi2023federatedunlearningefficientlyerase, 10234397, 10355067, dhasade2023quickdropefficientfederatedunlearning, Liu_2022, 10269017, wang2022federatedunlearningclassdiscriminativepruning, gu2024unlearninglearningefficientfederated}. Only these three research works focus in vertical federated unlearning \citep{zhang2023securecutfederatedgradientboosting, 10.1145/3657290, electronics12143182}. \citep{zhang2023securecutfederatedgradientboosting} introduce vertical federated unlearning in gradient boosting tree. \citep{10.1145/3657290} introduce passive party unlearning on deep learning model with fast retraining on remaining parties. \citep{electronics12143182} introduce passive party unlearning on logistic regression model.

\paragraph{Vertical Federated Learning \& Privacy Leakage} Vertical Federated Learning (VFL) is introduced to meet the needs of enterprises looking to utilize features distributed across multiple parties for improved model performance, compared to models trained by a single entity, all while preserving data privacy \citep{yang2019federated}. In VFL, privacy is of utmost importance because the participants are typically companies that handle valuable and sensitive user information. \section{Label Leakage during Vertical Federated Unlearning}

This section illustrates the risk of label during the label unlearning process. 

\subsection{General Setup} \label{sec:setup}
\noindent\textbf{VFL Training.}
We assume that a vertical federated learning setting consists of one active party $P_0$ and $K$ passive parties $\{P_1, \cdots, P_K\}$  who collaboratively train a VFL model $\Theta=(\theta, \omega)$ to optimize the following objective: 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss-VFL}
\begin{split}
        \min_{\omega, \theta_1, \cdots, \theta_K} &\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\ell(F_{\omega} \circ (G_{\theta_1}(x_{1,i}),G_{\theta_2}(x_{2,i}), \\
        & \cdots,G_{\theta_K}(x_{K,i})), y_{i}),
\end{split}
\end{equation}

in which Party $P_k$ owns features $\bx_k = (x_{k,1}, \cdots, x_{k,n})$ and the passive model $G_{\theta_k}$, the active party owns the labels $\by=  \{y_1, \cdots, y_m\}$ and active model $F_\omega$. Each passive party $k$ transfers its forward embedding $H_k$ to the active party to compute the loss. The active model $F_\omega$ and passive models $G_{\theta_k},k \in \{1,\dots,K\}$ are trained based on backward gradients. Note that, before training, all parties leverage Private Set Intersection (PSI) protocols \cite{} to align data records with the same IDs. 
\noindent\textbf{Unlearning Label in VFL.}
When the active party requests to unlearn some sensitive labels $ \by^u$, where the corresponding feature is $\{\bx^u_k\}_{k=1}^K :=\{\{x_{k,i}^u\}_{i=1}^{n_u}\}_{k=1}^K $. The active party aims to remove the influence of $\by^u$ on both the active model $F_\omega$ and $K$ passive models $\{G_{\theta_k}\}_{k=1}^K$.

\noindent\textbf{Threat Model.}
We assume all participating parties are \textit{semi-honest} and do not collude with each other. An adversary (i.e., the passive party) faithfully executes the training protocol but may launch privacy attacks to infer the private labels of the active party. 

\noindent\textbf{Assumption.}
We assume that the passive party possesses corresponding labels for a limited number of features, defined as  $\calD^p =\{(\bx_k^p, \by^p)\}_{k=1}^K= \{\{(x_{k,i}^p,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_p} \}_{k=1}^K$, where $n_p << n_u$. This assumption is reasonable, as the active party must convey some label information to the passive party in order to effectively remove that information. Furthermore, this assumption is widely employed in prior works \citep{fu2022label,gu2023fedpass,zou2022defending}.

\subsection{Label leakage during unlearning} \label{sec:leakage-lab}
To remove the influence of the passive models $\{G_{\theta_k}\}_{k=1}^K$, there exists a risk of unlearning label leakage ($\by_u= \{y_{1}^u, \ldots, y_{m_u}^u\}$)
to the passive parties. During the unlearning process, the active party is required to transfer information to the passive party, e.g., gradients $\bg_u = \{g_{1}^u, \ldots, g_{n_u}^u\}$, in order to effectively unlearn the label associated with the passive model. Consequently, the passive party may infer the label based on this information.

In particular, when unlearning a single class $y_{u,1}$, we consider two representative unlearning methods: retraining \citep{foster2023fastmachineunlearningretraining} and Boundary unlearning \citep{chen2023boundaryunlearning}. For retraining methods, the active party must inform the passive party regarding which features do not require training, thus, the label is leaked. In the case of Boundary unlearning, the gradients transferred to the passive party correspond to the features associated with the label $y_{u,1}$ may leak the label.
Furthermore, when multiple labels ($m_u$) are targeted for unlearning, we consider the Boundary unlearning as an example. This method illustrates that the passive party can infer label information from the gradients $\bg_u$ transmitted by the active party during the unlearning process. Specifically, the passive party employs clustering on $\bg_u$ to derive $m_u$ clusters by optimizing the following objective function:
\begin{equation} 
\label{eq:clustering}
\min \sum_{g_{i} \in \mathcal{C}_j} \sum_{j=1}^{m_u} |g_{u,i} - \bar{g}_{u,j}|, 
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}_j$ denotes the set of points assigned to cluster $j$, and $\bar{g}_{u,j}$ represents the centroid of cluster $j$. Consequently, the passive party can deduce the labels of the features in $\mathcal{X}$. Figure \ref{fig:label_leakage} presents the label leakage accuracy during unlearning in vertical federated learning for varying numbers of unlearning classes. For instance, even with four classes from CIFAR-100, the passive party achieves a clustering accuracy of 62.45\%.

\section{Challenges of class unlearning in VFL}

\label{headings}

\subsection{Vertical Federated Class Unlearning}
In VFL, all participant parties share the dataset with same sample space but different feature space. There is one active party who own the data label, and some passive parties own the data features. The deep learning model is split to active party and passive parties in VFL. Active party own the model with classification layers and passive parties own the model with input and features layer. In the training stages, passive parties generate data embedding from the data features and deep model they own with forward propagation and send to active party. Then, active party concatenate the received embedding and generate label prediction with forward propagation. Then, loss is being calculated at active party site and backward propagation is being performed. The generated gradient for each embedding is being sent to each passive parties. Each passive parties perform a backward propagation with the received gradients.  

In inference stage, all passive parties generate embedding with the data features they own and send it to active party. Active party make a class prediction with the embedding receive. In VFL, all parties are require to participate in the training and inference stage. 

Hence, during unlearning stage, all parties are also require to participate to induce unlearning. Due to this problem, there are some challenges in VFU with current unlearning methods:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Privacy protection of data from all parties.; 
    \item Limited computational resources.;
    \item Difficulties in keeping all data.;
    \item Excess unlearning features layer of deep model.
\end{enumerate}

We will further explain these challenges in Section \ref{challenges}.
\subsubsection{Challenges for class unlearning in VFL}
\label{challenges}
In VFL, only active party has data label access and only passive parties have data feature access, all party have no access to data features and label from other parties. This privacy protection nature of VFL ensure passive parties don't know the label of the data they hold to protect privacy of active party and vice-versa. When active party need to unlearn a single class, passive parties need to send the embedding of the unlearn class data to active party. Hence, passive will know the data involve in this class unlearning are belong to same class. This class unlearning process has leaked the label of the data to passive parties indirectly. 

In VFL, every party might have different computational resources for unlearning task. Hence, existing unlearning methods which require high computational resources such as weight scrubbing, noise introducing and fine tuning are impractical in VFU.

The very first step for a VFL system to start a collaborative training process is to align the data used for the training. This process can be referred to as entity alignment, which adopts private set intersection techniques to find the common sample IDs without revealing unaligned dataset. Hence, any data which are not completely align cannot use in training, inference and unlearning. It is hard for all party keep all data. 

Most of the existing machine unlearning method typically require access to $\mathcal{D}_r$ or $\mathcal{D}$. Only gradient ascent, random label and boundary unlearning require access to $\mathcal{D}_f$ for unlearning. Even $\mathcal{D}_f$ alone may consist large amount of data, e.g. single class in Cifar10 \citep{krizhevsky2009learning} have 5000 number of images. This requirement is impractical in the VFL context, where each parties may loss some data and have limited computational resources. Therefore, finding a method to effectively unlearn a single class with very limited number of data is crucial and practically in VFL settings.

There are many existing label inference attacks literature which show effectiveness in leaking labels to passive parties in VFL \citep{277244, li2022labelleakageprotectiontwoparty, 10136175, sun2022labelleakageprotectionforward, 9899694}. \citep{277244} introduce Passive Model Completion (PMC) which a malicious passive party train a self-defined top model with its well trained bottom model and small number of label data. Hence, an efficient unlearning in features layer yet not lead to catastrophic forgetting is important in VFU. Current state of arts machine unlearning methods such as fisher forgetting do model perturbation on deep model do unlearning features layer well but lead to catastrophic forgetting on $\mathcal{D}_{r}$. Therefore, finding a method to effectively unlearn bottom models in passive parties while preserving the overall performance of VFL on $\mathcal{D}_r$ is crucial in VFU.

\section{The Proposed few-shot label unlearning methods}

This section introduces the proposed methods few-shot label unlearning methods, utilizing a limited amount of labeled data. Figure \ref{fig:2} shows the overview of our proposed solution. The methods comprise two primary steps: first, augmenting the forward embedding through manifold mixup to address the scarcity of labeled data for unlearning, as discussed in Section \ref{EE}. Second, employing gradient ascent on the augmented embedding to influence both the passive and active models, thereby facilitating the removal of the specified class, as elaborated in Section \ref{fsvfu}.


\subsection{Vertical Manifold Mixup}
\label{EE}
Due to the label privacy leakage described in Section \ref{sec:leakage-lab}, directly applying traditional machine unlearning methods poses challenges. We assume that the active party discloses a limited number of labels to the passive party to facilitate the unlearning of a specific class. Consequently, this scenario can be framed as a few-shot unlearning problem, wherein a minimal set of labels is employed to unlearn all associated labels.

\caption{Our Method}
\label{alg:cap}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Statex \textbf{Input:} Bottom models parameters $\theta_k$ of $K$ passive parties, top model parameters $\omega$ , unlearn data $\mathcal{D}_u$, learning rate $\eta$, unlearn epoch $N$.
\Statex \textbf{Output:} Unlearned bottom models parameters $\theta_k^{u}$, unlearned top model parameters $\omega^{u}$

\For {$n$ in $N$}:
    \For {$(x^{u}_i, y^{u}_i)$ in $\mathcal{D}_u$}:
        \State \gray{$\triangleright$ \textit{Passive parties $k$:}}
        \State Split $x^{u}_i$ to $K$ parts.
        \For {$k=1$ to $K$}:
            \State $H_k = G_{\theta_k}(x_{k,i}^u)$
            \State Generate $H_k'$ from $H_k$ according to \eqref{eq:loss-VFLmixup}.
        \EndFor
        \State \gray{$\triangleright$ \textit{Active party:}}
        \State $H' = [{H_1'},...,{H_K'}].$
        \State $y = F_\omega(H').$
        \State $\textit{L} = \ell(y, y').$
        \State $\omega = \omega + \eta \cdot \frac{\partial \textit{L}}{\partial \omega}$
        \State Active party compute $\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial H_k'}$ to transfer all passive parties.

       \State \gray{$\triangleright$ \textit{Passive parties $k$:}}
        \For{$k=1$ to $K$}:
            \State ${g_k} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial H_k'} \cdot \frac{\partial H_k}{\partial \omega}$
            \State $\theta_k = \theta_k + \eta \cdot g_k$
        \EndFor
    \EndFor
\EndFor
\Statex \textbf{Return} $\theta_k^{u}$ and $\omega^{u}$.

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
    \end{minipage}
\end{wrapfigure}

Drawing inspiration from few-shot learning principles, we adopt the manifold mixup mechanism \citep{verma2019manifold} by interpolating hidden embeddings rather than directly mixing the features. We propose a manifold mixup framework for Vertical Federated Learning (VFL) by optimizing the following loss function:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
& \min_{\omega, \theta_1, \cdots, \theta_K}  \frac{1}{n_p^2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_p}\ell(F_{\omega} \circ (\text{Mix}_\lambda (G_{\theta_1}(x_{1,i}^p),G_{\theta_1}(x_{1,j}^p)), \\
        & \quad \cdots,\text{Mix}_\lambda(G_{\theta_K}(x_{K,i}^p),G_{\theta_K}(x_{K,j}^p)), \text{Mix}_\lambda(y_{i}^p,y_{j}^p)),
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

where 

\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss-VFLmixup}
    \text{Mix}_\lambda(a,b) = \lambda \cdot a +(1-\lambda) \cdot b.
\end{equation}

The mixed coefficient $\lambda$ ranges from 0 to 1. The advantage of the manifold mixup approach lies in its ability to flatten the state distributions \citep{verma2019manifold}. Specifically, for each passive party $k$, mixup is applied to the forward embeddings $\{{H}^p_k = G_{\theta}(x_{k,i}^p)\}$ to generate numerous mixed embeddings $H_k'$. Subsequently, all passive parties transfer their respective mixed embeddings $H_k'$ to the active party.
\subsection{Vertical Label Unlearning via Gradient Ascent}
\label{fsvfu}

Once the augmented embeddings $\{H_1', \ldots, H_K'\}$ for the representative unlearned data $\mathcal{D}_p$ (label is known) are generated, a straightforward yet effective strategy is to implement gradient ascent for both the active and passive models using these augmented embeddings. Specifically, the active party concatenates all embeddings $\{H_k'\}_{k=1}^K$ into a single tensor $H' = [H_1', \ldots, H_K']$, and optimizes it according to the following formulation:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:active}
    \min_\omega \ell(F_\omega(H'), y') = \ell(F_\omega([H_1', \ldots, H_K']), y'),
\end{equation}

where $y'$ represents the mixture of the representative unlearned labels and $\eta$ is the learning rate. 

\noindent\textbf{Unlearning for active model $F_\omega$.} On one hand, the active model undergoes unlearning for active model $F_\omega$ via gradient ascent as follows:

\begin{equation}
    \omega = \omega + \eta \nabla_\omega \ell(F_\omega(H'), y').
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Unlearning for passive model $G_{\omega_k}$.} Subsequently, the active party computes the gradients $g_k' = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial H_k'}$ in accordance with \eqref{eq:active} and transmits these gradients to the corresponding passive party $k$. Finally, the passive party $k$ updates the passive model $G_{\theta_k}$ using the following expression:

\begin{equation}
    \theta_k = \theta_k + \eta \nabla_{H_k'}\ell(F_\omega(H'), y') \cdot \nabla_{\theta_k}H_k'.
\end{equation}

It is important to note that gradient ascent may lead to significant degradation in model utility or even result in vanishing gradients if the parameters are not appropriately tuned.





\begin{comment}
Hence, we proposed a method which require only small number of samples from $\mathcal{D}_{f}$ and do embedding expansion to generated additional synthetic feature points in embedding space in order to exploit additional relevant information for unlearning. Gradient ascent is chosen as the unlearning method due to some reasons: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Gradient ascent require only $\mathcal{D}_{f}$ for unlearning.
    \item Gradient ascent is fast and less memory consumption.
    \item Gradient ascent process have small number of epochs of model training with direction of increasing loss for $\mathcal{D}_{f}$. Hence, it can unlearn bottom model well. 
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experimental Result}

This section presents the empirical analysis of the proposed method in terms of utility, unlearning effectiveness, time efficiency and some ablation studies.

\subsection{Experiment Setup}
\paragraph{Unlearning Scenarios}
\textit{Single-class unlearning}: We forget a single class from all datasets.  \textit{Two-classes unlearning}: We forget two classes from CIFAR10 and CIFAR100. \textit{Multi-classes unlearning}: We forget four classes from CIFAR100.

\paragraph{VFL Setting \& Datasets \& Models} We stimulate a VFL scenario by splitting a neural network into two bottom models and a top model and assigning the two bottom models to each two passive parties and the top model to the active party. We conduct experiments on four datasets: MNIST\citep{726791}, CIFAR10, CIFAR100\citep{krizhevsky2009learning} and ModelNet\citep{wu20153dshapenetsdeeprepresentation}. We adopt Resnet18\citep{he2015deepresiduallearningimage} on dataset MNIST, CIFAR10, CIFAR100 and ModelNet. We do extend our experiments with Vgg16\citep{simonyan2015deepconvolutionalnetworkslargescale} on dataset CIFAR10 and CIFAR100. Experiments are repeated over five random trials, and results are reported as mean and standard deviation.

\paragraph{Evaluations Metrics} We evaluate the utility of unlearning by measuring accuracy of $\mathcal{D}_{r}$ before and after unlearning. The higher accuracy on $\mathcal{D}_{r}$ indicates stronger utility. To evaluate the unlearning effectiveness, we construct a simple MIA from \citep{shokri2017membershipinferenceattacksmachine} to test Attack Success Rate (ASR) and measuring the accuracy of $\mathcal{D}_{u}$ before and after unlearning. MIA seeks to determine if a specific data record was included in the training of a target machine learning model. Time efficiency is evaluated by comparing the runtime of each baseline. 
\paragraph{Baselines} We compare our method with the following baselines: Retrain, Fine Tuning\citep{golatkar2020eternalsunshinespotlessnet, jia2024modelsparsitysimplifymachine}, Fisher Forgetting\citep{golatkar2020eternalsunshinespotlessnet}, Amnesiac Unlearning\citep{graves2020amnesiacmachinelearning}, UNSIR\citep{Tarun_2024} and Boundary Unlearning \citep{chen2023boundaryunlearning}.

\subsection{Experimental Results}
\subsubsection{Utility Guarantee}
To assess the utility of our proposed unlearning method, we evaluate accuracy on $\mathcal{D}_r$ before and after unlearning (Tab \ref{tab:1classunlearn}, \ref{tab:2classunlearn}, \ref{tab:multiclassunlearn}). An effective unlearning method should retain as much information as possible from $\mathcal{D}_r$.

From tab \ref{tab:1classunlearn}, \ref{tab:2classunlearn}, \ref{tab:multiclassunlearn}, we observe that: 1) Fine-tuning achieve good preservation on $\mathcal{D}_r$, but its unlearning effectiveness is low (see Section \ref{unlearning effectiveness}). 2) Fisher forgetting badly preserve the information of $\mathcal{D}_r$, resulting in huge degradation on $\mathcal{D}_r$ accuracy. 3) Random incorrect labeling of $\mathcal{D}_u$ from Amnesiac Unlearning causes the decision boundaries for $\mathcal{D}_r$ to shift unpredictably, resulting in a drop in accuracy on $\mathcal{D}_r$. This degradation is more pronounced in datasets with a large number of classes, such as CIFAR100 and ModelNet. 4) The repair step from UNSIR fails to fully retain the information in $\mathcal{D}_r$, leading to some performance degradation on $\mathcal{D}_r$.  5) Boundary unlearning exhibit inconsistencies across different datasets, models, and scenarios. In some cases, they show huge degradation on $\mathcal{D}_r$, while in other instances, they preserve $\mathcal{D}_r$ well. 

Our solution show good unlearning utility in all experiments.

2) Fisher forgetting shows strong unlearning effectiveness, reducing accuracy of $\mathcal{D}_u$ to 0.00\%. However, it do hugely degrade the performance on $\mathcal{D}_r$. Fisher forgetting fail to unlearn two-classes and multi-classes and yet show degradation on $\mathcal{D}_r$.

\begin{comment}
Fine-tuning demonstrates good unlearning performance on certain datasets in single-class unlearning. However, it retains some information from $\mathcal{D}_f$ on some datasets and performs poorly in 2-classes and multi-classes unlearning. While fine-tuning can achieve effective unlearning with more epochs, it requires additional time and computational resources. Fisher unlearning also shows strong unlearning accuracy in single-class scenarios, reducing the accuracy on $\mathcal{D}_f$ to 0.00\% across all datasets. However, it results in significant degradation on $\mathcal{D}_r$ and is ineffective for 2-classes and multi-classes unlearning. Additionally, Fisher unlearning has a high execution time, which will be discussed further in Section \ref{time_eff}.
Amnesiac unlearning demonstrates strong unlearning performance, reducing the accuracy of $\mathcal{D}_f$ to 0.00\% across all datasets, models, and scenarios. However, the random incorrect labeling of $\mathcal{D}_f$ causes the decision boundaries for $\mathcal{D}_r$ to shift unpredictably, resulting in a drop in accuracy on $\mathcal{D}_r$. This degradation is more pronounced in datasets with a large number of classes, such as CIFAR100 and ModelNet.
Unsir introduces a noise matrix to $\mathcal{D}_f$ to degrade the model using generated noise, followed by a repair step using $\mathcal{D}_r$. Both the impairment and repair processes are completed within a single epoch at a high learning rate. The noise matrix effectively removes the information from $\mathcal{D}_f$, reducing its accuracy to 0.00\% across all datasets, models, and scenarios. However, the repair step fails to fully retain the information in $\mathcal{D}_r$, leading to some performance degradation on $\mathcal{D}_r$.
\end{comment}

\subsubsection{Unlearning Effectiveness}
\label{unlearning effectiveness}
For unlearning effectiveness, we run MIA to evaluate if the unlearned model leaks any information about the $\mathcal{D}_{u}$ and measure the accuracy of $\mathcal{D}_{u}$ before and after unlearning. We present the MIA graph of single-class unlearning in Resnet18 (fig \ref{fig:resnet18_MIA}) and Vgg16 (fig \ref{fig:vgg16_MIA}). 
From tab \ref{tab:1classunlearn}, \ref{tab:2classunlearn}, \ref{tab:multiclassunlearn}, we observe that: 1) Fine-tuning show bad unlearning effectiveness on CIFAR10 and CIFAR100 datasets. The unlearning effectiveness of fine tuning is worse on two-classes (Tab \ref{tab:2classunlearn}) and multi-classes unlearning scenarios (Tab \ref{tab:multiclassunlearn}); 2) Fisher forgetting, Amnesiac Unlearning and UNSIR shows strong unlearning effectiveness, reducing accuracy of $\mathcal{D}_u$ to 0.00\%; 3) Boundary unlearning exhibit inconsistencies across different datasets, models, and scenarios. In some cases, they show good unlearning effectiveness on $\mathcal{D}_u$, while in other instances, they show bad unlearning effectiveness. 

Our solution demonstrates strong effectiveness across all models, datasets, and scenarios. It achieving successful unlearning of $\mathcal{D}_u$. From fig \ref{fig:resnet18_MIA}, \ref{fig:vgg16_MIA}, we observe that: 1) Fine tuning shows consistent ASR score in experiments. 2) Fisher forgetting show high ASR score in many experiments. 3) Amnesiac unlearning show inconsistencies in ASR score such as very high ASR score and very low ASR score on different experiments. 4) UNSIR show high ASR score on almost all experiments.  5) Boundary unlearning show relatively consistent ASR scores. 

Our solution show consistent ASR performance across all datasets, models and scenarios.

\section{Conclusions}


In conclusion, this paper presents a pioneering approach to label unlearning within Vertical Federated Learning, addressing a critical gap in the existing literature. By introducing a few-shot unlearning method that leverages manifold mixup, we effectively mitigate the risk of label privacy leakage while ensuring efficient unlearning from both active and passive models. Our systematic exploration of potential label privacy risks and extensive experimental validation on benchmark datasets underscores the method's efficacy and rapid performance. Ultimately, this work not only advances the understanding of unlearning in VFL but also sets the stage for further innovations in privacy-preserving collaborative machine learning practices.
