\title{A prescriptive theory for brain-like inference}

\begin{abstract}
The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) is a widely used objective for training deep generative models, such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs). In the neuroscience literature, an identical objective is known as the variational free energy, hinting at a potential unified framework for brain function and machine learning. Despite its utility in interpreting generative models, including diffusion models, ELBO maximization is often seen as too broad to offer prescriptive guidance for specific architectures in neuroscience or machine learning. In this work, we show that maximizing ELBO under Poisson assumptions for general sequence data leads to a spiking neural network that performs Bayesian posterior inference through its membrane potential dynamics. The resulting model, the iterative Poisson VAE (\ipvae), has a closer connection to biological neurons than previous brain-inspired predictive coding models based on Gaussian assumptions. Compared to amortized and iterative VAEs, \ipvae learns sparser representations and exhibits superior generalization to out-of-distribution samples. These findings suggest that optimizing ELBO, combined with Poisson assumptions, provides a solid foundation for developing prescriptive theories in NeuroAI.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\keywords{ELBO, iterative inference, variational inference, sparse coding, out-of-distribution generalization}
Optimizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) serves as a unifying objective for training deep generative models \citep{hinton1995wake, dayan1995helmholtz, kingma2014auto, rezende2014stochastic, luo2208understanding}. Even when models don't explicitly reference ELBO, they're often optimizing objectives closely related to it \citep{luo2208understanding, kingma2023understanding}. This is directly paralleled by the Free Energy Principle (FEP) in neuroscience, which absorbs previous theoretical frameworks like Predictive Coding, Bayesian Brain, and Active Learning \citep{friston2005theory, friston2009free, friston2010free}. FEP states that a single objective, the minimization of variational free energy, is all that is needed. Because this is equivalent to maximizing ELBO, it suggests a powerful unifying theoretical framework for neuroscience and machine learning \citep{friston2010free}.

However, in many ways, Free Energy (and by proxy, ELBO) is too general to be useful as a theory \citep{gershman2019does, andrews2021math}. In practice, the specific implementations of FEP predictive coding have been difficult to map directly onto neural circuits \citep{millidge2019predictive, millidge2022predictive}, struggling with negative rates and prediction signals that have not been observed empirically \citep{walsh2020evaluating, millidge2022predictive}. Similarly, in machine learning, it is often discovered after the fact that a new objective is actually ELBO maximization (or KL minimization; \citet{hobson1969new}) masquerading as something else \citep{kingma2023understanding}---and not the other way around. If ELBO is \say{all you need,} then why is ELBO not prescriptive?

One possibility, at least in neuroscience, is that ELBO's lack of prescriptive theory results from incorrect approximating distributions. In fact, most of the difficulty mapping predictive coding onto neural circuits has to do with terms that result from the Gaussian assumption \citep{millidge2022predictive}. In contrast, biological neurons are largely modeled as conditionally Poisson \citep{goris2014partitioning}.

Recent work provides a potential prescriptive route: replacing Gaussians with Poisson distributions. To this end, \citet{vafaii2024pvae} introduced a reparameterization algorithm for training Poisson Variational Autoencoders (\pvae). They observed that replacing Gaussians in ELBO reduces to an amortized version of sparse coding, an influential model inspired by the brain that captures many features of the selectivity in early visual cortex \citep{olshausen1996emergence, olshausen2004sparse}. \pvae learns sparse representations, avoids posterior collapse, and performs better on downstream classification tasks. However, the authors identified a large amortization gap in \pvae \citep{vafaii2024pvae}, adding to a growing body of work that highlights limitations of amortized inference \citep{cremer2018inference, kim2021reducing}. A potential solution is to develop more general iterative inference solutions, or hybrid iterative-amortized ones \citep{marino18iterative, kim2018semi}.

Here, we extend the Poisson VAE to include iterative inference (\say{iterative \pvae,} or \ipvae). This results in a generalization of predictive coding that maps well onto biological neurons. \ipvae implements Bayesian posterior inference via private membrane potential dynamics, resembling a spiking version of the Locally Competitive Algorithm (LCA) for sparse coding \citep{rozell2008sparse}. This solution avoids the major problems with predictive coding: there is no explicit prediction, neurons communicate through spikes, and feedback is modulatory---all consistent with real neurons \citep{gilbert2013top, kandel2000principles}. But how effective is \ipvae as a machine learning model?

We evaluate \ipvae in terms of convergence, reconstruction performance, efficiency, and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. We find that \ipvae converges to sparse posterior representations, outperforming state-of-the-art iterative VAEs \citep{kim2018semi, marino18iterative}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We introduce a new architecture, \ipvae, that accomplishes the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Deriving the ELBO for sequences with Poisson-distributed latents results in a neural network that spikes, and performs predictive coding in the dynamics of the membrane potential.
    \item By reusing the same set of weights across iterations and utilizing sparse, integer spike counts, \ipvae is well-suited for hardware implementations and energy-efficient deployment.
    \item \ipvae demonstrates robust out-of-distribution generalization, excelling in both within-dataset perturbations and across-dataset generalization.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, \ipvae is a powerful brain-inspired architecture that tightly maps onto biological neurons while outperforming much larger models in key objectives such as performance, parameter count, sparsity, and out-of-distribution generalization.



\section{Background and related work}

\paragraph{Generative models and ELBO.} Generative models learn to represent the data distribution, $p_\theta(\xx)$, typically by invoking latent variables $\zz$, such that $p_\theta(\xx)=\int \! p_\theta(\xx \vert \zz)p_\theta(\zz)d\zz$ \citep{bishop2006pattern}. The key challenge is computing, $p_\theta(\zz \vert \xx)$, the posterior distribution of these latent variables given the data, which is typically intractable except for simple cases. 

Variational inference \citep{blei2017variational}, offers a practical solution by introducing an approximate posterior $q_\phi(\zz \vert \xx)$ parameterized by $\phi$. The goal is to make this approximation as close as possible to the optimal posterior $p_\theta(\zz \vert \xx)$. Ideally, one would minimize the $\mathtt{KL}$ divergence between $q_\phi(\zz \vert \xx)$ and $p_\theta(\zz \vert \xx)$, but since we cannot compute $p_\theta(\zz \vert \xx)$ exactly, direct minimization is not feasible.

The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) provides a tractable objective that indirectly minimizes the $\mathtt{KL}$ divergence between the approximate and optimal posteriors. Specifically, the relationship is:

\begin{equation}
    \log p_\theta(\xx)
    \,=\,
    \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\zz \vert \xx)} \left[ \log \frac{p_\theta(\xx, \zz)}{q_\phi(\zz \vert \xx)} \right]}_{\text{ELBO}}
    \,+\,\,
    \KL{q_\phi(\zz \vert \xx)}{p_\theta(\zz \vert \xx)} 
\end{equation}

Since $\log p_\theta(\xx)$ does not depend on $\phi$, and the $\mathtt{KL}$ divergence is non-negative, maximizing the ELBO effectively minimizes the intractable $\mathtt{KL}$ divergence \citep{hinton1995wake, kingma2014auto, rezende2014stochastic}. Interestingly, even when generative models seem to optimize a different loss function, like diffusion models \citep{chan2024tutorialdiffusionmodelsimaging, ho2020denoising}, they are often still performing KL minimization through the ELBO \citep{kingma2023understanding, luo2208understanding}.
\paragraph{ELBO in Neuroscience.} The Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) has an identical formulation in neuroscience, where it is referred to as the \say{variational free energy} \citep{friston2005theory, friston2009free, friston2010free}. The Free Energy Principle (FEP) extends the framework of perception as inference \citep{alhazen, helmholtz1867handbuch, lee2003hierarchical}, drawing concepts from predictive coding (PC; \citet{srinivasan1982predictive, rao1999predictive}). Extensive research has explored how PC might be implemented by neurons \citep{boerlin2013predictive, millidge2019predictive}, and PC has been applied in machine learning for predictive models \citep{lotter2017deep, wen2018deep, millidge2024predictive}. 

Despite their neural inspiration, FEP is difficult to map directly onto neuronal circuits \citep{kogo2015predictive, aitchison2017or}. This challenge stems from assuming Gaussian distributions for the approximate posterior and prior \citep{millidge2022predictive}. The Gaussian assumption results in models with explicit predictions or prediction errors, which have not been observed empirically \citep{mikulasch2023error}. Solutions also struggle with avoiding negative firing rates due to subtraction operations \citep{bastos2012canonical, keller2018predictive}. While leaky integrate-and-fire circuits can be engineered to perform predictive coding \citep{boerlin2013predictive}, this approach goes in the opposite direction of deriving equations governing dynamics from first principles \citep{ramstead2023bayesian}. Here, we start with the FEP and derive dynamics equations that resemble spiking neurons. Therefore, we refer to our approach as \say{prescriptive,} in contrast to the more \say{postdictive} approach of \citet{boerlin2013predictive}.

In addition, the related framework of sparse coding \cite{olshausen1996emergence, olshausen2004sparse}, particularly the biologically plausible locally competitive algorithm (LCA; \citet{rozell2008sparse}), can be viewed as a form of predictive coding with a sparse prior. LCA naturally results in a dynamic update rule that resembles neural circuits. However, LCA relies on maximum a posteriori inference, which is restrictive if we aim to sample from the full posterior distribution.

\paragraph{Bayesian posterior inference: iterative versus amortized.}

In contrast to predictive coding, Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) introduced a computationally efficient solution to maximize ELBO through \textit{amortized} inference \citep{kingma2014auto, rezende2014stochastic}. Amortized inference uses a parameterized neural network, the \say{encoder}, to produce the parameters of an approximate posterior, $q_\phi(\zz|\xx)$, in one shot (\cref{fig:overview}a). The term \say{amortized} reflects that the computational cost of inference is paid during training, not at test time, similar to cost distribution in accounting \citep{gershman2014amortized}. While amortized inference is considered efficient, it can suffer from an \textit{amortization gap}—the discrepancy between the approximate posterior provided by the encoder and the optimal variational parameters—which can be significant \citep{cremer2018inference}.
To address the amortization gap, hybrid approaches have been developed that introduce iterative elements into the VAE framework \citep{marino18iterative, kim2018semi, marino2021iterative}. For example, \citet{marino18iterative} proposed a method where the encoder network takes as input both the data sample $\xx$ and the gradients of the loss with respect to the variational parameters $\nabla_{\bm{\lambda}} \mathcal{L}$, with $\bm{\lambda} = \{ \bm{\mu}, \bm{\sigma}^2 \}$. Alternatively, semi-amortized inference \citep{kim2018semi} starts with an amortized initial estimate and refines it using stochastic variational inference (SVI; \cite{hoffman2013stochastic}) updates. Our method is closely related to these approaches, and we compare to them in the results.

Although VAEs and predictive coding are related through their optimization of ELBO \citep{marino2022predictive}, recent work has made that connection more explicit, demonstrating that classical predictive coding networks can be seen as a subclass of iterative inference in VAEs \citep{boutin2020iterative}. A key difference between our work and \citet{boutin2020iterative} is that they show the objective used by \citet{rao1999predictive} arises from assuming a delta-function posterior in the ELBO. In our work, predictive coding naturally emerges in the dynamics of the log spike rates, which comes from a fairly general assumption of Poisson distributions.

\paragraph{Poisson VAE (\pvae).} A large body of literature in neuroscience has demonstrated that neuron spike counts are well described by a Poisson process over short counting windows \citep{goris2014partitioning}. Building on this, \citet{vafaii2024pvae} recently introduced the Poisson Variational Autoencoder (\pvae), which performs posterior inference using discrete spike counts. They developed a Poisson reparameterization trick and derived the ELBO for Poisson-distributed VAEs. 

In \pvae ELBO, a firing rate penalty naturally arises from the $\mathtt{KL}$ term, which resembles sparse coding. Theoretically, the \pvae ELBO, when combined with a linear generative model, reduces to amortized sparse coding. Empirically, when trained on natural image patches, \pvae develops sparse latent representations with Gabor-like basis vectors, much like sparse coding.

While \pvae outperformed Gaussian VAEs in sparsity and downstream classification tasks, the authors noted a significant performance gap with traditional sparse coding, likely arising from an amortization gap due to the absence of iterative updates. Our work builds upon \pvae, providing further evidence that to obtain a neurally plausible architecture, Poisson is the right choice for parameterizing the prior and approximate posterior distributions in ELBO (see \cref{apdx:poissonassumption} for a discussion).



\section{Iterative Poisson VAE (IP-VAE)}

In this section, we derive the ELBO for sequences with Poisson distributions. We show the resulting architecture (\ipvae; \cref{fig:overview}b) implements iterative Bayesian posterior inference with dynamics on the log rates. We relate this directly to membrane potential dynamics in a spiking neural network and show that it solves many of the implementation limitations of classic predictive coding.

\paragraph{General setup.} 
We conceptualize iterative inference by starting with the more general framework of inference over a sequence \citep{chung2015rVAE}. From there, we can treat iterative inference for images as a sequence of the same image repeated at all time points. This approach is appealing because dynamics emerge necessarily, and it builds a foundation for future work on dynamic sequences.

Consider a sequence of $T+1$ observed data points, $\bm{X} = \{\xx_t\}_{t=0}^T$ where $\xx_t \in \RR^M$, and corresponding latent variables, $\bm{Z} = \{\zz_t\}_{t=0}^T$ where each $\zz_t$ is $K$-variate. We denote the full probabilistic generative model as the joint distribution, $p_\theta(\bm{X}, \bm{Z})$. A reasonable starting assumption for modeling the physical world is Markovian dependence between consecutive data points \citep{van1992stochastic}, resulting in the marginal distribution:
\begin{equation}
    p_\theta(\bm{X})
    =
    \int \! p_\theta(\bm{X}, \bm{Z}) \, d\bm{Z}
    =
    p_\theta(\xx_0)
    \prod_{t=1}^T
    p_\theta(\xx_t \vert \xx_{t-1}),
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta(\xx_0) = \int\! p_\theta(\xx_0 \vert \zz_0) p_\theta(\zz_0) d\zz_0$, and $p_\theta(\xx_t \vert \xx_{t-1}) = \int\! p_\theta(\xx_t \vert \zz_t) p_\theta(\zz_t \vert \xx_{t-1}) d\zz_t$. For an intuitive derivation, see \cref{apdx:generative-model-intuition}. The ELBO for our sequence data can be written as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ELBO}
\begin{aligned}
    \log p_\theta(\bm{X})
    &\geq
    \EXPECT{q_\phi(\bm{Z} \vert \bm{X})}{
        \log\frac{
            p_\theta(\bm{X}, \bm{Z})
        }{
            q_\phi(\bm{Z} \vert \bm{X})
        }
    }
    \\
    &=
    \expect{q_\phi(\bm{Z} \vert \bm{X})}{
        \log p_\theta(\bm{X} \vert \bm{Z})
    }
    -
    \KL{q_\phi(\bm{Z} \vert \bm{X})}{p_\theta(\bm{Z})}
    \\
    &=
    \LL_\text{ELBO}(\bm{X}; \theta, \phi),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $p_\theta(\bm{Z})$ is a prior (either learned or fixed) over latents and $p_\theta(\bm{X} \vert \bm{Z})$ is the conditional likelihood distribution, which is computed via a decoder network. The model parameters $(\phi, \theta)$---corresponding to the encoder and decoder networks of a VAE---are jointly optimized. For readability, we will omit the explicit dependence on $(\phi, \theta)$ moving forward. We will next express the ELBO for sequences within the Poisson VAE (\pvae) framework.
\paragraph{Iterative Poisson VAE.} To extend the \pvae to sequences, \ipvae needs to make explicit how the prior and posterior distributions update with each sample. The simplest starting point is assuming stationarity, implying that the posterior over the previous stimulus should act as a prior for the current one (\cref{fig:graphical-model}; although future extensions could extend to nonstationary signals such as videos with a more sophisticated update rule). Because of the Markovian assumption, the prior, $p(\bm{Z})$, then factorizes into the initial prior, $p(\zz_0)$ and a product over all future time steps:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior}
    p(\bm{Z})
    =
    p(\zz_0)
    \prod_{t=1}^T
    p(\zz_t \vert \xx_{t-1})
\end{equation}
The initial prior, $p(\zz_0)=\pois(\zz_0; \dec{\rr}_0)$, is Poisson with learned prior rates, $\dec{\rr}_0 \in \RR_{>0}^K$. Subsequent time steps have prior rates that depend on the stimulus from the previous time step, $p(\zz_t \vert \xx_{t-1}) = \pois(\zz_t; \dec{\rr}_t(\xx_{t-1}))$, with $\dec{\rr}_t \in \RR_{>0}^K$ for all $t$. The approximate posterior factorizes as well:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posterior}
q(\bm{Z} \vert \bm{X}) =
    q(\zz_0 \vert \xx_0)
    \prod_{t=1}^T
    q(\zz_t \vert \xx_t, \xx_{t-1}),
\end{equation}
with initial posterior, $q(\zz_0 \vert \xx_0) = \pois(\zz_0; \dec{\rr}_0 \odot \enc{\dr}(\xx_0))$, and time-dependent posterior, $q(\zz_t \vert \xx_t, \xx_{t-1}) = \pois(\zz_t; \dec{\rr}_t(\xx_{t-1}) \odot \enc{\dr}(\xx_t))$, both parameterized as Poisson distributions. We follow the formulation in \citet{vafaii2024pvae}, and define the posterior rates via an element-wise multiplicative interaction between $\dec{\rr}$ and some gain modulator, $\enc{\dr} \in \RR_{>0}^K$. This is a natural choice because rates must be positive. Without loss of generality, the relationship between two positive variables can be expressed as a base rate with a multiplicative gain applied to it.

The conditional log-likelihood for \ipvae factorizes into a sum over individual log-likelihoods: $\quad \log p(\bm{X} \vert \bm{Z}) = \sum_{t=0}^T \log p(\xx_t \vert \zz_t)$. The $\mathtt{KL}$ term of the ELBO from \cref{eq:ELBO} also factorizes:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:KL}
\begin{aligned}
    \KL{q(\bm{Z} \vert \bm{X})}{p(\bm{Z})}
    &=
    \KL{q(\zz_0 \vert \xx_0)}{p(\zz_0)}
    +
    \sum_{t=1}^T
    \KL{q(\zz_t \vert \xx_t, \xx_{t-1})}{p(\zz_t \vert \xx_{t-1})}
    \\
    &=
    \dec{\rr}_0 \cdot f(\enc{\dr}(\xx_0))
    +
    \sum_{t=1}^T
    \dec{\rr}_t(\xx_{t-1}) \cdot f(\enc{\dr}(\xx_t)),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\cdot$ represents a vector dot product, and $f(y) = 1 - y + y\log y$ is applied element-wise. See \cref{apdx:poisson-kl} for a derivation. Since rates are positive, the $\mathtt{KL}$ term penalizes large rates, functioning as a sparsity penalty \citep{vafaii2024pvae}. We provide the full set of equations defining \ipvae generative model in \cref{apdx:summary-theory} (see also \cref{fig:graphical-model}). The following sections outline how we define the multiplicative gain, $\enc{\dr}$, which results in adaptive Bayesian posterior updating in the model dynamics.



\paragraph{Bayesian posterior updates using membrane potential dynamics.}
Because rates are positive and prior and posterior rates interact multiplicatively, it is difficult to implement dynamic updates directly on rates. A natural solution is to define updates on log rates, $\uu(t) \coloneqq \log \rr(t)$, with $\RR^K$ as our state space for a $K$-dimensional latent space. 

Dynamic updates on log rates is both a mathematical convenience and biologically realistic. Because of internal noise, the spike threshold of real neurons is best modeled as an expansive nonlinearity like an exponential \citep{priebe2004contribution, fourcaud2003spike}. Here, we take $\log(\cdot)$ to be the synaptic nonlinearity and $\exp(\cdot)$ to be the spiking nonlinearity. For the aforementioned reasons, $\uu(t)$ can be interpreted quite literally as membrane potentials.

We define the model updates as $\uu_{t+1} = \uu_t + \du_t$, with $\dec{\rr}_t = \exp(\uu_t)$ acting as the corresponding prior rates at time $t$, and $\dec{\rr}_t \odot \enc{\dr}_t = \exp(\uu_{t+1})$, as the posterior rates at time $t$. This implies $\enc{\dr}_t = \exp(\du_t)$. When processing the next input in the sequence, we take the previous posterior and use it as our current prior (\cref{fig:graphical-model}). This works, because in the present paper, we restrict ourselves to stationary inputs comprised of the same image presented multiple times.

A natural choice for $\du$ is the gradient of the loss with respect to $\uu$, through the samples $\zz$. However, the $\mathtt{KL}$ term results in high-order terms, which for this implementation we approximate as the following dynamics (see \cref{apdx:dynamics} for a detailed derivation):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:du}
    \du_t
    =
    \mJ_\dec{\theta}
    \cdot
    \Delta_t
    =
    \frac{\partial \dec{f_\theta}(\zz)}{\partial \zz}\bigg|_{\zz = \zz_t}
    \cdot
    \big(\xx_t - \dec{f_\theta}(\zz_t)\big),
\end{equation}
where $\mJ_\dec{\theta}$ is the Jacobian of the decoder, $\dec{f_\theta}$, which is a function of sampled spike counts, $\zz$ (\cref{fig:overview}b). 

Importantly, this form aligns with real neuronal properties for several reasons. Since the comparison, $\xx_t - \dec{f_\theta}(\zz_t)$, is based on spikes, each neuron's update does not directly depend on the internal states of other neurons, which matches how real neurons function \citep{kandel2000principles}. Additionally, because the comparison happens on membrane potential (log rates), feedback will appear as a modulatory signal on rate, which is also consistent with neuroscience literature \citep{gilbert2013top}. Finally, this update (\cref{eq:du}) resembles a generalization of \citet{rao1999predictive} for nonlinear generative models and avoids hacky solutions to keep rates positive, after subtracting them.

It is straightforward to see how \cref{eq:du} reduces to a spiking neural network (SNN; \cite{taherkhani2020snn}) for linear decoder networks. If $\dec{f_\theta}(\zz) = \dec{\bm{\Phi}}\zz$, then:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \du_t &= \dec{\bm{\Phi}}^T(\xx_t  - \dec{\bm{\Phi}}\zz_t)
    \\
    &=
    \dec{\bm{\Phi}}^T\xx_t
    -
    \dec{\bm{\Phi}}^T\dec{\bm{\Phi}}\zz_t
    \\
    &=
    \dec{\bm{\Phi}}^T\xx_t
    -
    \dec{\bm{W}}\zz_t,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where the first term is the feedforward receptive fields (the input current) and the second term, $\dec{\bm{W}}$, are the recurrent weights between neurons, implementing lateral competition. Note that they only communicate with each other through spikes, $\zz_t$. Thus for linear generative models, \ipvae closely resembles the locally competitive algorithm for sparse coding (LCA; \citet{rozell2008sparse}), except that it is explicitly spiking and does not have a leak term (although this could be included by replacing the diagonal of the recurrent term with a leak rather than having neurons operate on their own spikes).

In this section, we showed how following some fairly general assumptions for optimizing ELBO with Poisson distribution, led us to a spiking neural network that implements Bayesian posterior updates via predictive coding in the membrane potential dynamics. The model architecture and inference algorithm are visualized in \cref{fig:overview,fig:archi} and \Cref{alg:iterative-inference}, in increasing levels of detail.

In the next section, we evaluate \ipvae and compare it to amortized \pvae, as well as iterative Gaussian VAEs.


\section{Experiments}


We performed empirical analyses of \ipvae and alternative iterative VAE models. 
In section \ref{sec:exp:converge}, we test the general performance and stability of inference dynamics, including generalization to longer sequence lengths. Section \ref{sec:exp:amortgap} shows \ipvae closes the gap with sparse coding. Section \ref{sec:exp:rotation} demonstrates robustness to out-of-distribution (OOD) samples by evaluating models trained on MNIST \citep{lecun2010mnist} with perturbed samples (e.g., rotated MNIST). We then evaluate OOD generalization from MNIST to other character-based datasets in section \ref{sec:exp:crossDS}. Finally, in section \ref{sec:exp:compositional}, we visualize the learned weights of \ipvae, revealing their compositional nature, which is consistent with \ipvae's strong generalization capabilities. We push the limits of MNIST-trained models by testing their performance on natural images.

\paragraph{Architecture notation.} We experimented with both convolutional and multi-layer perceptron (MLP) architectures. We highlight the \enc{encoder} and \dec{decoder} networks using \enc{red} and \dec{blue}, respectively. We use the \architecture{enc}{dec} convention to clearly specify which type was used. For example, \mlpmlp means both encoder and decoder networks were mlp. We use the notation \jacmlp to denote our fully iterative (non-amortized) \ipvae, with a Jacobian-based encoder (\cref{fig:overview}b). We chose symmetrical architectures, such that \mlpmlp has exactly twice as many parameters as \jacmlp.

\paragraph{Datasets.} For the generalization results, we use MNIST, extended MNIST (EMNIST; \citet{cohen2017emnist}), Omniglot \citep{lake2015human} and Imagenet32x32 \citep{chrabaszcz2017downsampled}. We resize Omniglot and ImageNet32 down to $28 \times 28$ for more straightforward comparisons. We also replicated the sparsity analysis in Fig.~3 of \citet{vafaii2024pvae} in our \Cref{tab:sparsity}, using the van Hateren natural images dataset with whitened, contrast normalized $16 \times 16$ patches.

\paragraph{Alternative models.} We compare our iterative \pvae (\ipvae) to \pvae. The main difference between their two architectures is that the latter independently parameterizes an encoder, whereas the former constructs its encoder adaptively by inverting the decoder. We also compare to state-of-the-art methods that combine iterative with amortized inference. These include iterative amortized VAE (ia-VAE; \citet{marino18iterative}), and semi-amortized VAE (sa-VAE; \citet{kim2018semi}). Since ia-VAE comes with both hierarchical (h) and single-level (s) variants, we compare to each of these.

\paragraph{Number of iterations.} For \ipvae, we experimented with different numbers of training iterations, $T_\text{train}$. During training, we differentiate through the entire sequence of iterations, which can lead to qualitatively different dynamics. We report results for $T_\text{train} = 4, 16, 32, 64$. For generalization results, we use a model with $T_\text{train} = 64$. At test time, we report results using $T_\text{test} = 1{,}000$ iterations, unless stated otherwise. For semi-amortized models, we use their default number of train and test iterations found in their code, unless stated otherwise (sa-VAE: $T_\text{train} = T_\text{test} = 20$; ia-VAE: $T_\text{train} = T_\text{test} = 5$).

This holds true irrespective of the \ipvae architecture; left, \jacmlp; middle, \jacconv. In contrast, hybrid amortized/iterative models do not improve, and either remain flat or diverge (right). \textbf{(b)} \ipvae trained on MNIST generalizes to Omniglot at test time. All models in this figure were trained on MNIST, and tested either on MNIST (a), or Omniglot (b).}
    \label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Stability beyond the training regime and convergence.}
\label{sec:exp:converge}
An algorithm with strong generalization potential should learn how to perform inference that extends beyond the training regime. We evaluated this by training models on MNIST under different numbers of training iterations, $T_\text{train} = 4, 16, 32,$ and $64$. We used both \jacmlp and \jacconv architectures and then tested each model on its ability to keep improving beyond the training number of iterations. In \cref{fig:convergence}a, we show that \ipvae converges. Even with as few as 4 iterations, \ipvae learns to keep improving. We also observe that increasing the number of training iterations has an interesting effect: \ipvae trained with a larger number of iterations starts from worse performance, but converges to better solutions (\cref{fig:convergence}a). This suggests \ipvae learns dynamics that depend on the training sequence length, but generalizes beyond the training set in all cases.

In contrast, the two hybrid models (sa-VAE and ia-VAE) start with strong amortized initial guesses, but plateau rapidly (\cref{fig:convergence}a; right), and converge to a much higher MSE than \ipvae models, which have a fraction of the parameters. We also see that ia-VAE (single-level) starts to diverge outside its training regime. \footnote{It’s worth noting that in our hands, ia-VAE (s) often resulted in nans at test time upon going beyond $T_\text{train}$.}

Overall, \ipvae achieves the best reconstruction performance and continues to improve outside the training regime, unlike other models. This shows the first sign of OOD generalization in \ipvae: temporal generalization. In later sections, we test whether \ipvae can generalize OOD in vision tasks, but first, we evaluate the performance and sparsity on natural images, as in \citet{vafaii2024pvae}.

\subsection{IP-VAE closes the gap with Sparse Coding}\label{sec:exp:amortgap} One of the limitations of previous work with \pvae, was that the authors identified a large performance gap between \pvae and LCA sparse coding \citep{vafaii2024pvae}. Here, we evaluated \ipvae and compared models on their ability to reconstruct whitened natural image patches (\cref{tab:sparsity}). Unlike \pvae, \ipvae performs as well as LCA with similar sparsity levels. \pvae, and the two hybrid approaches, have many more parameters and achieve much worse performance. \footnote{The performance of ia-VAE and sa-VAE might be modestly improved by tuning the tradeoff between reconstruction and the $\mathtt{KL}$ term.}

\subsection{Out-of-distribution generalization.}\label{sec:exp:omni}
In this section, we evaluate whether MNIST-trained models generalize to OOD perturbations and datasets. First, we tested whether MNIST-trained models generalize to Omniglot (see \cref{fig:convergence}b). We found that \ipvae improves over iterations and outperforms alternative models in terms of reconstruction quality. In this section, we evaluate two levels of generalization tasks: (1) within-dataset perturbations; and, (2) across similar datasets (i.e., digits to characters).

\paragraph{OOD generalization to within-dataset perturbation.}
\label{sec:exp:rotation}
We tested whether models trained on standard MNIST generalized to rotated MNIST digits. We rotate MNIST between 0 and 180 degrees, with incremental steps of 15 degrees. We then test (a) whether models are capable of reconstructing the rotated digits, and (b) whether the representations of rotated digits can be used to classify them (\cref{fig:rot}). \ipvae and sa-VAE demonstrated consistent performance across angles, both in terms of reconstruction loss and classification accuracy. Amortized \pvae shows worse reconstruction performance than all iterative models, but its classification accuracy is remarkably consistent across angles, beating or matching all models except for \ipvae. ia-VAE variants were greatly affected by the rotation, with significant falloff in both their classification score and reconstruction. Overall, \ipvae maintains stable performance across rotations at levels above alternative models.
\paragraph{OOD generalization across similar datasets.}
\label{sec:exp:crossDS}
If a model learns compositional features, and if it employs an effective inference algorithm that leverages those features, it should be able to represent datasets that are within the same distributional vicinity as the training set. To test this, we evaluated MNIST-trained models on EMNIST and Omniglot. We report both mean squared error (MSE) of reconstruction and classification accuracy \footnote{We omit classification accuracy for Omniglot due to its large number of classes (over $1{,}000$)}.

Again, \ipvae exhibited superior reconstruction performance over other models, both visually and MSE (\cref{fig:ood}). It also had substantially higher classification accuracy, suggesting it learns a compositional code and has strong generalization potential.

\subsection{A compositional code that generalizes across domains .}
\label{sec:exp:compositional}
Using the \jacmlp variant of \ipvae, we visualized the 512 learned features of the last layer of the mlp decoder. In \cref{fig:ftrs}, we show the features learned by \ipvae trained on MNIST and contrast them to features learned by \pvae, also trained on MNIST. We see a stark contrast. \ipvae features are Gabor-like, while \pvae features look like digits or strokes of the digits. While previous work highlighted strokes as the compositional subcomponents of digits \citep{lee2007sparse}, \ipvae learns an even more general code that generalized to cropped, grey scaled natural images (\cref{fig:imgnet}).

Since both \ipvae and \pvae are spiking models, this result suggests that the difference lies in the inference algorithm: \ipvae is iterative and adaptive; whereas, \pvae is one-shot amortized.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}


In this work, we introduced the \ipvae, a spiking neural network that maximizes ELBO and performs Bayesian posterior updates via membrane potential dynamics. Empirically, \ipvae demonstrates strong adaptability, robustness to OOD samples, and the ability to dynamically trade off compute and performance. \ipvae outperforms amortized versions and recent hybrid iterative/amortized inference VAEs on every task we tested while using substantially fewer parameters.

\ipvae results directly from the choice of Poisson in the ELBO and it avoids many of the problems with predictive coding. First, there is no population-wide prediction signal, only a feedforward receptive field and recurrent terms. Second, neurons only communicate through spikes and all dynamics are private on the membrane potential. Finally, additive terms in the membrane potential appear as gains in the spike rate, which avoids negative rates and is more consistent with real neurons \citep{gilbert2013top}.

The solid theoretical foundation of \ipvae, along with the promising empirical results, position it as a strong candidate for neuromorphic implementation. With the rise of neuromorphic hardware offering performance improvements, new algorithms are needed to leverage this architecture \citep{schuman2022neuromorphic}. We found that \ipvae with a linear decoder reduces to a spiking LCA, bridging the performance gap noted by \citet{vafaii2024pvae}. Both algorithms share key features: sparsity, recurrence, and parameter efficiency. Since LCA has been implemented as an SNN \citep{zylberberg2011sparse} and on neuromorphic hardware \citep{du2024generalized}, we expect the same for \ipvae.


In summary, the choice of Poisson in the ELBO results in a spiking neural network, \ipvae, that performs iterative Bayesian inference. This lays the groundwork for a prescriptive theoretical framework for building brain-like generative models that can leverage neuromorphic hardware.


 





\paragraph{Limitations and future work.} In our experiments, we tested the simplest version of \ipvae, showing the practical benefits of the derived theory. There are a few avenues that we did not test, and we think are exciting for future work. The design of a hierarchical model is a natural extension for brain-like algorithms, especially given evidence that hierarchical VAEs are more aligned to the brain \citep{vafaii2023hierarchical}. In addition, training and evaluating on nonstationary sequences like videos would be a straightforward extension, as we derived the theory with this in mind. When attempting to use such sequences, it may also be beneficial to explore more sophisticated forward-predictive models \citep{fiquet2023a} that \say{evolve} current posteriors to future priors.
