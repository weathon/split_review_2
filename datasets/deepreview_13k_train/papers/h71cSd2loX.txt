\title{On Extending Direct Preference Optimization to Accommodate Ties}

\begin{abstract}

We derive and investigate two DPO variants that explicitly model the possibility of declaring a tie in pair-wise comparisons. We replace the Bradley-Terry model in DPO with two well-known modeling extensions, by Rao and Kupper and by Davidson, that assign probability to ties as alternatives to clear preferences. Our experiments in neural machine translation and summarization show that explicitly labeled ties can be added to the datasets for these DPO variants without  the degradation in task performance that is observed when the same tied pairs are presented to DPO. We find empirically that the inclusion of ties leads to stronger regularization with respect to the reference policy as measured by KL divergence, and we see this even for DPO in its original form. These findings motivate and enable the inclusion of tied pairs in preference optimization as opposed to  simply discarding them.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


The original formulation of DPO~\citep{DPO-Rafael} does not allow for ties.  DPO requires training data consisting of paired options, $y_w \succ y_l$, and each of these pairs should represent a clear preference in judgment with no ambiguity as to which is the winner and which is the loser.  From this data, the DPO learning procedure  encourages the underlying policy to prefer $y_w$ over $y_l$.  This formulation does not allow for any ambiguity or uncertainty in the comparison of the paired examples in the training data. 

    This certainty is not easy to achieve in practice. A common approach is simply to discard data.  
    \citet[Sec. 4.2.1]{dubey2024llama3herdmodels} apply DPO in post-training of Llama 3 models and note that for  
    ``DPO, we use samples that are labeled as the chosen response being significantly better or better than the rejected counterpart for training and discard samples with similar responses.''  Similarly, Qwen2 developers~\citep[Sec. 4.3]{yang2024qwen2technicalreport} ``sample multiple responses from the current policy model, and the reward model selects the most and the least preferred responses, forming preference pairs that are used for DPO.'' 
    Over-generation followed by aggressive selection is  effective in producing the strongly ordered judgments needed for DPO.  However the process appears wasteful: many potentially useful, and expensively collected,  preference judgments are discarded simply because they are ties. As \citet{rao1967ties} note: ``any model which does not allow for the possibility of ties is not making full use of the information contained in the no-preference class.''

Motivated by this, we investigate DPO variants that can incorporate ties.  
We replace the Bradley-Terry preference model at the heart of DPO by two well-known extensions  by \citet{rao1967ties} and by \citet{Davidson-On-Extending-Bradley-Terry} that explicitly assign probability to tied judgments alongside winners and losers. Since  these models are generalizations of the Bradley-Terry model, we find that they are readily incorporated into the DPO modeling framework.  In experiments in neural machine translation and summarization, we find that ties can be added to the datasets for these DPO variants without  the degradation in task performance that results from adding ties to the original DPO.  We also observe improved regularization, in reduced KL-divergence to the reference policy, by adding ties. 


    
    
    
    
     


\section{Methodology}



\subsection{DPO and the Bradley-Terry Preference Distribution}

The Bradley-Terry model assigns probability that an item $y_i$ will be preferred to item $y_j$ 
in terms of their `strength' parameters $\lambda$.   
In the RLHF setting, strengths are expressed as  rewards $r$, $\lambda=e^r$  ~\cite[Eq. 1]{DPO-Rafael},
so that the preference distribution for item $i$ over item $j$  depends on the  difference in their rewards, $d_{ij} = r_i - r_j$  
\begin{equation}
    p^{BT}(y_i\succ y_j) = \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i + \lambda_j} = \frac{e^{r_i}}{e^{r_i} + e^{r_j}} = \sigma(r_i - r_j) = \sigma(d_{ij})
\label{eq:bt}
\end{equation}

One of the enabling observations made by \citet{DPO-Rafael} is that when a policy $\pi_\theta$ is sought to maximize the KL-regularized objective 
$    \max_{\pi_\theta}  \mathbb{E} ~ [ r(x,y) ] - 
    \beta ~ D(\pi_\theta(y|x) ~ || ~ \pi_{ref}(y|x))
    \label{eq:dpo_obj}
    $, 
the reward associated with the policy has the form  
$
    r_\theta(x,y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y|x)} + \beta\log Z_\theta(x)
    \label{eq:optrisk}
$.
This allows expressing the difference in rewards between hypotheses $y_w$ and $y_l$ under a parameterized policy $\pi_\theta$ as the {reward margin}
\begin{equation}
                    d(y_w, y_l; \theta) = r_\theta(x,y_w) - r_\theta(x,y_l) = 
                    \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)}
                \label{eq:rewarddiff}
            \end{equation}
so that  the corresponding Bradley-Terry probability that item $y_w$ beats item $y_l$ is 
\begin{equation}
 p^{BT}_\theta(y_w \succ_x y_l) = \sigma(d(y_w, y_l; \theta)) =
 \sigma( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)}  ).
\end{equation}  
The DPO policy objective~\cite[Eq. 7]{DPO-Rafael} follows by incorporating the parameterised form of the preference distribution into a maximum likelihood objective
\begin{align}
    \mathcal L_{DPO}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{ref}) &=  - \mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l} \log p_\theta(y_w \succ_x y_l)
    \label{eq:dponll} \\ &= - \mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l} \log \sigma( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)})
    \label{eq:dpoloss}
\end{align}
We note that Eq.~\ref{eq:rewarddiff} follows from the regularized risk optimization~\citep[A.1]{DPO-Rafael}.  It does  not rely on any assumption that  limits its use to the Bradley-Terry model. 

\subsection{Bradley-Terry Extensions that Accommodate Ties}
\label{ssec:bte}
An observed weakness of the Bradley-Terry model is that it does not allow for ties.    Unless two items have exactly the same strengths (so that $d_{ij}=0$), the model always assigns a higher probability of winning to the stronger item.  This may be reasonable if one item is much stronger than the other, but when items are relatively comparable it may be desirable to allow some probability for tied outcomes.   


The Rao-Kupper~\citep{rao1967ties} model assigns win and tie probabilities as:
\begin{align}
    p^{RK}(y_i\succ y_j) & =  \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i + \nu_{RK} \lambda_j} 
    && \mbox{item $y_i$ beats item $y_j$}     \label{eq:rkwin} \\
    p^{RK}(y_i\sim y_j) & =  \frac{(\nu_{RK}^2-1) \lambda_i \lambda_j}{(\lambda_i + \nu_{RK} \lambda_j)(\lambda_j + \nu_{RK} \lambda_i)}
    && \mbox{items $y_i$ and $y_j$ tie}
    \label{eq:rktie}
\end{align}
while the 
Davidson~\citep{Davidson-On-Extending-Bradley-Terry} model assigns win and tie probabilities as:
\begin{align}
    p^{D}(y_i\succ y_j) & =  \frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i + \lambda_j + 2 \nu_D \sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}}  
    && \mbox{ item $y_i$ beats item $y_j$} \label{eq:davidsonwin}\\
    p^{D}(y_i\sim y_j) & =  \frac{ 2 \nu_D \sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}}{\lambda_i + \lambda_j + 2 \nu_D  \sqrt{\lambda_i\lambda_j}}  
    && \mbox{ items $y_i$ and $y_j$ tie}
\label{eq:davidsontie}
\end{align}

The probabilities of the three outcomes sum to one for both of these Bradley-Terry extensions: $p(y_i \succ y_j) + p(y_j \succ y_i) + p(y_i \sim y_j)  = 1$.
For both models, $p(y_i \sim y_j) = p(y_j \sim y_i)$ and $p(y_i \sim y_j)$ 
tends towards 0 if $\lambda_j \gg \lambda_i$.
Both variants have parameters $\nu$ that control how much probability is allocated to ties.  
Apart from $\nu_{RK} = 1$ or $\nu_D=0$, when both variants agree with Bradley-Terry, some probability is reserved for tied outcomes.    







The Rao-Kupper and Davidson models arise from different considerations.   
\citet{rao1967ties} begin with the formulation 
$
p^{BT}(y_i \succ y_j) = \frac{1}{4} \int_{-(r_i-r_j)}^\infty \mbox{sech}^2(y/2) dy
$ \cite[Eq.~13]{bradley_statistical_1953} and note its sensitivity to the  difference in values $r_i-r_j$. 
They   
 note that  some judges ``may not be able to express any real preference''  in paired-comparisons   if their ``sense of perception is not sharp enough'' to detect small differences.  They  
reason that a ``threshold of sensory perception'' is needed such that if the observed difference is less than the threshold, a  judge declares a tie.  They introduce the sensitivity threshold $\alpha_{RK}$ as follows,    
$
p^{RK}(y_i \succ y_j) = \frac{1}{4} \int_{-(r_i-r_j)+\alpha_{RK}}^\infty \mbox{sech}^2(y/2) dy
$, and 
Eqs.~\ref{eq:rkwin} and~\ref{eq:rktie} follow for 
$\nu_{RK} = e^{\alpha_{RK}}$.

\citet{Davidson-On-Extending-Bradley-Terry} starts from Luce's ``choice axiom'' \citep{luce1959individual} which states  that a complete system of choice probabilities should satisfy  ${p(y_i\succ y_j)}/{p(y_j\succ y_i)} = {\lambda_i}/{\lambda_j}$, which the Rao-Kupper model fails to do.  \citet{Davidson-On-Extending-Bradley-Terry} observes that it is desirable for the probability of a tie to ``be proportional to the geometric mean of the probabilities of preference''.  Adding this requirement $p(y_i\sim y_j) \propto \sqrt{p(y_i \succ y_y)p(y_j\succ y_i)}$ to the choice axioms yields   Eqs.~\ref{eq:davidsonwin} and~\ref{eq:davidsontie} as a preference model that allows for ties and also satisfies the choice axiom.  
\begin{align}
    p_\theta^{RK}(y_w \succ_x y_l) & =  \sigma(d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l) - \alpha_{RK}) 
\label{eq:rkwinpprob}\\
    p_\theta^{RK}(y_w \sim_x y_l) & = (\nu_{RK}^2-1) \, \sigma(-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l) - \alpha_{RK}) \, \sigma(d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l) - \alpha_{RK})  \notag    
 \\
    & =  (\nu_{RK}^2-1) \, \sigma(-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l) - \alpha_{RK}) \, p_\theta^{RK}(y_w \succ_x y_l)
    \label{eq:rktiepprob}
\end{align}
and the Davidson win and tie probabilities can be written as
\begin{align}
    p^{D}_\theta(y_w\succ_x y_l) &= \frac{ 1 }{ 1 + e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)} + 2 \nu_D e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)/2}} \label{eq:dwinpprob} \\ 
    p^{D}_\theta(y_w\sim_x y_l) 
    &= 2\, \nu_D\, e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)/2}\, p_\theta^{D}(y_w\succ_x y_l) 
    \label{eq:dtiepprob}
\end{align}
~\ref{fig:probability analytical}).   For pairs $(x,y_w, y_l)$ treated as wins, higher likelihood is assigned for  higher values of the reward margin $d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)$.  For the Rao-Kupper this is particularly clear, in that the Bradley-Terry preference distribution is simply shifted by $\alpha_{RK}$.  Conversely, 
for pairs $(x,y_w, y_l)$ treated as ties, the probability of declaring a tie is high for small reward margins $d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)$.  

\paragraph{Balancing Wins and Ties.}
In the special case of two evenly matched players ($\lambda_i = \lambda_j$), we are interested in the probability of a tie $p(y_i\sim y_j)$ versus a clear win by either player, $p(y_i\succ y_j)+p(y_j\succ y_i)$. It follows that $P_{RK}(\mbox{tie}) = \frac{\nu_{RK}-1}{2}P_{RK}(\mbox{no tie}) $ and $P_D(\mbox{tie}) = \nu_{D} P_D(\mbox{no tie})$. This shows that the parameters $\nu$ determine the probability that equally-matched items are judged as tied or not. 
$\nu$ can be tuned, but in our work, we assume that equally-matched items will tie with a probability of $1/2$ and so we set $\nu_{RK}=3$ and $\nu_{D}=1$. 
\subsection{Incorporating Rao-Kupper and Davidson Models into DPO}

We extend the DPO policy objective (Eq. \ref{eq:dponll})  to include a binary flag $t$ to indicate a tie:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal L(\pi_\theta; \pi_{ref}) =  
    - \mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l,t=0} \log p_\theta(y_w \succ_x y_l)
    - \mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l,t=1} \log p_\theta(y_w \sim_x y_l) 
    \label{eq:extended_obj_with_tie}
\end{equation}
where  $p_\theta(y_w \succ y_l) $  and $p_\theta(y_w \sim y_l)$ are taken  from either the Rao-Kupper model (Eqs.~\ref{eq:rkwinpprob},~\ref{eq:rktiepprob} or the Davidson model (Eqs.~\ref{eq:dwinpprob}, ~\ref{eq:dtiepprob}).  Note that in  Eq.~\ref{eq:extended_obj_with_tie} preference pairs in the dataset are unambiguously either wins ($t=0$) or ties ($t=1$).
The policy objectives for these two DPO variants are:
\begin{align}
&\mathcal{L}_{RK}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{ref}) = - 
\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l,t=0} \Bigl[ \log \sigma(d_\theta(x, y_w, y_l) - \alpha_{RK}) \Bigr]  \\
&- 
\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l,t=1} \Bigl[ \log \sigma(-d_\theta(x, y_w,y_l) - \alpha_{RK}) + \log \sigma(d_\theta(x, y_w, y_l) - \alpha_{RK}) - \log(\nu_{RK}^2-1)    \Bigr] \notag
\label{eq:dpork_loss}
\end{align}
and
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}_{D}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{ref}) = &- 
\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l,t=0} \Bigl[ \log \frac{ 1 }{ 1 + e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)} + 2 \nu_D e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)/2}} \Bigr] \notag \\
&- 
\mathbb{E}_{x,y_w,y_l,t=1} \Bigl[ \log \frac{ 2 \nu_D e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)/2} }{ 1 + e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)} + 2 \nu_D e^{-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)/2}} \Bigr] 
\end{align}
We refer to these DPO variants as DPO-RK and DPO-D.  Like DPO, these  objectives depend on the policy $\pi_\theta$ through the reward margin $d(x, y_w, y_l; \theta)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:rewarddiff}).    
Unlike DPO, the training objective Eq.~\ref{eq:extended_obj_with_tie} consists of two competing terms.
For pairs $(x, y_w, y_l)$ labeled as wins the objective is to find $\pi_\theta$ to increase the reward margin $d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)$.  However, for pairs labeled as ties
the objective is to find $\pi_\theta$ to minimize  $|d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)|$.  To simultaneously achieve both these objectives, the underlying policy should learn to model both wins and ties.
\subsubsection{DPO-RK and DPO-D Updates}

\citet{DPO-Rafael} show that DPO dynamically adjusts the gradient according to how well the preference objective is optimized for each sample 
\begin{align}
    \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta^{BT}(y_w \succ_x y_l) = - \beta\underbrace{\sigma(-d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l))}_{\substack{\text{higher weight when reward} \\ \text{estimate is wrong}}} \,
    \nabla_\theta \log  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}
\end{align}
 We define the gradient scale factors $\Delta_{win}$ and $\Delta_{tie}$ to illustrate the DPO-RK and DPO-D gradient updates on wins and ties:
\begin{align}
    \nabla \log p_\theta^{RK}(y_w \succ_x y_l) & =  \underbrace{\sigma( \alpha - d_\theta(x, y_w, y_l) )}_{  \Delta^{RK}_{win}(d_\theta)}
    \nabla_\theta \log  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}
    \label{eq:rkgrad_preference}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
   \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta^{RK}(y_w \sim_x y_l)  = \bigl[ \underbrace{\sigma(\alpha -d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)) - \sigma(\alpha+d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l))}_{\Delta^{RK}_{tie}(d_\theta)} \bigr]
\nabla_\theta \log  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}
\label{eq:rkgrad_tie}
\end{align}
\begin{align}
    \nabla_\theta \log p^{D}_\theta(y_w\succ_x y_l)  &= \underbrace{\frac{  e^{-d_\theta} + \nu e^{-d_\theta/2} }{  1+e^{-d_\theta}+ 2\nu e^{-d_\theta/2}}}_{  \Delta_{win}^{D}(d_\theta) }
    \nabla_\theta \log  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}
    \label{eq:dgrad_preference}
    \\
    \nabla_\theta \log p^{D}_\theta(y_w\sim_x y_l)  &= \underbrace{\Bigl[  \Delta_{win}^{D}(d_\theta) - \frac{1}{2} \bigr]}_{ \Delta_{tie}^{D}(d_\theta)} 
    \nabla_\theta \log  \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}
    \label{eq:dgrad_tie}
\end{align}
\paragraph{$\nabla \log p_\theta(y_w \succ_x y_l)$:} For data labeled as wins, the DPO-RK gradient scale factor has same form as DPO, but shifted by $\alpha_{RK}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:graident scale factor analytical}).  DPO-D has a symmetric scale factor that is not as steep as DPO-RK.  All three methods work to increase the reward margin  $d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)$.
\paragraph{$\nabla \log p_\theta(y_w \sim_x y_l)$:} For data labeled as ties, the DPO-D and DPO-RK gradient scale factors are odd and work to drive $d_\theta(x,y_w,y_l)$ towards zero, although the DPO-RK scale factor is more aggressive. This is a mechanism not present in DPO.  



\subsubsection{Rao-Kupper and Davidson Classifiers}
\label{sssec:classifiers}
The above DPO variants yield probability distributions  $p_\theta(y_w \succ_x y_l)$ and $p_\theta(y_w \sim_x y_l)$ in terms of the policy $\pi_\theta$ and the reference model $\pi_{ref}$. 
We can use these distributions as classifiers to label a pair $(x, y_1, y_2)$ as either a win ($y_1 \succ_x y_2$ or $y_2 \succ_x y_1$) or a tie ($y_1 \sim_x y_2$), whichever has the highest probability under either the Rao-Kupper or the Davidson model (Eqs.~\ref{eq:rkwinpprob},~\ref{eq:rktiepprob}, or ~\ref{eq:dwinpprob},~\ref{eq:dtiepprob}). We will evaluate  classification performance on held-out data not used in training to see if policies produced by our DPO variants learn to distinguish wins from ties.



\section{Experiments}


\subsection{Adding Ties to DPO}
\label{sec:adding ties}
DPO in its original formulation relies on a static dataset of comparisons $\mathcal{D} = \{x^{(i)}, y_w^{(i)}, y_l^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ where $y_w^{(i)}$ and $y_l^{(i)}$ are  preferred and dispreferred responses to a prompt $x^{(i)}$~\citep{DPO-Rafael}.  These preferences are assumed to be sampled from some latent reward model and we refer to this dataset as {\bf Clear Preference Pairs} ({\bf CPs}, for short) because they are typically selected to reflect a clear preference between winner and loser as assessed either by human judges or by some trusted automatic metric.   We distinguish these Clear Preference Pairs from 
{\bf Tied Pairs} ({\bf TPs)}.  Tied Pairs also consist of a winner and a loser,  but are very similar in quality.   Human judges might be less consistent, or have less confidence,  in selecting the winner in a tied pair, and automatic metrics will assign more similar quality scores to Tied Pairs than to Clear Preference Pairs.
As noted,  DPO datasets typically are constructed to include only Clear Preference Pairs.  We will extend the data selection procedures to generate Tied Pairs along with Clear Preference Pairs so that we can investigate how DPO  changes when Tied Pairs are included in the training data. 
We report experiments on Neural Machine Translation (NMT) and Summarization.


\paragraph{Clear Preference Pairs and Tied Pairs in NMT.} We use DPO to improve translation quality similar to that done in \citet{bruce-dpo-mbr}.  
We apply DPO with BLOOMZ-mt-7b~\citep{Bloomz-model-paper} as the baseline model.
To construct a DPO preference dataset for the WMT21 ZH-EN test set,   we use BLOOMZ-mt-7b to generate 32 translations (via sampling) for each source sentence in the WMT20 ZH-EN test set. For each source sentence, the translations are ranked by their BLEURT scores computed with respect to the reference translations.  The highest and lowest scoring translations form the Clear Preference Pairs; for each source sentence, these are the two translations with the greatest difference in BLEURT score.  By contrast, we take the Tied Pairs as the two non-identical translations with the minimum absolute BLEURT difference; the translation with higher BLEURT is labeled as the winner of each Tied Pair. This yields ca. 16K CPs and TPs for use in DPO.   The same procedure is applied to the IWSLT17 validation set, yielding ca. 800 CPs and TPs for use as DPO preference datasets. 

\paragraph{Clear Preference Pairs and Tied Pairs in Summarization.} 
We follow \citet{ODPO-Direct-Preference-Optimization-with-an-offset-Amini} in DPO fine-tuning of Pythia-2.8B~\citep{Pythia-model-paper} on the TL;DR dataset~\citep{TLDR-dataset-OpenAI} with evaluation via win-rate against human-written summaries. Previous works use GPT-4 to compute the win-rate~\citep{DPO-Rafael, ODPO-DirectPreferenceOptimizationWithAnOffset}. 
In the TL;DR task, each prompt is associated with a collection of paired summaries, with a winner and loser identified for each pair.  There is no immediately obvious way to distinguish tied pairs from clear preference pairs in the collection and so we use DPO itself to select tied pairs.   We first apply DPO with $\beta=0.1$ on the full TL;DR training dataset. Using the reward model formed by this model and the reference model, we compute the reward margins of all pairs of summaries in the training split. 
For each prompt, the pair with minimal reward margin is treated as a tied pair, with all other pairs kept as clear preference pairs, yielding ca. 14k (15.3\%) TPs. 
 

\subsubsection{Task Performance vs. KL to the Reference Policy}
\label{ss:tpvkl}

For each of the three tasks we form two training sets: CP, which contains the Clear Preference Pairs; and CP+TP, which contains both the Clear Preference Pairs and the Tied Pairs.  We refer to DPO training on these sets as DPO(CP) and DPO(CP+TP) (Figure~\ref{fig:DPO(CP) vs DPO(CP+TP) KL-Performance frontier}).

The obvious conclusion from these experiments is that including tied pairs in  DPO  is not good for task performance.  All best performing systems are obtained by DPO(CP), with DPO(CP+TP) underperforming for nearly all values of KL relative to the reference policy.  This performance degradation from including ties is consistent with common practice in the DPO literature which only keeps pairs with clear preference, filtering others to obtain the best-performing system~\citep{yang2024qwen2technicalreport, dubey2024llama3herdmodels}.  Consistent with this,  the TL;DR results show that removing tied pairs from the DPO dataset leads to improved summarization performance, even when ties are identified by a DPO model in an unsupervised manner. These results also suggest that tied pairs in the DPO datasets can enhance regularization.  By this we mean that including tied pairs causes DPO to find models that are closer to the reference policy as measured by KL divergence.   The overall effect of the reduced task performance and more regularization is to shift the frontier `down and to the left'.
Theorem 3.1 of~\cite{PL-paper-chen} suggests how these regularization effects might arise
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi^*(y_l|x)} = \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}(\frac{\gamma(x,y_w,y_l)}{1-\gamma(x, y_w, y_l)})^{1/\beta}
    \label{eq:ideal DPO}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma(x, y_w, y_l)$ is the true preference probability of $y_w \succ y_l$ under prompt $x$. 
If we assume that tied pairs have a true preference probability $\gamma(x, y_w, y_l)$ of 0.5,  from Equation~\ref{eq:ideal DPO} we have 
}.  By this analysis, the ideal DPO model should maintain the same chosen/rejected likelihood ratio as the reference model on tied pairs, and this constraint serves as a form of regularization. 
In our NMT experiments (Figures \ref{fig:WMT21 - DPO(CP) vs DPO(CP+TP) KL-Performance frontier}, \ref{fig:IWSLT17 - DPO(CP) vs DPO(CP+TP) KL-Performance frontier}), where half of the pairs are constructed to be ties, the regularization effect is especially pronounced 
as the DPO model should keep to the reference model likelihood ratio on 50\% of the training data. Regularization  is less pronounced on TL;DR (Figure \ref{fig:TLDR - DPO(CP) vs DPO(CP+TP) KL-Performance frontier}) where only 1/8 of the pairs are ties. Furthermore, Eq \ref{eq:ideal DPO} can be rearranged as follows: 

\begin{equation}
    d^*_\theta(x, y_w, y_l)=\beta\Big(\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)}-\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)}\Big) = \beta \log \frac{\gamma(x,y_w,y_l)}{1-\gamma(x, y_w, y_l)}
    \label{eq:ideal DPO target margin}
\end{equation}

From this it follows that the reward margin on tied pairs should ideally be close to zero, which we verify experimentally in the next section.

\subsection{Adding Ties to DPO-RK and DPO-D}
In the previous section we investigated the effects of including tied preference pairs in DPO datasets.   
Using the same data we now evaluate DPO-RK and DPO-D as DPO variants that  explicitly model both ties and clear preferences.  
We use the DPO datasets CP+TP (Sec. \ref{ssec:bte}) with the DPO-D and DPO-RK algorithms to produce models DPO-D(CP+TP) and DPO-RK(CP+TP).   
We follow the protocols of Sec.~\ref{sec:adding ties} so that results are directly comparable to earlier DPO(CP) and DPO(CP+TP) results. For all experiments we set $\nu^{RK}=3$ and $\nu^D=1$ for DPO-RK and DPO-D (as described in Sec.~\ref{ssec:bte}). 
\subsubsection{Task Performance vs. KL to the Reference Policy}
When tied pairs are added to the dataset, DPO-D and DPO-RK do not suffer the same drops in task performance that DPO exhibits (Fig.~\ref{fig:rkdavidon-pareto}, orange and purple vs. green).  DPO-RK(CP+TP) and DPO-D(CP+TP)  reach similar levels of task performance to each other, and to DPO(CP), but do so at smaller KL values than DPO (Fig.~\ref{fig:rkdavidon-pareto}, orange and purple vs. blue).  These are the regularization effects of including tie pairs in the DPO datasets reported in Section~\ref{sec:adding ties}, but without decrease in task performance.  For a given level of KL to reference policy, DPO-D(CP+TP) and DPO-RK(DP+TP) yield higher task performance than DPO(CP). 
Compared to DPO as it is usually done, DPO-RK and DPO-D frontiers are shifted leftwards, showing similar task performance but stronger regularization.

\subsubsection{Preference Pair Classification Accuracy}
\label{sec:adding ties - Preference Pair Classification Accuracy}
We assess the performance of the Rao Kupper and Davidson classifiers introduced in Sec.\ref{sssec:classifiers} in terms of their ability to label preference pairs as either clear preferences or ties. 
Ideally, classification performance will improve: (1) as tied pairs are added to the clear preference data sets (CP vs CP+TP); and (2) with margins generated from models produced by DPO variants that emphasize the distinction between  tied pairs and clear preference pairs (DPO-D(CP+TP), DPO-RK(CP+TP)).     

We assess classifier performance on the held-out set 
created by collecting CPs and TPs from the WMT18 ZH-EN test set as was done for WMT20 ZH-EN (Sec.\ref{sec:adding ties}); this yields pairs with gold labels as either clear preference pairs or tied pairs. Classification and assessment proceeds as follows:  we generate reward margins for the WMT18 ZH-EN pairs using DPO(CP), DPO(CP+TP), DPO-RK(CP+TP), DPO-D(CP+TP) models; we use these reward margins to label the unseen pairs using the Davidson and Rao-Kupper classification rules (Sec.~\ref{sssec:classifiers}); and finally compute the classification accuracy relative to the gold  labels.  

Results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:RK-based and D-based accuracy for DPO, DPO-RK, DPO-D}.  
We find that smaller beta in training consistently leads to better overall RK-classification accuracy (+10\% overall Acc. from $\beta=1.0$ to $\beta=0.1$), suggesting heavy regularization with respect to the reference model impedes preference ranking. 
Classifiers based on reward margins generated from DPO(CP) models perform well in identifying clear preference pairs ($\text{Acc.}> 85\%$) but poorly in identifying tied pairs ($\text{Acc.}< 35\%$).  This imbalance is likely explained by the DPO(CP) model never having seen tied pairs in training.  Adding TPs to the DPO datasets (DPO(CP+TP)) significantly improves the classification accuracy of tied pairs ($+30\%$) with more balanced classification accuracies for CPs and TPs.   The best overall classification accuracies ($\approx73\%$) are obtained with reward margins generated by models trained to match its classifier.   Across all beta values, DPO-RK(CP+TP) and DPO-D(CP+TP) achieve better overall accuracy and more-balanced CP accuracy and TP accuracy under their respective decision rules.

\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:related work}
\paragraph{Variants of Direct Preference Optimization} A range of variants of Direct Preference Optimization have been proposed based on problem-specific or theoretical motivations. \citet{DisentanglingLengthDPO} tackle excessive response length by introducing explicit length normalization in the DPO objective. SimPO~\citep{SimPO} modifies the DPO objective to remove the need for a reference model and to include length normalization. KTO~\citep{KTOpaper} is motivated by Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory and learns from non-paired preference data. ODPO~\citep{ODPO-Direct-Preference-Optimization-with-an-offset-Amini} incorporates preference strength in the objective by introducing an offset parameter. In deriving ODPO, the offset parameter of \citet[Theorem 3]{ODPO-DirectPreferenceOptimizationWithAnOffset})  plays a role similar to 
 the sensitivity threshold of \citet{rao1967ties}. To our knowledge, our work is the first to consider accommodating tied pairs in DPO. We note that the ODPO objective with a fixed offset agrees with our proposed DPO-RK objective restricted to clear preference data, but does not extend to ties. 

\paragraph{Frameworks for Pair-Wise Preference Optimization} Several works propose theoretical frameworks for understanding general Preference Optimization from which DPO can be obtained as a special case. \citet{IPO-AGeneralTheoreticalParadigmtoUnderstandLearningfromHumanPreferences} introduces the $\Psi$PO formalism which allows alternative expression of the reward in terms of the model's predicted probability. IPO is derived when the identity mapping is used, and DPO arises under a log-ratio mapping. \citet{DensityEstimationPerspectiveForPreferenceOptimization} formulate learning from pair-wise preference as learning the implicit preference generating distribution of the annotators. In this formalism, DPO is a well-specified model for the implicit preference distribution assuming that the human preference generative process follows the Bradley-Terry model. Our work can be viewed as assuming an annotator preference generating distribution that allows for the outcome of a tie (i.e. the Rao-Kupper or the Davidson model).
\citet{GPO-GeneralizedPreferenceOptimization-AUnifiedApproachtoOfflineAlignment} propose a generalized approach to deriving offline preference optimization losses through binary classification. In this work, we consider the ternary classification with the possibility of declaring a tie.

\paragraph{Pair-wise Comparison Models}  \citet{TheManyRoutesToTheUbiquitousbradleyterrymodel} review the history and the range of motivations for the Bradley-Terry model, including its relation to the logistic distribution~\citep{BradleyTerryandLogisiticDistribution}, and the Luce choice axiom~\cite{LuceChoice}. The Rao-Kupper~\citep{rao1967ties} and the Davidson model~\citep{David-TheMethodOfPairedComparisons} are two notable extensions to Bradley-Terry (Sec.~\ref{ssec:bte}). 
We point interested readers to a review by \citet{David-TheMethodOfPairedComparisons}  and a bibliography by \citet{A-Bibliography-on-the-Method-of-Paired-Comparisons}. Modeling ties remains an active research topic in fields such as sport team ranking~\citep{ImprovePageRankSport} and  medical treatments~\citep{TieMedicalRecord}.



\section{Conclusion}

We have derived and investigated two tie-compatible DPO variants, DPO-RK and DPO-D, by replacing the Bradley-Terry preference model with the Rao-Kupper model and the Davidson model, respectively. 
 Our experiments on translation and summarization show that by explicitly modeling the probability of declaring a tie, DPO-RK and DPO-D can accommodate tied pairs in preference data without the degradation in task performance that is observed when the same tied pairs are added to the original DPO. 
 We find empirically that the inclusion of ties in preference learning leads to stronger regularization with respect to the reference model as measured by KL divergence, gives better-behaved reward margin distribution on held-out sets and improves the trained policy's overall accuracy in classifying clear preference and tied pairs. 
 These findings alongside with the proposed DPO variants motivate and enable the use of tied pairs in available preference data as opposed to wastefully discarding them.
