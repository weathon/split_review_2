\title{Log-Concave Sampling on Compact Supports: A Versatile Proximal Framework}

\begin{abstract}
 In this paper, we explore sampling from strongly log-concave distributions defined on convex and compact supports. 
We propose a general proximal framework that involves projecting onto the constrained set, which is highly flexible and supports various projection options. 
Specifically, we consider the cases of Euclidean and Gauge projections, with the latter having the advantage of being performed efficiently using a membership oracle. 
This framework can be seamlessly integrated with multiple sampling methods. 
Our analysis focuses on Langevin-type sampling algorithms within the context of constrained sampling. 
We provide nonasymptotic upper bounds on the $\wsone$ and $\wstwo$ errors, offering a detailed comparison of the performance of these methods in constrained sampling.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Sampling from probability distributions plays a critical role in various fields of science and engineering, especially when dealing with convex and compact sets~\cite{andrieu2003introduction,gelman1995bayesian,stuart2010inverse}. In this context, the problem involves sampling from a probability measure $\nu$ on such sets, characterized by its density function
\eqn{
\nu(x)=\frac{e^{-U^{}(x)}}{\int_{\rp} e^{-U^{}(s)}\rmd s}\,,
}
Here, $U(x) = f(x) + \ell_{\mathcal{K}}(x)$, where $f(x)$ represents a potential function and $\ell_{\mathcal{K}}(x)$ is an indicator function ensuring $x$ lies within the convex and compact set $\mathcal{K}\subset \rp$. Specifically, $\ell_{\mathcal{K}}(x)$ takes the form
\eqn{
\ell_{\K}:=
\begin{cases}
+\infty & \text{ if } x\notin\K\\
 0& \text{ if } x\in\K\,.
\end{cases}
}
Solving this constrained sampling problem is challenging and has garnered considerable interest across various fields, including computer science and statistics. 
In the realm of computer science, a line of research initiated by~\cite{dyer1991random} explored polynomial-time algorithms for uniformly sampling convex bodies. 
This has been followed by seminal studies on the convergence properties of the Ball Walk and the Hit-and-Run algorithm toward uniform density on a convex body or, more broadly, to log-concave densities~\cite{kannan1997random,kook2024and,lovasz1999hit,lovasz1993random,lovasz2007geometry,smith1984efficient}. Other Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, such as Gibbs sampling~\cite{gelfand1992bayesian} and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo~\cite{brubaker2012family,gurbuzbalaban2022penalized,kook2022sampling}, have also been adapted and enhanced to sample from distributions defined on convex and compact sets.

In recent years, leveraging optimization techniques to facilitate sampling has become a prevalent approach. By formulating the sampling challenge as an optimization problem, methods like projected stochastic gradient descent~\cite{bubeck2015finite,bubeck2018sampling,lamperski2021projected,lehec2023langevin}, proximal approaches~\cite{brosse2017sampling,durmus2018efficient,salim2020primal}, particle-based algorithms~\cite{li2022sampling}, and mirror descent~\cite{ahn2021efficient,chewi2020exponential,hsieh2018mirrored,zhang2020wasserstein} have proven effective in navigating the target distribution to generate samples. 
Further innovations have emerged from the intersection of deep learning and neural networks, leading to the development of novel sampling techniques via generative adversarial networks~\cite{goodfellow2014generative} and variational autoencoders~\cite{kingma2013auto}. 
These advanced methodologies offer promising pathways for sampling from intricate and high-dimensional distributions, particularly those defined on convex and compact sets~\cite{ortiz2022structured}.

In this work, we tackle the challenge posed by a non-smooth target density $\nu$ by employing a proximal method.
This method leverages a regularization technique that involves projecting onto set $\K$, effectively transforming the constrained sampling problem into an unconstrained one. 
Our framework is versatile, accommodating various projection options, such as the Euclidean projection—which corresponds to the Moreau envelope of the indicator function $\ell_{\mathcal{K}}$~\cite{rockafellar2009variational,durmus2018efficient,brosse2017sampling,pereyra2016proximal}—and the Gauge projection~\cite{lu2022projection,mhammedi2022efficient}, which can be efficiently performed using a membership oracle.
Specifically, we introduce a smooth and strongly convex surrogate distribution that closely mimics the target density. 
By fine-tuning the regularization parameter, we can significantly reduce approximation errors. 
The advantageous properties of our surrogate distribution enable the effective application of various sampling techniques.
In this work, we specifically explore the vanilla Langevin Monte Carlo~\cite{RobertsTweedie96,Dalalyan14,durmus2017,erdogdu2021convergence,mousavi2023towards,raginsky2017non,Erdogdu18,mou2022improved,erdogdu2022convergence}, kinetic Langevin Monte Carlo~\cite{cheng2018underdamped,dalalyan_riou_2018,shen2019randomized,ma2019there,zhang2023improved}, and the parallelized versions of the midpoint randomization method for these algorithms~\cite{yu2024parallelized} within the context of constrained sampling. 
We derive the convergence rates for these algorithms in Wasserstein-1 and Wasserstein-2 distances, and provide a detailed comparison of their performance.
To this end, we make the following contributions.
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0.1em}
\item We establish both general upper and lower bounds for the distance between the smooth approximation of the target density and its original form in Wasserstein-$q$ distance for any $q \geqslant 1$. These bounds are detailed in Proposition~\ref{prop:w2_tight} and Proposition~\ref{prop:lb}, respectively.
   \item In Section~\ref{sec:approx_target}, we demonstrate that our proposed framework can seamlessly incorporate various projection options, including both Euclidean and Gauge projections.
    \item In Section~\ref{sec:comp}, we incorporate several MCMC sampling methods, such as (kinetic) Langevin Monte Carlo and the parallelized midpoint randomization method for these algorithms, into our general framework. We present a detailed convergence analysis of these methods in both Wasserstein-1 and Wasserstein-2 distances.
\end{itemize}

In summary, we develop a comprehensive framework specifically designed for sampling from convex and compact sets, utilizing a regularization technique that involves projecting onto the constrained sets. 
This framework is exceptionally flexible and adaptable, compatible with various projection operators and sampling methods.
Additionally, it facilitates a lucid comparison of the behaviors of sampling methods when deployed in constrained sampling scenarios.
Notably, our study presents an improved error bound for Langevin Monte Carlo applied to constrained sampling settings, as well as the first convergence analysis of kinetic Langevin Monte Carlo and the parallelized midpoint randomization method for (kinetic) Langevin Monte Carlo within the context of constrained sampling. 
This analysis offers new insights into the dynamics of constrained sampling.

 
 \noindent \textbf{Notation.}
Denote the $p$-dimensional Euclidean space by $\rp$.
The letter $\btheta$ denotes the deterministic vector and its calligraphic counterpart 
$\bvartheta$ denotes the random vector.
We use $\bfI_p$ and $\mathbf 0_p$ to denote, respectively, the $p \times p$ identity and zero matrices.  
Define the relations $\bfA
\preccurlyeq\bfB$ and $\bfB\succcurlyeq \bfA$ 
for two symmetric $p \times p$ matrices $\bfA$ 
and $\bfB$ to mean that $\bfB - \bfA$ is 
positive semi-definite. 
The gradient and the Hessian of a function $f:\rp \rightarrow \R$ are denoted by $\nabla f$  and 
 $\nabla^2 f$, respectively.
Given any pair of measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ defined on $(\rp,\mathcal{B}(\rp))$,
the Wasserstein-$q$ distance between $\mu$ and $\nu$ is defined as
\eqn{
\wass_q(\mu,\nu) = \Big(\inf_{\varrho\in \Gamma(\mu,\nu)} \int_{\rp\times \rp}
\normtwo{\btheta -\btheta'}^q\,\rmd\varrho(\btheta,\btheta')\Big)^{1/q}, ~~~q\geqslant 1\,,
}
where the infimum is taken over all joint distributions $\varrho$ that have $\mu$ and $\nu$ as marginals. 
For a set $\K\subset\rp,$ we use $\K^c$ to denote its complement.
The ceiling function maps $x\in\R$ to the smallest integer greater than or equal to $x$, denoted by $\ceil{x}.$



\section{Smooth Approximation for the Target Density}

\label{sec:approx_target}
The absence of smoothness in the target distribution $\nu$ presents significant challenges because sampling algorithms often depend on the smoothness properties of the target distribution to effectively explore the space and produce representative samples.
Motivated by this, we consider the approximation for $\ell_{\K}$ of the form
\eqn{
\ell_{\K}^\lambda(\btheta) := \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\,\normtwo{\btheta-\proj_{\K}(\btheta)}^2\,,
}
where $\lambda>0$ is the tuning parameter and $\proj_{\K}:\rp\to\K$ denotes a projection operator which projects the vector $x\in\rp$ onto the set $\K$.
Define $U^\lambda(\btheta):=f(\btheta)+\ell_{\K}^\lambda(\btheta),$
and we the define the corresponding surrogate target density $\nu^\lambda$ 
\eq{
\label{eq:surrogate}
\nu^\lambda(\btheta)=\frac{e^{-U^{\lambda}(\btheta)}}{\int_{\rp} e^{-U^{\lambda}(\btheta')}\rmd \btheta'}\,.
}
Throughout the paper, we define $\mu_k(\nu) = \int\normtwo{\btheta}^k\,\nu(\rmd \btheta)$ as the $k$-th moment of the  distribution~$\nu$ ($ k\geqslant 1$), and we assume the convex and compact set $\K$ satisfy the following assumption.
\begin{assumption}\label{asm:radius}
Given a positive constant $r\in(0,\infty)$, we assume the Euclidean ball centered at the origin with radius $r$, denoted by $\ball_2(r)$, is contained in $\K$
\eqn{
\ball_2(r)=\{\btheta\in\rp: \normtwo{\btheta}\leqslant r\}\subset \mathcal{K}
\,.}
\end{assumption}
This assumption has been commonly made in the work of constrained sampling~\cite{lamperski2021projected,bubeck2018sampling,brosse2017sampling,gurbuzbalaban2022penalized}.
Moreover, we assume that the potential function $f$ satisfies the following assumption.
\begin{assumption}\label{asm:f}
   The function $f: \mathbb R^p\to\mathbb R$ 
   is continuously differentiable, and its Hessian 
   matrix $\nabla^2 f$ satisfies 
\eqn{
    m\bfI_p\preccurlyeq \nabla^2 f(\btheta)
    \preccurlyeq M\bfI_p,\qquad \forall 
    \btheta\in\mathbb R^p.
}
Moreover, we assume the function $f$ is lower bounded and $\mathbf{0}=\argmin_{\btheta\in\K} f(\btheta).$
\end{assumption}
\noindent The assumption that $f$ attains the minimum at the origin simplifies the presentation of our results.
All our results will still hold even if this condition is not met. Given the minimizer of the function $f$ over $\K,$ denoted by $\btheta_*$, we simply need to shift all the coordinates and consider the constrained set $\K+\btheta_*$. 
Additionally, we note that the approximation $U^\lambda$ inherits the strong convexity of $f.$
Moreover, we require $U^\lambda$ to be integrable, continuously differentiable, and smooth, even if $U$ is not.
\begin{condition}
\label{asm:Ulambda}
The function $U^{\lambda}$ is $m$-strongly convex, continuously differentiable, and 
$M^{\lambda}$-smooth.
 Moreover, it holds that
$\int_{\rp} e^{-U^{\lambda}(\btheta)}\rmd \btheta<\infty$.
\end{condition}
Below, we present two examples of the approximation $\ell_{\mathcal{K}}^{\lambda}$, each utilizing a different, commonly used projection operator.
We will demonstrate that these examples effectively approximate the indicator function $\ell_{\mathcal{K}}$ and the resulting potential functions fulfill Condition~\ref{asm:Ulambda}.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:euclidean}
[Moreau envelope]
One example of a projection operator is the Euclidean operator, which results in the Moreau envelope of the indicator function $\ell_{\K}$
\eqn{
\ell_{\K}^{E,\lambda}(\btheta) 
= 
\inf_{\btheta'\in\rp} \big(\ell_{\K}(\btheta')+ \frac{1}{2\lambda^2} \,\normtwo{\btheta-\btheta'}^2 \big)
=
\frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\,\normtwo{\btheta-\proj^E_{\K}(\btheta)}^2\,,
}
where $\proj_{\K}^E$ denotes the Euclidean projection on $\K$. 
\end{example}
\noindent Define the corresponding surrogate potential $U^{E,\lambda}(\btheta):=f(\btheta)+\ell_{\K}^{E,\lambda}(\btheta).$
This surrogate potential $U^{E,\lambda}$ satisfies the following property.
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:eproj}
Assume Assumptions~\ref{asm:radius} and~\ref{asm:f} hold, 
the potential 
$U^{E,\lambda}$ satisfies Condition~\ref{asm:Ulambda} with $M^{\lambda}=1/\lambda^2+M$. 
\end{lemma}
\begin{example}
\label{ex:gauge}
[Gauge projection]
Another example of the projection operator is the Gauge projection $\proj_{\K}^G:\rp\to\R$, which is defined as
\eqn{
\proj_{\K}^G(\btheta):=\frac{\btheta}{g_{\K}(\btheta)}
}
with a variation of Gauge function (also known as the Minkowski function) $g_{\K}:\rp\to\R$ of set $\K$
\eq{
\label{eq:gauge_fnc}
g_{\K}(\btheta):=\inf\{t\geqslant 1: \btheta\in t\K\}\,.
}
This function encapsulates the scaling required to project $\btheta$ onto the set $\K.$
\end{example}
\noindent
Set 
$
\ell_{\K}^{G,\lambda}(\btheta) 
= 
\frac{1}{2\lambda^2}\,\normtwo{\btheta-\proj^G_{\K}(\btheta)}^2\,.
$
The corresponding surrogate function is defined via $U^{G,\lambda}(\btheta):=f(\btheta)+\ell_{\K}^{G,\lambda}(\btheta).$
This surrogate potential $U^{G,\lambda}$ satisfies the following property.
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:gproj}
Assume Assumptions~\ref{asm:radius} and~\ref{asm:f} hold, %
the surrogate potential
$U^{G,\lambda}$ satisfies Condition~\ref{asm:Ulambda} with $M^{\lambda}=1/\lambda^2+M$. 
\end{lemma}

In the following, we aim to measure the discrepancy between the surrogate distribution and the target distribution. 
To this end, we establish an upper bound in $\wass_q$ distance between the target distribution $\nu$ and the approximated distribution $\nu^{\lambda}$ defined in~\eqref{eq:surrogate}.
Our aim is to demonstrate that as $\lambda$ approaches zero, the distance between these two distributions converges to zero.
\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:w2_tight}
Under Assumption~\ref{asm:radius}, assume that the potential function $f$ is convex with $\textbf{0}$ as its minimizer. 
Then, for any $q\geqslant 1$ and any $\lambda\in\big(0,\frac{r}{2(p+q)}\big),$ it holds that
\eqn{
\wsq^q(\nu,\nu^{\lambda}) \leqslant  3 \mu_q(\nu) {
    \lambda}\, (2p+q)/r\,, 
}
where $\mu_q(\nu) = \int
\normtwo{\btheta}^q\,\nu(\rmd \btheta),q\geqslant 1.$
\end{proposition}
\noindent In this proposition, we provide a general and precise quantification of the distance between the approximate distribution~$\nu^\lambda$ to the target distribution $\nu$ in $\wsq$ distance for any $q\geqslant 1.$
When $q=1,$ our result aligns with the the bound established for $\wsone(\nu,\nu^\lambda)$ as stated in~\cite[Proposition 5]{brosse2017sampling}.

We also derive the lower bound for the distance between the approximate distribution~$\nu^\lambda$ to the target distribution $\nu$ in $\wsq$ distance, specifically for the scenario where $\mathcal{K} = \mathcal{B}_2(r)$ and the projections discussed in Examples~\ref{ex:euclidean} and~\ref{ex:gauge}. 
In this case, the Gauge projection and Euclidean projection coincide, yielding the following results.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:lb}
Let $\K=\ball_2(r)$ and assume the potential function $f$ is convex and 
$M$-smooth, with $\mathbf{0}$ as its minimizer and $f(\mathbf{0})=0.$ 
Set $\ell_{\K}=\ell_{\K}^{E,\lambda}.$
Under Assumptions~\ref{asm:radius}, for any $q\geqslant 1$ and any $\lambda\in\big(0,\frac{r}{2(p+q)}\wedge \frac{1}{\sqrt{M}} \big),$ it holds that
\eqn{
\wsq^q(\nu,\nu^{\lambda}) \geqslant 
    \mu_q(\nu)\min\bigg\{ \Big|\Big(\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}e^{-\frac{3}{4}Mr^2}\frac{\lambda(p+q)}{r+3\lambda p}+\frac{r}{r+3\lambda p}\Big)^{1/q} -1\Big|^q,  \Big|\Big(\frac{3\lambda(p+q)}{r}+1\Big)^{1/q}-1\Big|^q \bigg\} \,. 
}
\end{proposition}

Before we discuss the results of the lower bound, we present the following corollary.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:lb}
Under the assumptions stated in Proposition~\ref{prop:lb}, when $\lambda =o(r/p)$ and $ q=1$, it holds that
\eqn{
\wass_1(\nu,\nu^{\lambda}) \geqslant 
   C_{M,r} \mu_1(\nu) \frac{p+1}{r}\lambda\,,
}
where $C_{M,r}>0$ is a constant that depends on $M$ and $r$ exponentially.
\end{corollary}
Based on the results outlined in the preceding corollary, when $q=1$ and with a sufficiently small $\lambda,$ the lower bound aligns with the rate estimates from Proposition~\ref{prop:w2_tight} up to a constant factor, thereby confirming the optimality of the upper bound for the case of $q=1.$
However, when $q>1$ and $\lambda$ is small, the lower bound follows the order $\big((1+\lambda)^{1/q}-1\big)^q$, which increases at a rate slower than $\lambda.$  
This indicates the suboptimality\footnote{We conjecture that this suboptimality arises from the general technique used in our proof. With specific assumptions about the density, a refined lower bound for the Wasserstein distance between 
$\nu$ and $\nu^\lambda$.} of the rate stated in Proposition~\ref{prop:w2_tight}, highlighting a difference in behavior for higher values of $q.$

Owing to the desirable properties of the surrogate density $\nu^\lambda$, we can utilize various sampling methods that are effective for distributions with smooth and strongly convex densities.

In this section, we explore Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) and its variant, Kinetic Langevin Monte Carlo (KLMC), along with the parallelized randomized midpoint method for both algorithms, denoted as pRLMC and pRKLMC, respectively, to sample from the density $\nu^\lambda$.

\subsection{Langevin Monte Carlo}
\label{sec:lmc}
Let $\bvartheta_0$ be a random vector drawn from a 
distribution $\nu^{\lambda}$ on $\mathbb R^p$ and let $\bW 
=(\bW_t: t\geqslant 0)$ be a $p$-dimensional 
Brownian motion that is independent of $\bvartheta_0$. 
To sample from the approximation distribution $\nu^{\lambda}$, we consider the vanilla Langevin diffusion, which is a strong solution to the stochastic differential equation
\eq{
\label{eq:LD}
    \rmd\bL_t^{\sf LD} = -\nabla U^{\lambda}(\bL_t^{\sf LD})\, \rmd t 
    + \sqrt{2}\,\rmd\bW_t,
    \qquad t\geqslant 0,\qquad \bL^{\sf LD}_0=\bvartheta_0.
}
This equation has a unique strong solution, which 
is a continuous-time Markov process, termed Langevin 
diffusion. 
Under the  further assumptions on the potential~$U^{\lambda}$, 
such as strong convexity, the 
Langevin diffusion is ergodic, geometrically
mixing and has $\nu^\lambda$ as its unique invariant 
distribution~\cite{bhattacharya1978}. 
Moreover, we can sample from the distribution defined by $\nu^\lambda$ 
by using a suitable discretization of the Langevin 
diffusion. 
LMC algorithm is 
based on this idea, combining the considerations with the Euler discretization. 
Specifically, for small values of 
$h \geqslant 0$ and $\Delta_h\bW_t = \bW_{t+h} - 
\bW_t$, the following approximation holds
\eqn{
    \bL^{\sf LD}_{t+h} & = \bL^{\sf LD}_t- \int_0^h  
    \nabla U^{\lambda}(\bL_{t+s}^{\sf LD})\,\rmd s + \sqrt{2}\;\Delta_h\bW_t \approx \bL_t^{\sf LD} - h  
    \nabla U^{\lambda}(\bL_t^{\sf LD}) + \sqrt{2}\;\Delta_{h}
    \bW_t. 
}
By repeatedly applying this approximation with a small 
step-size $h$, we can construct a Markov chain 
$(\bvartheta^{\sf LMC}_k:k\in\mathbb N)$ that converges 
to the target distribution $\nu^\lambda$ as $h$ goes to zero. 
More precisely, $\bvartheta^{\sf LMC}_k\approx \bL^{
\sf LD}_{kh}$, for $k\in\mathbb N$, is given by
\eqn{
    \bvartheta_{k+1}^{\sf LMC} = \bvartheta_k^{\sf LMC} 
    -h\nabla U^{\lambda}(\bvartheta_k^{\sf LMC})
    +\sqrt{2}\,(\bW_{(k+1)h}-\bW_{kh}).
}
We now provide explicit upper bounds for the error of the LMC algorithm in terms of $\wsone$ and $\wstwo$ distances within the context of constrained sampling.
\begin{theorem}\label{thm:lmc}
Under Assumptions~\ref{asm:radius} and~\ref{asm:f}, we further assume that the potential $U^{\lambda}$ satisfies Condition~\ref{asm:Ulambda}.
 Let the step size $h\in(0, 1/M^\lambda)$ and the tuning parameter $\lambda\in \big(0,   \frac{r}{2(p+2)} \big)$. 
Then, for every $n\geqslant 1$, the distribution $\nu_n^{\sf LMC}$ of~$\bvartheta_n^{\sf LMC}$ satisfies 
\eqn{
  \wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf LMC}, \nu) &\leqslant  
 e^{-\frac{mnh}{2}}\wstwo(\nu_0^{\sf LMC},\nu)+2\sqrt{ \frac{3\mu_2(\nu)\lambda(2p+2)}{r}}
 + \sqrt{\frac{2phM^\lambda}{m}}\,.
}
Moreover, when the initial distribution $\nu_0^{\sf LMC}$ is set to be the Dirac measure at the minimizer of $f,$ it holds that   
\eqn{
\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf LMC}, \nu) &\leqslant    e^{-\frac{mnh}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{p}{m}}
+ \frac{3\mu_1(\nu)\lambda(2p+1)}{r}
+ \sqrt{\frac{2phM^\lambda}{m}}    \,.
}
\end{theorem}
The term $M^\lambda$ introduces an additional factor of $\lambda$ into the convergence rate. 
To clarify this dependence on $\lambda$, we specify in the following corollary that $M^{\lambda}=M+1/\lambda^2$. This specification corresponds to the specific projection operators used in Example~\ref{ex:euclidean} and Example~\ref{ex:gauge}. We then optimize $\lambda$ to obtain the results presented below.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:lmc}
 Let $\varepsilon \in(0,1)$ be a small number, and $M^{\lambda}=M+\frac{1}{\lambda^2}.$ \\
\noindent \textbf{(a)} Set $ \lambda = \sqrt{2/3} (ph/m)^{1/4}\sqrt{r/(\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1))}, $ choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
    \eqn{
   h= 2^{-10}3^{-2}(p/m) r^2 \big(\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1)\big)^{-2} \varepsilon^4
   \quad \text{and} \quad
        n \geqslant \frac{2}{mh}\log(2/\varepsilon)\,,
   }
    then 
    we have $\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf LMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$. 

\noindent \textbf{(b)}  Set $\lambda=2^{-2/3}3^{-1/3}(r h)^{1/3}(\mu_2(\nu)m)^{-1/3}$, choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
    \eqn{
    h=2^{-20}3^{-2}r^2(\mu_2(\nu)m)^{-2}\varepsilon^6 \quad
    \text{and} 
    \quad
        n\geqslant \frac{2}{mh}\log(2/\varepsilon)\,,
    }
    then 
    we have $\wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf LMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$.

   
 
\end{corollary}

According to this corollary, when evaluating the error in 
$\wsone$ distance, the required sample size $n$ to achieve a prespecified error level is of order $\tilde\bigo(\varepsilon^{-4}).$ This represents an improvement over the rate of $\tilde\bigo(\varepsilon^{-6})$ obtained in~\cite{brosse2017sampling}.
Additionally, our general framework provides the convergence rate in $\wstwo$ for the proximal LMC algorithm, thus complementing the study of the proximal LMC presented in~\cite{brosse2017sampling}.


\subsection{Parallelized Randomized Midpoint Discretization of Vanilla Langevin Diffusion}

As an alternative to the Euler discretization method discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lmc} for the stochastic differential equation~\eqref{eq:LD}, we explore another discretization framework known as the randomized midpoint method, introduced in~\cite{shen2019randomized}. 
This method enables the discretization and simulation of the Langevin diffusion~\eqref{eq:LD}, ultimately converging to the distribution $\nu^{\lambda}$.
Building upon the foundations laid by~\cite{shen2019randomized,yu2023langevin}, \cite{yu2024parallelized} developed a parallel computing scheme for this algorithm, significantly enhancing the efficiency of the sampling process.
The formal definition of  the 
parallelized randomized midpoint method for the Langevin Monte 
Carlo (pRLMC) is defined in Algorithm~\ref{algo:pRLMC}. For simplicity, the superscript $\sf pRLMC$ is omitted therein. 
\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\small
\caption{Parallelized RLMC (pRLMC)}\label{algo:pRLMC}
\noindent\textbf{Input}: number of parallel steps $R$, number of sequential iterations $Q$, step size $h$, number of iterations $K$, initial point~$\bvartheta_0.$\\
\textbf{Output}: iterate $\bvartheta^{}_{K+1}$
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{\texttt{$k=1$ to $K$}}
   \State Draw $U_{kr}$ uniformly from $\big[\frac{r-1}{R},\frac{r}{R}\big]$, $r=1,\dots,R$.
   \State Generate $\bxi_{kr}=\bW_{(k+U_{kr})h}-\bW_{kh}, r=1,\dots,R.$
   \State Set $\bvartheta_{k}^{(0,r)}=\bvartheta_k, r=1,\dots,R$.
   \For{\texttt{$q=1$~to~$Q-1$}}
    \For{\texttt{$r=1$ to $R$} in parallel} \hspace{4cm} $\tikzmark{listing-4-end}$
    \State
    $a_{kj}= \min\{\frac{1}{R},U_{kr}-\frac{j-1}{R}\},j=1,
    \dots,r$ 
     \State $\bvartheta_{k}^{(q,r)}=\bvartheta_k-h\sum_{j=1}^r a_{kj} \grad U^{\lambda}\big(\bvartheta_{k}^{(q-1,j)}\big) 
     + \sqrt{2}\bxi_{kr}$.
     \EndFor $\tikzmark{listing-7-end}$
    \EndFor		
    \State  $\bvartheta_{k+1}=\bvartheta_k -\frac{h}{R}\sum_{r=1}^R \grad U^{\lambda}\big(\bvartheta_{k}^{(Q-1,r)}\big)
    +\sqrt{2}(\bW_{(k+1)h}-\bW_{kh})$.
\EndFor	
\end{algorithmic}
\AddNote{listing-4-end}{listing-7-end}{listing-4-end}{In parallel}
\end{algorithm}
We now introduce the primary findings in the work, which establish explicit upper bounds for the error associated with the pRLMC algorithm, measured in $\wsone$ and $\wstwo$ distances, specifically within the framework of constrained sampling.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:rlmc}
Under Assumptions~\ref{asm:radius} and~\ref{asm:f}, we further assume that the potential~$U^{\lambda}$ satisfies Condition~\ref{asm:Ulambda}.
 Let the the tuning parameter $\lambda\in \big(0,  \frac{r}{2(p+2)} \big)$.
 Choose the step size $h$ such that $M^\lambda h \leqslant 1/10.$
Then, for every $n\geqslant 1$, the distribution $\nu_n^{\sf pRLMC}$ of~$\bvartheta_n^{\sf pRLMC}$ satisfies
\eqn{
  \wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf pRLMC}, \nu) &\leqslant  
\Big(1+\sqrt{\kappa M^\lambda h}\big(0.82(M^\lambda h)^{Q-1} + 
			0.94 M^\lambda h /R\big)\Big) e^{-mnh/2} \wass_2(\nu_0^{\sf pRLMC},\nu)  \\ 
      &\qquad  + \sqrt{\kappa M^\lambda h}\big(3.98(M^\lambda h)^{Q-1} 
			+ 6.91M^\lambda h/\sqrt{R}\big)\sqrt{p/m}\\
   &\qquad \Big(2+\sqrt{\kappa M^\lambda h}\big(0.82(M^\lambda h)^{Q-1} + 
			0.94 M^\lambda h /R\big)\Big)\sqrt{3\mu_2(\nu)\lambda (2p+2)/r}\,,
}
where $\kappa = M^\lambda/m$. 
Moreover, when the initial distribution $\nu_0^{\sf pRLMC}$ is set to be the Dirac measure at the minimizer of $f,$ it holds that   
\eqn{
\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf pRLMC}, \nu) &\leqslant
\Big(1+\sqrt{\kappa M^\lambda h}\big(0.82(M^\lambda h)^{Q-1} + 0.94 M^\lambda h /R\big)\Big) e^{-mnh/2} \sqrt{p/m} \\ 
 &\qquad  + \sqrt{\kappa M^\lambda h}\big(3.98(M^\lambda h)^{Q-1} + 6.91M^\lambda h/\sqrt{R}\big)\sqrt{p/m}\\
   &\qquad + 3\mu_1(\nu)\lambda (2p+1)/r.
}

\end{theorem}
To our knowledge, this represents the first convergence rate for the pRLMC algorithm within the context of constrained sampling.
When $R=1$, these results recover the convergence rate for the randomized midpoint method applied to Langevin Monte Carlo algorithm (RLMC)~\cite{he2020ergodicity,yu2023langevin,yu2024parallelized} in constrained sampling.
Below, we define $M^{\lambda}=M+1/\lambda^2$, a setting that corresponds to the projection choices outlined in Examples~\ref{ex:euclidean} and~\ref{ex:gauge}. 
We then optimize $\lambda$ to derive the following corollary.
\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:rlmc}
 Given the number of parallel steps~$R,$ we set $Q= \ceil{\log R}+1.$
  Let $\varepsilon \in(0,1)$ be a small number, and  $M^{\lambda}=M+\frac{1}{\lambda^2}.$ \\
  \noindent\textbf{(a)}
  Set 
     $\lambda=(\frac{2^4\cdot 7}{3})^{1/5}(\frac{p}{R})^{1/10}(\frac{r}{m\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1)})^{1/5}h^{3/10},$
     choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
    \eqn{
   h= 15.6^{-10/3}p^{2/5}m^{-1}\Big(\frac{r}{\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1)}\Big)^{8/3}R^{1/3}\varepsilon^{10/3}
   \quad \text{and} \quad
        n \geqslant \frac{2}{mh}\log\Big(\frac{2.22}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,,
   }
    then 
    we have $\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf pRLMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$.

   \noindent\textbf{(b)}
$\lambda=320^{2/9}3^{-1/3} \big(\frac{rp}{m^2\mu_2(\nu)(2p+2)R}\big)^{1/9}h^{1/3}$, 
choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
 \eqn{
 h=13^{-6}m^{-7/3}\Big(\frac{rp}{\mu_2(\nu)(2p+2)}\Big)^{8/3}R^{1/3}\varepsilon^{6} \quad
    \text{and} 
    \quad
        n\geqslant \frac{2}{mh}\log\Big(\frac{2.22}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,,
   }
    then 
    we have $\wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf pRLMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$.

\end{corollary}
Comparing this convergence rate with that obtained for proximal LMC in Corollary~\ref{cor:lmc}, we observe superior performance of pRLMC over LMC when $R>1$. %
When assessing the error in 
$\wstwo$ distance for the case of $R=1$, the sample complexity $\tilde\bigo(\epsilon^{-6})$ aligns with that of LMC. 
This outcome results from our specific choice of $\lambda$ during the optimization of the upper bound concerning $\lambda$, placing it within a region where the performance of LMC is comparable to that of RLMC.
\subsection{Kinetic Langevin Monte Carlo}
In this part, we explore the application of kinetic Langevin diffusion in constrained sampling.
Recall that the kinetic Langevin process $\bL^{\textup{\sf KLD}}$
is a solution to a second-order stochastic
differential equation that can be informally 
written as
\eq{\label{KLD:1}
{\textstyle\frac1{\gamma}}\ddot\bL_t^{\sf KLD} + 
  \dot\bL_t^{\sf KLD} = -\nabla U^{\lambda}(\bL_t^{\sf KLD}) 
  + \sqrt{2}\,\dot\bW_t,
}
with initial conditions $\bL_0^{\sf KLD} = 
\bvartheta_0$ and $\dot\bL_0^{\sf KLD} = \bv_0$. 
In \eqref{KLD:1}, $\gamma>0$, $\bW$ is a standard
$p$-dimensional Brownian motion and dots are used
to designate derivatives with respect to time $t
\geqslant 0$. This can be formalized using It\^o's
calculus and introducing the velocity field 
$\bV^{\sf KLD}$ so that the joint process 
$(\bL^{\sf KLD}, \bV^{\sf KLD})$ satisfies
\eq{\label{KLD:2}
    \rmd\bL^{\sf KLD}_t = \bV_t^{\sf KLD}\,\rmd t;
    \quad  
    \tfrac1{\gamma}\rmd\bV^{\sf KLD}_t = -\big(\bV_t^{\sf KLD} + 
    \nabla U^{\lambda}(\bL_t^{\sf KLD})\big)\,\rmd t + 
    \sqrt{2}\, \rmd \bW_t.
}
Similar to the vanilla Langevin diffusion \eqref{eq:LD}, 
the kinetic Langevin diffusion $(\bL^{\sf KLD}, \bV^{\sf
KLD})$ is a Markov process that exhibits ergodic 
properties when the potential $U^{\lambda}$ is strongly 
convex (see \cite{eberle2019} and references 
therein). The invariant density of this process 
is given by
\eqn{
    p_*(\btheta_,\bv) \propto \exp\{-U^{\lambda}(\btheta) - {\textstyle\frac1{2\gamma}}\|\bv\|^2\}, \qquad
    \text{for all}\quad \btheta,\bv\in\mathbb R^p.
}
Note that the marginal of $p_*$ corresponds to 
$\btheta$ coincides with the target density $\nu^\lambda$. 
The kinetic Langevin Monte Carlo (KLMC) algorithm is a discretized version of
KLD equation~\eqref{KLD:2}, where the term $\nabla U^{\lambda}(\bL_t)$ 
is replaced by $\nabla U^{\lambda}(\bL_{kh})$ on each interval 
$[kh, (k+1)h)$.
The resulting error bounds are given in the following theorem.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:klmc}
Under Assumptions~\ref{asm:radius} and~\ref{asm:f}, we further assume that the potential $U^{\lambda}$ satisfies Condition~\ref{asm:Ulambda}.
 Let the tuning parameter $\lambda\in \big(0,  \frac{r}{2(p+2)} \big)$. 
Choose
$\gamma$ and $h$ so that $\gamma \geqslant 5M^\lambda$
and  $\sqrt{\kappa}\,\gamma h \leqslant 0.1$, 
where $\kappa = M^\lambda/m$. Assume that $\bvartheta_0^{\sf KLMC}$ 
is independent of $\bv_0^{\sf KLMC}$ and that $\bv_0^{\sf KLMC}\sim
\mathcal N_p(0, \gamma\bfI_p)$. Then, for any
$n \geqslant 1$, the distribution $\nu_n^{\textup{ 
\sf KLMC}}$ of $\bvartheta_n^{\textup{\sf KLMC}}$
satisfies
\eqn{
  \wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf KLMC}, \nu) &\leqslant 2\varrho^n \wstwo(\nu_0^{\sf KLMC}, \nu) 
  + 3\sqrt{\frac{3\mu_2(\nu)
  \lambda(2p+2)}{r}}
   + 0.05\sqrt{\frac{\varrho^n\E[U^{\lambda}(\bvartheta_0^{\sf KLMC})-f(\mathbf{0})]}{m}}
  + 0.9\gamma h\sqrt{\frac{\kappa p}{m}}\,,
}
where $\varrho=e^{-mh}$.
{Moreover, let the staring points $\bvartheta_0^{\sf KLMC}=\mathbf{0}$, it holds that   
\eqn{
\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf KLMC}, \nu) &\leqslant    2\varrho^{n}\sqrt{\frac{p}{m}
}+ \frac{3\mu_1(\nu)\lambda(2p+1)}{r}
  + 0.9\gamma h\sqrt{\frac{\kappa p}{m}}  \,.
}
}
\end{theorem}
To our knowledge, these results are the first reported convergence rate for the KLMC algorithm within the context of constrained sampling. 
Below, we set $M^{\lambda}=M+1/\lambda^2$, aligning with the projection methods detailed in Example~\ref{ex:euclidean} and Example~\ref{ex:gauge}. We proceed by optimizing $\lambda$ to establish the subsequent corollary.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:klmc}
Let $\varepsilon \in(0,1)$ be a small number. 
 Set  $\gamma =5M^{\lambda}, M^{\lambda}=M+\frac{1}{\lambda^2}$.\\
 \noindent \textbf{(a)} Set $ \lambda = 
     3^{3/4}2^{1/8}r^{1/4}\big(\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1)\big)^{-1/4}p^{1/8}m^{-1/4}h^{1/4}
     ,$ choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
    \eqn{
   h= 2^{-7/2}r^3\big(\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1)\big)^{-3}p^{3/2}m^{-1}\varepsilon^4
   \quad \text{and} \quad
        n \geqslant \frac{1}{mh}\log(4/\varepsilon)\,,
    }
    then 
    we have $\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf KLMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$.

\noindent\textbf{(b)}
     Set $\lambda=2^{1/7}3^{3/7}(r/\mu_2(\nu)) ^{1/7}(h/m)^{2/7}$, choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
    \eqn{
    h=32.26^{-7}m^{-5/2} r^3 \mu_2(\nu)^{-3}\varepsilon^7 \quad
    \text{and} 
    \quad
        n\geqslant \frac{1}{mh}\log(4/\varepsilon)\,,
  }
    then 
    we have $\wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf KLMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$.

\end{corollary}

We note that our error bounds depend in part on the synchronous coupling between the KLMC and the KLD. However, for the vanilla Langevin algorithm,~\cite{durmus2019analysis} have shown that the dependency of the error bound on $\kappa$ can be improved by employing alternative couplings. We propose that similar improvements might be achievable for the KLMC algorithm in constrained sampling scenarios using non-synchronous coupling. For further insights, interested readers are directed to~\cite{yu2023langevin}.

\subsection{
Parallelized Randomized Midpoint Discretization of Kinetic
Langevin Diffusion}
The randomized midpoint method, introduced and 
studied in~\cite{shen2019randomized}, aims at providing
a discretization of the kinetic Langevin process~\eqref{KLD:1}
that reduces the bias of sampling as compared to
more conventional discretizations. 
The parallel computing of this algorithm is outlined in~\cite{yu2024parallelized}. 
This algorithm is referred to as pRKLMC, and for the convenience of the readers, we restate it in Algorithm~\ref{RKLMCp}. 
To ease the notation, we omit the superscript $\sf pRKLMC$ 
therein.
\begin{algorithm}[ht]
\small
\caption{Parallelized  RKLMC (pRKLMC)}\label{RKLMCp}
\noindent\textbf{Input}: number of parallel steps $R$, 
number of sequential iterations $Q$, step size $h$, 
friction coefficient~$\gamma$, number of iterations $K$, 
initial points~$\bv_0$ and $\bvartheta_0.$\\
\textbf{Output}: iterates $\bvartheta^{}_{K+1}$ and 
$\bv_{K+1}$
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\For{\texttt{$k=1$ to $K$}}
   \State Draw $U_{k1},\dots,U_{kR}$ uniformly from 
		$\big[0,\frac{1}{R}\big],\dots,\big[\frac{R-1}{R}, 
		1\big],$ respectively.
   \State Generate $\bar{\bW}_{s}=\bW_{kh+s}-\bW_{kh}$
   \State Generate  $\bxi_{kr}=\int_0^{U_{kr}h}\big(1 
		- e^{-\gamma(U_{kr}h-s)}\big)d\bar{\bW}_{s},r=1,\dots,R$. 
   \State Set $\bvartheta_{k}^{(0,r)}=\bvartheta_k, r=1,\dots,R$.
   \For{\texttt{$q=1$~to~$Q-1$}}
   \For{\texttt{$r=1$ to $R$} in parallel} \hspace{5cm} $\tikzmark{listing-4-end}$
    \State
    $a_{kr}=\frac{1-e^{-\gamma hU_{kr}}}{\gamma}.$
    \State
    $b_{kj}=\int_{\frac{(j-1)h}{R}}^{h\min(\frac{j}{R},U_{kr})} (1-e^{-\gamma(U_{kr}h-s)})ds, j=1,\dots,r.$
     \State  $\bvartheta_{k}^{(q,r)}=\bvartheta_k
     + a_{kr}\bv_k-\sum_{j=1}^r b_{kj}\grad U^{\lambda}\big(\bvartheta_{k}^{(q-1,j)}\big) + \sqrt{2}\bxi_{kr}.$
     
     \EndFor $\tikzmark{listing-7-end}$
    \EndFor		
    \State  $\bvartheta_{k+1}=\bvartheta_k 
    +\frac{1-e^{-\gamma h}}{\gamma}\bv_k
    -\sum_{r=1}^R  \frac{h}{R}(1-e^{-\gamma h(1-U_{kr})})
		\grad U^{\lambda}\big(\bvartheta_{k}^{(Q-1,r)}\big)
    +\sqrt{2}\int_0^h \big(1-e^{-\gamma(h-s)}\big)d\bar{\bW}_s$.
    \State  $\bv_{k+1}=e^{-\gamma h}\bv_k- \gamma\sum_{r=1}^R
		\frac{h}{R}e^{-\gamma h(1-U_{kr})}\grad U^{\lambda}\big(\bvartheta_{k}^{(Q-1,r)}\big)
    +\sqrt{2}\gamma\int_0^h e^{-\gamma(h-s)}d\bar{\bW}_s$.
\EndFor	
\end{algorithmic}
\AddNote{listing-4-end}{listing-7-end}{listing-4-end}{In parallel}
\end{algorithm}
\normalsize
In the theorem below, we quantify the error bounds for this algorithm when applied to constrained sampling.
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:rklmc}
Under Assumptions~\ref{asm:radius} and~\ref{asm:f}, we further assume that the potential $U^{\lambda}$ satisfies Condition~\ref{asm:Ulambda}.
 Let the tuning parameter $\lambda\in \big(0,  \frac{r}{2(p+2)} \big)$. 
Choose
$\gamma$ and $h$ so that $\gamma \geqslant 5M^\lambda$ 
and  $\gamma h \leqslant 0.1\kappa^{-1/6}$, 
where $\kappa = M^\lambda/m$. 
Assume that $\bvartheta_0^{\sf pRKLMC}$ 
is independent of $\bv_0^{\sf pRKLMC}$ and that $\bv_0^{\sf pRKLMC}\sim
\mathcal N_p(0, \gamma\bfI_p)$. 
Then, for any
$n \geqslant 1$, the distribution $\nu_n^{\textup{ 
\sf pRKLMC}}$ of $\bvartheta_n^{\textup{\sf pRKLMC}}$
satisfies
\eqn{
  \wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf pRKLMC}, \nu) &\leqslant 1.8\varrho^n \wstwo(\nu_0^{\sf pRKLMC}, \nu) 
  + 2.8\sqrt{\frac{3\mu_2(\nu)
  \lambda(2p+2)}{r}}
+0.28\sqrt{\frac{\varrho^n\E[U^\lambda(\bvartheta_0^{\sf pRKLMC})-f(\mathbf{0})]}{m}}\\
   &\qquad + 44.78\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma M^\lambda h^2)^3}{R^2}+ (\gamma M^\lambda h^2)^{2Q-1}} \sqrt{\frac{\kappa p}{m}}\,,
}
where $\varrho=e^{-mh}$.
Moreover, let the staring points $\bvartheta_0^{\sf pRKLMC}=\mathbf{0}$, it holds that 
\eqn{
\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf pRKLMC}, \nu) &\leqslant     
1.8\varrho^n \sqrt{\frac{p}{m}} 
  + \frac{3\mu_1(\nu)
  \lambda(2p+1)}{r} 
  + 44.78\sqrt{\frac{(\gamma M^\lambda h^2)^3}{R^2}+ (\gamma M^\lambda h^2)^{2Q-1}} \sqrt{\frac{\kappa p}{m}} \,.
}
\end{theorem}
To our knowledge, this represents the first convergence analysis for the pRKLMC algorithm within the context of constrained sampling.
When $R=1,$ we recover the convergence rate for RKLMC~\cite{shen2019randomized,yu2023langevin,yu2024parallelized} when applied in the context of constrained sampling. 
We define  $M^\lambda =M+1/\lambda^2,$ in line with the projection options described in Examples~\ref{ex:euclidean} and~\ref{ex:gauge}. We then select the optimal $\lambda$ to derive the following corollary.
\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:prklmc}
 Given the number of parallel steps~$R,$ set $Q= \ceil{\log R}+2.$
 Let $\varepsilon \in(0,1)$ be a small number, $\gamma =5M^{\lambda}$ with $M^{\lambda}=M+1/\lambda^2$.\\
 \noindent \textbf{(a)}
  Set 
  $\lambda=(\frac{7\cdot 8011}{3})^{1/8} (\frac{rh^3}{R\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1)})^{1/8}(\frac{p}{m^2})^{1/16}$,
     choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
   \eqn{
   h= 23.6^{-8/3}p^{7/6} m^{-1} r^{7/3}\big(\mu_1(\nu)(2p+1)\big)^{-7/3}R^{1/3}\varepsilon^{8/3}
   \quad \text{and} \quad
        n \geqslant \frac{1}{mh}\log\Big(\frac{3.6}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,,
   }
    then 
    we have $\wsone(\nu_n^{\sf pRKLMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$.
    
\noindent \textbf{(b)}
Set 
 $\lambda=(\frac{14\cdot 8011}{5})^{2/15} (\frac{rp}{m^2\mu_2(\nu)(2p+2)})^{1/15}(\frac{h^3}{R})^{2/15}$,
 choose $h>0$ and $n\in\mathbb N$ so that 
 \eqn{
h=20.9^{-5}p^{7/3}m^{-13/6}r^{7/3}\big(\mu_2(\nu)(2p+2)\big)^{-7/3}R^{1/3}\varepsilon^5 \quad
    \text{and} 
    \quad
        n\geqslant \frac{1}{mh}\log\Big(\frac{3.6}{\varepsilon}\Big)\,,
   }
    then 
    we have $\wstwo(\nu_n^{\sf pRKLMC}, 
    \nu) \leqslant \varepsilon\sqrt{p/m}$.

\end{corollary}
The corollary demonstrates that pRKLMC achieves the best performance in terms of sample complexity compared to the other three algorithms considered in this work.



\section{Discussion}

\noindent\textbf{Strongly convexity}~~~
In this work, we focus on a strongly convex potential function $f$ coupled with specific choices of projection operators, resulting in a strongly log-concave surrogate density $\nu^{\lambda}$. 
However, it is important to note that the approximation error bound between the target density $\nu$ and the surrogate density $\nu^{\lambda}$, as stated in Proposition~\ref{prop:w2_tight}, depends solely on the convexity of $f$. 
 By relaxing the strong convexity requirement of $f$ and considering alternative projection operators, one can generate a surrogate density $\nu^\lambda$ that is less restrictive than strongly log-concave. 
 This modification enables the extension of these findings to a broader spectrum of sampling methods.

\noindent \textbf{Other metrics}~~~
The Wasserstein distance we employ is a natural metric for measuring sampling errors due to its relevance to optimal transport theory. 
However, recent advancements in gradient-based sampling have investigated other metrics, including total variation distance, KL divergence, and $\chi^2$ divergence. 
An intriguing avenue for future research would be to establish error guarantees for constrained sampling concerning these alternative metrics.
