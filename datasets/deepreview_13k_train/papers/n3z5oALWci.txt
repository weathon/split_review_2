\title{ReX: A Framework for Incorporating Temporal Information in Model-Agnostic Local Explanation Techniques}

\begin{abstract}

Neural network models that can handle inputs of variable lengths are powerful, but often hard to interpret. The lack of transparency hinders their adoption in many domains.
Explanation techniques are essential for improving transparency. However, existing model-agnostic general explanation techniques do not consider the variable lengths of input data points, which limits their effectiveness. 
To address this limitation, we propose \textsc{ReX}, a general framework for adapting various explanation techniques to models that process variable-length inputs, expanding explanation coverage to data points of different lengths. Our approach adds temporal information to the explanations generated by existing techniques without altering their core algorithms. 
We instantiate our approach on two popular explanation techniques: LIME and Anchors. To evaluate the effectiveness of \textsc{ReX}, we apply our approach to three models in two different tasks. Our evaluation results demonstrate that our approach significantly improves the fidelity and understandability of explanations.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

 

\label{sec:intro}
As more critical applications employ machine learning systems, how to explain the rationales behind their results has emerged as an important problem.
Such explanations allow end users to 1) judge whether the results are trustworthy~\cite{Ribeiro0G16, account} and 2) understand knowledge embedded in the systems so they can use the knowledge to manipulate future events~\cite{PoyiadziSSBF20, ProsperiGSKMHRW20, ZhangSS18}.
This paper focuses on the problem of explaining deep learning systems that are widely applied to processing sequential data of different lengths, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Transformers\cite{wolf-etal-2020-transformers}, and others.

One dimension to classify explanation techniques is based on whether they are {\it global} or {\it local}~\cite{molnar2020interpretable}.
The former explains how the model behaves on all inputs, while the latter explains how the model behaves on a particular set of inputs (typically ones that are similar to a given input).
Most of the existing explanation techniques for variable-length models are global.
Take techniques for RNNs as an example: they employ deterministic finite automaton (DFAs) as global surrogates of target RNNs~\cite{OmlinG96, Jacobsson05, WangZOXLG18, WeissGY18, DongWSZWDDW20}.
However, due to the complex nature of any practical problem domain, these techniques can produce very large DFAs.
These explanations are hard for a human to digest.
Moreover, they take a long time to generate, making these techniques hard to scale.
As a result, these techniques are often limited to toy networks such as ones that learn regular expressions.
Moreover, even for these simple domains, the explanations can still be complex as RNNs often fail to internalize the perfect regular expressions and contain noise.

Due to the complexity of the problem domain and the networks, we turn our attentions to local methods~\cite{Ribeiro0G16, Ribeiro0G18, ZhangSS18, ArrasMMS17, Counterfactual, LundbergL17}.
Local methods produce more tractable and understandable explanations at the cost of covering much fewer inputs.
Such methods provide explanations for individual inputs and have a wide range of applications.
While there is a rich body of techniques in this category, to our knowledge, there are few that are specialized to capture the temporal information of inputs, which plays an important role in underlying models like RNNs and transformers.
Without incorporating temporal information, the explanations lack fidelity and understandability.

Consider Anchors~\cite{Ribeiro0G18}, a popular local technique that generates sufficient conditions to explain specific outputs. 
Table ~\ref{tab:example1} shows Anchors explanations of a sentiment analysis RNN on two sentences.
For sentence I, Anchors generates the following explanation: {\it Sentence I is positive because it contains both ``never'' and ``fails''}.
For sentence II, Anchors produces the same explanation as for Sentence I, but the sentence is negative.
The key difference is in Sentence II, ``never'' and ``fails'' do not form a phrase.
Anchors fails to capture the difference, which confuses users.

We see similar issues with other existing local explanation techniques.
For example, a large group of techniques such as LIME~\cite{Ribeiro0G16} uses linear models as local surrogates.
Figure ~\ref{fig:example2} shows LIME explanations of a sentiment analysis transformer on a sentence.
LIME assigns a high positive score to ``not" and ``bad".
Intuitively, it means that either ``not'' or ``bad'' can make the sentence positive.
However, such an explanation will lead to some ridiculous results. The user will predict ``Bob is a bad boy" as a positive sentence since the word ``bad'' should have a strong positive effect. 
However, this is not the case. ``Not bad'' together is a positive phrase, while ``not'' or ``bad'' alone is a negative word.

To address this issue, we propose \toolname, a general framework for extending various local \textit{model-agnostic} explanation techniques with temporal information.
In particular, \toolname adds temporal information in the form of $Pos_f\ op\ d$ and $Pos_f - Pos_g\ op\ d$ ($op$ can be $=$, $>$, $<$, $\geq$, or $\leq$ ), where $Pos_f$ means the position of feature $f$.
The way to present temporal information depends on the specific explanation technique.
Consider the sentences in Table~\ref{tab:example1} again.
After \toolname augments Anchors, the explanation to Sentence I becomes {\it the sentence is positive because it contains both ``never'' and ``fails'', and ``never'' is right before ``fails''}.
For sentence II,  the explanation becomes {\it the sentence is negative because it contains ``never'' and ``fails'', and ``never'' is not right before ``fails''}.
Now, consider applying LIME with \toolname to the sentence in Figure~\ref{fig:example2}.
The fact that {\it ``not'' is before ``bad''} gets the highest positive score, while ``not'' and ``bad'' both get negative scores respectively.
This new information 1) associates the two words, and 2) captures that ``not" comes before ``bad''.

Figure~\ref{fig:adexp} shows another example when explaining an anomaly detection RNN.
Anchors generates an explanation: {\it the anomaly is detected because of the presence of several separated data points.}
After \toolname augments Anchors, the explanation becomes {\it the anomaly is detected because of the presence of data points 413 and 425 with at least 3 points between them.}
Compared to the original explanation, the new explanation is more general and more understandable to end users.
The examples show that temporal information improves the fidelity of explanations and makes them easier to understand. How to add such information to various local model-agnostic explanation techniques? 
We notice that these techniques treat target models as black boxes and generate surrogates by learning from input-output pairs.
In particular, we make two key observations:
1) these techniques use a perturbation model to generate inputs that are similar to the original input so it captures the local behavior of the network through these inputs; 2) these techniques generate explanations that are described in features.
Based on 1), in order to capture temporal information which the network internalizes, we can modify the perturbation model to generate inputs whose lengths and feature orders vary.
Based on 2), we can treat temporal information such as $Pos_f - Pos_g \geq d$ as a predicate used to form the explanation.
Although this feature will not affect the result of the original network, they reflect temporal information that is internalized by the network.
More importantly, now the explanation techniques can generate explanations that utilize them without changing their core algorithms.

To evaluate the effectiveness of \toolname, We have applied it to LIME and Anchors and then use their augmented versions to explain a sentiment analysis LSTM, a sentiment analysis transformer, and an anomaly detection RNN.
Our results show that \toolname improves existing techniques in both the fidelity and understandability of generated explanations. \toolname helps explanations cover  275.6\% more inputs on average and describe the model more precisely.
Moreover, a user study shows that augmented explanations make it easier for human users to predict the behaviors of the models.

In summary, we have made the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,nolistsep,topsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,partopsep=0pt, leftmargin=*, topsep=0pt]
    \item We have proposed to incorporate temporal information in the form of $Pos_f\ op\ d$ and $Pos_f - Pos_g\ op\ d$ in local explanations to machine learning models that can capture temporal information in the inputs, which makes these explanations easier to understand.
    \item We have proposed a general framework \toolname to automatically incorporate the above information in popular local explanation techniques.
    \item We have demonstrated the effectiveness of \toolname by applying it to LIME and 
    Anchors and evaluating the augmented versions on representative models.
\end{itemize}



\section{Preliminaries}

\label{sec:pre}

In this section, we describe the necessary background to introduce our approach.
Without loss of generality, we assume the target model is a black-box function from a sequence of real numbers to a real value, $f: R^{*} \mapsto R$.
We limit our discussion to classifiers and regressors.

Given an input $i \in R^{*}$, its corresponding output $o \in R, o := f(i)$, a local explanation is $exp(i, o)$. It reflects why the model produces output $o$ given input $i$ and the target model's behavior around input $i$.
Typically, a local explanation is an expression of features.
Specifically, $exp(i,o)$ is an expression formed with \textbf{feature predicates} $\{p_j\}_{j=1,2\dots,|i|}$.
Formally, we define
$p_j := f_j\ op\ c$, where $f_j$ represents the $j$th feature of input $i$, $op$ is a binary operator (e.g., $=$, $\geq$, $\leq$), and $c$ is a constant.
The feature predicate $p_j$ compares the $j$th feature of input $i$ to the constant.
Based on the specific form of the expression, a feature predicate can be interpreted as a Boolean variable or a \{0, 1\} integer.
We call the set of feature predicates the {\textbf{vocabulary}} of the local explanation.
For example, in Anchors, $exp(i,o)$ is a conjunction $\bigwedge_{j \in \{1, 2, ..., |i|\}} p_j$ which must evaluate to true on $i$;
in LIME, $exp(i, o)$ is a linear expression in the form of $\Sigma_{j = 1}^{|i|} \omega_j p_j$;
in a counterfactual explanation~\cite{ZhangSS18,Counterfactual,DandlMBB20}, $exp(i,o)$ is a conjunction $\bigwedge_{j \in \Delta} p_j \wedge \bigwedge_{k \notin \Delta} p_k$ where $\Delta\subseteq \{1, 2, ..., |i|\}$, none of $p_j (j \in \Delta)$ holds on $i$, and all $p_k (k \notin {\Delta})$ has the form of $f_k = i[k]$.
Moreover, if an input $i'$ satisfies a counterfactual explanation $exp(i,o)$, then $|f(i) - f(i')| \geq d$ for a given $d$. In the case of binary classification, d is 1.

A perturbation model $per: I \mapsto 2^I$ describes a set of inputs that are similar to an input.
A local explanation technique $t$ is parameterized by a perturbation model, and generates a local explanation to a model given an input: $t_{per}(f, i) := exp(i, f(i))$.
The local explanation reflects the behaviors of the target model over the input space given by the perturbation model.
Existing local techniques implement the perturbation models as changing values of given features, i.e., $per(i) \subseteq R^{|i|}$. 

As we can see, both the vocabularies and the perturbation models of existing local explanations are limited to describing inputs that have the same lengths as the original input\footnote{When explaining NLP models, some perturbation 
models allow replacing a word with an empty string. This enables the explanations to cover inputs of shorter lengths to some extent.}.
This limits their effectiveness on models processing sequential data of different lengths.


\section{Our Framework}


We now describe \toolname.
Our goal is to provide a general approach to incorporate temporal information in explanations without heavily modifying the explanation generation technique.
We introduce \toolname in three steps: 
1) we introduce the definition of local explanations with temporal information, 
2) we describe how to augment existing techniques to generate such explanations, 
and 3) we describe the workflow of generating explanations with temporal information.

\subsection{Local Explanations with Temporal Information}
Our key observation is that while the form of explanation expression varies, the expressions are all built from the corresponding vocabulary.
If we can add predicates that reflect temporal information to the vocabulary, then naturally the explanations provide temporal information.

Our temporal predicates describe the temporal relationship between a set of features that satisfy basic predicates.
We limit the number of features in a temporal predicate upto two because 1) in most cases, the temporal relationship between two features suffices to cover a large range of inputs of different lengths, therefore providing useful information, 2) humans are bad at understanding high-dimensional information, and 3) covering too many inputs makes the generation process slow, especially for sampling-based techniques (e.g., LIME and Anchors).
We give their definitions below:

\begin{definition}[1-D Temporal Predicate] A 1-D temporal predicate takes the form of
\[
     \exists j \in Z^{+}. f_j\ op_1\ c \wedge j\ op_3\ d  
     \quad\text{ where }c \in R, d\in Z^{+}, op_1,op_3 \in \{=, >, <,\geq,\leq\}.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[2-D Temporal Predicate] A 2-D temporal predicate takes the form of
\[
\begin{array}{r@{}l}
    &\exists j, k \in Z^{+}. f_j\ op_1\ c_1 \wedge f_k\ op_2\ c_2 \wedge k - j\ op_3\ d \\
    &\text{ where }c_1, c_2 \in R, d\in Z\setminus\{0\}, op_1, op_2, op_3 \in \{=, >, <,\geq,\leq\}.
\end{array}
\]
\end{definition}

To illustrate the effects of a single feature’s abstract position, we use 1-D temporal predicates. To illustrate the effects of the relative position between two features, we use 2-D temporal predicates. 
Moreover, we can use 2-D temporal predicates when the presence of two features together is important but their order is not. For example, feature $k$ can come before or after feature $j$ when the distance $d$ is negative and $op_3$ is $\geq$. This means the order between these two features does not matter.

Compared to conventional feature predicates, temporal predicates no longer describe properties of features at fixed positions in an input.
Instead, it requires positions of features to satisfy given temporal constraints.

We now introduce the definition of local explanations with temporal information:

\begin{definition} [Explanation with Temporal Information] 
A local explanation with temporal information is a local explanation whose vocabulary consists of regular feature predicates, 1-D temporal predicates, or 2-D temporal predicates.
\end{definition}

\textbf{Examples.} Consider Input sentence I in Table~\ref{tab:example1}, 
a 2-D temporal predicate is
\[\exists j,k \in Z^{+}. f_j = ``never" \wedge f_k = ``fails" \wedge k - j = 1.\]
The explanation generated by Anchors augmented with \toolname in Table~\ref{tab:example1} is a conjunction that only consists of the above 2-D predicate.
Consider another sentence ``I hate that man but love her'' which is judged as positive.
When applying Anchors with \toolname on it, the explanation is 
\[\exists j\in Z^+. f_j = ``love"\quad \wedge\quad \exists k \in Z^+. f_k = ``but" \wedge k \geq 3.\]
The second conjunct above is a 1-D predicate.
The sentence contains both ``love'' and ``hate'' but is judged as positive because there is a ``but'' in the middle.
On the other hand, the sentence ``but I hate that man loves her'' is judged as negative.
Our 1-D predicate captures such information.

\subsection{Augmenting Generation Techniques}
We aim to provide a general approach to extend existing local model-agnostic techniques with temporal information without heavily modifying their algorithms.
Our key idea is to simply extend the vocabulary and the perturbation model of a technique.
The reason is that these techniques essentially treat the target model as a black box: they generate surrogate models as explanations that are (1) described using features in the vocabulary, and (2) trained from input-output pairs that are obtained from the perturbation model.
As long as we can change these two components, we can generate explanations augmented with temporal information without changing the core algorithms.

\textbf{Extending Vocabularies.} In order to add a predicate to a vocabulary, the predicate must be able to serve as a feature of an input.
As a result, the work required to add a predicate to a vocabulary is quite light: as long as a way is provided to evaluate the predicate on a given input, it is fine.
Obviously, both our 1-D and 2-D temporal predicates can be easily evaluated on any input of a target model.
By adding temporal predicates to the vocabulary, now an explanation technique has the language to describe behaviors of the target model over inputs of variable lengths.

\textbf{Extending Perturbation Models.} In order to cover inputs of different lengths, we next modify perturbation models of existing techniques.
Concretely, we add a preprocessor over a given perturbation model.
For a given input $i$, the preprocessor does two modifications in sequence to generate more inputs: 1) it can delete certain features from the input, and 2) it can switch the positions of two features.
To control the number of generated inputs, we add a parameter $\pi$ to limit the switching operation to two features that are at most $\pi$ apart.
In our experiment, we set $\pi = 1$. This corresponds to the aforementioned setting of 2-D temporal predicates where the distance $d \geq -1$.
More formally, given a perturbation model $per$, a constant $\pi$, the new perturbation model our approach generates is
\[
\begin{array}{rll}
per^t(\bar{i}) &:=& \bigcup_{\hat{i} \in prep(\bar{i})}per(\hat{i})
\text{ where }  prep(i) = \\
&&\ \ \ \{ i'' \mid i' = rf^{*}(i) \wedge i'' = sf(i', m, n) \wedge\ n-m\leq \pi 
\wedge 0\leq m < n\leq |i'|\}.
\end{array}
\]

In the above definition, $rf$ returns a new input that removes a random feature from a given input and it can be applied for arbitrary times; $sf$ switches the positions of feature $m$ and feature $n$ of a given input and returns a new input.
Careful readers may have noticed that our augmented perturbation model can be made more complex.
For example, instead of only allowing switching the positions of two features, one can completely shuffle the features in an input;
besides deleting or switching features, one can add new features.
While these methods can generate more inputs, the drawbacks are 1) the generation techniques can run slower -- many of these techniques require sampling from the perturbation model, which now alone takes a much longer time, and 2) a larger input space is harder for a simple explanation to fit.
In practice, we find our definition above a good balance among generality, efficiency, and accuracy.

With \toolname, we can generate explanations following the scheme of local model-agnostic black-box explanation techniques.
The only difference is the extended explanation techniques now include temporal predicates in their vocabularies.
Note the target model still takes the original input. The outputs of the extended perturbation model are also expressed in the original input features, so we can feed them to the target model. Then we evaluate the predicates on these inputs to get the representation in terms of temporal predicates. 
Figure \ref{fig:perturb} shows a typical workflow of the explanation-generating process of these techniques without (a) and with (b) \toolname.
After applying \toolname, the perturbation model provides sampled inputs to the target model to obtain corresponding output, and sampled inputs in the temporal predicate form to generate an explanation with temporal predicates.



\section{Instances}

We next describe how to instantiate our framework on several popular model-agnostic local explanation methods~\cite{molnar2020interpretable}.
In particular, we discuss how the augmented explanation looks, how the temporal predicates are plugged, and how the perturbation model interacts with the core generation algorithm.
Since model-agnostic techniques treat target models as black boxes, they typically sample different inputs and observe how the model outputs change.
In our experiment, we implement the instances that correspond to two representative approaches, Anchors and LIME. 

\textbf{LIME~\cite{Ribeiro0G16}.} As the example in Figure~\ref{fig:example2} shows, the augmented explanation is now a linear expression whose 0-1 variables can either be a conventional predicate or a temporal predicate.
There are two main steps in LIME to generate explanations:
1) to obtain several sample inputs around a given input using a perturbation model and the corresponding outputs, and 2)
to perform regression on the input-output pairs.

We now explain how to instantiate \toolname on LIME.
Since LIME trains such a linear expression by fitting it on a given set of input-output pairs, it only needs to be able to evaluate the linear expression and calculate the loss value after adding temporal predicates.
For regression algorithms, it is easy to cope with additional predicates.
As for the perturbation model, it is used to draw the aforementioned inputs and is viewed as a black box by the core algorithm in LIME.
As a result, replacing the perturbation model is straightforward. 

\textbf{Anchors\cite{Ribeiro0G18}.} As the example in Table~\ref{tab:example1} shows, the augmented explanation is now a conjunction of conventional predicates and temporal predicates.
Similar to LIME, Anchors are generated by a sampling-based approach. But in contrast to LIME, Anchors uses if-then rules as its explanation form.
However, except the perturbation model, the other parts of Anchors can work well with any predicates.
To incorporate temporal information, the new perturbation model only needs to be modified in the same way that LIME does.%, which support evaluating sampled instance on new predicates.
Therefore, adding the temporal predicates and substituting the perturbation model are straightforward.

Besides LIME and Anchors, other model-agnostic local explanation methods can cooperate with \toolname in similar ways. Such approaches, like Shapley Values\cite{StrumbeljK14,LundbergL17} and Counterfactual Explanations\cite{Counterfactual, DandlMBB20, van2021interpretable}, also follow the above algorithm structure. We only need to follow suit while applying \toolname on these methods.



\section{Empirical Evaluation}

We have instantiated \toolname on Anchors and LIME.
We refer to the extended version as Anchors* and LIME*.
We study how much \toolname improves Anchors and LIME by comparing them with the extended versions.
Following previous works, we use fidelity and understandability~\cite{markus2021role} as metrics. They mean good explanations should 1) describe the ML model completely and precisely and 2) be understandable to a human.
We conducted two experiments with three models to assess them.

\subsection{Models and Datasets}

\textbf{Sentiment analysis RNN.}
We trained an LSTM with paraphrastic sentence embedding \cite{wieting2015towards} as a sentiment predictor on the \textit{Stanford Sentiment Treebank} \cite{socher2013recursive} dataset.
We followed the train/validate/test split in the original dataset.
We used words as the basic building blocks of explanations.
As described previously, the explanations produced by the original Anchors and LIME only use the presence of words, while \toolname adds the 1-D and 2-D temporal predicates into the explanations. 
In the perturbation model, besides deleting and switching words, we also applied BERT~\cite{devlin2018bert} to replace words with other words that can appear in the context.

\textbf{Anomaly Detection RNN.}
We trained an Anomaly Detection RNN following \citet{park2018anomaly} on an ECG dataset~\cite{UCRArchive2018}. 
Similarly, we followed the train/validate/test split in the original dataset.
We used whether a data point in a series is fixed to a given value as the basic building block of explanations.
While the explanations generated by the original Anchors and LIME only contain such predicates, \toolname adds temporal predicates.
To make the explanation generation process tractable, we limited the explanations to only consist of data points that are at most 20 steps before the detected anomalous point in a time series.
In the perturbation model, besides deleting and switching data points, we allowed changing each data point by sampling from a Gaussian distribution with its original value as the mean and one as the standard deviation.

\textbf{Sentiment analysis transformer.}
We used \textit{DistilBERT base uncased finetuned SST-2 model}\cite{hf_canonical_model_maintainers_2022}, a pre-trained model, 
as a sentiment predictor.
We ran evaluations on \textit{Stanford Sentiment Treebank} with the same settings as the evaluation with the sentiment analysis RNN.

In this experiment, we evaluated how much \toolname improves fidelity,  which consists of completeness and soundness~\cite{markus2021role}:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep,nolistsep,topsep=0pt,parsep=0pt,partopsep=0pt, leftmargin=*, topsep=0pt]
    \item \textbf{Completeness}: How many inputs that are similar to the original input can an explanation 
 cover?%explain the result produced by the target model?
    \item \textbf{Soundness}:  Does the result implied by an explanation follow the target model?
\end{itemize}
We answered these two questions by calculating the coverage and precision of explanations, respectively.
We took the test sets of the two datasets, and applied Anchors, Anchors*, LIME, and LIME* to generate explanations for each input in the sets.
Since normal points are much more than anomalous points in the anomaly detection dataset, we only looked at anomalous inputs. 
There are 2210 inputs in the test set of the sentiment analysis dataset, while there are 9 (anomalous) inputs in that of the anomaly detection dataset.
We applied our perturbation models that vary input lengths to generate 10,000 similar inputs for each input.
For each generated input, we tried to predict the target model's output following each explanation, which was in turn used to calculate the coverage and precision of each approach.
In the case of Anchors and Anchors*, as long as the given input satisfies the explanation (a sufficient condition), we gave a ``yes'' to the coverage question; if the actual classification also matches that of the original input, we gave a ``yes'' to the precision question.
In the case of LIME and LIME*, the explanations are linear expressions and are evaluated to real numbers on each input.
If the absolute value of an evaluation result is greater than a threshold (0.1 for sentiment analysis, and 0.05 for anomaly detection), we gave a ``yes'' to the coverage question;
further, if the sign of the value matches the actual output, we gave a ``yes'' to the precision question.

Table \ref{table:sound} summarizes the average precision, coverage, and executing times of the four approaches across all the generated inputs.
Overall, \toolname improves the coverage significantly while maintaining roughly the same level of precision or slightly improving it compared to the original approaches, with an acceptable increase in execution time.
This is in line with our assumption that \toolname can augment existing local techniques to explain more inputs.

We report the 10th percentile, median, and 90th percentile of the completeness results in Table \ref{table:completeness_detail}. The methods with \toolname outperform the original ones in each statistic. \toolname helps improve most of the instances in the experiments.
We did a 1-tail \textbf{paired t-tests} on these 12 pairs of setups where the only difference is whether to apply \toolname. The significance values are all far smaller than 0.01. This shows with more than 99\% confidence, \toolname significantly helps LIME and Anchor explain more inputs.

To assess how much \toolname helps an end user understand a model, we conducted a user study.
Similarly, we use coverage and precision as metrics but with different meanings.
In particular, we measure how many inputs the user feels confident to predict the model's outputs on and how precise these predictions are.
We employed 19 computer science undergraduates and studied how much \toolname improves Anchors on the sentiment analysis RNN.
Each participant has taken courses in machine learning but had no experience with explanation techniques before.
The questionnaire contains five sets of tests.
Each test first presents a sentence, the network's output on the sentence, and explanations produced by Anchor and Anchor*, respectively.
The sentences are randomly chosen from the test set.
Then the user is asked to predict the RNN's output on 10 new sentences.
The new sentences are produced using our perturbation model (with BERT~\cite{devlin2018bert}).
They can answer ``positive'', ``negative'', or ``I don't know''.
If a user did not answer ``I don't know'', we gave a ``yes'' to the coverage question;
further, if their prediction matches the actual model output, we gave a ``yes'' to the precision question.

Table~\ref{table:user_study} shows the average coverage and precision across the 19 users and 10 sentences for each question.
Anchors* is better than Anchors on all questions in terms of both coverage and precision.
Across these questions, Anchors* yields an average precision of 87.3\% and an average coverage of 68.8\%, while these numbers are only 48.3\% and 43.9\% for Anchors.
The improvements are 80.9\% and 56.7\% respectively. 
We did 1-tail \textbf{paired t-tests} on these paired data. With more than 99\% confidence, \toolname significantly helps Users predict more instances more precisely.

Users can utilize the explanations to predict the behaviors of the target models on more inputs and more precisely.
On one hand, adding temporal information generates clearer explanations (e.g., ones in Table \ref{tab:example1} and Figure \ref{fig:example2}). On the other hand, the new perturbation model considers the fact that a word can appear at any position due to variable input lengths.
Strictly speaking, an explanation produced by Anchors only covers inputs with the same lengths as the original input.
We inspected the answers closely and found out that sometimes users would misuse explanations produced by the original Anchors.
Some users would ignore input length constraints and apply the explanations when they were supposed to answer ``I don't know''.
This situation happened for 24.8\% of all the answers.
As a result, they often give incorrect predictions to these sentences while the coverage is increased.
But such misuses rarely happen with Anchors* because the corresponding explanations highlight temporal information and cover much more inputs.


Our user study shows that \toolname can 
help users predict more instances with higher precision.
It also shows the shortcomings of existing techniques when they are applied to models processing sequential data of different lengths.
In particular, % their precision or coverage drops 
users are likely to misuse and make wrong predictions when trying to apply the explanations to inputs whose lengths are different from the original input.



\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:related}
Our work is closely related to explanation techniques for models processing inputs of variable lengths and general local model-agnostic explanation techniques.
We refer to the surveys by \citet{WangZOXLG18}, \citet{Jacobsson05}, and \citet{rojat2021explainable} for comprehensive introductions.

There is a long line of works on extracting DFAs~\cite{Jacobsson05} and their variants~\cite{AyacheEG18,DuXLM0Z19,DongWSZWDDW20} from RNNs.
The focus has been on developing more scalable algorithms.
For example, \citet{WeissGY18} adapt Angluin's $L^{*}$ algorithm.
These approaches are not scalable to practical RNNs due to their global nature.
Their applications are mostly limited to explaining RNNs that learn regular expressions.
\citet{wisdom2016interpretable} and \citet{sha2017interpretable} choose to reach high fidelity at the cost of lowering understandability by generating relatively \textit{interpretable} models as surrogates.
But only experts can analyze these models by inspecting their internal states.
In contrast, \toolname can help regular end users.

Local explanation techniques exist for these models.
Many explain them by attributing importance to features~\cite{ArrasOMS19,schlegel2019towards}.
However, they fail to capture temporal information and their explanations are in the same form as those generated by model-agnostic approaches such as LIME.
The difference is that by treating explained models as white boxes they can generate explanations more efficiently and precisely~\cite{LiCHJ16, DenilDF14, DingLLS17, ArrasMMS17, Arjona-MedinaGW19, MurdochLY18, karim2017lstm,vinayavekhin2018focusing}.
However, utilizing internal information of target models makes these methods model-specific.

Some consider the effect of multiple features together, including approaches for specific applications~\cite{chen2020generating, singh2018hierarchical, sivill_limesegment_2022}, and general approaches~\cite{tsang2020does}.
But all these methods do not take account of temporal information, and do not have the ability of handling inputs of different lengths.
Moreover, they are extending explanations in an orthogonal direction to \toolname, so it is possible to combine them with \toolname to generate explanations that consider both temporal information and the effect of multiple features.




Local model-agnostic explanation techniques apply to a wide range of machine-learning models by treating models as black boxes.
Popular explanation forms include feature importance attribution~\cite{Ribeiro0G16, StrumbeljK14, LundbergL17}, boolean expressions as sufficient conditions~\cite{Ribeiro0G18}, and counterfactuals~\cite{Counterfactual, DandlMBB20, ZhangSS18}.
Individual Conditional Expectation plots visualize how the model output changes as one feature changes~\cite{ICE}.
For more details, we refer to Chapter 9 of the popular book by ~\citet{molnar2020interpretable}.
These approaches fail to capture temporal information in models processing sequential data of variable lengths.


\section{Limitations}


Having demonstrated the effectiveness of \toolname, we now discuss its limitations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Realistic perturbation model:} Perturbation model is a key component of black-box explanation techniques. But for some domains, finding a realistic perturbation model is challenging. With temporal predicates, we also cannot escape from it. For example, the perturbation for time series data like stock prices is not clear.
\end{itemize}


\section{Conclusion and Limitation}

\label{sec:con}

We have proposed \toolname, a general framework that adds temporal information to existing local model-agnostic explanation methods.
\toolname allows these methods to generate more useful explanations for models that handle inputs of variable lengths (e.g., RNNs and transformers).
\toolname achieves this by extending vocabularies of explanations with temporal predicates, and modifying perturbation models so they can generate inputs of different lengths.
We have instantiated \toolname on Anchors and LIME, and demonstrated the effectiveness by applying them to two RNNs and one transformer.

One limitation of our approach is that it relies on finding realistic perturbation models which is a common problem for model-agnostic explanation techniques.
For example, it is unclear what a good perturbation model for the time series data of stock prices is.
