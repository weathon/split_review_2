\title{Spatiotemporal Learning on Cell-embedded Graphs}

\begin{abstract}
Data-driven simulation of physical systems has recently kindled significant attention, where many neural models have been developed. In particular, mesh-based graph neural networks (GNNs) have demonstrated significant potential in predicting spatiotemporal dynamics across arbitrary geometric domains. However, the existing node-edge message passing mechanism in GNNs limits the model's representation learning ability. In this paper, we proposed a cell-embedded GNN model (aka CeGNN) to learn spatiotemporal dynamics with lifted performance. Specifically, we introduce a learnable cell attribution to the node-edge message passing process, which better captures the spatial dependency of regional features. Such a strategy essentially upgrades the local aggregation scheme from the first order (e.g., from edge to node) to a higher order (e.g., from volume to edge and then to node), which takes advantage of volumetric information in message passing. Meanwhile, a novel feature-enhanced block is designed to further improve the performance of CeGNN and relieve the over-smoothness problem, via treating the latent features as basis functions. The extensive experiments on various PDE systems and one real-world dataset demonstrate that CeGNN achieves superior performance compared with other baseline models, particularly reducing the prediction error with up to 1 orders of magnitude on several PDE systems.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Solving Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) is often  essential for analyzing and modeling complex spatiotemporal dynamic processes across various scientific and engineering fields. For example, weather prediction ~\citep{scher2018toward, schultz2021can, grover2015deep}, ocean current motion prediction ~\citep{zheng2020purely}, nonlinear engineering structure earthquake response prediction ~\citep{zhang2019deep}, material mechanical properties simulation ~\citep{wang2018multiscale}, etc. Traditionally, classical numerical methods (e.g., Finite Difference Method (FDM) ~\citep{godunov1959finite,ozicsik2017finite}, Finite Volume Method (FVM) ~\citep{eymard2000finite}, and Finite Element Method (FEM)~\citep{hughes2012finite}) are utilized to solve these PDEs, requiring substantial analytical or computational efforts. Although this problem has been simplified via discretizing the space, the issue of trade-off between cost and precision intensifies when dealing with varying domain geometries (e.g., different initial or boundary conditions or various input parameters), especially in real-world scenarios. In the last few decades, deep learning ~\citep{pinkus1999approximation, tolstikhin2021mlp, albawi2017understanding, koutnik2014clockwork, sundermeyer2012lstm}  models have made great progress in approximating high-dimensional PDEs benefiting from existing rich labeled or unlabeled datasets. However, there are certain drawbacks in this simple approach of learning the non-linear mapping between inputs and outputs from data. For example, their performance is severely limited by the training datasets, the neural network lacks interpretability and generalizes poorly.

Embedding domain-specific expertise (e.g., Physics-informed Neural Networks (PINNs) ~\citep{raissi2019physics}) has shown the potential to tackle these problems ~\citep{krishnapriyan2021characterizing, he2023learning}. However, the core part of PINNs, \textit{Automatic Differentiation} (AD) approach, has two major drawbacks: (1) it is necessary to formulate explicit governing equations into the loss function, and (2) the parameters in high-dimensional feature spaces cannot be efficiently optimized when facing highly complex networks like graph networks. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:examples}\textbf{e}, there are no predefined equations available to learn the evolution pattern of sea surface temperature in varying depths. Neural Operators, such as DeepONet ~\citep{lu2021learning}, have emerged as another paradigm to learn these complex non-linear behaviors. The most well-known models, Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) ~\citep{li2021Fourier} and its variants, utilize neural networks to learn parameters in the Fourier domain for fast and effective turbulence simulation. Likewise, they inevitably have the same shortcoming as those traditional methods: over-reliance on data and biased towards the grid domain.

The other representative models, Transformer ~\citep{vaswani2017attention} and GNNs ~\citep{liu2020towards}, have demonstrated significant influence in predicting spatiotemporal dynamics across arbitrary geometric domains. In particular, mesh-based graph neural networks (GNNs) ~\citep{gilmer2017neural, gilmer2020message, pfaff2021Learning, brandstetter2022message} learn vastly different dynamics of various physical systems, ranging from structural mechanics and cloth to fluid simulations. However, the existing node-edge message passing mechanism in GNNs overestimates the primary role of the ``message'' passing on the neighbor ``edges'', limiting the model's representation learning ability.  In general, this strategy leads to homogeneous node features after multiple rounds of message passing, making the features ineffective at representing distinct characteristics, also known as the over-smoothness problem.

To further address the above issues, we proposed an end-to-end graph-based framework, Cell-embedded Graph Neural Network (CeGNN), to model spatiotemporal dynamics across various domains with improved performance. Specifically, after detecting discontinuities in space, we introduce a learnable cell attribution to the node-edge message passing process, which better captures the spatial dependency of regional features. Such a strategy essentially upgrades the local aggregation scheme from the first order (e.g., from edge to node) to a higher order (e.g., from volume to edge and then to node), which takes advantage of volumetric information in message passing. Meanwhile, a novel feature-enhanced (FE) block is designed to further improve the performance of CeGNN and relieve the over-smoothness problem, via treating the latent features as basis functions and further processing these features on this concept. The FE block treats node latent features $\mathbf{h}$ as basis and build a higher-order tensor feature map via outer-product operation, e.g., $\mathbf{h}\otimes\mathbf{h}$. This process creates 2nd-order nonlinear terms (e.g., $\mathbf{u}_{x}\mathbf{u}_{y}$) to enrich the feature map. We then use a mask operation to randomly sample these terms into a learnable weight tensor, enhancing the model's representation capacity. Figure \ref{fig:architecture} shows an outline of our proposed model. Our extensive experiments on many PDE-centric systems and real-world datasets show that CeGNN can significantly enhance spatiotemporal dynamic learning in various scenarios, particularly with limited datasets. The key contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item We introduce cell attributions legitimately to learn second-order information with data from connected nodes of a cell, allowing us to rapidly identify non-local relationships that traditional message-passing mechanisms often fail to capture directly.
\item We propose the FE block to enrich the feature representations and filter more effective information via learnable parameters.
\item Our approach stands out for its lower error, better interpretability, and robust generalizability, making a substantial progress in the spatiotemporal dynamic field.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related works}

Spatiotemporal dynamics research, as one of the important frontier research areas, s integral to fields ranging from traditional fluid dynamics to economics and finance. Firstly, we give a brief introduction to spatiotemporal PDEs. Then, the relevant progress in spatiotemporal dynamics research is described from the perspectives of classical and neural solvers. Meanwhile, the content related to graph neural network is discussed separately in one subsection.
\subsection{PDE formulation}\label{Problem formulation}
Without loss of generality, the time-dependent PDEs generally describe the evolution of a continuous field over certain time intervals, which can be cast into the following form:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pde form}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t}={\mathcal{F}} \left  (\theta, t, \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\nabla} \mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2} \mathbf{u}, \dots \right ),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{F} (\cdot)$ denotes an unknown linear or nonlinear function comprised of the spatiotemporal variable $\mathbf{u} (\mathbf{x}, t) \in\mathbb{R}^d$, its corresponding partial derivatives  (e.g., $\boldsymbol{\nabla} \mathbf{u}, \boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2} \mathbf{u}$), and some related parameters $\theta$. Here, $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^m$ denotes the $m$-dimensional spatial coordinate, $t \in \mathbb{R}^{1}$ the time, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ the Nabla operator, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}$ the Laplacian operator. The PDE is subjected to specific initial and boundary conditions.

\subsection{Classical solvers}
To solve time-dependent PDEs, a common way is the method of lines (MOL). By discretizing in all but one dimension, it allows solutions to be computed via methods and software developed for the numerical integration of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and differential-algebraic equations (DAEs). Meanwhile, the multigrid method ~\citep{wu2020multigrid} is another algorithm for solving PDEs via a hierarchy of discretizations. Other classical numerical methods (e.g., Finite Difference Method (FDM) ~\citep{godunov1959finite,ozicsik2017finite}, Finite Volume Method (FVM)~\citep{eymard2000finite}, and Finite Element Method (FEM)~\citep{cao1999anr}) have also been utilized for practical applications ~\citep{reich2000finite,hughes2012finite}.

\subsection{Neural solvers}
With the emergency of artificial intelligence, data-driven methods offers convenience for the scientific exploration of complex spatiotemporal systems. In the last decade, conventional data-driven machine learning techniques ~\citep{pinkus1999approximation, tolstikhin2021mlp, albawi2017understanding, koutnik2014clockwork, sundermeyer2012lstm} have been widely used to model and predict the state of complex space-time systems. For example, climate evolution prediction ~\citep{rolnick2022tackling}, fluid or turbulence simulation ~\citep{deng2019time, wang2020towards}, and flood prediction ~\citep{kao2020exploring}. Although relevant methods cannot completely replace traditional physical models, especially, in dealing with complex physical information, they show obvious advantages of system modeling and exploration. Additionally, two main approaches, Physics-informed Neural networks (PINNs) ~\citep{raissi2019physics, krishnapriyan2021characterizing, he2023learning} and Neural Operators ~\citep{hao2023gnot, kovachki2023neural, li2021physics}, are both utilized to to learn fluid and solid mechanisms. With formulating the explicit governing equation as the loss function, PINNs constrain the latent feature spaces to a certain range, effectively learning from small data or even without any labeled data. Such a novel method immediately attracts the attention of many researchers and has been utilized in a wide range of applications governed by differential equations, such as heat transfer problems ~\citep{cai2021physics}, power systems ~\citep{misyris2020physics}, medical science ~\citep{sahli2020physics}, and control of dynamical systems ~\citep{antonelo2024physics}. Meanwhile, neural operator combines basis transforms (e.g., Fourier, multipole kernel, wavelet ~\citep{gupta2021multiwavelet}) with neural networks to accelerate PDE solvers in diverse applications. For example,  FNO and its variants ~\citep{tran2021factorized,wen2022u,ashiqur2023u} learn parameters in the Fourier domain for turbulence simulation. Especially, geo-FNO ~\citep{li2024geometry} maps the irregular domain into an uniform mesh with a  specific geometric Fourier transform to fit irregular domains. However, they all follow the assumptions of periodicity and time-invariance property, making them fail in complex boundaries. As another paradigm, Transformer ~\citep{vaswani2017attention} and its ``x-former'' family have also been utilized to solve complex PDEs.  Although the attention mechanism results in higher complexity, many researchers are trying to alleviate this issue through various means. For example, linear attention ~\citep{li2022transformer,hao2023gnot} is a well-known method to address this limitation. Although the above methods alleviate the need for specific domain expertise, they all share the same limitations: instability and weak generalization ability.

\subsection{Graph solvers} 
Recent works in Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) ~\citep{liu2020towards} and geometric learning ~\citep{bronstein2017geometric} are abundant and attempt to utilize customized substructures to generalize message passing to more complex domains. For example, graph kernel methods ~\citep{anandkumar2020neural, li2020multipole} try to learn the implicit or explicit embedding in Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) for identifying differential equations. ~\citep{belbute2020combining} considers solving the problem of predicting fluid flow using GNNs. Meanwhile, Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs) ~\citep{gilmer2017neural,gilmer2020message} are utilized to tackle these issues, which learn latent representations on graphs via message passing mechanism. Especially, MeshGraphNets ~\citep{pfaff2021Learning} and MP-PDE ~\citep{brandstetter2022message} are two representative examples. While powerful and expressive, we still find that there are some problems with these methods: the expression ability is still not strong enough, the prediction error is still somewhat high, and they still rely on a large amount of data. Hence, we designed our model to address these three problems.


\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Feature-enhanced block}
\label{alg:fe_algorithm}
\textbf {\bfseries Input:} The current node states $\mathbf{h}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*D}$ \\
\textbf {\bfseries Parameter:} The weight tensor $\mathbf{W}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{D*D*D}$, a mask matrix $\mathbf{M}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{D*D}$\\
\textbf {\bfseries Output:} The updated node states $ \hat{\mathbf{h}}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*D}$ 
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Reshape the input states $\mathbf{h}^{l}$ and obtain the $\mathbf{h}^{l*} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*D*1}$ and $\mathbf{h}^{*l} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*1*D}$.
\STATE Multiply the $\mathbf{h}^{l*}$ and $\mathbf{h}^{*l}$ to get new states $\mathbf{h}^{*l*} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*D*D}$.
\STATE Filter the new states $\mathbf{h}^{*l*}$ by the mask matrix $\mathbf{M}^{l}$ to learn the states $\hat{\mathbf{h}}^{*l*} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*D*D}$.
\STATE Multiply the masked states $\hat{\mathbf{h}}^{*l*}$ with the weight $\mathbf{W}^{l}$ to construct the final states $\mathbf{h}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*D}$.
\STATE \textbf{return} The updated states $\hat{\mathbf{h}}^{l}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}



\section{Methodology}

Usually, various parameters  (e.g., the initial or boundary conditions, constant or variable coefficients) within a given spatiotemporal system create diverse visual effect. In this section, we aim to illustrate how our method can be effectively applied to learn the solution of spatiotemporal PDEs. All the source code and data would be posted after peer review.

\subsection{Network architecture}
\label{Network architecture}
To enhance the performance of long-range prediction of PDEs, we adapt the conventional ``Encoder-Processor-Decoder'' framework from ~\citep{pfaff2021Learning, brandstetter2022message} as the backbone of our method, which is primarily designed to effectively learn the complex spatiotemporal dependencies in graphs. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:architecture}, our proposed method consists of two key components:  (1) the Feature-Enhanced (FE) block, and (2) the Cell-embedded MPNN (CellMPNN) block. 
These two blocks update features in a sequential process to achieve the cascaded enhancement effect, described as follows: (1) step 1 -- call the CellMPNN block to update node-edge-cell features; (2) step 2 -- call the following FE block to enrich higher-order node features; (3) iteratively repeat the above steps 1 and 2 until the specified number of layers is reached. The synergy of these two sequentially placed blocks in turn improves the model's representation learning capacity and generalization ability.


\subsubsection{Feature-enhanced block}
This block is designed to enhance the latent features from the upstream block. When facing the problem of insufficient expression ability in GNNs, we regard these latent features as different pieces of information stored in a multidimensional space. Naturally, this problem turns into the issue of these latent features not holding the right and rich information. In a highly complex nonlinear network like GNNs, we need to find a way to provide or produce abundant information and select from them. The node representation at layer $l$ is given by ${\mathbf{h}}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{N*D}$ where $N$ is the number of nodes and $D$ the latent dimension. The corresponding feature updating form of node $i$ is described as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{h}}^{l}_{i} = \sigma^{l} \circ {\mathbf{W}}^{l} \circ {\mathbf{M}}^{l} \circ \left(\phi_{1}^{re}({\mathbf{h}}^{l}_{i}) * \phi_{2}^{re}({\mathbf{h}}^{l}_{i})\right),
\end{equation}
where the function $\phi_{1}^{re}: \mathbb{R}^{D} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{D*1}$ and $\phi_{2}^{re}: \mathbb{R}^{D} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{1*D}$ represent the features are reshaped to another sizes, ${\mathbf{W}}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{D*D*D}$ the learnable weight parameters, ${\mathbf{M}}^{l} \in \mathbb{R}^{D*D}$ the mask matrix, and $\sigma^{l}$ the activation function. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:architecture}, the procedure of FE block can summarized in Algorithm \ref{alg:fe_algorithm}, which alleviates the over-smoothness issue, commonly seen in GNNs where node features become similar due to excessive aggregation. With higher-order tensor representation, the learnable FE block maintains the distinction of node features, reducing similarity. Please see the ablation results on FE's efficacy in Table \ref{tab:Ablation Study}.

Consider the additional number of parameters in the FE module and their GPU memory requirements for training, we further provide a feature splitting scheme within latent features to dramatically reduce the number of parameters and computation cost caused by the full FE block, as described in Figure \ref{fig:fe}. Simply put, this strategy divides every feature into multiple sub-features with different window sizes, and then processes each part separately. Finally, these sub-features are combined for the learning of the next layer of the network. Note that there are many means to split the features, so the resulting performance will vary depending on the method used. In this article, we utilize the simplest window method, where the features are divided to 4 parts. Please see the ablation results of the feature splitting scheme in Table \ref{tab:impact_of_window_size_and_number_of_sub-features}.
\subsubsection{The main architecture of CeGNN}

\paragraph{\textit{Encoder}.}
As mentioned in Section \ref{fig:architecture}, our encoder block maps the low-dimensional variables to corresponding high-dimensional latent feature via differential functions (e.g., MLPs). The initial node features $\mathbf{h}_{i}^{0}$ include the node feature, one-hot feature of node type, and their position information. The initial edge features $\mathbf{e}_{ij}^{0}$ contain the relative position vector, the distance of neighbor nodes, and etc. The initial cell features the centroid position of cell, the area of cell, and the relative position vector from three nodes to their centriod position. The form is described as follows:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
\mathbf{h}_{i}^{0}&=\phi_{v}^{en} \left(\mathbf{u}_{i} , \mathbf{x}_{i} , \kappa_{i} , \dots \right),\\
\mathbf{e}_{ij}^{0}&=\phi_{e}^{en} \left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\right) , d_{ij} , \dots \right),\\
\mathbf{c}_{ijk}^{0}&=\phi_{c}^{en} \left(\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{\triangle ijk}\right),\left(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{\triangle ijk}\right),\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{\triangle ijk}\right), \mathbf{x}_{\triangle ijk}, A_{\triangle ijk}, \dots \right),
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the learnable functions $\phi_{v}^{en}(\cdot)$, $\phi_{e}^{en}(\cdot)$, and $\phi_{c}^{en}(\cdot)$ are applied to learn the latent features of node, edge, and cell; $(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{x}_{i})$ a relative position vector between the nodes $i$ and $j$; $d_{ij}$ the relative physical distance; $\kappa_{i}$ the $i$th node type; the $\mathbf{x}_{\triangle ijk}$ the centriod of the cell $\triangle ijk$; the $A_{\triangle ijk}$ the area of the cell $\triangle ijk$. In addition, $(\cdot , \cdot)$ denotes the concatenation operation.

\paragraph{\textit{Processor}.}
The processor iteratively processes the latent features via cell-embedded MPNN block. As discussed above, the key contribution of cell-embedded MPNN block lies in the introduction of the concept of cell. Figure \ref{fig:cell} depicts the process from ``node-edge'' interaction to ``node-edge-cell'' mode. Obviously, we divided the original edge channel $e_{ij}^{l}$ into two parts: itself and its adjacent cells. With this simple process, the node can exchanges information with itself (0-order), its immediate neighbors (1-order), and its adjacent cells (2-order). In general, the cell features $c^{l+1}_{ijk}$ and edge features $e^{l+1}_{ij}$ firstly learn the effective information from adjacent nodes features, and then these features are aggregated to formulate the next node states $h^{l+1}_{i}$. The procedure of CellMPNN block can be described by the following forms:
\begin{subequations}
    \begin{align}
    \mathbf{c}_{ijk}^{l+1}&=\phi_{c}^{l} \left (\mathbf{h}_{i}^{l},\mathbf{h}_{j}^{l},\mathbf{h}_{k}^{l},\mathbf{c}_{ijk}^{l} \right),\\
    \mathbf{e}_{ij}^{l+1}&=\phi_{e}^{l} \left (\mathbf{h}_{i}^{l},\mathbf{h}_{j}^{l},\mathbf{e}_{ij}^{l} \right),\\
    \mathbf{h}_{i}^{l+1}&=\phi_{v}^{l} \left (\mathbf{h}_{i}^{l},\sum\nolimits_{j \in \mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{e}_{ij}^{l+1},\sum\nolimits_{jk \in \mathcal{N}_{i}}\mathbf{c}_{ijk}^{l+1} \right),
    \end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $j \in \mathcal{N}_{i}$ represents every neighbor edge $ij$ at node $i$; $jk \in \mathcal{N}_{i}$ every neighbor cell $ijk$ at node $i$. $\phi_{c}^{l}, \phi_{e}^{l}, \phi_{v}^{l}$ are the differential functions of cell, edge, and node. Note that the message passing mechanism has been newly reformulated, where edge and cell features, without interaction, are used to simutaneously update the node features. Please see the additional results in Table \ref{tab:computational_cost_and_scalability}. 

\paragraph{\textit{Decoder}.}
The decoder maps the latent features back to physical variables on the underlying graph. With a skip connection, we acquire new states $u_{t_{k+1}}$ in the form of incremental learning. The form is described as following:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
    \hat{\mathbf{u}}_{i,t_{k+1}} =\phi_{v}^{de} \left(\mathbf{h}_{i}^{L} \right)+\mathbf{u}_{i,t_{k}},
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\phi_{v}^{de}(\cdot)$ is a differentiable function and $L$ the total number of CellMPNN layers.

\subsection{Experimental setup}
In our experiments, we mainly focus on predicting over much longer time steps and attempt to achieve better generalization ability of various initial conditions (ICs) and boundary conditions (BCs). For fairness, we set the latent dimension to 128 and utilize the one-step training strategy (i.e., one-step forward, one-step backward) for all tasks. All experiments are run on one NVIDIA A100 GPU. The Adaptive Moment Estimation (Adam) optimizer is utilized for the model training. All models are trained with Gaussian random noise of fixed standard deviation added to the input to improve stability during rollout and correct small errors. Meanwhile, a Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) loss function is utilized to optimize parameters $\theta$ in networks. Given the ground-truth values $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N*d}$ and the predictions $\hat{Y}\in \mathbb{R}^{N*d}$ at time $t$, the loss function is defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
    RMSE(Y, \hat{Y}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N*d} \sum_{i=i}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{d}\left( y_{ij}-\hat{y}_{ij}\right)^{2}},
\end{equation}
where $y_{ij}\in Y$ and $\hat{y}_{ij} \in \hat{Y}$.

All our experiments revolve around the following questions: \textit{\textbf{Can our model generalize well? Can our model achieve lower error with small data? }}

\paragraph{\textit{Generalization test}.} 
We varied the initial input field, randomly sampled from a Gaussian distribution with various means and standard deviations, in order to test the generalization ability. According to the results in Figures \ref{fig:main_result} and \ref{fig:bs_result}, we found that CeGNN generalizes to different ICs robustly on all datasets. It is evident that the performances of CeGNN and all baselines in the multi-step long-term prediction vary significantly. However, the experiment results of FNO on all systems show that it achieves almost the worst performance on all datasets but the Burgers equation, especialy when only small data is provided for training. All results in Table \ref{tab:Performance Summary} demonstrate that all graph-based models have great generalization ability. The advanced variant of GAT, GATv2, underperforms GAT on Burgers, FN, 2D GS RD, and BS datasets, but outperforms GAT on 3D GS RD equation, yet both methods fall short of MGN. Surprisingly, the performance of MP-PDE is mediocre. Although MP-PDE is trained by the multi-step prediction strategy during the training stage, its results are only slightly better than MGN on the BS dataset. In constrast, our method performs robustly with much smaller errors in the multi-step prediction problem on all datasets. 

\paragraph{\textit{Feature-enhanced effect}.}
We investigate the effectiveness of the FE block on all graph-based network over all datasets. The results are reported in Table \ref{tab:Effect}, showing that the feature-enhanced block somewhat changes the performance of these networks. We can directly see that this module has improved the performance of all baselines on real-world datasets. Specifically, it achieves the best and worst promotions in the cases of ``MGN + FE'' on the 3D GS RD equation and ``GATv2 + FE'' on the 2D Burgers equation. Intriguingly, after embedding the FE block in the processor block, the attention-based graph networks (e.g., GAT and GATv2) perform worse on the governing equation and better on the real-world dataset (even though the promotion is not large). 
The negative impact of the FE block on attention-based methods is due to a logical conflict of design motivation. The attention mechanism essentially aggregates the features by the normalized weighted summation. For example, consider the latent features of two adjacent nodes, $\mathbf{h}_1$ and $\mathbf{h}_2$, near a node with $\mathbf{h}_0$. The attention mechanism assigns weights $w_1$ and $w_2$ (e.g., $w_1+w_2 = 1$) and the aggregated feature is $w_1 \mathbf{h}_1+w_2 \mathbf{h}_2$. In this case, the combination of FE block and attention mechanism disrupts this global normalization rule and reduces the expression capability of attention-based methods.

\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we proposed an end-to-end graph-based framework (namely, CeGNN) to learn the complex spatiotemporal dynamics by utilizing the local information, CeGNN predicts future long-term unobserved states and addresses the over-smoothness problem in GNNs. Firstly, the learnable cell attribution in CellMPNN block captures the spatial dependency of regional features, upgrading the local aggregation scheme from the first order to a higher order. Secondly, the FE block enriches the node features, maintaining strong representational power even after multiple rounds of aggregation. The effectiveness of CeGNN has been proven through results on various datasets.

Although CeGNN achieves superior performance on extensive experiments, there are several directions for future work, including that (1) pushing our model to learn on a finer mesh with more complex boundary conditions, and (2) further exploring the potential of cell attribution to learn higher-order information in a more refined way, rather than the rough handling in our article. We attempt to accomplish these goals in our future research work.
