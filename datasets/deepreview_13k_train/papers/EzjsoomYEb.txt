\title{Topological Blindspots: Understanding and Extending Topological Deep Learning Through the Lens of Expressivity}

\begin{abstract}
    Topological deep learning (TDL) facilitates learning from data represented by topological structures. The primary model utilized in this setting is higher-order message-passing (HOMP), which extends traditional graph message-passing neural networks (MPNN) to diverse topological domains. Given the significant expressivity limitations of MPNNs, our paper aims to explore both the strengths and weaknesses of HOMP's expressive power and subsequently design novel architectures to address these limitations. We approach this from several perspectives: First, we demonstrate HOMP's inability to distinguish between topological objects based on fundamental topological and metric properties such as diameter, orientability, planarity, and homology. Second, we show HOMP's limitations in fully leveraging the topological structure of objects constructed using common lifting and pooling operators on graphs. Finally, we compare HOMP's expressive power to hypergraph networks, which are the most extensively studied TDL methods. We then develop two new classes of TDL models: multi-cellular networks (\ourmethod) and scalable multi-cellular networks (SMCN). These models draw inspiration from expressive graph architectures. While \ourmethod can reach full expressivity but is highly unscalable, \secondmethod offers a more scalable alternative that still mitigates many of HOMP's expressivity limitations. Finally, we construct a synthetic dataset, where TDL models are tasked with separating pairs of topological objects based on basic topological properties. We demonstrate that while HOMP is unable to distinguish between any of the pairs in the dataset, \secondmethod  successfully distinguishes all pairs, empirically validating our theoretical findings. Our work opens a new design space and new opportunities for TDL, paving the way for more expressive and versatile models. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Topological deep learning (TDL) is an emerging field focused on learning from data structured as topological objects or spaces 
Recent work \cite{hajij2022higher, hajij2022topological}, has introduced combinatorial complexes (CCs), a novel topological domain that unifies previously studied simplicial complexes, cellular complexes, and hypergraphs.  CCs are combinatorial objects composed of nodes and cells, combining the flexibility of hypergraphs for supporting multi-relational data with the hierarchical encoding capabilities of simplicial and cellular complexes through a rank function. This versatile structure is capable of representing diverse data types, from 3D meshes to molecules.

Higher-order message-passing (HOMP) \cite{hajij2022higher, hajij2022topological} has emerged as a key framework in topological deep learning, with applications spanning various complex structures. Originally introduced for simplicial complexes \cite{pmlr-v139-bodnar21a}, HOMP has been successively adapted for cellular complexes \cite{bodnar2021weisfeiler}, and more recently, for combinatorial complexes \cite{hajij2022higher, hajij2022topological}. Each adaptation represents a direct generalization of its predecessor. At its core, the HOMP architecture extends traditional message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) used on graphs \cite{gilmer2017neural} to higher-dimensional topological spaces by assigning a representation vector to each cell, which is iteratively updated using the representations of its neighboring cells.

Despite their widespread adoption in various learning applications on graphs, MPNNs are known to suffer from expressivity limitation, often failing to distinguish even simple non-isomorphic graphs \cite{morris2019weisfeiler, xu2018powerful}. This realization has inspired an exciting new subfield in graph learning, spurring the development of more expressive architectures \cite{morris2023weisfeiler}. In light of the similarity between HOMP and MPNNs, a natural question arises: \emph{What are the expressive capabilities of Higher-Order message-passing architectures on topological domains such as combinatorial complexes?} This intriguing question,  highlighted in a recent position paper \cite{papamarkou2024position}, opens up new avenues for exploration in topological learning.
\noindent\paragraph{Current paper.} In an attempt to answer this question, our paper aims to explore both the limitations and benefits of HOMP through the lens of expressivity. Subsequently, we design novel TDL architectures, building on the HOMP framework, mitigating the aforementioned expressivity limitations. 
In the first part of the paper, we investigate the expressive power of HOMP. To facilitate this, we introduce a topological criterion that can bu used to discern when a pair of combinatorial complexes is indistinguishable by HOMP. We then study the expressivity of HOMP from three key perspectives: (1) the ability to express topological and metric invariants; (2) the capacity to leverage pooling and lifting operations; and (3) the advantages of HOMP compared to established hypergraph architectures.
 
 We first discuss HOMP's expressive power limitations, investigating its ability to differentiate between complexes based on several well-known topological and metric invariants (which are also CC invariants), including diameter, orientability, planarity, and homology groups. For each invariant, we identify examples of complexes that differ on that specific invariant yet remain indistinguishable by HOMP. This reveals a paradoxical limitation of HOMP as one of the main tools of TDL: its inability to express fundamental topological and metric invariants. 
 
 Secondly, since many topological data objects are constructed using graph lifting and pooling operations, we examine HOMP's ability to fully leverage the information these operations provide. Our findings reveal instances of complexes, constructed from graphs using common operations such as triangular lifting \cite{pmlr-v139-bodnar21a} and MOG pooling \cite{hajij2018mog}, that HOMP fails to distinguish despite differing in natural topological properties.
 
 
 
Finally, we discuss the expressivity \emph{gains} of using HOMP compared to established hypergraph models adapted to the domain of combinatorial complexes. These models are the earliest and most extensively researched in TDL. Specifically, we compare HOMP to equivariant hypergraph neural networks (EHNNs), which were proven in \cite{kim2022equivariant} to be as expressive as several prominent hypergraph networks. We find that through its use of multiple neighborhood functions, HOMP is able to distinguish between complexes that EHNN is unable to. 

In the second part of the paper, we develop a new class of TDL neural networks, termed multi-cellular networks (\ourmethod), to address the limitations of HOMP. \ourmethod draws inspiration from high-order\footnote{In this paper, we use two notions of ``high-order''. The first is higher-order relational data which is leveraged by the HOMP framework. The second, is high-order graph models which reach higher expressivity on graph by using high-order tensors.} architectures on graphs \cite{maron2019provably,morris2019weisfeiler,keriven2019universal,azizian2020expressive}, which have successfully resolved expressivity limitations in graph message-passing. Specifically, \ourmethod utilizes the characterization of equivariant linear layers provided in \cite{maron2018invariant} and integrates them into the HOMP pipeline, resulting in a model reminiscent of the Invariant Graph Networks (IGNs) framework introduced in the same paper. We demonstrate that \ourmethod can reach full expressivity\footnote{Here we refer to the model's abillity to distinguish between any pair of non-isomorphic CCs.} for combinatorial complexes.

Recognizing the scalability challenges and implementation difficulties associated with the linear layers in both IGNs and \ourmethod, we propose a scalable version called scalable \ourmethod (\secondmethod).  This approach utilizes the fact that components of combinatorial complexes give rise to multiple graph structures and their corresponding adjacency matrices. \secondmethod applies expressive graph models, often used as practical alternatives to IGNs, to these graphs. The resulting approach is capable of leveraging the fact the higher-order cells often appear in small numbers and exhibit a sparse connectivity structure to create provably more expressive architectures with reduced computation overhead. We demonstrate that \secondmethod mitigates many of HOMP's inherent expressivity issues while maintaining the flexibility of the framework.

To validate our theoretical findings, we create the Torus dataset: a synthetic dataset comprised of pairs of CCs that are provably indistinguishable by HOMP. Our empirical results show that \secondmethod successfully distinguishes each pair, while HOMP fails to differentiate any of them, thus verifying our theoretical analysis.

\noindent\paragraph{Our contributions.} Summarising, the key contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We provide a comprehensive analysis of HOMP's expressive power, comparing it to hypergraph architectures and evaluating its ability to capture topological and metric invariants. We also identify specific limitations in HOMP's ability to leverage information via lifting and pooling operations.

    \item We introduce multi-cellular networks (\ourmethod), a novel class of combinatorial complex neural networks inspired by high-order graph architectures which we prove can reach full expressivity. 

    \item We develop \secondmethod, a scalable version of \ourmethod that addresses HOMP's expressivity limitations while maintaining computational efficiency.

    \item We introduce the Torus dataset, an open-source synthetic dataset that represents a first step towards creating synthetic benchmarks for evaluating the expressivity of topological deep learning models. We use this dataset to demonstrate the expressive power gains achieved by our architectures.  
    

\end{enumerate}



\section{Previous Work}

\paragraph{Topological deep learning.} TDL facilitates learning from data represented as a topological object. Specifically, TDL focuses on four primary object families: hypergraphs, simplicial complexes, cellular complexes, and combinatorial complexes. The most prominent deep learning model for the latter three is higher-order message-passing (HOMP). HOMP was initially defined for simplicial complexes in \cite{pmlr-v139-bodnar21a} and subsequently extended to cellular complexes in \cite{bodnar2021weisfeiler}. Both papers discuss the expressive power of HOMP in the context of graph neural networks (GNNs), demonstrating that these models, when combined with various lifting procedures, enhance the expressive capabilities of graph message-passing. Notably, in certain cases, HOMP models can distinguish between graphs that are $3$-WL indistinguishable. However, neither paper directly explores the expressivity limitations of HOMP regarding its ability to distinguish between different cellular or simplicial complexes beyond the scope of the graph WL test. Recently, HOMP was extended to the domain of combinatorial complexes in \cite{hajij2022higher, hajij2022topological}. This topological domain is the most general, combining the hierarchical structure induced by cellular complexes with the flexibility in modeling set relations of hypergraphs. One notable example of the benefits of using CCs is in molecular representation. Simplicial and cellular complexes are too rigid to capture the intricate hierarchical structures of molecules. In contrast, combinatorial complexes, with their flexible yet hierarchical hyperedges, can fully capture these molecular structures. Another advantage of this approach is the seamless integration of a large variety of lifting and pooling operators into the learning framework through its flexibility in  constructing  higher-order cells \footnote{The outputs of some of these operators can be represented as simplicial or cellular complexes, but the flexibility of combinatorial complexes allows for a much more diverse set of operators.}. Several models building on the HOMP framework have been proposed. \cite{battiloro2024n} extends HOMP to combinatorial complexes with geometric cell features, by introducing $E(n)$-equivariant topological neural networks. \cite{verma2024topological} incorporates persistent homology into HOMP to enhance its expressive power. We emphasize that, to the best of our knowledge, most previous works have explored the expressivity of TDL models primarily in relation to graph architectures. Specifically, the ability of HOMP  to learn topological and metric invariants in order to distinguish between non-isomorphic combinatorial complexes is unexplored to date.

\paragraph{Expressive power of GNNs.} The expressivity of GNNs is typically evaluated based on their ability to assign distinct values to non-isomorphic graphs. Seminal works by \cite{morris2019weisfeiler, xu2018powerful} demonstrated that the separation power of message-passing neural networks (MPNNs) is equivalent to the limited separation power of the $1$-WL algorithm for the graph isomorphism problem \cite{weisfeiler1968reduction}. These findings inspired the development of more expressive GNNs, which surpass the $1$-WL test's capabilities, albeit often requiring greater computational resources. For example, \cite{morris2019weisfeiler, maron2018invariant} proposed architectures equivalent to the more powerful $k$-WL algorithm. Additionally, various other expressive GNNs have been introduced in the literature, utilizing techniques such as random features \cite{abboud2020surprising}, subgraph counts \cite{bouritsas2022improving}, equivariant polynomials \cite{puny2023equivariant}, and more. Among these approaches, PPGN \cite{maron2019provably} and subgraph networks \cite{bevilacqua2021equivariant, frasca2022understanding, zhang2023complete, zhang2021nested, cotta2021reconstruction}  stand out for offering strong expressive power with relatively low runtime overhead. We later use these frameworks to construct an efficient, expressive model for combinatorial complexes. For a comprehensive review, refer to the following surveys: \cite{jegelka2022theory, morris2023weisfeiler, zhang2023expressive}.



\section{Preliminaries}

\paragraph{Notation.} Graphs are denoted by \(G = (V, E)\), where \(V\) are the nodes and \(E\) are the edges. Multisets are denoted by \(\ldblbrace \rdblbrace\). The size of a set \(S\) is denoted by \(|S|\). We define \([n] = \{1, 2, \dots, n\}\) and \([n]_0 = \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}\). The vector $\mathbf{e}_{i} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ represents the unit vector with one at the $i$-th positions and zeros elsewhere.

\paragraph{Combinatorial complexes.}
Most topological domains, including simplicial complexes, cellular complexes, and hypergraphs, can be considered subclasses of combinatorial complexes. Thus, we begin by providing their precise definition. For definitions of simplicial and cellular complexes, we refer the reader to \cite{pmlr-v139-bodnar21a} and \cite{bodnar2021weisfeiler}.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[]{definition}{CombinatorialComplexDef}
\label{def: Combinatorial Complex}
 A combinatorial complex (CC) is a triplet $(S, \mathcal{X} , \rk)$ consisting of a set $S$, a subset $\mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(S) \setminus \emptyset$, and a function $\rk: \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ with the following properties:
 \begin{enumerate}
     \item For all $s \in S$, $\{s\} \in \mathcal{X}$.
     \item The function $\rk$ is order-preserving, i.e. if $x, y \in \mathcal{X}$ satisfy $x \subset y$, then $\rk(x) < \rk(y)$.
 \end{enumerate}
\end{restatable}
Elements of $S$ are called nodes or vertices, elements of $\mathcal{X}$ are called cells, and $\rk$ is the rank function of the CC. The set of cells of rank $i$ in a CC is called the $i$-skeleton and is denoted by \(\mathcal{X}_i\).

\paragraph{Neighborhood functions.} Neighborhood functions are a key component in HOMP, enabling dynamic aggregation of information across different cells. For a given CC, \( C = (S, \mathcal{X}, \rk) \), a neighborhood function is a function \(\mathcal{N}:\mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\). The four most common neighborhood functions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The \((i,j)\)-adjacency and co-adjacency, defined for $x \in \mcalX_i$ by: 
    \begin{equation} \label{eq: k-adjacency-co-adjacency}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{A}_{i,j}(x) &=  \{ y \in \mathcal{X}_i \mid \exists z \in \mathcal{X}_j \text{ s.t. } x, y \subseteq z \} 
    \end{aligned}
    \qquad
    \begin{aligned}
        \operatorname{co}\mathcal{A}_{i,j}(x) &= \{ y \in \mathcal{X}_i \mid \exists z \in \mathcal{X}_j \text{ s.t } z \subseteq x, y \}
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
    
    \item The $(i,j)$-upper and lower incidence, defined for $x \in \mcalX_i$ by:
    \begin{equation}\label{eq: k-upper-incidence}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{B}_{i,j}(x) &= \{ y \in \mathcal{X}_j \mid x \subseteq y \} 
    \end{aligned}
    \qquad
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathcal{B}^\top_{i,j}(x) &= \{ y \in \mathcal{X}_j \mid y \subseteq x \}
    \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Note that for $x \notin \mcalX_i$, all of the above $(i,j)$ neighborhood functions return an empty set. These neighborhood functions are often represented in matrix form \footnote{We note that to encode properties such as the relative orientation of cells, the entries of these matrices can be signed. However, in this paper, we focus on the non-signed case.}. We call the set of all neighborhood functions belonging to one of these four types the natural set of neighborhoods and denote it by $\mathcal{N}_{\text{nat}}$.
As an example, we can represent a graph \(G = (V, E)\) as a combinatorial complex \(C = (S, \mathcal{X}, \rk)\) by setting \(S = V\), \(\mathcal{X}_0 = \{\{ v \} \mid v \in V\}\), and \(\mathcal{X}_1 = E\). In this case, the matrix form of the adjacency neighborhood function \(\mathcal{A}_{0,1}\) is the graph adjacency matrix, while the incidence neighborhood function \(\mathcal{B}_{0,1}\) in matrix form corresponds to the graph incidence matrix. 

\paragraph{Cochain maps.} 
Data supported on the cells of an $\ell$ dimensional CC can be viewed as a collection of functions $\{h_i: \mathcal{X}_i \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d_i}\}_{i \in [\ell]_0}$. Note that, in many cases, the feature dimensions of cells of different ranks differ, so in general \( d_i \neq d_j \). Each of these functions is called a \textit{cochain map} or a \textit{cell feature map}. The space of all cochain maps is denoted by \(\mathcal{C}^i(\mathcal{X}, \mathbb{R}^{d_i})\), often abbreviated as \(\mathcal{C}^i\). The cell feature of \(x \in \mathcal{X}\) is denoted by \((h_i)_x\), or simply \(h_x\) when the rank is clear from the context.

\paragraph{Higher-order message-passing.}
Let \(\mathcal{CN}\) be a collection of neighborhood functions. Higher-order message-passing (HOMP) \cite{hajij2022topological} is defined via the following update rule:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: high order Message Passing simple}
    h^{t+1}_x = \beta \left(\bigotimes_{\mathcal{N} \in \mathcal{CN}} \bigoplus_{y \in \mathcal{N}(x)} M^t_{\mathcal{N}, \mathrm{rk}(x)}(h^{t}_x, h^{t}_y) \right).
\end{equation}
where $h^t_x$ is the feature associated with cell $x \in \mcalX$ at layer $t$, $\beta$ is a non-linear activation, \(\bigoplus\) is a permutation-invariant aggregation function, \(\bigotimes\) is an aggregation function (possibly not permutation-invariant), and \(M^t_{\mathcal{N}, \rk(x)}\) is an MLP that depends on the layer \(t\), neighborhood \(\mathcal{N}\), and cell rank \(\rk(x)\). In the following, unless specified otherwise we assume $\mathcal{CN}$ is the set of natural neighborhoods $\mathcal{N}_\text{nat}$. For complex level tasks, we add a final readout layer of the form:
\begin{equation}
     h_\text{out} = \beta \left(\bigotimes_{ i \in [\ell]_0 } \bigoplus_{x \in \mcalX_i} M^T_{ i}(h^{T}_x) \right).
\end{equation}
were $\ell$ is the dimension of the CC, $T$ is the final layer of the model, and  \(\bigoplus\), \(\bigotimes\),  \(M^T_{i}\) are defined as before.

Update rules based on multiple neighborhood functions and diverse cochain spaces offer considerable flexibility, enabling selective updating of features based on specific neighborhood structures. To navigate the space of HOMP architectures \cite{hajij2022higher} introduce tensor diagrams, a notation scheme for specifying HOMP architectures using directed acyclic graphs. Tensor diagrams allow for selective aggregation using different neighborhood functions for different cochain spaces in different layers of the network. The nodes of a tensor diagram correspond to cochain spaces, and the edges to neighborhood functions. At each level of the diagram the signal flows from the source nodes to the target nodes using the update rule specified in Equation \ref{eq: high order Message Passing simple}, where aggregation is performed only over neighborhood functions associated with the incoming edges of the target nodes. Example of a tensor diagram can be seen in Figure \ref{fig:homp_tensor_diagram}. For a more thorough overview of tensor diagrams, we refer the reader to \cite{hajij2022topological}.  

\paragraph{Equivariant linear layers.}
In this paper, we draw inspiration from a specific type of GNN: \( k \)-invariant graph networks (\( k \)-IGNs), which were constructed in \cite{maron2018invariant}. These networks offer a principled approach for creating GNNs that are as expressive as the \( k \)-WL test \cite{maron2019provably}. They achieve this by constructing a basis for the space of \textit{equivariant} linear maps $L: \mathbb{R}^{n^{k} \times d} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n^{k'} \times d'}$, i.e. linear maps that satisfy:
\begin{equation}
    L(\sigma \cdot \mathbf{x}) = \sigma \cdot L(\mathbf{x}) \quad \forall \sigma \in S_n, \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{n^{k} \times d}
\end{equation}
where $S_n$ is the symmetric group on $n$ elements, and $\cdot$ denotes its standard action on space $\mathbb{R}^{n^{k} \times d}$:
\begin{equation}
    (\sigma \cdot \mathbf{x})_{i_1, \dots, i_k} = \mathbf{x}_{\sigma^{-1}(i_1), \dots, \sigma^{-1}(i_k)}
\end{equation}
\cite{maron2018invariant} then use this basis to construct equivariant layers, which consist of a linear combination of these basis elements with learnable weights as coefficients, interleaved with pointwise non-linearities. This approach is feasible because the number of basis elements, and thus the number of learnable parameters, is independent of \( n \). \cite{maron2019provably} proves that \( k \)-IGNs are as expressive as the \( k \)-WL test. They additionally introduces a new GNN architecture, based on matix multiplication, called PPGN. PPGN is as expressive as $3$-IGNs, while having a lower memory requirement, making it a more scalable alternative. 



\section{Expressivity of Higher-Order Message-Passing}

We then examine the expressivity of HOMP from three distinct perspectives: First, we demonstrate HOMPs limited ability to distinguish between CCs based on topological and metric information. Next, we assess HOMP's ability to leverage the information provided by pooling and lifting operators. Finally, we compare the expressive power of HOMP to that of hypergraph networks.

\subsection{A Topological Criterion for HOMP Indistinguishability}
\label{subsec: topological criterion for HOMP}
The expressivity of graph models is often evaluated based on their ability to distinguish between pairs of non-isomorphic graphs. Generalizing to other TDL domains, we develope tools to understand when a pair of CCs is indistinguishable by HOMP. This is done by extending the topological graph WL indistinguishability characterization introduced by \cite{bamberger2022topological} to combinatorial complexes. As a first step, we introduce the notion of a covering CC. 
\newline
\begin{restatable}[]{definition}{coveringInformal}
\label{def: covering informal}
A combinatorial complex $\tilde{C} = (\tilde{S},\tilde{\mcalX},\tilde{\rk} )$ is a cover of a combinatorial complex $C = (S,\mcalX,\rk)$ if there exists a surjective map $\rho: \tilde{\mcalX} \rightarrow \mcalX$ which is a local isomorphism with respect to all neighborhood functions.
\end{restatable} 
Here, a surjective map $\rho: \tilde{\mcalX}  \rightarrow \mcalX$ is said to be a local isomorphism if it bijectively maps each $\mcalN(\tilde{x})$ to $\mcalN(\rho(x))$ for all cells $\tilde{x} \in \tilde{\mcalX}$ and all neighborhood functions $\mcalN \in \mcalN_\text{nat}$. The next theorem describes the relation between CC coverings and HOMP indistinguishabillity:
\newline
\begin{restatable}[HOMP cannot distinguish CCs covered by the same space.]{theorem}{CoveringThm}
\label{thm: covering}
 Let  $C =(S, \mathcal{X}, \rk)$,  $C^* =(S^*, \mathcal{X}^*, \rk^*)$ be two CCs of dimension $\ell$ with no cell features. Assume that $C $  and $C^*$ admit decompositions into connected components:
 \begin{equation}
     C = \bigsqcup_{j \in \mathcal{J}}C_{j} \text{ and }  C^* = \bigsqcup_{j^* \in \mathcal{J}^*}C^*_{j^*}
 \end{equation}
 such that the following holds:
 \begin{enumerate}
     \item  For every $ i \in [\ell]_0$, $|\mcalX_i| = |\mcalX^*_i|$.
     \item There exists a complex $\tilde{C}$ that covers all complexes $C_{j}$ and $C^*_{j^*}$ for all $j \in \mathcal{J}$ and $j^* \in \mathcal{J}^*$.
 \end{enumerate}
 Then for every HOMP model $M$ we have:
 \begin{equation}
     M(C) = M(C^*).
 \end{equation}
\end{restatable}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
    We provide a proof sketch for the case where both $C$ and $C^*$ are connected and are covered by the same CC. Let $\tilde{C}$ be a CC that covers $C$ and $C^*$ via the maps $\rho: \tilde{\mcalX} \rightarrow \mcalX$ and $\rho^*: \tilde{\mcalX} \rightarrow \mcalX^*$ respectively. 
    Let $M$ be an HOMP model with $T$ layers and let \(h^{t}_{x}\), \((h^*)^ t_{x^*}\), and \(\tilde{h}^{t}_{\tilde{x}}\) denote the cell feature maps of \(C\), \(C^*\), and \(\tilde{C}\) at layer \(t\) and cells \(x\), \(x^*\), and \(\tilde{x}\) respectively.
  First notice that since all CCs mentioned above have no cellular feature maps, they satisfy:
    \begin{equation}
        h^{0} = (h^*)^0 = \tilde{h}^{0} = \text{Const}.
    \end{equation}
    Secondly,  since $\rho, \rho^*$ are local isomorphisms, the HOMP update in Equation \ref{eq: high order Message Passing simple} can be used to show by induction that for all $t \in [T], \tilde{x} \in \tilde{\mcalX}$ we have:
    \begin{equation}
        \tilde{h}^{t}_{\tilde{x}} = h^{t}_{\rho(\tilde{x})} = (h^*)^t_{\rho^*(\tilde{x})}
    \end{equation}
In addition, since $C$ is connected, we can show that $\forall x \in \mcalX$,  $|\rho^{-1}(x)| = \frac{|\tilde{S}|}{|S|}$.  This implies that the multiset $\ldblbrace \tilde{h}^{T}_x \mid \tilde{x} \in \tilde{\mcalX}\rdblbrace$ is composed of $\frac{\tilde{S}}{S}$ copies of the multiset $\ldblbrace h^{T}_x \mid x \in \mcalX\rdblbrace$. The same reasoning shows that $\ldblbrace \tilde{h}^{T}_x \mid \tilde{x} \in \tilde{\mcalX}\rdblbrace$ is composed of $\frac{\tilde{S}}{S^*}$ copies of $\ldblbrace (h^*)^T_x \mid x \in \mcalX^*\rdblbrace$. Since the two CCs are of the same size, $\frac{\tilde{S}}{S} = \frac{\tilde{S}}{S^*}$ and so the two multisets are equal, proving they are indistinguishable by HOMP.
\end{proof}

As HOMP has been established as a key framework within topological deep learning, a natural question arises: can it utilize common topological or metric properties to distinguish between different CCs? This section answers this question negatively for four fundamental properties.

\paragraph{HOMP cannot distinguish CCs based on diameter.} In its most general form, the diameter of a CC w.r.t a (co)adjacency matrix $(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i,j}$ and an integer  $k \geq i$ is defined by:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq: diameter main}
    \text{diam}^k_{(\mathrm{co})\mathcal{A}_{i,j}}(C) = \max_{\substack{x\in \mcalX_i \\ y\in \mcalX_k }} \min_{\substack{x' \in \mcalX_i \\ x' \subseteq y}} d_{(\mathrm{co})\mathcal{A}_{i,j}}(x,x').
\end{equation}
were $d_{(\mathrm{co})\mathcal{A}_{i,j}}$ is the shortest path distance with respect to neighborhood function $\mcalA_{i,j}$. In the case $k=i$ this reduces to
\begin{equation}
    \text{diam}_{(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i, j}}(C) = \max_{x, y \in \mcalX_i} d_{(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i, j}}(x, y)
\end{equation}
The diameter of a CC is a natural metric invariant which intuitively measures how ``spread out'' the CC is. There are several examples of graphs with different diameters which are indistinguishable by graph message-passing (e.g. circular skip link graphs \cite{murphy2019relational, dwivedi2023benchmarking}). The following proposition shows that HOMP suffers from the same limitation.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{PropDiameterInformal}
\label{prop: diameter_informal}
There exist \(\ell\)-dimensional tori with different diameters that HOMP cannot distinguish.
\end{restatable}

Note that we are referring to a natural realization of the torus as a CC defined as a product of cyclic graphs of lengths $r_1, \dots, r_\ell$. See Figure \ref{fig:diameter} e.g. for a visualization for the two dimentional case (product of two cycles).
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch] 
Let $T$ be an $\ell$-dimensional torus with cycle lengths $r_1,\dots r_\ell$. For any sequence of integers $ n_1, \dots, n_\ell$ the torus $\tilde{T}$ whose cycle lengths are $r_1\cdot n_1,\dots r_\ell \cdot n_\ell$ covers $T$ by ``wrapping'' around it $n_i$ times along the $i$-th axis. Let $T^*$ be a torus with cycle lengths $r^*_1,\dots r^*_\ell$ such that $\prod_{i=1}^\ell r_i = \prod_{i=1}^\ell r^*_i $. $\forall i \in [\ell]_0$, the number of $i$-rank cells in $T$ and $T^*$ is the same. Additionally, $T$ and $T^*$ are both covered by a torus with cycle lengths $r_1\cdot r^*_1 , \dots, r_\ell \cdot r^*_\ell$ and are therefore indistinguishable by HOMP accoridng to Theorem \ref{thm: covering}. In addition, $r_1, \dots, r_\ell$ and $r_1^*, \dots, r_\ell^*$ can be choses such that $T$ and $T^*$ have different diameters. To see that e.g. for the diameter corresponding the adjacency $\mcalA_{0, 1}$, note that  the graph induced on the nodes of $T$ by neighborhood function $\mcalA_{0,1}$ is the Cartesian product of the cyclic graphs $\text{Cyc}(r_1), \dots ,\text{Cyc}(r_\ell)$. Therefore:
\begin{equation}
    \text{diam}^0_{\mcalA_{0,1}}(T_{r_1, \dots , r_\ell}) = \sum_{i=1}^\ell \text{diam}(\text{Cyc}(r_i)) = \sum_{i=1}^\ell \left\lfloor \frac{r_i}{2} \right \rfloor.
\end{equation}
Thus, choosing $r^*_1, \dots, r^*_\ell$ such that $\sum_{i=1}^\ell \left \lfloor \frac{r_i}{2} \right \rfloor \neq \sum_{i=1}^\ell \left \lfloor \frac{r^*_i}{2} \right \rfloor$ will result in indistinguishable tori of different diameters.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{HOMP cannot detect orientability or planarity.} The second property we focus on orientability, which intuitively captures whether a space has a consistent ``side'' or direction across its entire structure. The following proposition implies that this property cannot learned by HOMP:
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{ProporientabilityInformall}
\label{prop: orientability informal}
HOMP cannot differentiate between a Möbius strip and a cylinder, despite the former being non-orientable and the latter being orientable.
\end{restatable}
Note, that we are referring to a natural realization of the Möbius strip and the cylinder as CCs, illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:orientability}.
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
    Let \(C\) be a cylinder with height \(h\) and cycle length \(r\), and let \(C^*\) be a Möbius strip with the same cycle length and height. The cylinder is orientable, while the Möbius strip is not (see, e.g. \cite{MR1867354}). Since both CCs are of the same size and are connected, Theorem \ref{thm: covering} implies that to show that \(C\) and \(C^*\) are indistinguishable by HOMP, it is sufficient to find a common covering. Let \(\tilde{C}\) be a cylinder with height \(h\) and cycle length \(2r\). \(\tilde{C}\) covers \(C\) by ``wrapping'' around it twice along the perimeter. Similarly, \(\tilde{C}\) covers \(C^*\) by ``wrappin'' and ``twisting'' around it twice along the perimeter, see Figure \ref{fig:topological_covering} for an illustration.
\end{proof}
A topological space is considered planar if it can be continuously embedded in \(\mathbb{R}^2\). The Möbius strip is not planar (see e.g. Therefore, we get the following as a corollary of Proposition \ref{prop: orientability informal}:
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{corollary}{PlanarityInformalCor}
\label{cor: planarity informal}
HOMP cannot differentiate between cellular complexes based on the planarity of their induced topology.
\end{restatable}

\paragraph{HOMP cannot distinguish CCs based on homology.}  Finally, we examine the homology groups of CCs.
The $i$-th homology group of a cellular complex \footnote{Homology is note defined for general combinatorial complexes, only for simplicial / cellular complexes.} encodes the structure of ``$i$-dimensional holes'' in the space (e.g. a circle has a singe 1-dimensional hole, a sphere has a single 2-dimensional hole etc). Since cellular complexes directly generalize simplicial complexes, we will refer to cellular complexes for the rest of the section. However, all results apply to simplicial complexes as well. For an in depth discussion on both cellular complexes and homology groups see \cite{MR1867354}. The following proposition states that HOMP is unable to distinguish between CCs based on their homology groups:
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{PropHomologyInformal}
\label{prop: homology informal}
HOMP fails to differentiate between a single larger \(\ell\)-dimensional torus and a CC composed of two smaller disconnected tori, despite these CCs having distinct homology groups for all \(i \in [\ell]\).
\end{restatable}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Let $T = (S, \mcalX, \rk)$ be an $\ell$-dimensional torus and let $T^* = (S^*, \mcalX^*, \rk^*)$ be a CC composed of two disconnected tori, $T_1$ and $T_2$ such that $|S| = |S^*|$. As discussed before, The tori $T, T_1, T_2$ all have a common cover. Thus, since $T$ and $T^*$ are of the same size, and can be decomposed into connected components which are all covered by the same space, Theorem \ref{thm: covering} implies these two CCs are indistinguishable by HOMP. On the other hand, the homology groups of $T$ are:
\begin{equation}
    H_i(T) = \mathbb{Z}^{\binom{l}{i}}.
\end{equation}
and since $T^*$ is composed of two disconnected tori, we have:
\begin{equation}
     H_i(T^*) =  H_i(T_1) \times H_i(T_2) = \mathbb{Z}^{2\binom{l}{i}}.
\end{equation}
Thus, for all \(i \in [\ell]_0\), $T$ and $T^*$ have distinct $i$-th homology groups. 
\end{proof}
 These three propositions demonstrate that despite HOMP's wide adoption for TLD tasks, it is unable to leverage many fundamental topological and metric properties.
\subsection{Lifting and Pooling}
\label{subsec: lifting and pooling}
One of the main advantages of combinaroial complexes is their flexability in integrating lifting and pooling operations to define higher-order cells. Common graph lifting procedures\footnote{We note that while there are many types of lifting procedures, structural lifts are among the most commonly used and well-studied methods, aside from pooling procedures. Therefore, we focus on them in this paper.}, such as those described in \cite{bodnar2021weisfeiler, pmlr-v139-bodnar21a}, add cells by detecting meaningful substructures that standard graph message-passing procedures cannot detect. This approach provably improves expressivity while adding a relatively small number of cells for many natural data domains (e.g. molecules often contain only a few instances of these substructures). Graph pooling procedures\footnote{While pooling operators can be viewed a subclass of lifting operators, they historically predate them and are often treated as a distinct field of research. Therefore, we choose to distinguish between the two.}, such as spectral pooling \cite{ma2019graph}, Mapper \cite{singh2007topological, hajij2018mog, dey2016multiscale}, and DiffPool \cite{Ying2018HierarchicalGR}, coarsen information from the input graph in a sparse manner, thus allowing for more efficient learning.

A commpon property of many lifting and pooling procedures is their ability to produce informative combinatorial complexes with a low number of higher-order cells. These CCs typically differ in fundamental topological and metric properties such as those discussed in the previous section. Furthermore, the sparsity of higher-order cells often enables efficient computation of these properties, offering a straightforward method to distinguish the original graphs. The following proposition demonstrates that despite these advantages, there are instances where HOMP is unable to leverage these properties to distinguish between the resulting CCs.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{PropPoolingLiftingInpormal}
\label{prop: pooling and lifting informal}
There exist pairs of combinatorial complexes, produced by applying standard lifting and pooling operators to non-isomorphic graphs, that HOMP fails to distinguish. This occurs even though the resulting combinatorial complexes  differ in simple topological properties which can be computed efficiently because of the sparsity of higher-order cells.
\end{restatable}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We show this result for triangular lifting, a fundamental lifting procedure first defined in \cite{bodnar2021weisfeiler}. Given two integers $n,k \in \mathbb{N}$ where $k > 3$,  the star graph $\text{Star}_{n,k}$ is constructed by gluing $k$ triangles to a cyclic graph of length $n\cdot k$. For a rigorous definition see the proof of Proposition \ref{prop: Inability to use lifting well}. Define $G = \text{Star}_{n,2k}$ and $G^* = \text{Star}_{n,k} \sqcup \text{Star}_{n,k}$ where $\sqcup$ represents disjoint union, and let $C$, $C^*$ be the combinatorial complexes obtained by applying triangular lifting on $G$, $G^*$ . $C$ and $C^*$ are depicted in Figure \ref{fig: triangilar lifting CCs}. Notice that $\text{diam}^2_{\mathcal{A}_{0,1}}(C) < \infty $ while $\text{diam}^2_{\mathcal{A}_{0,1}}(C^*) = \infty$, so $C$ and $C^*$ can be distinguished by computing $\text{diam}^2_{\mathcal{A}_{0,1}}$. Furthermore, $\text{diam}^2_{\mathcal{A}_{0,1}}$ can be computed more efficiently than e.g. $\text{diam}^0_{\mathcal{A}_{0,1}}$, since it only requires calculating distances between pairs of nodes where one node is within a triangle. Given the sparsity of triangles, this approach can significantly reduce computation time. Even though $C$ and $C^*$ can be efficiently distinguished, since $C$ and $C^*$ are of the same size, and each connected component of $C^*$ can be covered by $C$, Theorem \ref{thm: covering} implies these two CCs are indistinguishable by HOMP. 
\end{proof}

\subsection{Comparison to Hypergraph Networks}
\label{subsec: comparison to hypergraphs}
} are the oldest and most extensively studied domain in topological deep learning. Both hypergraphs and combinatorial complexes capture higher-order information. However, combinatorial complexes introduce a rank function that induces a hierarchy over the cells, and exhibit multiple neighborhood functions. Since hypergraph architectures support the use of hyperedge features, the rank function can be seamlessly integrated into these architectures. Consequently, hypergraph networks can take combinatorial complexes as input. This raises a natural question: what are the expressivity benefits of HOMP over combinatorial complexes compared to hypergraph networks? To address this, we compare HOMP with the EHNN hypergraph architecture, which has been shown to be as expressive as several prominent hypergraph networks in \cite{kim2022equivariant}.  Our first result states that the expressive power of HOMP networks which only use (upper and lower) incidence neighborhoods is bounded by that of EHNNs:
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{PropEHNNAsExpressiveinformal}
\label{prop: EHNN is as expressive as HOMP with incidence neighborhoods informal}
EHNN, which incorporates the rank of cells as a hyperedge feature, is as expressive as HOMP that uses only incidence neighborhoods.
\end{restatable}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
EHNN updates its cell features the following way:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: EHNN update main}
h^{t+1}_x = \beta\left(\sum_{y \in \mathcal{X}}h^{t}_y \cdot W^{t}(|x|, |y|, |x \cap y|) + B^{t}(|x|)\right).      
\end{equation}
Here $x,y \in \mcalX$ are cells in the input CC, $W^t, B^t$ are MLPs and $\beta$ is a non-linear activation function. For each such $x,y$ we focus on the term:
\begin{equation}
    m_{x,y} = h^{t}_y \cdot W^{t}(|x|, |y|, |x \cap y|) + h^{t}_x \cdot W^{t}(|x|, |x|, |x \cap x|).
\end{equation}
Since the last coordinates of the initial cell features \(h^0_x\) and \(h^0_y\) encode the ranks of \(x\) and \(y\), respectively, we can propagate this information and assume that the last coordinates of \(h^t_x\) and \(h^t_y\) at any iteration \(t\) also encode these ranks. In addition, note that $x \subseteq y$ if and only if $|x| = |x\cap y|$. Since we have access to both \(|x|\) and \(|x \cap y|\), we can determine whether \(x\) and \(y\) are neighbors with respect to any upper or lower adjacency neighborhood. This access to the ranks of $x,y$ and their incidence information allows us to adjust the update in Equation \ref{eq: EHNN update main} and reconstruct the HOMP update in Equation\ref{eq: high order Message Passing simple} which uses only incidence neighborhoods.
\end{proof}
 The last proposition suggests that from an expressivity standpoint, the use of the rank function alone is not enough to justify the use of HOMP over hypergraph networks. Our next proposition shows that when using both incidence and adjacency neighborhoods, the expressivirty of HOMP with respect to EHNN improves.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{PropEHNNLessExpressiveInpormal}
\label{prop: EHNN is less expressive then HOMP with incidence neighborhoods informal}
There exists a pair of combinatorial complexes with cell features that HOMP, utilizing both adjacency and incidence neighborhoods, can distinguish, but EHNN cannot.
\end{restatable}

\begin{proof}[Proof Sketch]
    Let $C = (S,\mcalX, \rk)$ and $C^*= (S,\mcalX^*, \rk^*)$ be the two CCs depicted in Figure \ref{fig:EHNN_vs_HOMP} and let $h, h^*$ represent  their corresponding cell feature maps ($0$-cells marked in blue have feature value $1$  while all other cells have feature value $0$). Note that \(C\) and \(C^*\) are structurally isomorphic, differing only in their cell features. First, notice that the degrees of all marked 0-cells  with respect to neighborhood function $\mcalA_{0,1}$  is $4$ in $C$ but $3$ in $C^*$. Thus HOMP can easily distinguish between $C$ and $C^*$. The cells of both \(C\) and \(C^*\) fall into three distinct classes: $1$-cells, marked $0$-cells, and unmarked $0$-cells. A thorough examination reveals that the EHNN updates for all cells within the same class are identical in both \(C\) and \(C^*\). Since the number of cells in each class is the same for \(C\) and \(C^*\), EHNN cannot distinguish between the two.
\end{proof}

While we hypothesize that EHNN can also distinguish between pairs of CCs that are indistinguishable by HOMP, making the two models incomparable, we leave the validation of this statement for future work. In contrast to the previous result, Proposition \ref{prop: EHNN is less expressive then HOMP with incidence neighborhoods informal} highlights some of the advantages of CC basesd HOMP over hypergraph models. In an attempt to understand the source of this improvement, we observe that the information expressed by the adjacency neighborhood function can be thought out as \emph{multi-cellular} -- two cells are adjacent if there exists a third cell that contains both. This suggests that leveraging this type of information may result in expressivity gains. As we develop our model, extending HOMP using multi-cellular information, we see that this is indeed the case.



\section{Multi-cellular Networks}

\label{sec: method}
We have explored various examples of combinatorial complexes that differ in natural topological or metric properties, which HOMP models cannot distinguish. Furthermore, even when integrating common lifting and pooling procedures that emphasize various aspects of the underlying topology of the data in a sparse manner, there remain instances where the HOMP model fails to distinguish between simple examples of combinatorial complexes. Similar expressivity problems in the realm of GNNs have been addressed by invariant graph networks (IGNs), as demonstrated in \cite{maron2019provably, keriven2019universal, azizian2020expressive}. These models employ stacks of equivariant linear layers between tensor spaces. We propose a similar approach by incorporating equivariant linear layers into the current HOMP architecture. We integrate these layer by introducing new node and edge types into existing HOMP tensor diagrams. We first define multi-cellular cochain spaces, which serve as an analogue to the tensor spaces used in constructing the linear layers of IGNs. 

\subsection{Multi-cellular Cochain Spaces} 
The multi-cellular cochain space associated with an \(\ell\)-dimensional combinatorial complex \(C = (S, \mathcal{X}, \mathrm{rk})\) and a tuple of integers \(\mathbf{k} = (k_0, \dots, k_\ell)\), denoted by \(\mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}(\mathcal{X}, \mathbb{R}^d)\), is the space of functions \(h: \mcalX^\mathbf{k} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^d\) where $\mcalX^\mathbf{k} := \mathcal{X}_0^{k_0} \times \cdots \times \mathcal{X}_\ell^{k_\ell}$. Here, both exponentiation and multiplication of sets are defined in terms of Cartesian products. Denoting the number of $i$-ranked cells $n_i$, notice that each map $h \in \mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}(\mcalX, \mathbb{R}^d)$ can be identified with a tensor $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{n^{k_0}_0 \times \dots \times n^{k_\ell}_\ell \times d}$ defined by: \begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{x}_0, \dots, \mathbf{x}_\ell, j} = h(\mathbf{x}_0, \dots, \mathbf{x}_\ell)_j
\end{equation}
For $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mcalX^{k_0}, \dots, \mathbf{x}_\ell \in \mcalX^{k_\ell}, j \in [d]$. This representation assumes an order on the set of $i$-ranked cells $\mcalX_i$, thus identifying $\mathcal{X}^\mathbf{k}$ with the index set $[n_0]^{k_0} \times \cdots \times [n_\ell]^{k_\ell}$. For brevity, we sometimes denote \(\mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}(\mathcal{X}, \mathbb{R}^d)\) simply as \(\mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}\). The group \(G = S_{n_0} \times \cdots \times S_{n_\ell}\) acts on \(\mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}\) in the following way: for $h \in \mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}$, $(\x_{0}, \dots, \x_{\ell}) \in \mathcal{X}^\mathbf{k}$, and $\boldsymbol{\sigma} = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_\ell) \in G$: 
\begin{equation}
    (\boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot h)(\x_{0}, \dots, \x_{\ell}) = h(\sigma_0 \cdot \x_{0}, \dots, \sigma_\ell \cdot \x_{\ell}).
\end{equation}
The action of each subgroup \(S_{n_i}\) changes the order of \(i\)-rank cells in the tuple \(\mathbf{x}_{i}\). To ensure our model remains independent of cell ordering, we aim to construct architectures invariant to the action of \(G\).
\paragraph{Representing CC data using multi-cellular cochain spaces.}
Multi-cellular cochain spaces provide a natural way of representing CC data. For example, \(\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i} = \mathcal{C}^{i}\), so elements of   $\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i}$ can be viewed as cell feature maps. Additionally, each incidence neighborhood function \(\mathcal{B}_{i,j}\) can be represented in matrix form as an element of the multi-cellular cochain space \(\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}\). Furthermore, each (co)adjacency neighborhood function \((\mathrm{co})\mathcal{A}_{i,j}\) can be represented in matrix form as an element of the multi-cellular cochain space \(\mathcal{C}^{2 \mathbf{e}_i}\). Finally, for a graph $G=(V,E)$ viewed as a CC, the space $\mathcal{C}^{k\cdot \mathbf{e}_0}$ is the space of tensor features over $G$ used by $k$-IGN models.

\subsection{Architecture}
As in many previous works, we begin by characterizing the spaces of linear equivariant layers between multi-cellular cochain spaces. Constructing invariant architectures using equivariant linear layers is a common strategy in geometric deep learning, as seen in works such as \cite{maron2018invariant, zaheer2017deep, bronstein2021geometric}.

\paragraph{Equivariant linear maps between multi-cellular cochain spaces.} We aim to find a basis for the space of equivariant linear layers $L:\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}^1} \rightarrow \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}^2} $ for each pair of tuples $\mathbf{k}_1, \mathbf{k}_2$. Since each space $\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k^j}}$ can be identified with the vector space $\mathbb{R}^{n^{k^j_0}_0 \times \dots \times n^{k^j_\ell}_\ell \times d_j}$, the space of linear maps between the two can be considered as the space of matrices:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}^1} \otimes \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}^2} = \mathbb{R}^{n_0^{k^1_0+k^2_0} \times \dots \times n_\ell^{k^1_\ell+k^2_\ell} \times d_1 \times d_2}.
\end{equation}
where, we use $\otimes$ to denote tensor product of vector spaces. The index set corresponding to this space of matrices is $\mcalX^{\mathbf{k}^1} \times [d_1] \times \mcalX^{\mathbf{k}^2} \times [d_2]$. Notice that the group \(G\) acts naturally on $\mcalX^{\mathbf{k}^1} \times \mcalX^{\mathbf{k}^2}$. For each $G$-orbit $\gamma$ and integers \(j_1 \in [d_1]\), \(j_2 \in [d_2]\), we define a matrix $B^{\gamma, j_1, j_2}$:
\begin{equation}
B^{\gamma, j_1, j_2}_{\mathbf{a}, i_1, \mathbf{b}, i_2} = \begin{cases}
    1 & (\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}) \in \gamma, \quad i_1 = j_1,\quad i_2 = j_2\\
    0  & \text{ otherwise.}
\end{cases}     
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{a} \in \mcalX^{\mathbf{k}^1}$, $\mathbf{b} \in \mcalX^{\mathbf{k}^2}$, $i_1 \in [d_1] $, $i_2 \in [d_2]$. If $h \in \mcalC^{\mathbf{k}^1}$ and $h' = B^{\gamma, j_1, j_2} h$ we  have:
\begin{equation}
    h'(b)_j = \begin{cases}
        \sum_{(a,b)\in \gamma} h(a)_{j_1} & j=j_2\\
        0 & \text{otherwhise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
 These maps were established as a basis for the space of all equivariant linear functions \( L: \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}^1} \rightarrow \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}^2} \) in \cite{maron2018invariant}. This framework encompasses many layers from previously studied models. For example, by setting $\mathbf{k}_1 = \mathbf{k}_2 = k \cdot \mathbf{e}_i$, the space of equivariant linear layers corresponds to the space of $k$-IGN layers, which take as input graphs defined on the $i$-rank cells of the input complexes (i.e. graphs whose node sets are $ \mathcal{X}_i $). Similarly, with \( \mathbf{k}_1 = \mathbf{k}_2 = \mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j \) for \( i \neq j \in \mathbb{N} \), this space aligns with the space of linear maps used to construct the exchangeable matrix layer as described in \cite{hartford2018deep}. 

We can now use this basis to construct learnable equivariant linear layers by taking a parametric linear combination of the basis functions. We augment HOMP architectures with these equivariant layers, constructing a new class of TDL architecture we call multi-cellular networks (MCNs). This is embodied by incorporating equivariant linear layers into the tensor diagram scheme. Figure \ref{fig:tensor_diagrams} depicts a \ourmethod tensor diagram. We now describe the components of the \ourmethod scheme.

\paragraph{Diagram.} Similar to HOMP tensor diagrams, \ourmethod tensor diagrams are layered directed graphs with labeled nodes and edges. Each node is labeled by a multi-cellular cochain space, extending the class of node labels used in HOMP tensor diagrams. Directed edges with source and target nodes labeled by \(\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i}\) can be labeled by any neighborhood function, while edges between nodes labeled by other types of multi-cellular cochain spaces are labeled with the new label ``equiv''. 

\paragraph{Input.} The input to the \ourmethod model is determined by the $0$-th layer of the tensor diagram, whose nodes of can labeled by the following type of multi-cellular cochain spaces:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Nodes labeled by $\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i}$ which take the $i$-rank cell features as input.
    \item Nodes labeled by $\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}$ which take the incidence matrix $\mathcal{B}_{i,j}$ as input.
    \item Nodes labeled by $\mathcal{C}^{2\mathbf{e}_i}$ which take the (co) adjacency matrices $(\mathrm{co})\mathcal{A}_{i,j}$ for all $j \in [\ell]$ in concatenated form as input.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Update.} Each layer update in the \ourmethod model is represented by a layer of the corresponding tensor diagram. At the $t$-th layer of the diagram, a node \(v_t\) labeled by \(\mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}\) stores a multi-cellular cochain \(h^{v_t} \in \mathcal{C}_{\mathbf{k}}\). For each directed edge \(e_t = (v_t, v_{t+1})\) with target $v_{t+1}$ labeled by $\mcalC^{\mathbf{k}'}$, we compute a message cochain map \(m^{e_t} \in \mcalC^{\mathbf{k}'}\) based on \(h^{v_t}\) and the label of \(e_t\). If $e_t$ is labeled by a neighborhood function $\mathcal{N}$, then by definition of \ourmethod tensor diagrams, $\mathbf{k}_1 \mathbf{e}_i$ and $\mathbf{k}' = \mathbf{e}_j$ for some \(i, j \in [\ell]_0\). In this case, the message \(m^{e_t}\) is computed by
\begin{equation}
    m^{e_t}_x = \bigoplus_{y \in \mathcal{N}(x)}M_{e_t}(h^{v_t}_x, h^{v_t}_y),
\end{equation}
where $M_{e_t}$ is an MLP and $\bigoplus$ is a permutation invariant aggregation function. Note that this is the exact message computation used in HOMP tensor diagrams. On the other hand, if \(e_t\) is labeled by ``equiv'', then $m^{e_t}$ is computed by
\begin{equation}
    m^{e_t} = \beta \left(\sum_{\gamma, j_1, j_2} w^{\gamma, j_1, j_2}B^{\gamma, j_1, j_2}h^{v_t} \right),
\end{equation}
where \(\beta\) is an activation function, \(\{B^{\gamma, j_1, j_2}\}\) is the basis of all equivariant linear maps from \(\mathcal{C}_{v_t}\) to \(\mathcal{C}^{v_{t+1}}\), and \(\{w^{\gamma, j_1, j_2}\}\) are learnable parameters. 

After computing the message \(m^{e_t}\) for all edges \(e_t\) with target \(v_{t+1}\), we compute a new multi-cellular cochain \(h^{v_{t+1}} \in \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}'}\) by aggregating the different messages:
\begin{equation}
    h^{v_{t+1}}_{\mathbf{x}} = \bigotimes_{e^t \in T(v_{t+1})}m^{e_t}_{\mathbf{x}}.
\end{equation}
Here, $T(v_{t+1})$ is the set of edges in the tensor diagram whose target node is $v_{t+1}$, and $\bigotimes$ is an aggregation function. Note that this generalizes the second aggregation in Equation \ref{eq: high order Message Passing simple} which defines the HOMP update.

\paragraph{Output.} The last layer of the tensor diagram contains a single node. In the case of cell prediction tasks, that node is labeled by $\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i}$ where $i$ is the rank of the cells of interest. In the case of CC level prediction tasks, the final cell will be labeled by  $\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{0}}$.

In the following section, we demonstrate that \ourmethod addresses many of the expressivity limitations we have identified in HOMP. The asymptotic runtime complexity of an equivariant linear layer \(L: \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}} \rightarrow \mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}}\) is \(O\left(\prod_{i=0}^\ell n_i^{k_i}\right) \). Consequently, \ourmethod allows to perform more computationally intensive operations on higher-rank cells, which are often sparser. 

\subsection{The Expressive Power of Multi-cellular Networks}
Similarly to $k$-IGNs, by using large enough multi-cellular cochain spaces \ourmethod can be fully expressive.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{OurmethodExpressivityInformalProp}
\label{prop: ourmethod is fully expressive informal}
Any pair of non-isomorphoc combinatorial complexes can be distinguished by an \ourmethod model.
\end{restatable}

 Despite its strong expressive power, \ourmethod in its full generality is infeasible to implement. In the next section, we construct a more scalable version of \ourmethod that still addresses many of the expressivity issues observed in HOMP. 



\section{Scalable Multi-cellular Networks}

Although linear equivariant layers offer a principled approach to constructing expressive architectures, it is often possible to find parallel architectures that are equally expressive but are more efficient in terms of runtime/space complexity and are simpler to implement. We take a similar approach here. First, we restrict our \ourmethod architectures to only use multi-cellular cochain space \(\mathcal{C}^\mathbf{k}\) for which \(\sum_{i=0}^\ell k_i \leq 3\). Of these, the two types of cochain spaces that introduce the largest computational overheads are \(\mathcal{C}^{3 \mathbf{e}_i}\) and \(\mathcal{C}^{2 \mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}\).

In the following, we leverage two expressive graph architectures -- provably powerful graph networks (PPGN) \cite{maron2019provably} and coarsening-based subgraph GNN (CS-GNN) -- to construct new corresponding multi-cellular updates: provably powerful complex networks (PPCN) and subcomplex networks (SCN). These updates aim to replace equivariant updates related to nodes labeled by \(\mathcal{C}^{3 \mathbf{e}_i}\) and \(\mathcal{C}^{2 \mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}\) respectively. 

The adaptation of these graph architectures to the domain of combinatorial complexes is achieved using augmented Hasse graphs, defined below:
\newline
\begin{restatable}[]{definition}
{HasseDef}
\label{def: augmented hasse graph }
Given a combinatorial complex $C =(S, \mcalX, \rk)$, its augemented Hasse graph w.r.t the (co)adjacency neighborhood $(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i,j}$, is given by $\mcalH_{(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i, j}} = (V_{\mcalH_{(\mathrm{co}\mcalA_{i, j})}}, E_{\mcalH_{(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i, j}}})$, where:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq: Hasse graph}
\begin{aligned}
   V_{\mcalH_{(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i, j}}} &= \mcalX_i\\
   E_{\mcalH_{(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i, j}}} &= \{(x,y) \mid y \in \mcalA_{i,j}(x) \}.
\end{aligned} 
\end{equation}
\end{restatable}
See Figure \ref{fig:hasse_graph} for an example. Intuitively, augmented Hasse graphs encodes the relational data between $i$- rank cells given by the neighborhood function $(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i,j}$ as a graph. This allows us to use GNN tools on these higher-order structures. Using augmented Hasse graphs, we define both the PPCN and SCN updates, intuitively justify the choice of these updates, and use them to create a more scalable version of \ourmethod called scalable multi-cellular networks (\secondmethod). \secondmethod addresses many of the expressivity issues identified in HOMP, reuiqures only on multi-cellular spaces of the form \(\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{k}}\) with \(\sum k_i \leq 2\), has both lower runtime and space complexity than \ourmethod, and is easier to implement. For comprehensive reviews of PPGN and IGN, see \cite{morris2023weisfeiler}. For subgraph models, see \cite{bronstein2021subgraphs}.
\paragraph{Provably powerful complex network (PPCN).} An edge $e_t = (v_t, v_{t+1})$ in a tensor diagram can be labeled ``PPCN'' only if both $v_t$ and $ v_{t+1}$ are labeled by $\mathcal{C}^{2\mathbf{e}_i}$ for some integer $i$. Recall that after applying $t$ layers of the tensor diagram, $v_t$ stores a multi-cellular cochain $h^{v_t} \in \mcalC^{2\mathbf{e}_i}$ which can be written in matrix form as $H^{v_t} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_i \times n_i \times d}$. For example, $H^{v_t}$ can be a concatenation of the adjacency matrices of the Hasse graphs $\{\mcalH_{(\mathrm{co})\mcalA_{i,j}}\}_{j}$. PPGN layers, operating on $\mathbb{R}^{n_i \times n_i \times d}$ are equivariant to the action of $S_{n_i}$.  We can therefore compute the message $m^{e_t}$ by processing $H^{v_t}$ with a PPGN architecture. For the readers convenience, this update is provided below:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item  First, we apply three MLPs $M_0,M_1 : \mathbb{R}^{d} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{b}$, $M_2 : \mathbb{R}^{d} \rightarrow  \mathbb{R}^{b'}$ to the input tensor. These MLPs are applied on each of the features of the input tensor independently, i.e. $M_j(H^{v_t})_{k_1,k_2} = M_j(H^{v_t}_{k_1,k_2,:})$.

    \item Second, we perform matrix multiplication between matching features of the first two matrices, i.e. $P_{:,:,k_3} = M_0(H^{v_t})_{:,:,k_3} \cdot M_1(H^{v_t})_{:,:,k_3}, k_3 \in [b]$. 
    
    \item The resulting message is given by $m^{e_t} = [P, M_2(H^{v_t})]$ where $[\cdot,\cdot]$ stands for concatenation along the last axis (feature dimension). 
\end{itemize}
For an illustration of the PPCN update, see  Figure \ref{fig:ppcn}. PPCN aims to replace equivariant updates of the form $L:\mcalC^{3\mathbf{e}_i} \rightarrow \mcalC^{3\mathbf{e}_i}$ in \ourmethod. To see why this is a natural choice, notice that $L$ can be seen as a $3$-IGN layer operating on the space of $3$-tensors indexed by $\mcalX_i$, which can be identified with $\mathbb{R}^{n_i^3 \times d}$. PPGN has been shown to be as expressive as $3$-IGN \cite{maron2019provably, azizian2020expressive, geerts2020expressive}, while having lower space complexity ($O(n^2)$ compared to the $O(n^3)$ for $3$-IGN). Additionally, as PPGN is implemented via matrix multiplication, its runtime can be reduced to $O(n_i^{2.x})$ compared to the $O(n^3)$ complexity of $3$-IGN, and its computations can be easily parallelized. Thus PPCN, which effectively applies a PPGN update on the space $\mcalC^{2\mathbf{e}_i}$, is a tractable alternative to equivariant updates over $\mcalC^{3\mathbf{e}_i}$.

\paragraph{Subgraph Complex network (SCN).}  An edge $e_t = (v_t, v_{t+1})$ in a tensor diagram can be labeled by ``SCN'' only if both $v_t$ and $ v_{t+1}$ are labeled by $\mathcal{C}^{\mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}$ for some integers $i < j$. The space $\mcalC^{\mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}$ can be identified with $\mathbb{R}^{n_i \times n_j \times d}$. We aim to process $h^{v_t}$ in an $S_{n_i} \times S_{n_j}$-equivariant way. The literature of subgraph neural networks provides several architectures for equivariant models acting on similar spaces \cite{bevilacqua2021equivariant, zhang2023complete, bar2024flexible}. Using this framework in our context results in a message $m^{e_t} \in \mcalC^{\mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}$ given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq: sub-complex update}
\begin{split}
    m^{e_t, k_1, k_2}(x, y) &= M^{k_1, k_2} \Big( 
    h^{v_t}(x, y), 
        \sum_{x' \in (\mathrm{co})\mathcal{A}_{i, k_1}(x)} h^{v_t}(x', y), 
        \sum_{y' \in (\mathrm{co})\mathcal{A}_{j, k_2}(y)} h^{v_t}(x, y'), \\ 
        &\quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \quad \sum_{y' \in \mathcal{B}_{i, j}(x)} h^{v_t}(x, y'), \sum_{x' \in \mathcal{B}^\top_{j, i}(y)} h^{v_t}(x', y) 
    \Big), \\
    m^{e_t}(x, y) &= \bigotimes_{k_1, k_2 \in [\ell]_0} m^{e_t, k_1,k2}(x, y), 
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $x \in \mcalX_i, y \in \mcalX_j$, $\{M^{k_1, k_2}\}_{k_1, k_2}$ are MLPs and $\bigotimes$ is an aggregation function.

SCN updates aim to replace equivariant updates of the form $L:\mcalC^{2\mathbf{e}_i+\mathbf{e}_j} \rightarrow \mcalC^{2\mathbf{e}_i+\mathbf{e}_j}$ in \ourmethod. To see why this is a natural choice, note that the space \(\mathcal{C}^{2 \mathbf{e}_i + \mathbf{e}_j}\) can be identified with $\mathbb{R}^{n_i\times n_i \times n_j \times d}$. Under the group action of \(S_{n_i} \times S_{n_j}\), each element \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{n_i \times n_i \times n_j \times d}\) can be viewed as a bag of tensors \(\{H_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n_i\times n_i \times d} \mid k \in [n_j]\}\), where each $H_k$ is considered up-to row and column permutations by $S_{n_i}$. This is exactly the data object processed by subgraph neural networks, which take as input a bag of adjacency matrices defining different subgraphs on a fixed set of nodes. We specifically focus on CS-GNN \footnote{CS-GNN also uses the size of cells as input, we chose to ignore cell sizes to maintain model simplicity and focus more on the rank rather than the size of the cells. For the remainder of this paper, references to CS-GNN will pertain to this modified version.} \cite{bar2024flexible} which takes as input graphs indexed by ``super-nodes'' which can be viewed as higher order cells constructed by some lifting/pooling procedure. The intermediate message $m^{e_t, k_1, k_2}$ defined in Equation \ref{eq: sub-complex update} can be seen as applying a CS-GNN update to the bag of Hasse graphs $\{\mcalH_{\mcalA_{i,k_1}}^x \mid x \in \mcalX_j \}$, where $\mcalH_{\mcalA_{i,k_1}}^x$ denotes the graph $\mcalH_{\mcalA_{i,k_1}}$ with the cells $\{y \in \mcalX_i \mid y \subseteq x \}$ marked, and the set of ``super-nodes'' is $\mcalX_j$. For an illustration of this, see Figure \ref{fig:scn}. Equivariant layers $L:\mcalC^{2\mathbf{e}_i+\mathbf{e}_j} \rightarrow \mcalC^{2\mathbf{e}_i+\mathbf{e}_j}$ have both space and runtime complexity of $O(n_i^2 \cdot n_j)$. In contrast, the SCN update has a space complexity of $O(n_i \cdot n_j)$ and a runtime complexity of $O(d \cdot n_i \cdot n_j)$, where $d$ is the maximal degree over all cells in $\mathcal{X}_i \cup \mathcal{X}_j$ with respect to any natural neighborhood function.

\section{Expressivity of Scalable Multi-cellular Networks}

In this section, we analyze the expressive power of \secondmethod, demonstrating its ability to address many of the expressivity limitations identified in HOMP, as discussed in Section \ref{sec: expressivity}.

\subsection{Topological and Metric Properties}
\label{subsec: Topological and metric Properties secondmethod}
In Section \ref{subsec: Topological and metric Properties HOMP}, we establish that HOMP fails to differentiate between CCs based on the following four properties: diameter, orientability, planarity and homology. In this section, we examine how \secondmethod addresses each of these limitations. The improved expressivity of SMCN comes from its use of expressive graph learning primitives.

\paragraph{\secondmethod can fully express the diameter of a CC.}
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{SecondMethodCanComuteDiameterInformalProp}
\label{prop: second method cand compute diameter informal}
Any pair of combinatorial complexes with distinct diameters can be distinguished by an \secondmethod model.
\end{restatable}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
    It has been shown in \cite{zhang2023complete} that subgraph networks are able to compute the shortest path distance $d(u,v)$ between between any pair of nodes $u,v$. This result was extanded to CS-GNNs in \cite{bar2024flexible}, where it was shown that this architecture is able to compute the distance $d(S,v) = \min_{u \in v}d(v,u)$ for any node $v$ and ``super-node'' $S$. Applying these two results on the Hasse graphs, and taking the maximum over all such pairs we are able to compute any diameter of an input CC. 
\end{proof}

\paragraph{\secondmethod can separate a cylnder and a Möbius strip.}
The following proposition shows that \secondmethod can distinguish between a cylinder and a Möbius strip. This implies that \secondmethod is strictly better than HOMP at detecting planarity and orientability. Understanding \secondmethod's ability to fully detect orientability or planarity is still and open question and is left for future work.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{SecondMethodCanSepeateMobiosInformalProp}
\label{prop: second method cand seperate mobius and cylinder informal}
There exists an \secondmethod model which is able to separate the Möbius strip and the cylinder.
\end{restatable}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
    The key to distinguishing between the cylinder and Möbius strip is their boundary edges ($1$-cells). \secondmethod can identify these edges as they are exactly the ones whose degree w.r.t neighborhood $\mcalB_{1,2}$ is $1$. Additionally, note that in a cylinder of height $h$ and cycle length $r$, the boundary edges form two cycles of length $r$, while in a Möbius strip, the boundary edges form a singe cycle of length $2r$. This is a classical example of a pair of graphs that are indistinguishable by graph message-passing but are distinguishable by the $3$-WL test. We can use the PPCN updates of \secondmethod to simulate PPGN update on these boundary graphs. As PPGN is as expressive as the $3$-WL test, this allows us to separate the two CCs.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{\secondmethod's ability to distinguish CCs based on homology.}
We first focus on the $0$-th homology group $H_0$.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{SecondMethodCanComuteZeroHomInformalProp}
\label{prop: second method cand compute the zeroth homology informal}
Any pair of cellular complexes with distinct $0$-th order homology groups can be distinguished by an \secondmethod model.
\end{restatable}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
   The $0$-th homology group of a cellular complex \(C\), denoted \(H_0(C)\), is of the form \(\mathbb{Z}^{k_0}\), where \(k_0\) is the number of connected components in the graph induced by the $1$-skeleton of \(C\). This graph is identical to the augmented Hasse graph \(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}_{0,1}} = (V_\mcalH, E_\mcalH)\) defined in Equation \ref{eq: Hasse graph}. As before, we can use \secondmethod to simulate subgraph network updates  on the graph \(\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}_{0,1}}\). Thus, it suffices to show that subgraph networks can compute the number of connected components of a graph. Subgraph networks were shown in \cite{zhang2023complete} to be able to compute the feature map $h_{v,u} = d(v,u)$,  for any pair of nodes $u,v$, where if the shortest path distance $d$ is infinite, it is encoded by $-1$. Denote by $\left(\mcalH_{\mcalA_{0, 1}}\right)_v$ the $\mcalH_{\mcalA_{0, 1}}$ connected component $v$ is a part of. A subgraph network can then compute the feature map 
   \begin{equation}
       h'_{v,u} = \sum_{h_{v,u'}>-1} 1 = \lvert \left(\mcalH_{\mcalA_{0, 1}}\right)_v \rvert,
   \end{equation}
    Notice that:
   \begin{equation}
   \label{eq: num connected components main}
       \sum_{u,v \in V_{\mcalH}} \frac{1}{|V_\mcalH| \cdot h'_{v,u}} =  \sum_{v \in V_{\mcalH}} \frac{1}{\lvert \left(\mcalH_{\mcalA_{0, 1}}\right)_v \rvert} =  k_0,
   \end{equation}
   Using the left hand side of Equation \ref{eq: num connected components main} as our final pooling operation, we get that the output of our subgrph network is exactly the number of connected components in $\mcalH_{\mcalA_{0,1}}$, as required.
\end{proof}
Exploring \secondmethod's capacity to differentiate between any pair of CCs based on their higher-order homology groups is left for future work. As a first step, we show that \secondmethod can successfully separate a natural family of CCs -- two-dimensional surfaces embeddable in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) -- based on any homology group.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{SecondMethodCanComuteHomManifoldInformalProp}
\label{prop: second method cand compute the manifold homology informal}
Let $C, C^*$ be a pair of cellular complexes whose underlying topology corresponds to a $2$-dimensional surface that is embeddable in $\mathbb{R}^3$. If $C$ and $C^*$ differ in any homology group, there exists an \secondmethod model that can distinguish them.
\end{restatable}
\begin{proof}[proof sketch]
    We first focus on the case where $C$ and $C^*$ are connected. From the celebrated characterization of connected $2$-dimensional manifolds (see e.g. \cite{MR1867354}), $C$ either has a boundary or is orientable. In both of these cases, all of $C$'s homology groups are of the form $H_i(C) = \mathbb{Z}^{k_i}$, and since $C$ is $2$-dimensional, $k_i = 0$ for $i > 2$. In addition, since $C$ is connected $k_0 = 1$. In the case where $C$ doesn't have a boundary, it must be orientable and so Poincaré duality implies $k_2 = 1$. On the other hand, if $C$ does have a boundary, it is homotopic to its $1$-skeleton and so $k_2 = 0$. As having a boundary is equivalent to having $1$-cells whose degree with respect to $\mcalB_{1,2}$ is equal $1$, \secondmethod can detect weather $C$ has a boundary and thus compute $k_2$. Finally, as $C$ is a cellular complex the Euler characteristic of $C$ defined by $k_0 - k_1 + k_2$ is equal to $|\mcalX_0| - |\mcalX_1| + |\mcalX_2|$. This last quantity can be easily computed by \secondmethod. Since we can compute $k_0, k_2, k_0-k_1+k_2$ we can compute $k_1$ and so we can compute all homology groups of $C$.

    In the general case (where $C$ is not necessarily connected), $C$ can be decomposed into a disjoint union of connected complexes $C_1 \dots ,C_n$. We then have:
    \begin{equation}
        H_i(C) = H_i(C_1)\times\cdots \times H_i(C_n)  .
    \end{equation} 
   Using the same arguments as before, these groups can be determined by counting the number of connected components with a boundary and the number of connected components without a boundary.
\end{proof}
 Collectively, these propositions suggest that \secondmethod is more capable of leveraging the topological properties of CCs compared to HOMP.

\subsection{Lifting and Pooling}
\label{subsec: lifting and pooling secondmethod}
In Section \ref{subsec: lifting and pooling}, we demonstrated HOMP's inability to leverage the sparse higher-order cells generated by common lifting and pooling operations. The next propositions demonstrates that \secondmethod is able to leverage this sparse information to a greater extent.
\newline
\begin{restatable}[Informal]{proposition}{SecondMethodPoolongLiftingInformalProp}
\label{prop: second method pooling lifting informal}
There exist pairs of combinatorial complexes, produced by applying standard lifting and pooling operators to non-isomorphic graphs, that HOMP fails to distinguish, but \secondmethod can. Additionally, the layers of the \secondmethod model can be constructed to have a runtime complexity of \(O(d \cdot n_0 \cdot n_2) \), where \(n_0\) is the number of nodes in the original graph,  \(n_2\) is the number of higher-order cells produced and \(d\) is the maximal degree w.r.t any natural neighborhood function.
\end{restatable}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
We demonstrate this result for triangular lifting. We have seen that HOMP cannot distinguish between the combinatorial complexes depicted in Figure \ref{fig: triangilar lifting CCs}. These CCs were produced by applying triangular lifting on the star graphs $G = \text{Star}_{n,2k}$ and $G^* = \text{Star}_{n,k} \sqcup \text{Star}_{n,k}$ where $\sqcup$ represents disjoint union. We have also seen that these two CCs satisfy:
\begin{equation}
    \text{diam}^2_{\mcalA_{0,1}}(C) \neq \text{diam}^2_{\mcalA_{0,1}}(C^*).
\end{equation}
Finally, we have seen that \secondmethod which uses only SCN updates is able to compute the above diameters. By choosing an appropriate aggregation function $\bigotimes$ in Equation \ref{eq: sub-complex update}, the runtime complexity of each such layer is \(O(d \cdot n_0 \cdot n_2)\).
\end{proof}


\section{Experiments}

To empirically support the expressivity improvement of \secondmethod over HOMP, we constructed the Torus dataset. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first synthetic dataset designed specifically to measure the expressive power of TDL architectures on topological data.
\paragraph{Torus dataset.}
To construct the torus dataset we first select three parameters: the minimum size, which specifies the number of nodes in the smallest CC in the dataset; the maximum size, which specifies the number of nodes in the largest CC; and the number of components, which specifies the maximum number of connected components in any CC within the dataset. The dataset is then constructed by iterating over all possible choices for the the number of nodes and connected components, generating all possible 2-dimensional tori with the specified parameters. We then select all pairs of constructed tori that have the same size. As previously observed, each such pair is indistinguishable by HOMP. In addition, all of these pairs differ by basic metric/topological properties: they either have a different number of connected components, resulting in distinct homology groups, or they have the same number of components, in which case they differ in the diameters of some of the components. In our experiments, we selected a minimal size of $18$ (the smallest size that admits indistinguishable pairs), a maximal size of $40$, and a maximal component number of $3$, resulting in $223$ pairs.

\section{Conclusion}

In the first part of this paper, we introduced a topological criterion that detects numerous instances where HOMP cannot distinguish between pairs of combinatorial complexes. We then analyzed the expressive power of HOMP from three different perspectives: First, we demonstrated that HOMP is unable to differentiate between combinatorial complexes that vary in common topological or metric properties such as diameter, orientability, planarity and homology. Second, we showed that the exist CCs generated through lifting and pooling operators on graphs remain indistinguishable by HOMP despite their differing topological structures. Lastly, we established that HOMP can distinguish between CCs that are indistinguishable by EHNN, a provably expressive Hypergraph Neural Network. In the second part of the paper, we introduced \ourmethod, inspired by $k$-IGNs, and its more scalable version, \secondmethod, inspired by PPGN and subgraph GNNs. We then theoretically demonstrated that, analogously to IGNs, \ourmethod can reach full expressivity for CCs. We additionally showed that \secondmethod can tractably address many of the expressivity limitations observed in HOMP. Finally, we presented the torus dataset, a synthetic dataset designed to measure the ability of TDL models to distinguish between pairs of combinatorial complexes (CCs) based on simple topological properties. We observed that \secondmethod successfully distinguishes between all pairs in the dataset, whereas HOMP fails to distinguish between any pair, thereby validating our theoretical findings.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
Despite its flexibility, the additional components that make \secondmethod more expressive than HOMP result in a runtime complexity that scales superlinearly with respect to the number of $i$-rank. In cases like 3D meshes, where the number of cells remains in the thousands irrespective of rank, the "PPCN" and "SCN" updates within the \secondmethod framework become intractable. A worth while future direction is developing more scalable alternatives to \secondmethod that maintain greater expressiveness than HOMP, or designing custom pooling operations that enable applying \secondmethod in a scalable manner while maintaining its theoretical guarantees. Additionally, we have demonstrated that \secondmethod is more effective than HOMP at distinguishing between CCs with different orientability and homology. However, we have not yet determined whether \secondmethod can fully distinguish between CCs based on these properties. Future research can investigate the precise limitations of \secondmethod and \ourmethod in expressing these and other topological and metric invariants. Similarly, future work can more thoroughly analyze the expressive power of HOMP compared to prominent hypergraph networks. Finally, developing more complex synthetic datasets that encompass a wider variety of topological CC examples is another interesting future direction.
