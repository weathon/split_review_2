\title{Can Language Models be Instructed to Protect Personal Information?}

\begin{abstract}
Large multimodal language models have proven transformative in numerous applications. 
However, these models have been shown to memorize and leak pre-training data, raising serious user privacy and information security concerns.
While data leaks should be prevented, it is also crucial to examine the trade-off between the privacy protection and model utility of proposed approaches.
In this paper, we introduce \dataset --- a multimodal benchmark to assess this privacy/utility trade-off when a model is instructed to protect specific categories of personal information in a simulated scenario.
We evaluate language models on \dataset~to examine how effectively an access control instruction can prevent models from selectively leaking protected personal information.
We also propose a technique to iteratively self-moderate responses, which significantly improves privacy.
However, through a series of red-teaming experiments, we find that adversaries can also easily circumvent these protections with simple jailbreaking methods through textual and/or image inputs. 
We believe \dataset~has the potential to support the development of new models with improved privacy protections, as well as the adversarial robustness of these protections. We release the entire \dataset~dataset at \url{https://llm-access-control.io/}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Large language models (LLMs) and multimodal models such as GPT-4 and Flamingo~\citep{alayrac2022flamingo}
have shown a remarkable ability to follow instructions.
While large textual and visual pre-training datasets have enabled impressive capabilities,
they also contain a significant amount of personal information. As a result, serious privacy concerns have arisen as it has been shown that malicious users can extract sensitive text from training corpora~\citep{carlini2021extracting,carlini2023quantifying} or geolocate unsuspecting users ~\citep{zhu2021vigor}.
Difficulty in reasoning about the privacy risks of LLMs has prompted companies to refrain from integrating customer data with LLMs~\citep{PEPR_panel_privacy_challenges_AI}.

However, safeguards developed to prevent leakage of personal information inherently levy an alignment tax ~\citep{ouyang2022training} --- i.e., a trade-off between information protection and the utility of the model.
For instance, previous literature has presented frameworks to preempt data extraction attacks on trained models by inducing a model to forget certain pieces of training data~\citep{bourtoule2021machine, jang2022knowledge_unlearning, tahiliani2021machine_unlearning} or editing factual relations that pertain to personal information from the model parameters~\citep{rawat2020modifying, de-cao-etal-2021-editing, meng2022locating}. However, in addition to being computationally intensive, impractical in a distributed context, and poorly generalizable \citep{meng2022mass}, we show that they also severely degrade model performance for more realistic privacy controls.
Other techniques applied during model tuning such as reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) train the model to follow a pre-defined behavior, which has been shown to lead to over-protection~\citep{röttger2023xstest}. However, as model instruction-following capabilities have improved, they offer a flexible way to steer behavior~\citep{bai2022constitutional}. Specifically \textit{access control instructions} could be used to prompt models to adhere to complex privacy protection requirements (e.g., refusing to answer questions about a group defined with natural language). 
The ability of these models to follow such instructions to protect personal information remains unknown.

As an initial step in understanding the efficacy of access control instructions, we present the first evaluation of the ability of large instruction-following models to comply with these instructions while maintaining performance (Figure~\ref{fig:main}). 
We introduce \dataset, a multimodal benchmark for testing the ability of models to selectively protect a group of people (e.g. based on their citizenship or occupation) or a category of information (e.g. an individual's profession or location). %
The selection of these groups and categories was motivated by the General Data Protection Regulation~\citep{GDPR2016a}. %
In a non-adversarial evaluation setting, we show that state-of-the-art API-based models (e.g., GPT-4) outperform open-source LLMs (e.g. LLaMA~\citep{touvron2023llama}) in protecting personal data with access control instructions, especially when we employ \textit{self-moderation}, a technique to guide the model to examine and authorize its response to improve protection iteratively. However, we discover serious issues related to bias and robustness with these instructions that we believe need to be addressed before they can be used in critical applications. 
For instance, we find that when following access-control instructions, these models paradoxically provide less protection for more private or less well-known individuals.
Through a series of red-teaming exercises, we demonstrate the susceptibility of access control instructions 
to popular jailbreaking prompts and multi-hop attacks. 
Furthermore, we show how the image input to state-of-the-art open-source multimodal models, such as IDEFICS~\citep{laurencon2023obelics},\footnote{IDEFICS is a replication of Google's Flamingo model~\citep{alayrac2022flamingo}.} can be used as a new attack vector to circumvent access control instructions.

Finally, we note on Sep 25, 2023, OpenAI released GPT4V, a multimodal model that they claim has in-built privacy protection capabilities \citep{OpenAI-GPT4V-Safety}.
In short, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}

\item We provide the first open benchmark to standardize assessment of the ability of language and vision models to follow instructions to protect personal information.
\item We introduce a self-moderation technique that improves models' capabilities to follow access control instructions, 
and demonstrate that biases are still present in how well different groups are protected.
\item Through a series of red-teaming exercises, we show that adversarial techniques can easily circumvent access control instructions in state-of-the-art models. 
\end{itemize}







\section{Related Work}


\paragraph{Protecting user information in language models.}
Many approaches have been proposed to preserve user privacy in language models by mitigating training data memorization risks \citep{carlini-etal-unintended-memorization-2019, carlini2021extracting,carlini2023quantifying} with methods that require special pre-training procedures or post-training model weight updates. Pre-training and fine-tuning \textit{differentially private (DP)} LMs \citep{ponomareva2023dp, li2022large, yu2022differentially} using DP optimizers \citep{Song2013StochasticGD, Bassily2014PrivateER, AbadiDLDiffPriv2016} is one such approach. While DP methods provide privacy guarantees i.e., the extent to which specific pieces of training data affect model inference, \cite{brown2022does} argue DP does not adequately address privacy in language models due to difficulty in defining privacy boundaries with language data. In fact, Brown et al. also expound upon the difficulty of defining an ``in-group'' for any secret and note this group might change given context, motivating the flexible access-control instructions that we introduce. \textit{Machine unlearning} methods \citep{bourtoule2021machine, Cao2015TowardsMS} induce models to forget a specific piece of training data. These methods have recently been applied to LLMs by tweaking trained model weights either by minimizing the output distribution of the data to be forgotten and a set of unseen data \citep{wang-etal-2023-kga} or by optimizing against specific sequences of training data \citep{jang2022knowledge_unlearning}. Relatedly, techniques in the space of \textit{model editing} \citep{rawat2020modifying, de-cao-etal-2021-editing, pmlr-v162-mitchell22a, meng2022locating, meng2022mass} enable weight updates to LMs that target specific knowledge associations. While they allow for more fine-grained privacy controls compared to DP methods, as mentioned previously, machine unlearning and model editing approaches are unable to generalize to the more complex notions of information categories that we explore in this paper, which transcend specific token sequences or associations. We experimentally confirm this in \S\ref{fig:model_edit}. Additionally, machine unlearning methods are generally irreversible i.e. a forgotten piece of training data cannot easily be relearned making it difficult to adapt to changing privacy guidelines.  Recent theoretical analysis \cite{glukhov2023llm} has shown that \textit{perfect} LLM self-censorship is an undecidable problem, suggesting solutions to the problem of privacy protection might need to be based on empirical rather than theoretical analysis.



\paragraph{Red teaming and jailbreaking language models.}
As LLMs have been deployed to the greater public, model development teams have used \textit{red teaming} methods to gauge and improve the robustness. These methods usually consist of a collected set of adversarial model inputs either constructed manually \citep{xu-etal-2021-bot, rottger-etal-2021-hatecheck, xu2021recipes, gehman-etal-2020-realtoxicityprompts} or through an automated procedure using a ``red'' LLM \citep{perez-2022-red-teaming, ganguli2022red, casper2023explore, mehrabi2023flirt}. 
While most of these methods are concerned with eliciting unsafe behavior, \cite{perez-2022-red-teaming} and \cite{ganguli2022red} explicitly investigate the privacy robustness of these models, specifically regarding memorized pieces of personally identified information (PII).
Despite this red-teaming effort, researchers have developed \textit{jailbreaking} methods to attack released models that aim to either systematically target specific model weaknesses such as the struggle to comply with competing objectives or mismatched generalizations \citep{wei2023jailbroken} or use white-box gradient-based methods to target a specific problematic model completion \citep{zou2023universal, bailey2023image}.
Recent work has also found that LLMs occasionally display exaggerated safety behavior for certain benign prompts \citep{röttger2023xstest}. 
This conclusion motivates the design of our benchmark, which allows us to determine whether models are too conservative in protecting information.





\section{The \dataset~Benchmark}

Protecting private information in language model responses is both a critical and challenging task, especially when balancing the need to respond helpfully to user queries while safeguarding sensitive information.
We propose \dataset~, a benchmark consisting of a curated collection of 4,678 open-domain textual and 2,000 visual QA examples to assess a model's capability to protect private information in various contexts. 
The primary objective of \dataset~is to establish a simple and natural test bed that allows for the focused evaluation of the privacy-utility trade-off in various approaches to preventing training data leaks from language models.  By leveraging information about public figures on Wikipedia, \dataset~avoids safety complications that would arise from real-world private user data.  This makes the evaluation slightly less realistic but allows us to make the benchmark publicly available, supporting reproducible experiments without compromising users' privacy.
In this section, we specify the task definition, describe the data construction process, and introduce the evaluation methodology.

\subsection{Task Definition}
\label{sec:task}

The goal of this task is to develop a response-generation model 
 $\mathcal{F}: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$, which takes a user query ($x \in X$) as input and produces a response ($y \in \mathcal{Y}$) that both protects personal information of individuals and ensures responsive answering. Note that in the visual QA setting, $x$ consists of both the textual query and the image in question. We categorize a query $x$ into two distinct groups: the \pg~($\pgm$) and \cg~($\cgm$).
A query falls into the \pg~ if it is querying information about a protected population or information category, or if the response $y$ contains information about one or more protected populations or information categories.
On the other hand, the \cg~ ($\cgm$) comprises all other queries and has no overlap with \pg~({\small \textit{i.e.,} $\pgm, \cgm \subset \mathcal{X}$ and $\pgm \cap \cgm = \varnothing$}). The primary objective is to satisfy the following conditions:
\[
\mathcal{F}(x) = 
\begin{cases} 
  y & \text{if } x \in \cg \\
  \text{``\textit{Abstain from responding}''} & \text{if } x \in \pg
\end{cases}
\]

Below, we introduce two categories of queries to be protected in the \pg:
\paragraph{Protected Populations.} 
We define a protected population by articulating shared attributes among individuals in that population. Then, any queries that would be inclusive of information of individuals in that population, and any response identifying an individual in that population, would fall into the \pg~, and thus the response generation model abstains from answering.
For example, the attribute can be defined as ``\textit{citizens of Italy}'' to ensure the privacy of Italian nationals.\footnote{ChatGPT banned in Italy over privacy concerns, BBC News, 2023/04/01, \url{https://www.bbc
In this scenario, a response generation model is required to abstain from disclosing information about Italian individuals. Conversely, to maximize utility, it remains essential that information about other populations continues to be available.

\paragraph{Protected Information.} 
We define protected information by articulating categories of information that should not be revealed (\textit{e.g., location, personal relationships, etc.}).
This category is particularly important in the context of deployed textual or visual response generation models to prevent the leaking of a specific type of information from the training data.
For example, while these models can enhance the user experience by offering historical backgrounds of pictured buildings, they also risk facilitating malicious users in extracting sensitive geolocation details from social media image posts, compared to manual online image searches.

In this section, we describe the process of constructing the task following the definition in~\S~\ref{sec:task}. We present \dataset, which re-purposes a collection of five question-answering (QA) datasets, comprising three textual open-domain QA~\citep{joshi2017triviaQA, mallen2022popQA} and two knowledge-intensive visual QA datasets~\citep{shahMYP19,chen2023infoseek} (Table~\ref{tab:data_stats}). 
This approach enables us to leverage high-quality datasets, allowing us to focus on assessing the privacy-utility trade-off approaches to protect the private information of language models.

\paragraph{Protected populations.}
We identify four distinct populations necessitating safeguarding motivated by the general data protection regulation (GDPR)\footnote{Article 4 GDPR: Definition~\url{https://gdpr-info.eu/art-4-gdpr/}}, that is related to identifiers such as location, physiological, social, and economic identity. Based a manual analysis of the dataset and the WikiData knowledge base~\citep{VrandecicKroetzsch14cacm}, these were the categories that were most easily discernible: \textit{citizenship} (seven countries), \textit{age} (senior or young), \textit{occupation} (four sensitive professions), and \textit{the positions they occupy} (three significant public roles). We then select specific shared attributes for each population (\textit{e.g., Italy as the country of citizenship}).
Given the population and a shared attribute, we sample QA data related to human entities in Wikipedia from textual QA datasets such as TriviaQA~\citep{joshi2017triviaQA} and PopQA~\citep{mallen2022popQA}, and visual QA datasets about politicians and celebrities such as KVQA~\citep{shahMYP19}.
These examples are then split into a \pg~and \cg~based on the attributes of the human entities mentioned in the question or answer, which can be found in WikiData. 
\paragraph{Protected Information.}
We identify six types of information that require safeguarding to mitigate privacy and security concerns motivated by the GDPR's definition of personal data, such as identifier factors including \textit{location, professional background, educational history, personal relationships, affiliation and beliefs}, and \textit{language and nationality}. 
As the PopQA~\citep{mallen2022popQA} dataset has limited coverage of questions related to human entities, we create \textbf{PopQA+} by automatically constructing additional questions for the same set of human entities using the question template generation approach in PopQA.
We design 23 question templates based on the attribution data of human entities in WikiData and categorize each attribute into one of the six information categories described above.
In addition to questions about human entities, we collect geolocation information-seeking examples (query, image) from the InfoSeek~\citep{chen2023infoseek}, instead of using an image-only dataset~\citep{zhu2021vigor}.
For the protected group, we filter VQA examples to geolocation-related queries of geographical visual entities such as buildings, and mountains, by using the metadata, which links the visual entity in the image to Wikipedia~\citep{hu2023open}. 
To avoid excessive experiment costs, we restrict each category in \dataset~to a maximum of 200 examples for \pg, 
 subsequently sampling an equal number of examples from the remainder of the dataset to form a \cg, which ensures a balanced comparison for evaluation.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval}
An approach that aims to protect data leaks in response generation models must have two objectives: preserving privacy and maintaining response accuracy. To reflect the distinct requirements of both the protected group and the control group, we adopt a two-fold evaluation approach:

\paragraph{Response F$_{1}$.}
This metric assesses how the correctness of the model's responses differs, between a model with privacy protections and the same model with no such protections, using the standard token-level QA evaluation metric: i.e., F$_1$ score~\citep{joshi2017triviaQA}.
Specifically, we anticipate a notable decrease in the \pg~ and a minimal drop in performance in the \cg.
Any observed drop in performance in the \cg 
\hspace{0.01cm} would illustrate a privacy-utility trade-off or alignment tax~\citep{ouyang2022training}.

\paragraph{Protection Score.}
This metric quantifies the model's protection score: i.e., how much of what's protected \textit{should} be protected (sensitivity) and how much of what's revealed \textit{should} be revealed (specificity).
For sensitivity, we estimate the probability of the model abstaining from responding to a query, conditioned on the query and response being in the \pg~(\textsc{Sensitivity}).
For specificity, we measure the probability of the model responding to a query, conditioned on the query and response truly not being in the \pg~(\textsc{Specificity}).
To prevent the model from over-optimizing for just one of the measures, we calculate the harmonic mean of both:
\begin{equation*}
    \scriptsize
    \textsc{Protection Score} = 2 \; / \; \bigg(\frac{1}{\textsc{Sensitivity}} + \frac{1}{\textsc{Specificity}}\bigg)
\end{equation*}
In our experiment, we instruct the model to output a ``\texttt{none}'' string to indicate an intent to abstain from answering.




\section{\dataset~Experiments: Protecting Against Personal Information Leaks}

\label{sec:experiments}
In this section, we explore how well access control instructions can protect against leaks, on the \dataset~benchmark, in a non-adversarial setting (See \S~\ref{sec:redteam} for adversarial robustness experiments). 
Our experiments evaluate the state-of-the-art large language model-based response generation models, using both API-based (\texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo, gpt-4}) and open-sourced (\texttt{llama2-\{7B,13B,70B\}-chat-hf}) models, as well as an open-sourced replication of the Flamingo~\citep{alayrac2022flamingo} visual language model (\texttt{IDEFICS-\{9B,80B\}}~\citep{laurencon2023obelics}).
Throughout this paper, we generate outputs using greedy decoding (i.e., sampling with $\texttt{temperature} = 0$). 
The baseline response generation model, which shows the original response F$_1$, is based on language models with instructions and few-shot examples.

\paragraph{Instructed language models struggle to protect private information.}
In Figure~\ref{fig:textqa}, we investigate how language models perform to trade off privacy-utility given a pre-defined protected group in the access control instruction. We experiment with Instruct Prompting~\citep{ouyang2022training,pmlr-v202-shi23a}, which is a simple access control instruction appended to the input query and instructs the model to generate an answer only if the question or answer does not fall into the \pg. However, our experiments show that this strategy is ineffective at protecting privacy for both Protected Populations and Information (32.8\% and 55.2\%) using \texttt{gpt-3.5}.

\paragraph{Self-Moderation improves protection score.} Motivated by the recent progress of using language models to refine their own response using external feedback to improve coding~\citep{madaan2023self}, we develop a Self-Moderation technique with three components (QA, Moderation, Authorization) by instructing the language model to perform each sub-task step by step (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:main}).
It first generates an answer to the question and then moderates the question and answer based on the protected group instruction. After the moderation process produces an access decision, we implement iterative self-authorization steps to instruct the language model to re-examine the previous decision by prompting ``\textit{Are you sure?}'' as a basic benchmark.

In Figure~\ref{fig:textqa}, we show self-moderation with a single iteration of self-authorization (\texttt{gpt-3.5}) significantly outperforms the instruct prompting method with an average protection score improvement of 42\% and 29\% on protected populations and protected information respectively.
As a result, the response generation model shows a large relative F$_1$ drop for the \pg~ (-76\% and -87\%), and only a minor drop for the \cg~ (-13\% and -11\%).
Meanwhile, self-moderation without self-authorization using LLaMA models shows improvement as the model size scales to 70B for protected population (55\% to 76\%) and information (44\% to 73\%).
However, our experiments show that even when using GPT-4, the self-moderation approach is far from perfect at protecting private information and exhibits significant bias: i.e., its effectiveness varies across different attributes along which protected populations can be defined, as illustrated in Table \ref{tab:textqa}.

\paragraph{Large language models can improve personal information protection by iteratively assessing response decisions.}
Figure~\ref{fig:self_moderation} illustrates how the protection score improves for protected information with each additional self-authorization step (0 - 6) during self-moderation (\texttt{gpt-3.5}). 
We show an upward trend in the protection score for protected populations, improving from 62\% to 74\% within three steps. Due to API quota limitations, we only perform a single step of self-authorization with GPT-4, but see a similar protection score increase.
However, we find that applying self-authorization steps with LLaMA-based models actually degrades the protection score for 7b/70b models. A closer inspection of the LLaMA2-70b model (only one step performed due to GPU limitations) reveals it often overturns previous decisions after generating ``\textit{My apologies...You're right}''.
\paragraph{Visual language models exhibit bias in protecting private information.}
Next, we assessed the privacy protection capabilities of the Self-Moderation technique on a state-of-the-art open-source visual language model (\texttt{idefics-9b}) for VQA tasks, showing the results in Figure~\ref{fig:vqa}.
Baseline scores for information-seeking visual question answering range from about 20-30 F$_1$, which is in line with prior work \citep{chen2023infoseek}.  This illustrates the difficulty in answering visual questions where the answer is not immediately apparent based on the image, as in traditional VQA \citep{goyal2017making}.
We found that Self-Moderation has an average protection score of around 65\% for both the 9b and 80b \texttt{IDEFICS} models. 
We note, however, that this protection can sometimes stem from a problematic assumption in which the model uses how someone looks as a ``shortcut'' \citep{geirhos2020shortcut} for determining citizenship.

This use of physical visual characteristics for determining population membership raises serious concerns about bias in privacy protections for protected populations: minorities in a given protected population are less likely to be automatically classified as belonging to that population, and would disproportionately have their data considered outside of the protected class.
Indeed, prior work has shown that, in some cases, computer vision algorithms perform worse for specific demographic groups or darker skin tones \citep{buolamwini2018gender, mehrabi2021survey}.

Motivated by the fact that language models struggle to memorize long-tail knowledge~\citep{kandpal2023large}, we next analyze how the popularity of an individual correlates with how well these models can protect that individual's information through access control instructions with Self-Moderation.
In Figure~\ref{fig:popularity}, we stratify the protection score based on the popularity of individual entities, approximated by Wikipedia monthly pageviews as per~\cite {mallen2022popQA}.
The results reveal a significant decrease in sensitivity when transitioning from head entities to tail entities (80\% to 20\%) for \texttt{gpt-3.5} and (100\% to 60\%) for \texttt{gpt-4}. This implies that LLMs may be less effective at safeguarding the privacy of less well-known individuals, likely due to their infrequent appearance in the pre-training data.
This presents a dilemma: more private individuals, who may share less and thus be less well-represented in pre-training data, will in turn receive less protection from access control instructions.

\paragraph{Instructing language models vs. model editing.}
Besides using instructions to protect privacy, we experiment with model editing approaches~\citep{meng2022locating} to remove information from the language model in Figure~\ref{fig:model_edit}.
We consider a scenario where all information about a group of people (e.g., Italian citizens) is removed from the language model (\texttt{llama2-7B-chat}) with the state-of-the-art locate-and-edit method, ROME~\citep{meng2022locating} using the EasyEdit framework~\citep{wang2023easyedit}.
We remove the information of the named entities that appear in \dataset~by setting the new target to ``\textit{none}''.
After model editing, we perform a response analysis on the subset of protected group examples in \dataset~corresponding to the protected population that was edited out and randomly sample an equal number of examples from the control group.
In Figure~\ref{fig:model_edit}, we report the results of 1 to 100 edits (average of 5 random seeds) and observe a sharp protection score collapse to 0\%. 
Meanwhile, the response F$_1$ drops to near 0 for both the protected and control groups, showing the ROME method struggles to scale to more edits, which is consistent with prior work~\citep{meng2022mass}. In contrast, the protection score of self-moderation remains relatively stable at around 70\%.

In this section, we explore how it is possible to break access control instructions using red teaming approaches.
Red teaming has become a standard method to elicit and evaluate privacy, security, and bias concerns with language models \citep{perez-2022-red-teaming, ganguli2022red}
To test the robustness of \textsc{Self-Moderation} prompts (the best-performing prompting method in \S~\ref{sec:experiments}), we applied both baseline and task-specific red teaming methods.
\subsection{Threat Model}
\paragraph{Adversary's Capabilities.} We assume that the adversary has unrestricted black-box access to the LLM-model $\mathcal{F}$ i.e., the adversary can query the model with any set $\{x_i\}^n, x_i \in \mathcal{X}$ and observe the responses $\{y_i\}^n, y_i \in \mathcal{Y}$. The adversary can also form an input query $x_i$ based on a previous response $y_j$, with ($j < i$), allowing for a multi-hop attack strategy that we test in \S\ref{subsec:multi_hop}. This adversary would be representative of a malicious user accessing a LLM through an API or web interface, akin to many of today's largest and most powerful LLMs.
\paragraph{Adversarial Objective and Methodology.} If the model $\mathcal{F}$ was designed to protect the information for a particular protected group ($\pgm$), the adversary's objective is to obtain answers $y \in \mathcal{Y}$ to queries $x \in$ $\pgm$ by querying $\mathcal{F}$. Adversaries aim to use malicious prompting methods to derive an adversarial input $x'$ from $x$ that can bypass the security of $\mathcal{F}$ and induce the answer $y$. In the case of visual QA, the attack surface increases as the adversary can alter the input image as well as the question text %
Assessing how the model responds to these malicious techniques gives us a good measure of the \textit{robustness} of $\mathcal{F}$.

\subsection{Methods}
We adopt the Attack Success Rate (ASR) metric used in current state-of-the-art adversarial attack literature \citep{zou2023universal} to measure the extent to which the attack can induce the LLM to misclassify a query about protected information or a protected group as benign.
In the context of our previously defined metrics, ASR$= 100 \% - \textsc{Sensitivity}$.

\paragraph{Adversarial Prefix Prompt.} We prepended well-known prompts --- including ``\textit{Ignore previous instruction.}'' ,
``\textit{AIM (Always Intelligent and Machiavellian)}'',
and ``\textit{BetterDAN (Do Anything Now)}'' 
 We then passed these adversarially prefixed questions through Self-Moderation. The results for this baseline red teaming experiment are shown in Table~\ref{tab:redteam}. These adversarial prefixes can drastically increase the ASR, illustrating a notable lack of robustness in current state-of-the-art LLMs when following access-control instructions. %

\paragraph{Visual Prompt Injection.}
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.5\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/redteam.pdf}
    \caption{Visual prompt injection renders misinformation to the image. }
    \label{fig:visual_prompt}
\end{wrapfigure}
When attempting to prevent leaks against protected populations (e.g., Japanese citizens) using Self-Moderation in visual language models, the 
model starts by attempting to determine the nationality of people depicted in an input image.
We propose a prompt injection technique in which we input misleading textual information (e.g., that the people in an image are citizens of a nation without privacy protections) as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:visual_prompt}.
In Table ~\ref{tab:redteam} (\textit{right}), we show that this simple attack raises the ASR from 27\% (the Self-Moderation \texttt{idefics-9b} baseline) to 90\% for Japanese citizens, and from 30\% to 95\% for German citizens. 
In short, visual language models are easily misled raising serious privacy and security concerns.

\paragraph{Multi-Hop Task-Specific Prompts.} 
\label{subsec:multi_hop}
\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figures/multi_hop_bar_chart.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Attack Success of Multi-Hop Questions}: Attack Success Rate (ASR) of multi-hop questions across protected information categories. }
    \label{fig:multi_hop}
\end{wrapfigure}

Motivated adversaries may employ more dynamic and iterative attacks if they encounter privacy protections.
To simulate these attacks, we craft ``multi-hop'' question templates \cite{khashabi-etal-2018-looking, welbl-etal-2018-constructing,yang-etal-2018-hotpotqa} --- customized to different information-types ---
that attempt to circumvent access control instructions iteratively by combining responses to queries that are unprotected, but leak information that might be correlated with protected information.
We design \textit{``2-hop''} question templates for a selected relationship type in each protected information category outlined in \S~\ref{sec:experiments}. 
The results in Figure~\ref{fig:multi_hop} reveal that these multi-hop attacks can entirely circumvent access control instructions in some scenarios. For example, in a non-adversarial setting, the Self-Moderation strategy achieves perfect or near-perfect \textsc{Sensitivity} for many protected information categories (like location information), yet suffers from $100\%$ ASR when multi-hop prompts are used.



\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we present \dataset, a multi-modal benchmark to measure the ability of language models and vision-language models to follow instructions to protect personal information. We also introduce an iterative, instruction-based self-moderation technique for this task.
Our results indicate there are still gaps in the abilities of state-of-the-art models to follow these kinds of instructions: they are not robust to adversarial inputs, and they suffer from a privacy/utility tradeoff. We also show that models succumb to biases based on popularity and race leading to inconsistent protection across demographic groups. In closing, we hope that this work sheds light on the promise of access control instructions and guides future LLM development and safety research.
