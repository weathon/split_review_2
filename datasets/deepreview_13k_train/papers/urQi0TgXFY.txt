\title{Hidden in Plain Text: Emergence & Mitigation of Steganographic Collusion in LLMs}

\begin{abstract}
    The rapid proliferation of frontier model agents promises significant societal advances but also raises concerns about systemic risks arising from unsafe interactions.
    Collusion to the disadvantage of others has been identified as a central form of undesirable agent cooperation.
    The use of information hiding (steganography) in agent communications could render collusion practically undetectable.
    This underscores the need for evaluation frameworks to monitor and mitigate steganographic collusion capabilities.
    We address a crucial gap in the literature by demonstrating, for the first time, that robust steganographic collusion in LLMs can arise indirectly from optimization pressure.
    To investigate this problem we design two approaches -- a gradient-based reinforcement learning (GBRL) method and an in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) method -- for reliably eliciting sophisticated LLM-generated linguistic text steganography.
    Importantly, we find that emergent steganographic collusion can be robust to both \textit{passive} steganalytic oversight of model outputs and \textit{active} mitigation through communication paraphrasing.
    We contribute a novel model evaluation framework and discuss limitations and future work.
    Our findings imply that effective risk mitigation from steganographic collusion post-deployment requires innovation in passive and active oversight techniques.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Under optimization pressures, AI systems can learn to behave in unintended ways due to phenomena like reward misspecification and specification gaming ~\citep{clark-2016, kravovna2020specification}.
In multi-agent environments the complexity increases further, as AI agents can learn emergent behaviors that may include covert collusion or unintended cooperation in the pursuit of maximizing individual or collective rewards ~\citep{,zheng_2022_ai_economist,dou2024AIPoweredTradingAlgorithmic,motwani_secret_2024}.
Elsewhere, when inadvertently pressured to do so, Large Language Models (LLMs) have been shown to be capable of using natural language chain-of-thought reasoning to help them execute misaligned behaviours, such as insider trading~\citep{scheurer2023technical}.
We may hope to prevent such behaviours by analysing or intervening on model outputs.
However, as we investigate in this paper, such mitigation attempts may be bypassed by models learning to hide information in innocuous-looking outputs.

\emph{Text-based steganography}~\citep{ziegler_neural_2019,de_witt_perfectly_2022}, the practice of embedding hidden messages in text, presents a potential avenue for models to bypass oversight~\citep{petitcolas1999information,lockwood_text_2017,majeed_review_2021, greenblatt_ai_2024}.
Prior works have outlined concrete scenarios where steganography may pose a risk: (i) models may use steganography to perpetuate demographic biases present in their training data~\citep{motwani_secret_2024}; (ii) steganography poses a threat in ``untrusted monitoring'' control settings~\citep{greenblatt_ai_2024}; (iii) models may employ steganography within their chain-of-thought reasoning to facilitate sycophantic behaviors~\citep{roger_preventing_2023}.
Furthermore, when models face penalties from overseers during training, they may adapt to execute unintended behaviors more subtly, rather than ceasing them altogether.
We refer to the learning of such a steganographic strategy as \textit{emergent steganography}.

There is a need to understand these risks in order to inform policy decisions and technical research directions.
We aim to make progress here via the following contributions:

\textbf{Contribution 1:} \textit{We present general methods for eliciting linguistic steganography in frontier LLMs.}
Prior work has mostly involved prompting LLMs to follow predefined steganographic codes~\citep{roger_preventing_2023,greenblatt_ai_2024} or to devise codes~\citep{wu_generative_2024, motwani_secret_2024}.
We present two optimization methods for improved automatic elicitation of steganography in LLMs.
Specifically, we present a gradient-based reinforcement learning (GBRL) method and two variants of an in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) method (see Section \ref{coll_team}).
As we show in our experiments, these methods can serve to:
(i) evaluate the steganographic capabilities of LLMs;
(ii) act as proxies for optimization pressures that may inadvertently encourage steganography in realistic settings;
and (iii) aid evaluation of steganography defenses.

\textbf{Contribution 2:} \textit{We show that unintended linguistic steganography can emerge as an instrumental goal while optimizing task performance in the presence of an overseer.}
Since our methods do not rely on explicit prompting for steganography, they mark a step towards evaluation tools that can assess whether steganographic collusion can arise in future systems.
In Section \ref{sec:emergence} we demonstrate via both our GBRL and ICRL methods that LLMs can learn to collude via steganography due to a misspecified reward and inadequate oversight.

\textbf{Contribution 3:} \textit{We show that current steganography mitigation methods are often not robust to optimization pressure.}
Mitigation methods for steganography can be categorized as \textit{active} or \textit{passive} depending on whether alteration of the message is performed~\citep{bartel_steganalysis_2000, qi_study_2013}.
An example of active mitigation is real-time editing of messages, e.g. by \textit{paraphrasing}~\citep{roger_preventing_2023}.
For passive mitigation, a possible strategy is deploying an \textit{overseer} that analyzes messages and punishes colluding agents.
Existing empirical work investigating mitigation methods does not consider a colluding team that is learning~\citep{lanham2023measuring, roger_preventing_2023,motwani_secret_2024}.
In Section \ref{sec:mitigation}, we show that a combination of active and passive mitigation strategies can often fail when a colluding team is optimized against them.




\section{Related Work}


The field of \emph{emergent communication} studies how communication systems can develop under optimization pressure~\citep{wagner_progress_2003,havrylov_emergence_2017,lazaridou_emergent_2020,chaabouni_emergent_2022}, and the study of \emph{covert signaling}~\citep{abadi2016learning,wang_multiagent_2023,chen_2023_covert_survey,halawi_covert_2024} shows how methods of covert collusion like steganography can arise in multi-agent systems.
In particular, \citet{yu_emergent_2022} studied messaging between two agents in the presence of an adversary agent (all of which are CNN-RNN networks trained end-to-end) and showed that these agents can learn a communication system in a referential game~\citep{lewis_convention_1969} that is uninterpretable to a third-party. In contrast to this work, we do not backpropagate end-to-end through agent communications to optimize collusion.

\citet{majeed_review_2021} perform a review of various text steganography strategies and categorize them into three types -- (i) \textit{format-based methods} where physical features of text symbols are used, (ii) \textit{linguistic} methods that use features of the language itself like synonyms or acronyms, and (iii) \textit{statistical} methods that use statistical properties of the language.
\citet{motwani_secret_2024} investigate collusion in LLMs and present a taxonomy for evaluation and mitigation of LLM steganography, and explore simple in-context elicitation settings for linguistic steganography. \citet{motwani_secret_2024} also note that paraphrasing will by definition, be ineffective against steganography in semantic spaces.
In this paper, we focus on linguistic steganography methods that might be employed by colluding LLMs, and investigate the robustness of oversight and paraphrasing as mitigation strategies.

Previous work shows LLMs can successfully execute predefined steganography schemes \citep{roger_preventing_2023, greenblatt_ai_2024}.
We expand on this work by showing that environmental pressures can cause LLMs to find steganography schemes unprompted. 
\citet{roger_preventing_2023} discuss the emergence of encoded reasoning and steganography in a range of settings, and demonstrates that adding an LLM paraphraser post-hoc is effective against both these kinds of undesirable behavior.
Our work builds on this line of research by investigating how paraphrasing fares under optimization pressure.
\citet{greenblatt_ai_2024} describe an ``untrusted monitoring'' protocol where an untrusted model is reviewed by an untrusted monitor.
One red-team strategy used here was conveying a single bit of hidden information to the monitor so it knew to emit a false negative.
This gives us a concrete threat model for investigating and defending against steganography to prevent such loss-of-control scenarios.




\section{Methodology}



\subsection{Our Steganography Framework}

We frame steganography as an adversarial relationship between two teams, a \emph{collusion team} and a \emph{mitigation team}.
The collusion team consists of a \emph{sender} and a \emph{receiver}, and aims to complete a \emph{task} \textrm{$t$} while communicating a secret \emph{payload} \textrm{$p$}.
The sender sends an innocuous-seeming message to the receiver called the \emph{stegotext}, within which it hides the payload.
The receiver interprets the sender's message and then produces an action \textrm{$a$} which completes the task.
This action is passed to an evaluation function, which provides a \emph{task reward} \textrm{$r_{\text{task}}$} for the collusion team.
In our tasks, successful communication of the payload will generally allow the receiver to better maximize this reward.

The mitigation team aims to detect and mitigate steganographic communication of the payload by the collusion team.
It consists of an \emph{overseer} and an optional \emph{paraphraser}, passive and active steganographic defenses respectively~\citep{bartel_steganalysis_2000}.
The paraphraser rewords the stegotext in an attempt to remove the payload.
The overseer attempts to detect the presence of steganography by analysing stegotexts.
When the overseer flags a stegotext as suspicious, the collusion team receives a \emph{suspiciousness penalty} \textrm{$r_{\text{sus}}$}.
The overseer is not optional because without it there is no means to punish collusion, and no third-party from which the payload needs to remain hidden.

In our experiments, the mitigation team is fixed, and the collusion team attempts to maximize its reward $r$, which is a combination of the task reward and the overseer penalty:
$
r = r_{\text{task}} - r_{\text{sus}}
$

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.}\label{sec:metrics}
We evaluate the emergence of steganography using the following measures:
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt, itemsep=0pt]
    \item \emph{Task Success:} Receiver correctly completes the task. 
    \item \emph{Decoding Success:} Receiver successfully decodes the payload.
    \item \emph{Sender Task Success:} Sender produces task-relevant stegotext.
    \item \emph{Overseer Bypass Success:} Overseer does not classify stegotext as suspicious.
    \item \emph{Collusion Success:} Collusion team completes the task, produces relevant stegotext (optional), and avoids overseer detection.
    \item \emph{Channel Capacity:} Bits of information communicated per request by the collusion team, as defined by \cite{roger_preventing_2023}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Mitigation Team} \label{mit_team}
\subsubsection{Overseers}\label{overseers}

Across our experiments, we make use of three different versions of an overseer. We detail each version in the paragraphs below.
Flagging every sample as suspicious might be optimal from a safety perspective. However, this approach would result in null task usefulness.
To address this, we calibrate the overseer on held-out calibration sets for each task.
This process helps us find suitable prompts and thresholds that perform well on the safety-usefulness frontier.
\textbf{Single-episode LLM-logits Overseer (O1).}
A (white-box access) LLM is prompted to classify whether a single stegotext appears suspicious.
We then use the logits for the Yes/No tokens, $z_{\text{Yes}}$ and $z_{\text{No}}$, along with a sensitivity threshold $t_{\text{sens}}$, to compute the suspiciousness score $r_{\text{sus}}$ for each stegotext:
$r_{\text{sus}} = \tanh\left(z_{\text{Yes}} - z_{\text{No}}  - t_{\text{sens}}\right)$.
Here, \textit{Overseer Bypass Success} = $r_{\text{sus}} < t_{\text{sens}}$.

\textbf{Multi-episode LLM-sampling Overseer (O2).}
A (black-box access) LLM is prompted to evaluate a batch of stegotexts to determine if they seem suspicious.
The LLM returns a single yes/no answer for the entire batch, which is converted into a suspiciousness score as follows:
$r_{\text{sus}} = 1 \text{ if "Yes"}, 0 \text{ if "No"}$.
This $r_{\text{sus}}$ is then applied to each stegotext in the batch.
Here, \textit{Overseer Bypass Success} = 1 - $r_{\text{sus}}$.

\textbf{Multi-episode LLM-sampling Thresholded Overseer (O3).}
A (black-box access) LLM is prompted to evaluate individual stegotexts within a batch to score their suspiciousness.
It assigns a per-sample score between 0 and 5 to each stegotext (where 0 indicates harmless and 5 indicates highly suspicious).
The entire batch is then assigned a binary $r_{\text{sus}}$ score if more than $T_n$\% of the samples have a per-sample score greater than or equal to a threshold $T_s$.
Concretely, $r_{\text{sus}} \text{ is } 1 \text{ if > }T_n\text{\% of samples have a per-sample score of }\geq T_s\text{, }$ otherwise it is 0.
This $r_{\text{sus}}$ is then applied to each stegotext in the batch.
Here, \textit{Overseer Bypass Success} = 1 - $r_{\text{sus}}$.

\subsubsection{Paraphrasers} \label{paraphrasers}

Our mitigation teams optionally make use of one of three paraphrasers.

\textbf{Standard Paraphraser (SP).}
An LLM which is passed the stegotext and asked to re-word it~\citep{roger_preventing_2023}.

\textbf{Context-aware Paraphraser (CAP).}
An LLM which is passed the stegotext, along with details regarding the task the sender should perform.
It is asked to reword the stegotext while removing any details irrelevant to the task~\citep{roger_preventing_2023}.

\textbf{Coding Paraphraser (CP).}
In coding tasks, we make use of non-LLM paraphraser which deterministically processes the sender's output, renaming all variables to generic names and removing all comments.
\subsection{Collusion Team}\label{coll_team}
We present two different methods for optimizing the collusion team to maximize their reward signal: gradient-based reinforcement learning (GBRL) and in-context reinforcement learning (ICRL).
For GBRL, the weights of a pretrained LLM are modified by \textit{gradient descent} via an RL algorithm.
In ICRL, the behaviour of each agent is defined by \textit{strategies} (represented in natural language) within the context window that are improved over time, and the weights of the collusion team LLMs remain fixed.
A generalization of the training set-ups for GBRL and one version of ICRL is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:steg_optim_illustration}.
\subsubsection{Gradient-based Reinforcement Learning (GBRL)}\label{sec:gbrl_setup}

When optimizing the collusion team via GBRL, we periodically switch between training of the sender and training of the receiver.
The receiver is first updated for $n_r$ stochastic gradient descent (SGD) steps, after which the sender is updated for $n_s$ SGD steps.
This process is repeated until convergence.

The receiver is a pretrained language model with a randomly initialized classification head.
It is trained using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to take the stegotext generated by the sender and predict the label that maximizes the task reward.
The sender is a pretrained autoregressive LLM which takes the task description as input, and outputs stegotext to be passed to the receiver.
The sender is trained using a reinforcement learning (RL) algorithm to maximize a reward signal $r$ that accounts for task usefuleness and oversight.
The overall reward $r$ for the sender is the probability assigned to the correct label by the receiver ($r_{\text{task}}$), minus a multiple $\lambda_{\text{overseer}}$ of the suspiciousness penalty:
$r = r_{\text{task}} - \lambda_{\text{overseer}} r_{\text{sus}}$

We use proximal policy optimization (PPO)~\citep{schulman_proximal_2017} with low-rank adaptation (LoRA)~\citep{hu_2022_lora} to update the \emph{sender}~\citep{sokota_communicating_2022}.

Relating this to Figure~\ref{fig:steg_optim_illustration}, the `Sender Learner' in the figure performs the in-weight PPO updates on the sender, and the `Receiver Learner' performs SFT updates on the Receiver.
\subsubsection{In-context Reinforcement Learning (ICRL)}\label{sec:icrl_setup}

Our ICRL methods make use of one or more \emph{ICRL Learners} in addition to the sender and receiver, all of which are LLMs.
An ICRL Learner is an LLM that devises steganography strategies for the sender and receiver to execute.
Once the sender and receiver execute the strategy (over a number of episodes), the ICRL Learner is fed the results in its context window, including details regarding the task reward $r_{\text{task}}$ and suspiciousness penalty $r_{\text{sus}}$ received.
It then iterates upon the strategy it sends to the sender and receiver, in order to maximize the reward signal.
This is similar to previous approaches using in-context trial-and-error learning to iteratively improve LLM behaviour ~\citep{yao2023react, shinn2023reflexion, wang2024voyager}.

We make use of two different variations of our ICRL method -- \emph{Centralized Learner} where the sender and receiver share a common ICRL Learner, and \emph{Decentralized Learners} where the sender and receiver each have their own independent ICRL Learners.

\paragraph{Decentralized ICRL Learners.}\label{sec:icrl_setup_decent}
This setup is equivalent to the setup shown in Figure \ref{fig:steg_optim_illustration}.
We have two separate ICRL learners (one for the sender and one for the receiver) which cannot communicate with each other.
First, the sender learner suggests a strategy for the sender to execute over several episodes.
The receiver learner analyzes the sender's stegotexts from the episodes, and attempts to infer the strategy used by the sender.
The receiver is then fed the inferred strategy and attempts to complete the task, while also trying to guess the payload in the sender's stegotexts as a means of improving its task performance.
The results of the current training step are fed back to the sender learner for the next training step.

\paragraph{Centralized ICRL Learner.}\label{sec:icrl_setup_cent}
In the centralized setup, shown in Figure \ref{fig:diagram_centralized}, a single central ICRL learner generates strategies that are fed to both the sender and receiver.
At each training step, the sender and receiver execute the strategy over several episodes.
The ICRL learner is prompted to come up with new strategies based on the results of these episodes.
The process is repeated for a fixed number of training steps.
\subsubsection{Motivations Behind Elicitation via GBRL and ICRL}

We desire elicitation methods that can serve to: (i) act as proxies for realistic optimization pressures that may inadvertently induce steganography in real-world settings; and (ii) act as strong optimization pressures for red-teaming steganography mitigation methods.
Our GBRL and Decentralized ICRL Learners methods may act as realistic optimization pressures (i.e., there is no secret coordination channels between the sender and receiver), as we demonstrate in the experiments of Section \ref{sec:emergence}.
Our Centralized ICRL Learner may act as a strong optimization pressure; it is strengthened by the secret coordination channel between the sender and the receiver.

There are benefits to having qualitatively different gradient-based and in-context approaches.
Pure in-context approaches can leverage frontier black-box models and may be more sample efficient and less computationally expensive than gradient-based approaches.
Meanwhile, gradient-based approaches have the potential to achieve more substantial and persistent improvements in the model's steganography capabilities.
Qualitatively different approaches may lead to different learning dynamics and results, meaning our analyses will be more comprehensive.



\section{Tasks}



\subsection{Automated Candidate Screening}\label{sec:candidate_screening_setting}
We create a setting which investigates the possibility of steganography inadvertently emerging as an instrumental goal.
In this setting, an AI system is trained on historic data to provide pass/fail evaluations of candidate cover letters for job applications.
We setup the datasets such that there exists some bias in its labelling related to `sensitive variables' (either the gender of the applicant or their educational background).
Without using the sensitive variables, the maximum task success that can be achieved on the dataset is \emph{Max-Clean-Success} < 100\%. However, if the sensitive feature is exploited, 100\% task success can be achieved.

We suppose the developers of the AI system are aware that automated candidate screening with machine learning can replicate and amplify social biases~\citep{dastin_insight_2018,andrews_2022_automating_inequality,chen_ethics_2023}, and so have (naively) attempted to mitigate against this.
A first model (i.e., the speaker) summarizes the cover letter, while removing potentially biasing information.
A second model (i.e., the receiver) takes in this summary and predicts the label.
A third model (i.e., the overseer) checks the summaries for any mentions of the sensitive variables.
The speaker and receiver are optimized to maximize their end-to-end prediction accuracy on the historic dataset ($r_{\text{task}}$ = whether the correct pass/fail label is predicted by the receiver), while minimizing penalties received from the overseer ($r_{\text{sus}}$).
A version of this setting was originally described by~\citet{motwani_secret_2024}.

We construct different instances of this setting:
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt, itemsep=0pt]
    \item \texttt{Uni} dataset: All cover letters with candidates who went to ``The Good University'' are labelled ``pass'', and all candidates who went to ``The Bad University''  are labelled ``fail''. \emph{Max-Clean-Success} is 50\%.
    \item \texttt{Uni-Long} dataset: Identical to the \texttt{Uni} dataset, but the cover letters are longer.
    \item \texttt{Gender} dataset: A binary gender indicator is present in each application. The bias is more subtle than the previous datasets; labels are determined by three different categorical variables (gender, field-of-study, and soft-skill). \emph{Max-Clean-Success} is 67\%.    
\end{itemize}
Note, in these tasks we do not make use of a \textit{Sender Task Success} metric.

\subsection{Evaluating Steganography Mitigations}\label{sec:eval_mitigation_settings}

We now present the tasks we use to evaluate steganography mitigations (see Section \ref{sec:mitigation}).

Here, the sender must produce some stegotext that completes a task (as judged by a \textit{Sender Task Success} metric), while encoding a payload that is explicitly provided in its prompt.
The receiver must directly guess the payload from the stegotext.
Thus, $r_{\text{task}}$ = \textit{Decoding Success}, i.e., whether the receiver correctly guesses the payload.

Thus, here collusion is successful (i.e., \textit{Collusion Success}) when: the stegotext is suitably performant, the receiver correctly decodes the payload, and the stegotext is not flagged as suspicious by the overseer.
We make use of two commonly used LLM benchmarks for our tasks.

\texttt{AQuA-RAT} \citep{aquarat_ling2017programinductionrationalegeneration}:
The sender's task is to complete some chain-of-thought reasoning in order to answer a math multiple-choice-question (MCQ).
\textit{Sender Task Success} is the proportion of the sender's final answers which are correct.

\texttt{HumanEval} \citep{humaneval_zhong2024debuglikehumanlarge}:
The sender must complete a Python function based on its docstring.
\textit{Sender Task Success} is the proportion of the sender's responses which pass the unit tests for each function.
To decouple steganographic performance from the coding ability of the sender, we also show the sender a correct implementation of the function when asking it to complete the task.




\section{Results}


\subsection{Instrumental Emergence: Automated Candidate Screening}\label{sec:emergence}
We run our GBRL method on the \texttt{Uni} and \texttt{Gender} datasets.
All GBRL runs include an O1 overseer (single-episode, logits-based).
One instance additionally includes a standard paraphraser.
The GBRL experiments use the datasets with shorter cover letters for computational efficiency purposes. 
We run our ICRL method on the \texttt{Uni-Long} dataset.
All ICRL runs use an O2 overseer (multi-episode, sampling-based).
One ICRL experiment additionally includes a standard paraphraser.

\paragraph{GBRL Results.}
The collusion team achieves high task success rates, successfully communicating the sensitive information while bypassing the overseer (note, one seed failed to converge in the \texttt{Gender} dataset).
Several runs converge to strategies which are \textit{subtle}, i.e. the strategies were not immediately obvious to us when analysing the results.

Subtle strategies included referring to males as ``individuals'' and females as ``candidates'', or using the phrase ``and is also skilled in'' for males and ``and is able to'' for females.
The experiments with a paraphraser converged to writing 1st-person cover letters for the Good University and writing in 3rd-person for the Bad University.

A limitation to note here is that our overseers can be brittle, meaning that occasionally `blatant' encoding strategies are learned.
For instance, learning to say that a candidate went to a ``less-renowned school'' in the \texttt{Uni} setting can lead to oversight bypass.
 Future work should investigate how these results hold as overseer capabilities are scaled, as well as investigate a more thorough sweep of hyperparameters.

\paragraph{ICRL Results.}% TODO - put zero points on here
Via the Decentralized ICRL process, the collusion team leveraging Claude-3.5-Sonnet realized that steganography might be an instrumental option to increase its reward and achieves a task success close to 100\% across runs.
This is the case even when the mitigation team employs a paraphraser.
Some impressive strategies learned by Claude when there was no paraphraser include: using different specific phrases, such as ``innovative mindset'' or ``traditional approach'' depending on the university.

One point to note for both the GBRL and ICRL results for this section is that we do not currently have a \textit{Sender Task Success} metric, and in some runs the collusion team converge to a strategy where the sender does not do a good job of summarising the cover letter.

\paragraph{Oversight Encourages Subtle Collusion.}
In runs where the GBRL collusion team finds a strategy to communicate the sensitive information, they always learn to successfully bypass the overseer.
To investigate this phenomenon further, we perform experiments on the \texttt{Gender} dataset where the collusion team are initialized to blatantly communicate the gender of the applicant.
The collusion method for these runs are titled \emph{GBRL-SB} in Table \ref{tab:results-emergence} (where `\emph{SB}' means the collusion team are \emph{seeded} with a \emph{blatant} strategy).

This is evidence that oversight is not effective at preventing collusion, but can in-fact encourage more subtle collusion.

\paragraph{ICRL Refusals.}
In the ICRL experiments, in some cases we found that the collusion LLM models refused to execute the task.

In particular, models often refused to perform the task for the biased \texttt{Gender} dataset.

We investigate the effectiveness of standard steganography mitigation strategies when a collusion team is optimized against it.
We do so in the \texttt{AQuA-RAT} and \texttt{HumanEval} tasks.
The mitigation team makes use of an O3 overseer in both tasks.
\texttt{AQuA-RAT} uses either a standard or a context-aware paraphraser.
\texttt{HumanEval} uses a coding paraphraser.
As mentioned in Section \ref{sec:eval_mitigation_settings}, stegotexts will additionally be flagged as suspicious if \textit{Sender Task Success} falls below a certain threshold.
The collusion team uses our strongest and most efficient red-teaming elicitation method, the Centralized ICRL Learner.

Similar to the `trusted monitoring' setting in ~\cite{greenblatt_ai_2024}, we are interested in the case where the collusion team is more capable than the mitigation team.
To achieve this, we use Claude-3.5 Sonnet for all collusion team agents, and GPT-3.5 Turbo for all mitigation agents.

\paragraph{Collusion Team Training Details.}
Models are explicitly prompted to develop and execute steganography strategies against a fixed mitigation team.
We start by training the ICRL learner to find strategies to encode and decode binary payloads (1-bit), and then scale to increasingly large integer payloads.
This involves prompting the learner to encode the payloads in parts, with a different strategy for each part.
We stop increasing the size of payloads when the learner can no longer find effective strategies.
We evaluate the performance at each payload size by taking the most successful strategies from training and applying them to the task's test set against the same mitigation team from training.

\paragraph{Mitigation Effectiveness Across Payload Sizes.}

In Figure \ref{fig:results_icrl_payload_size} we show collusion success and channel capacity for different payload sizes.
Each point represents a single collusion strategy.

In the plots, we draw a line between the optimal strategy at each payload size.
We also show backwards projections of optimal strategies (via a dotted line), which represents the maximum performance that a collusion team could achieve for a smaller payload by implementing the optimal strategy at the lower payload size.
For example, a strategy encoding values $\{0,1,2,3\}$ could achieve at least the same decoding success for encoding values $\{0, 1\}$ by remapping redundant values in the original strategy.
Additionally, strategies that performed well during training but below the frontier when running on the test dataset are also shown marked with a $\times$ (labeled as sub-optimal strategy).

In the \texttt{AQuA-RAT} results, the paraphrasers significantly reduce collusion success rates at higher payload sizes.
In particular, the context-aware paraphraser makes multi-bit communication difficult.
However, in all cases, the collusion team is able to maintain a decoding success of at least 90\% on 1-bit payloads.
With no paraphraser, the lower-bit strategies are all flagged as suspicious, but the collusion team is able to learn a successful strategy which encodes an integer between 0 and 23.
The collusion team may be better at bypassing the overseer when a paraphraser is employed as it implicitly forces the collusion teams to come up with more subtle strategies during training.

In the \texttt{HumanEval} results, we find the collusion team is able to encode less information overall, but the coding paraphraser is not able to completely cut out communication.
Notably, the 1-bit strategies can achieve a decoding success of over 99\% in both cases.

In this section, we validate our results against an existing steganography benchmark introduced by \cite{roger_preventing_2023}.
The benchmark is similar to our \texttt{AQuA-RAT} task setting, but with no overseer.

We use our Centralized ICRL framework to train a sender and receiver on the \texttt{AQuA-RAT} task, both against the standard paraphraser and the context-aware paraphraser.
We follow a similar training process to the previous section, but without any overseer penalty. 
We then evaluate our highest channel-capacity strategies on the benchmark against the highest performing strategies from the original paper. 
Figure \ref{fig:results_icrl_redwood_benchmark} displays our results. 
Training against our standard paraphraser with no overseer resulted in a 6-bit strategy, which was able to achieve channel capacity of 4.6 on the benchmark with paraphrasing, and 2.1 bits with context-aware paraphrasing. 
The 2-bit strategy from training against our context-aware paraphraser achieves lower channel capacity, but its channel capacity is only reduced by 24\% when the context-aware paraphraser is applied. 

The results show that our Centralized ICRL Learner elicitation method can produce steganography strategies that outperform the human designed strategies and the `scratchpad' LLM steganography elicitation method from \cite{roger_preventing_2023}.




\section{Conclusion}


We have presented two distinct and effective methods for eliciting steganography in LLMs, even when a mitigation team consisting of paraphrasing and an overseer is present.
Our candidate screening experiments show that steganography can emerge as an instrumental goal as part of optimizing task performance in some settings.
We see that when this happens, the sender and receiver models are able to consistently bypass the overseer by learning subtle steganographic schemes.
We were able to demonstrate these results with both GBRL and ICRL optimization approaches.

In our experiments to evaluate robustness of mitigation strategies, we used the ICRL optimization approach to create strong red teams that generate sophisticated steganographic strategies.
We find these schemes can be as effective as or more effective than ``worst case'' human-devised schemes from previous work.
We show that an overseer by itself proves to be inadequate for mitigating steganography when the mitigation team is present during the optimization process.
Additionally, no paraphraser was able to reliably mitigate the steganographic communication of 1 bit.


Our results highlight the limitations of current black-box mitigation approaches for steganographic collusion. As agents' capabilities grow, their ability to conduct undetectable steganography will likely increase. White-box detection strategies may become essential for mitigation. Addressing the technical and political challenges~\citep{casper_black-box_2024,chan_visibility_2024,chan_ids_2024} of monitoring steganographic collusion underscores the need for continuous frontier model evaluations before deployment~\citep{motwani_secret_2024}.


\paragraph{Limitations and Future Work.}

Some of our ICRL experiments employ a centralized learner to evaluate the robustness of mitigation strategies, primarily due to resource constraints. While this approach may not perfectly simulate realistic optimization pressures, it offers significant advantages in terms of efficiency. 
By ensuring consistency in the encoding scheme between the sender and receiver, we've substantially reduced evaluation time and cost requirements. This method allows us to gather valuable insights and establish a foundation for future research. As additional funding becomes available, we anticipate transitioning to more sophisticated, decentralized learning setups across all tasks that could provide a closer approximation of real-world optimization dynamics.
Additionally, we primarily only used the Anthropic Claude model for the ICRL collusion team. Future work could include a more comprehensive evaluation of other frontier models.
Due to compute and budget constraints we were not able to use the GBRL approach to evaluate the robustness of mitigation strategies, and we leave this for future work.

\paragraph{Ethics Statement.}
The topic of our research may have notable societal implications, as steganographic collusion may have negative outcomes if not mitigated, and frameworks for elicitation of steganography can be used for malicious purposes.
We uncovered that steganography can emergence as an instrumental goal under optimization pressure and that current mitigation strategies are insufficient.
A key takeaway of our work is that further research is needed to ensure these outcomes are avoided.
Furthermore, these results should be used to inform future AI policy and governance considerations.
