\title{Contextualizing biological perturbation experiments through language}

\begin{abstract}
High-content genetic perturbation experiments provide insights into biomolecular pathways at unprecedented resolution, yet experimental and analysis costs pose barriers to their widespread adoption. In-silico modeling of unseen perturbations has the potential to alleviate this burden by leveraging prior knowledge to enable more efficient exploration of the perturbation space. However, current knowledge-graph approaches neglect the semantic richness of the relevant biology, beyond simple adjacency graphs. To enable holistic modeling, we hypothesize that natural language is an appropriate medium for interrogating experimental outcomes and representing biological relationships. We propose PerturbQA as a set of real-world tasks for benchmarking large language model (LLM) reasoning over structured, biological data. PerturbQA is comprised of three tasks: prediction of differential expression and change of direction for unseen perturbations, and gene set enrichment. As a proof of concept, we present SUMMER (SUMMarize, retrievE, and answeR), a simple LLM-based framework that matches or exceeds the current state-of-the-art on this benchmark. We evaluated graph and language-based models on differential expression and direction of change tasks, finding that SUMMER performed best overall. Notably, SUMMER's outputs, unlike models that solely rely on knowledge graphs, are easily interpretable by domain experts, aiding in understanding model limitations and contextualizing experimental outcomes. Additionally, SUMMER excels in gene set enrichment, surpassing over-representation analysis baselines in most cases and effectively summarizing clusters lacking a manual annotation.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


Large-scale high-content genetic perturbation experiments provide insight into the function of genes and enable the verification of biological hypotheses at an unprecedented scale (Replogle et al., 2022; Ramezani et al., 2023; Fay et al., 2023). A highly interpretable way of performing these experiments is Perturb-seq where genes are manipulated either at the protein- or transcription-level and singlecell gene-expression levels are measured in a pooled format (Dixit et al., 2016; Datlinger et al., 2017; Replogle et al., 2022). Yet, when applied at genome-scale, single-cell sequencing poses a significant cost burden and yields datasets of varying statistical power (Nadig et al., 2024). These challenges motivate in-silico approaches for predicting cellular responses to unseen perturbations to guide more efficient experimental design, and methods for automatically extracting high-level insights from perturbation screens.  

Current approaches for perturbation response prediction generalize to unseen perturbations by relating them to those perturbations that have been seen, often via knowledge graphs (Roohani et al., 2023). However, converting complex biology to structured graphs inevitably leads to loss of information, as textually-rich relationships between biological entities (e.g. genes) extracted from the literature are reduced to adjacency matrices. Furthermore, these methods are trained to regress the change in levels of genes upon perturbation: a task that is a precursor, but does not directly translate to downstream analyses like differential gene expression (Love et al., 2014) and gene set enrichment (Subramanian et al., 2005). Finally, most existing methods are black-box, revealing little about the learned biology without post-hoc probing.  

We posit that language is a natural medium for traversing the structured, biological knowledge relevant to perturbation experiments. To test this hypothesis, we propose PERTURBQA, a set of biologi

For example, rather than predicting the real-valued change in gene, we might ask: “does perturbation $p$ cause differential expression of $g?^{\bullet}$ These tasks are inspired by the standard analysis pipeline for extracting insights from perturbation experiments and evaluated over five high-quality single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets with CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) perturbations (Replogle et al., 2022; Nadig et al., 2024). While state-of-the-art graph and language-based methods attain reasonable performance on this benchmark, we find that these tasks are still far from solved.  

To demonstrate that language-based reasoning is effective on these tasks, we develop a simple LLMbased framework that matches or exceeds the current state-of-the-art on PERTURBQA. SUMMER (SUMMarize, retrievE, and answeR) is an inference-time strategy that uses knowledge graph relationships and existing experimental results to guide chain-of-thought reasoning (Wei et al., 2022) regarding perturbation outcomes. Specifically, an LLM is first asked to summarize textual descriptions associated with genes or gene sets, as well as their relationships to other biological entities. In addition to “featurizing” genes, this step can be applied iteratively to characterize clusters of genes that exhibit similar responses or effects. Next, based on knowledge graph proximity, we retrieve related perturbation-gene pairs from existing experimental data. Inspired by retrieval-augmented generation for language, this step helps ground the LLM’s reasoning and prevent hallucinations (Gao et al., 2024). Finally, the LLM answers the biological question via chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022) with respect to the knowledge graph summaries and retrieved examples. To summarize, our contributions are three-fold.  

1. PERTURBQA is a set of real-world and currently unsolved tasks that evaluate how large language models reason over structured data and discover new biology. We hope that this benchmark will increase the accessibility of in-silico modeling for biological perturbations.   
2. SUMMER is a LLM-based proof of concept that matches or exceeds state-of-the-art approaches on PERTURBQA without any finetuning, using a lightweight 8B model, while operating natively in biologist-interpretable language.   
3. We propose that perturbations should be modeled on the level of discrete outcomes, which reflect downstream analyses; and to use LLMs to reason about the outcomes of such experiments.  



\section{2 RELATED WORK  }


Perturbation response prediction Following Roohani et al. (2023), a number of works have approached the task of unseen perturbation prediction, primarily through the regression objective.  

Bai et al. (2024) replaces the MLPs in GEARS with a more expressive Transformer architecture, but maintains the same objective and evaluation. Ma¨rtens et al. (2024) utilizes language-based GenePT (Chen & Zou, 2024) embeddings, which we consider as a baseline. A related, but distinct line of work predicts the effect of seen perturbations in different cell populations (Bunne et al., 2023; Lotfollahi et al., 2019). Finally, there are also works that infer the effects of combinations of perturbations (Roohani et al., 2023; Gaudelet et al., 2024; Lotfollahi et al., 2023), which this work does not address, but could be extended to.   
Large language models for biology With respect to natural language, LLMs have been applied to a diverse set of areas, including but not limited to the following. Biological questionanswering (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Hao et al., 2024a) and bioinformatic coding (Laurent et al., 2024; Hou & Ji, 2023) are common benchmarks to assess LLM reasoning, but these works primarily focus on tasks that human specialists are already able to do. Hsu et al. (2024) uses LLMs to predict Gene Ontology terms (Ashburner et al., 2000) associated with known gene sets. In contrast, the focus of our gene set enrichment task is to characterize data-driven gene clusters, which may not be significantly enriched for any known gene set, but are of biological interest to understand. Finally, LLMs have been used towards active experimental design (Roohani et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024). More broadly, there are a number of single-cell (Rosen et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2024b; Kalfon et al., 2024) and biological sequence (Lin et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024; Schiff et al., 2024) foundation models, trained over raw biological data (gene count matrices, protein/DNA sequences). In this paper, we approach biological knowledge as described by natural language, but multimodal integration of foundation models could be a promising future direction (Wang et al., 2024b). Language-based reasoning and structured knowledge Significant research efforts have focused on improving LLM reasoning and reliability. Chain of thought (Wei et al., 2022) first demonstrated that explicitly instructing LLMs to reason enables them to solve much more complex tasks, compared to directly asking for answers. Subsequent works have explored how to navigate the space of “thoughts,” or in-progress generations (Yao et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2024). Orthogonally, retrieval augmented generation (RAG) Lewis et al. (2020) was developed to increase LLM reliability. Sets of relevant documents are first identified from a corpus, typically based on an embedding index, to act as source material for reasoning. Instead of querying an index, Graph RAG (Edge et al., 2024) was recently proposed to summarize corpuses into hierarchical graph structures, for richer languagebased comparisons. While these methods have seen significant success in natural language (Gao et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2023), they are less straightforward to implement in biology, where the vast majority of papers are inaccessible behind paywalls, and only abstracts are available (Wang et al., 2024a). As a result, these methods are difficult to evaluate in this paper’s setting.   
Finally, textually-rich knowledge graphs have been probed through language, both in the classical natural language processing literature (Guu et al., 2015) and in modern LLM literature (Jin et al., 2024; Besta et al., 2024). Language can provide embeddings to be processed by downstream graph models, or directly serve as the medium for prediction (Chen et al., 2024; Kau et al., 2024). In this work, we take the latter approach, which opens several design choices. Graphs can be embedded alongside text via parameter-efficient finetuning (He et al., 2024; Perozzi et al., 2024), directly serialized into language (Zhao et al., 2023), inform retrieval (Mavromatis & Karypis, 2024), or any combination of these options. To minimize the computational cost of our proof of concept, we serialize graph-based knowledge into text and also use graph structure to inform retrieval (of experimental outcomes, rather than textual information).   
3 BACKGROUND   
Modeling perturbations A perturbation experiment can be represented by a matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ , where $N$ is the number of perturbations, $D$ is the number of measurable entities, and entries $x_{p,g}\in$ $X$ represent the change in levels of entity $g$ under perturbation $p$ , relative to a control $p_{0}$ (Figure 1A). For example, in a CRISPRi Perturb-seq experiment (Replogle et al., 2022), the transcription of individual genes $p$ is suppressed and the resultant change $x_{p,g}$ in all genes $g$ is measured.   
Roohani et al. (2023) (GEARS) first proposed the task of predicting unseen perturbation outcomes in Perturb-seq data. Given $X_{\mathrm{train}}\subsetneq X$ , whose rows correspond to perturbations $P_{\mathrm{train}}$ , their goal was to  

complete the rows $X_{\mathrm{test}}$ , corresponding to unseen perturbations $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ . To generalize to $P_{\mathrm{test}}$ , GEARS and subsequent works leverage knowledge graphs that relate the two sets of perturbations (Figure 1B). That is, they solve a regression problem making use of a given directed graph $G=(V,E)$ , where $V$ is a set of biological entities (e.g., genes and pathways) and $E$ is a set of relationships.  

There are several aspects of this prevailing formulation that diverge from the insights biologists extract from these experiments. First, when converting knowledge graphs into adjacency matrices, the semantics of each edge are discarded, as they are typically annotated in free text (Ashburner et al., 2000). This loss of information may negatively impact model performance, as biological knowledge graphs often contain relationships of conflicting semantics, e.g. “enables” vs. “does not enable.” Second, current machine learning methods are evaluated with respect to “differentially expressed” genes, a small number of pre-selected features per perturbation, and often trained to focus on these genes (Roohani et al., 2023; Bai et al., 2024). These genes are not known prior to actual experimentation, and their actual identity is of high biological interest. Finally, the goal of perturbation experiments is to understand the underlying biology, but current methods focus solely on recapitulating the data distribution, errors in which may propagate to downstream analyses.  

Statistical insights Biologists draw conclusions of the form “ $\dot{p}$ impacts gene or pathway $g^{\ast}$ through statistical techniques like differential expression (Love et al., 2014) and gene set (Subramanian et al., 2005) analyses. In differential expression analysis, one assumes that $x\sim P_{x}$ , where $P_{x}$ is often taken to be approximately normal (Cui & Churchill, 2003) or negative binomial (Love et al., 2014; Ahlmann-Eltze & Huber, 2020). The goal is to test between  

$$
H_{0}:x_{p,g}=0\qquad\mathrm{and}\qquad H_{1}:x_{p,g}\neq0,
$$  

where rejection of $H_{0}$ translates to ${}^{\bullet\bullet}g$ is differentially expressed under perturbation $p$ compared to the control perturbation $p_{0}$ .” Differentially expressed genes may also be assessed by their direction of change, i.e. $x_{p,g}\leqslant0$ .  

Due to biological and technical noise, the measurement of individual genes may be unreliable, motivating statistical analyses at the level of gene sets. A data-driven approach for identifying gene sets is to cluster the rows and/or columns of the expression matrix $X$ and test whether more members of well-characterized sets are present in these clusters than expected by chance (Huang et al., 2008). While these “enriched” gene sets serve as the basis for annotating data-driven clusters, they do not consider the context of each experiment, e.g., the profiled cell line. Furthermore, significance cutoffs are difficult to assess, as the inclusion or exclusion of genes in gene sets was determined manually. As a result, data-driven clusters may exhibit consistent behavior in the experiment but fail to be enriched for known biological phenomena, thus eluding annotation (Replogle et al., 2022).  



\section{4 CONTEXTUALIZING BIOLOGICAL PERTURBATIONS  }


Our hypothesis is that traversing biological knowledge through language not only enables us to predict perturbation effects, but also to rationalize perturbation outcomes. To validate these ideas, we develop PERTURBQA, a suite of tasks that assess language-based reasoning over structured knowledge, in the context of biological perturbations (Section 4.1). These tasks are non-trivial, both for graph-based methods and naive large language model (LLM) applications (Section 6). Thus, as a proof of concept, we present SUMMER, a simple LLM-based approach that matches or exceeds the current state-of-the-art on PERTURBQA by jointly considering the structure and semantics of knowledge graphs (Section 4.2).

Domain knowledge PERTURBQA tests whether LLM frameworks can effectively leverage structured domain knowledge and contextual information. Thus, in addition to test examples, we provide:  

1. Harmonized knowledge graphs, with identifiers aligned to the perturbation data (Figure 2C). These provide high-quality, biological insights to aid reasoning. 2. Train examples, to be used as a retrieval corpus or for downstream finetuning. These are crucial for contextualizing the predictions on each dataset, as perturbation responses differ by cell line (Nadig et al., 2024).  

Specifically, for each gene $v$ , we have prepared textual descriptions $\{t_{v}\}$ , as well as textual relationships to other biological entities, $\bar{\{t_{v\rightarrow v^{\prime}}\}}$ for $(v,v^{\prime})\,\in\,E$ . To ensure coverage of poorly characterized genes, we extracted descriptions from the union of several knowledge graphs and databases, including: UniProt (Consortium, 2022), Ensembl (Martin et al., 2022), Gene Ontology (Ashburner et al., 2000; Consortium et al., 2023), CORUM (Tsitsiridis et al., 2022), STRING (Szklarczyk et al., 2022), Reactome (Milacic et al., 2023), and BioPlex (Huttlin et al., 2021). The first three were used for gene-centric descriptions, while the latter five were used for relationship descriptions (Gene Ontology used for both).

\section{4.2 OVERVIEW OF SUMMER  }


SUMMER is an inference-time framework that consists of three steps which draw inspiration from different aspects of message-passing neural networks and large language model reasoning strategies (Figure 3). First, to “featurize” each gene, we summarize database descriptions of the gene and its relationships to other genes/pathways. To capture directionality, we generate two summaries for each gene: as a perturbation, and as a downstream gene. Next, we incorporate the “train” set of seen perturbations as a retrieval corpus, where perturbation-gene pairs are selected based on knowledge graph proximity to $(p,g)$ . Finally, we formulate a set of questions to guide the LLM’s reasoning via chain-of-thought prompting.  

Neighborhood aggregation as summarization Inspired by message-passing on graphs (Kipf & Welling, 2017), we characterize genes and their relationships to other biological entities by summarizing their knowledge graph neighborhoods. Given a gene $v$ , we extract knowledge graph entries $\{t_{v}\}$ , describing node $v$ , and $\{t_{v\rightarrow v^{\prime}}\}$ , describing its relationships with neighbors $v^{\prime}$ . As illustrated in Figure 3A, we concatenate all entries $t$ to generate two versions of each gene summary $s_{v}$ . One asks for the downstream pathways that may be affected by the gene ( $v$ as perturbation $p$ ), and the other focuses on the upstream pathways that may affect the gene $v$ as downstream gene $g$ ).  

We can apply this step hierarchically to characterize gene sets. Specifically, to summarize cluster $P$ , we concatenate single gene summaries $[s_{v}]_{v\in P}$ as input to the LLM, with an emphasis on downstream/upstream effects, as appropriate. A variant of this is used to address the gene-set enrichment task, asking the LLM for both a short description (“desc”) and a “name” for the gene set.  

Graph-based retrieval of relevant training samples Let $N(v)$ represent the top $k=10$ nodes $v^{\prime}$ that share the most neighbors with $v$ in $G$ . For each pair $(p,g)$ , we sample up to 15 examples $(p^{\prime},g^{\prime})$ from $X_{\mathrm{train}}$ that match the following criteria.1  

1. Up to 5 examples with any $g^{\prime}$ and where the perturbation is related to $p$ , i.e., $p^{\prime}\neq p,p^{\prime}\in$ $N(p)$ .  

2. Up to 5 examples with any $p^{\prime}$ and where the downstream gene is related to $g$ , i.e., $g^{\prime}=g$ , or $g^{\prime}\in N(g)$ .   
3. Up to 5 examples where both the perturbation as well as the downstream gene are related to $p$ and $g$ , resp., i.e., $p^{\prime}\neq p,p^{\prime}\in N(p)$ and $g^{\prime}\neq g,g^{\prime}\in N(g)$ .  

2).  

Perturbation outcome prediction as question-answering We introduce a simple strategy to ensure that the LLM’s reasoning incorporates both knowledge graph summaries and retrieved experimental outcomes. For both $p$ and $g$ , we ask the LLM to identify the most similar $p^{\prime}$ and $g^{\prime}$ among those sampled, and to summarize their downstream/upstream effects. Finally, we ask the LLM to answer the overall question, while citing specific examples.2).  

Implementation details We ran all experiments with Llama3 (AI@Meta, 2024) with default parameters of top $p\ 0.9$ and temperature 0.6, using the LMDeploy framework (Contributors, 2023). Due to computational limitations, the gene summaries were generated by the 70B model, while all other inference utilized the 8B model. To quantify uncertainty, we ran the retrieval step three times and report the average prediction over these three trials.

\section{5.1 BASELINES  }


Differential expression and direction of change We compare against a variety of baselines for unseen perturbation response prediction. All baselines were run with their published code and best reported hyperparameters, where applicable.4). MLP is a multi-layer perceptron with access to gene identities only, trained with a ternary classification objective (no change, up, down). This baseline does not consider knowledge graphs at all. GEARS (Roohani et al., 2023) is a state-of-the-art Graph Attention Network trained over the Gene Ontology (Ashburner et al., 2000) and gene co-expression graphs with a regression objective that focuses on differentially expresses genes. Absolute predicted log-fold change is normalized to $(0,1)$ for differential expression, and signed log-fold change is normalized for direction of change. GAT (Velicˇkovic´ et al., 2018) is a Graph Attention Network trained with a ternary classification objective over the same knowledge graphs used to generate the prompts for SUMMER. This setup quantifies the information content contained in graph adjacencies alone.  

We also consider language-centric baselines and ablations for SUMMER. GENEPT (Chen & Zou, 2024) encodes textual descriptions of genes (-GENE) and their protein (-PROT) products using commercial OpenAI embedding models, trained on natural language. These embeddings are input to a logistic regression classifier, trained separately for differential expression and direction of change. Recent work has reported that this baseline achieves the state-of-the-art on the regression formulation (Ma¨rtens et al., 2024). LLM $(\bf N o\subset O T)$ provides the LLM with two examples (one of each label) and directly asks for the final answer without explanation. LLM (No retrieval) emulates our chain-of-thought style questioning, but does not retrieve any experimental outcomes. Instead, we provide the LLM with a hypothesis (each answer option is sampled twice) and ask the LLM to extract supporting and refuting evidence from the gene summaries, before answering the question. Finally, to understand the information content in our retrieved samples, Retrieval (No LLM) takes the mean label over $(p^{\prime},g^{\prime})$ without appealing to the LLM for further processing.  

Gene set enrichment We compare to gene set over-expression analysis (Fang et al., 2022), run over the gene clusters with a variety of gene set libraries – Gene Ontology, Reactome, CORUM – as well as their union (Combined). We take the concatenation of the top $k$ gene set names as the predicted summary, where gene sets are ordered by the size of their intersection with each cluster.

\section{5.2 METRICS  }


For differential expression and direction of change, it has been reported that gene responses tend to be correlated across perturbations (Kernfeld et al., 2023), e.g., stress response genes respond promiscuously. Thus, we compute binary AUROC over the predictions associated with each downstream gene, and take the average over downstream genes, corresponding to a macro AUROC score over downstream genes. For gene set enrichment, our ground truth consists of short textual descriptions (under 10 words), while our predictions and baseline outputs are long and vary in style. Standard text generation metrics like BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) or ROUGE F1 (Lin, 2004) do not account for this difference in length as they were designed for machine translation. To assess whether the predictions adequately cover the ground truth annotation without penalizing for longer lengths, we report ROUGE-1 recall and BERT Score (Zhang et al., 2020), using BioBERT 1.2 (Lee et al., 2020), which was finetuned on 1M biological texts (18B words).  



\section{6 RESULTS  }


6.1 DIFFERENTIAL EXPRESSION AND DIRECTION OF CHANGE  

We evaluated both graph and language-based models on the differential expression (DE) and direction of change (Dir) tasks (Table 1). SUMMER performs well overall, while other baselines vary in performance depending on the dataset. These results indicate that PERTURBQA tasks are largely unsolved, and may benefit from larger models or more sophisticated reasoning strategies.  

GENEPT is a strong baseline, demonstrating the benefits of textual information towards these tasks. In terms of language-based reasoning, however, we observe that LLM (No CoT) and LLM (No retrieval) both perform no better than random guessing – highlighting that retrieving existing experimental outcomes and guiding LLM reasoning are both essential to completing this task. This is also reflected in the strong performance of Retrieval (No LLM). While SUMMER is able to extract more value than “sum of its (retrieved) parts” in 7 of 10 cases, direct access to numerical reasoning is valuable. GEARS performance is close to random on 4 of the 5 differential expression datasets. This may be due to the focus on differentially-expressed examples in their objective, which was designed to recapitulate real-valued changes in DEGs, rather than distinguish between DEGs and non-differentially expressed genes. On other hand, GEARS is decent at direction of change in 3 of 5 cases, reflecting that its directionality loss may be more effective here.

7 CONCLUSION  

In this work, we proposed PERTURBQA, a benchmark for language-based reasoning over structured data arising from real biological problems. We evaluated current state-of-the-art methods on these tasks and showed that while feasible, they are far from solved. To address these tasks, we introduced SUMMER, a LLM-based framework that draws upon both biological knowledge graphs and existing experimental data. SUMMER outperforms baselines on PERTURBQA, but leaves ample room for future study. We hope that this work will lower the barrier of entry into computational modeling of biological perturbation experiments and enable richer, more interpretable methods for these applications.
