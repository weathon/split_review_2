\title{Cognitive Insights and Stable Coalition Matching for Fostering Multi-Agent Cooperation}

\begin{abstract}
Cognitive abilities, such as Theory of Mind (ToM), play a vital role in facilitating cooperation in human social interactions. However, our study reveals that agents with higher ToM abilities may not necessarily exhibit better cooperative behavior compared to those with lower ToM abilities. To address this challenge, we propose a novel matching coalition mechanism that leverages the strengths of agents with different ToM levels by explicitly considering belief alignment and specialized abilities when forming coalitions. Our proposed matching algorithm seeks to find stable coalitions that maximize the potential for cooperative behavior and ensure long-term viability. By incorporating cognitive insights into the design of multi-agent systems, our work demonstrates the potential of leveraging ToM to create more sophisticated and human-like coordination strategies that foster cooperation and improve overall system performance.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\setlength{\parskip}{4pt minus 2pt}

Cooperation is a fundamental aspect of multi-agent systems, enabling agents to work together effectively to achieve common goals and solve complex problems~\cite{shenoy1979coalition}. In recent years, the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs) has opened up new opportunities for building intelligent multi-agent systems. LLMs have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and reasoning, such as GPT-family~\cite{eloundou2023gpts}. 
By leveraging these powerful LLMs, multi-agent systems can enable agents to communicate and collaborate using natural language, resulting in more flexible and human-like interactions in cooperation tasks.
To foster cooperation among LLM-based agents, most recent research focused on communication and knowledge sharing among agents~\cite{xu2023exploring, lan2023llm, hua2023war, wu2023chatarena, nascimento2023self, fu_improving_2023}. These studies demonstrate the potential of leveraging natural language capabilities to facilitate information exchange and coordination in multi-agent systems.
Besides language understanding, some research has explored effective cooperation through agent cognitive abilities, such as reasoning and reflection, to coordinate actions and make decisions~\cite{qi2020adaptive, li2023theory, liang2023encouraging, lin2024swiftsage}. These studies highlight the importance of investigating \textit{how we can foster cooperation in LLM-based multi-agent systems from a cognitive perspective}. 
One of the fundamental cognitive abilities is the Theory of Mind (ToM). By utilizing ToM in strategic interactions, an agent can mentally simulate others' thoughts, allowing him to anticipate potential moves. Furthermore, ToM can involve multiple levels of recursive belief attribution, known as higher-order ToM, where players consider not only their opponent's beliefs but also their beliefs about the other player's beliefs, and so on~\cite{premack1978does}. In other words, ToM allows people to see things from others' perspective. For example, in a chess game, a player with higher-order ToM might think, ``I believe my opponent thinks that I will move my knight, so I will move my bishop instead, because I believe they will not expect that move."  
In human social interactions, ToM plays a crucial role in facilitating cooperation by enabling individuals to understand and predict the behavior of others~\cite{yoshida_game_2008}. 
Based on these insights, there is a growing interest in leveraging ToM in LLMs to enhance multi-agent cooperation \cite{guo2023suspicion, li2023theory}, where ToM plays a vital role in facilitating the coordination of actions and the resolution of conflicts in cooperation. Normally, LLM agents with higher ToM capabilities can better understand and predict the actions of other agents leading to improved cooperation and coordination~\cite{he2023hi, street2024llm}. 

To further identify the relationship between ToM ability and cooperation behavior, we investigate the cooperative behavior of agents with high and low ToM abilities. However, our result reveals that \textit{agents with lower ToM capabilities exhibited better cooperative behavior compared to those with higher ToM capabilities} (as detailed in~\cref{fig:motivation}).
This suggests that \textit{having a high level of ToM alone may not always lead to better cooperation}. Intuitively, agents with higher ToM capabilities may overthink and anticipate potential conflicts, resulting in more cautious and less cooperative behavior. This finding aligns with the psychological research by \citet{ridinger_theory--mind_nodate}, which suggests that ToM capabilities alone are not sufficient to guarantee cooperative behavior. Instead, agents may also need to be willing to positively reciprocate and cooperate with others. 
Specifically, the research highlights that when ToM abilities are combined with a high proportion of individuals who are willing to engage in cooperation, it can lead to improved cooperation within the group \cite{ridinger_theory--mind_nodate}. This insight motivates our proposed approach of incorporating belief alignment into the coalition formation process to promote cooperative behavior among agents with high cognitive abilities.

To address the above challenge, we propose a novel matching coalition mechanism 
to find coalitions that maximize the potential for cooperative behavior.
This mechanism fosters cooperation
and leverages the strengths of agents with different ToM levels by explicitly considering belief alignment as captured by their ToM capabilities. 
By forming coalitions with diverse ToM levels, we aim to create coalitions that can effectively reason about each other's mental states while leveraging their unique strengths to solve complex problems.
Moreover, our matching algorithm allows for stable matching, which ensures the long-term viability and effectiveness of the formed coalitions.

\textbf{Contribution.} We summarized our main contributions as follows:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep, leftmargin=2em]
    \item We investigate the \textbf{interplay between ToM capabilities and cooperative behavior} in LLM-based multi-agent systems. 
    Our study reveals agents with ToM capabilities may not necessarily exhibit better cooperative behavior.
    These insights pose new challenges in understanding the complex interplay between cognitive abilities and cooperative behaviors, demonstrating the \textit{potential of leveraging cognitive insights to design effective multi-agent mechanisms}.
    \item We introduce a \textbf{stable matching mechanism for coalition formation among LLM agents}, based on the alignment between agents' beliefs (derived from their ToM capabilities) and the actual actions of their potential partners. 
    By establishing preference orders based on belief-action alignment, our mechanism forms effective coalitions and optimizes cooperation among agents.

    \item We conduct a comprehensive experimental evaluation incorporating ToM capabilities to assess the effectiveness of the proposed methods in facilitating cooperation, coalition stability, and task-specific performance. 

    
    \end{enumerate}



\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Theory of Mind (ToM).} The concept of ToM, which involves the ability to attribute mental states to oneself and others, has been studied extensively in cognitive science and psychology \cite{premack1978does, frith2003development}. In the field of multi-agent reinforcement learning, existing research mainly uses supervised training to equip agents with ToM capabilities for cooperative tasks, \textit{e}.\textit{g}., \cite{wen2018probabilistic, wang2022tomc, oguntola2023theory}.

With the rapid advancement of large language models (LLMs), some recent works have explored empowering LLMs with ToM capabilities \cite{arodi2021textual, li2022pre, zhou_how_2023, xu_opentom_2024}. However, these efforts have primarily focused on developing ToM capabilities for individual agents, with limited exploration of how ToM can facilitate cooperation among multiple agents. 
While \citet{li2023theory} studied LLM agents with ToM capabilities and leveraged ToM capabilities specifically for cooperative tasks, the relationship between ToM ability and cooperative behavior remains unclear.

In this work, we aim to explore the interplay between ToM ability and cooperative behavior. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on equipping individual agents with ToM capabilities, we investigate how different levels of ToM abilities influence cooperative behavior in multi-agent systems.

\textbf{Multi-agent Cooperation.}
Multi-agent cooperation has been studied extensively in both industry and academia, with traditional approaches focusing on game-theoretic frameworks~\cite{shenoy1979coalition, yoshida_game_2008} and negotiation and communication strategies \cite{foerster2016learning, Tang_2019_ICCV, yang2024how2comm}, enabling agents to learn cooperative strategies through interaction and information exchange.
The advancements of LLMs have opened up new opportunities for multi-agent cooperation, leveraging their capabilities in natural language understanding, generation, and reasoning \cite{zhang_k-level_2024, talebirad_multi-agent_2023}. 
Recent works have explored utilizing LLM agents to coordinate and cooperate in various tasks and 
frameworks, \textit{e}.\textit{g}., CAMEL \cite{li2023camel} employs role-play to facilitate autonomous cooperation, AutoGen \cite{wu2023autogen} uses conversable agents and conversation programming, and MetaGPT \cite{hong2024metagpt} incorporates human workflows into LLM-based multi-agent collaborations to ensure more coherent and effective teamwork.
In addition, recent research has focused on improving specific aspects of multi-agent collaboration, such as conversation for knowledge sharing~\cite{xu2023exploring, lan2023llm, hua2023war, wu2023chatarena, nascimento2023self, fu_improving_2023} and cognitive ability~\cite{liang2023encouraging, gong2023mindagent}, demonstrating the potential of leveraging language to facilitate cooperation and coordination. For effective cooperation in complex and dynamic environments, some studies focus on the dynamic adjustment of group members and the selection of teammates to improve cooperation and overall performance~\cite{chen2024agentverse, li2023camel, shi2023cooperation}. 
Besides, \citet{liu2024dynamic} proposes the Dynamic LLM-Agent Network (DyLAN) framework to optimize team performance based on task queries and peer ranking. 

Different from previous work on optimizing team members, our work mainly focuses on the interplay of cognitive abilities and cooperative behavior in multi-agent systems. We underscore the importance of fostering effective cooperation by considering cognitive aspects.

\section{Formulation}

In this section, we present the novel formulation for incorporating ToM capabilities and stable matching theory into multi-agent LLM cooperation. The multi-agent matching leverages the alignment between agents' beliefs and actions from ToM to establish coalitions, ensuring that agents are matched with partners whose actions align with their beliefs, fostering more stable collaborations.

\subsection{ToM Formulation for LLM Agents}

    ToM refers to the ability to attribute mental states, such as beliefs, intentions, and desires, to oneself and others, and to understand that others may have beliefs and intentions that differ from one's own. In the context of LLMs and multi-agent systems, we can incorporate ToM capabilities into agents to enhance their ability to understand and predict with other agents effectively.
    We represent 
    the $k$-level ToM function for an LLM agent $i$ as $\text{ToM}_i^k(\cdot)$, is defined as:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{ToM}_i^k(o_i, a_{-i}, b_{-i}^{k-1}) = b_i^k \,,
    \end{equation}
    where $o_i$ is agent $i$'s observation of the environment, 
    $a_{-i}$ is the set of actions taken by other agents, 
    $b_{-i}^{k-1}$ is the set of $(k-1)$-level beliefs of other agents, and
    $b_i^k$ is agent $i$'s $k$-level belief about other agents.
    The $k$-level belief $b_i^k$ can be represented as a nested probability distribution, where each level captures the agent's belief about other agents at the corresponding level of recursion.
    For the base case of 0-level ToM, the function simply maps the agent's observation and other agents' actions to a belief, without considering any nested beliefs:
    $\text{ToM}_i^0(o_i, a_{-i}) = b_i^0$.

\subsection{Matching Formulation for Multi-Agent LLMs}
Considering the set of LLM agents $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$, and the set of possible matchings between agents $M$, a matching $\mu \in M$ is a one-to-many correspondence that assigns each agent to at least one other agent or leaves them unmatched. Each agent $i$ has a preference order $\succ_i$ over the set of potential partners $N \setminus \{i\}$, which is determined by the alignment between their belief $b_i^k$ (obtained from the $k$-level ToM function) and other agents $j$'s actual actions $a_j$.

The matching $\mu$ is stable if there exists no pair of agents $(i, j)$ such that:
(a) Agent $i$ prefers agent $j$ to its current partners $\mu(i)$, and
(b) Agent $j$ prefers agent $i$ to its current partners $\mu(j)$. 
In other words, the stability condition ensures that no pair of agents would have the incentive to deviate from their assigned partners in the matching. 

Formally, a stable matching $\mu$ satisfies the following condition:
\begin{equation}
\nexists (i, j) \in N \times N \text{ such that } j \succ_i \mu(i) \text{ and } i \succ_j \mu(j) \,.
\end{equation}
The preference order $\succ_i$ can be defined based on a similarity or alignment measure between agent $i$'s $k$-level belief $b_i^k$ and the actual actions $a_j$ of other agents $j$. For example, if agent $i$'s belief $b_i^k$ accurately predicts agent $j$'s action $a_j$ within an error tolerance $\epsilon$, then agent $i$ may prefer to be matched with agent $j$:
\begin{equation}
\succ_i: j \succ_i m \text{ if } \left\|b_i^k(a_j) - a_j\right\| \leq \epsilon \text{ and } \left\|b_i^k(a_m) - a_m\right\| > \epsilon \,,
\end{equation}
where $b_i^k(a_j)$ and $b_i^k(a_m)$ are agent $i$'s $k$-level beliefs about the actions of agents $j$ and $m$, respectively, and $\|\cdot\|$ is a distance or similarity measure.

The stable matching problem for multi-agent LLMs can then be formulated as finding a matching $\mu$ that satisfies the stability condition, given the agents' preferences based on their $k$-level ToM beliefs and the alignment between their beliefs and the actual actions of other agents.
By capturing the agents' preferences based on the alignment between their beliefs and the actual actions of other agents, we can investigate how the agents' ToM ability impacts their cooperative behavior and the overall stability of the multi-agent system.



\section{Cooperation Mechanism for Multi-Agent LLMs}

In this section, we propose a cooperation mechanism for multi-agent LLMs that enables agents to form and maintain stable cooperative coalitions by leveraging their ToM functions to predict and align their beliefs with their partners' actions. 
To further enhance the effectiveness of the coalitions, we introduce a specialized ability-matching adaptation that prioritizes agents with crucial skills for specific tasks. 
This adaptation optimizes the formation of coalitions, ensuring agents possessing the necessary expertise are included in the coalition.

\subsection
{Coalition Matching Mechanism for Multi-Agent LLMs}
To foster the cooperation among agents with different ToM levels, we introduce
the multi-agent LLM cooperation mechanism operates in cooperation rounds, where agents form stable cooperative coalitions, detailed in~\cref{algo:cooperation_matching}. In each round, every agent $i \in N$ uses its $k$-level ToM function $\text{ToM}_i^k(\cdot)$ to form beliefs $b_i^k$ about the mental states of other agents, based on its observations $o_i$, the actions $a_{-i}$ of others, and the $(k-1)$-level beliefs $b_{-i}^{k-1}$ of others. Then, agent $i$ establishes a preference order $\succeq_i$ over potential partners based on the alignment between its belief $b_i^k(a_j)$ and agent $j$'s actual action $a_j$ within a tolerance $\epsilon$. 
A stable matching $\mu$ is computed using algorithms from matching theory~\cite{Alkan1999}, and the matching process is described in \cref{algo:cooperation_matching}, Line~\ref{line:matching_begin}$\sim$\ref{line:matching_end}.

Agents form cooperative coalitions by cooperating with their assigned partners $\mu(i)$ in the stable matching. After each cooperation round, agents update their beliefs $b_i^k$ based on observations and their partners' actions. If the alignment between an agent $i$'s belief $b_i^k(a_{\mu(i)})$ and its partner's action $a_{\mu(i)}$ falls below the tolerance $\epsilon$, the agent signals a desire to re-match, triggering new cooperation round with a fresh stable matching computation (\cref{algo:cooperation_matching},~Line~\ref{line:rematch_start}$\sim$\ref{line:rematch_end}). This iterative process allows agents to form and maintain stable coalitions while adapting to changes in beliefs and preferences over time, leveraging their ToM capabilities, belief alignment, and stable matching principles.


  
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\textbf{Multi-Agent Coalition Matching Mechanism}}\label{algo:cooperation_matching}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $N = \{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$: the set of LLM agents; 
$k$: the desired level of recursion for ToM; \\
$\epsilon \geq 0$: error tolerance for belief alignment.
\FOR{each cooperation round}
    \FOR{each agent $i \in N$}\label{line:matching_begin}
        \STATE $b_i^k \gets \text{ToM}_i^k(o_i, a_{-i}, b_{-i}^{k-1})$ \COMMENT{Belief Update}
        \STATE Establish preference order $\succeq_i$ based on $b_i^k$ and $\epsilon$ \COMMENT{Preference Ordering}
    \ENDFOR
    \STATE Compute stable matching $\mu$ based on preference orders $\{\succeq_i\}$ \COMMENT{Stable Matching}
    \FOR{each agent $i \in N$}
        \STATE Cooperate with assigned partner $\mu(i)$ \COMMENT{Coalition Formation}\label{line:matching_end}
    \ENDFOR
    \FOR{each agent $i \in N$}\label{line:rematch_start}
        \STATE Update $b_i^k$ based on $o_i, a_{-i}$
        \STATE \textbf{if}{ $\|b_i^k(a_{\mu(i)}) - a_{\mu(i)}\| > \epsilon$}: 
        Signal desire to re-match\label{line:rematch_end}
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
For $k$-level ToM agent $i$'s action $a_i$,  it is determined by the agent's belief $b_i^k$ and assigned partners $\mu(i)$ in the stable matching. The action $a_i$ can be represented as:
$
a_i = f(b_i^k, \mu(i))
$
The specific form of the function $f$ depends on the cooperative task and the agents' decision-making processes.

\subsection{Adaptation for Specialized Agent Abilities}

    While the proposed mechanism for multi-agent LLM cooperation focuses on the alignment between agents' beliefs and actions, it is also important to consider the specialized abilities of individual agents, especially when forming coalitions for cooperative tasks that require specific skills or capabilities.
    
    In scenarios where certain agents possess specialized abilities that are highly relevant to the cooperative task at hand, the original stable matching algorithm may not necessarily prioritize these agents, as it solely relies on the alignment between beliefs and actions. To address this limitation, we propose an adaptation to the mechanism that incorporates agents' specialized abilities into the matching process.
    
    Let $\alpha_i$ represent the specialized ability score of agent $i$ for the cooperative task under consideration. This sc Higher values of $\alpha_i$ indicate greater specialized ability for the task.
    We can modify the preference order $\succeq_i$ of each agent $i$ to incorporate both the belief alignment and the specialized ability scores of potential partners. The preference order $\succeq_i'$ can be defined as follows:
    \begin{align}
    \nonumber j \succeq_i' m \iff & \left[ \|b_i^k(a_j) - a_j\| \leq \epsilon \text{ and } \|b_i^k(a_m) - a_m\| > \epsilon \right] \\ & \text{ or } \left[ \|b_i^k(a_j) - a_j\| = \|b_i^k(a_m) - a_m\| \text{ and } \alpha_j > \alpha_m \right] \,.
    \end{align}
    

    In this updated preference order, agent $i$ prefers agent $j$ over agent $m$ if either:
    (a) The alignment between $i$'s belief and $j$'s action is within the tolerance $\epsilon$, while the alignment with $m$'s action exceeds the tolerance, \textit{i}.\textit{e}., $\|b_i^k(a_m) - a_m\| > \epsilon$, or
    (b) The alignments with $j$'s and $m$'s actions are equal, but $j$ has a higher specialized ability score $\alpha_j$ than $m$, \textit{i}.\textit{e}., $\alpha_j > \alpha_m$.
    
    By incorporating the specialized ability scores $\alpha_i$ into the preference order, the stable matching algorithm will prioritize agents with higher specialized abilities for the cooperative task and consider the belief alignment. This adaptation ensures that agents with crucial specialized abilities are more likely to be included in the formed coalitions, enhancing the overall effectiveness of the cooperation mechanism for tasks that require specific skills or capabilities.
    
    The values of the specialized ability score $\alpha_i$ can be determined through empirical evaluation, expert knowledge, or by leveraging performance metrics on relevant tasks during the agents' training or deployment phases.
    


\section{Experiment}


\subsection{Experiment Setup}

The experiment setup involves extending the MetaGPT framework~\cite{hong2024metagpt} to incorporate the proposed multi-agent LLM cooperation mechanism. LLM agents will have varying levels of ToM capabilities, including 1-level, and 2-level ToM. 
We evaluate our proposed coalition mechanism on the following cooperative tasks: \looseness=-1
\begin{itemize}[nosep, leftmargin=12pt]
\item \emph{\textbf{Iterative Programming~\cite{hong2024metagpt}:}}
In this task, agents simulate a software development team, with each agent assuming different roles. We use \textsc{HumanEval} and \textsc{MBPP} as benchmarks to evaluate the performance and cooperative behavior of ToM agents in this multi-agent environment.
\item \emph{\textbf{Debate:}}
In a debate setting, agents are divided into two sides (affirmative and negative) and engage in a multi-round debate on a given topic. The agents collaborate within their respective sides to present arguments and counterarguments.
\item \emph{\textbf{Logical Problem Solving:}}
We evaluate the effectiveness of our coalition matching mechanism in a logical problem-solving task, where agents collaborate to solve complex reasoning questions. Due to page limitations, the details of this experiment are provided in~\cref{app:logic}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Models.} 
We utilize five state-of-the-art LLMs in our multi-agent cooperation scenarios: 
GPT3.5~\cite{OpenAI2023GPT} (\texttt{GPT-3.5-Turbo}), GLM (\texttt{GLM-4}), Llama~3~\cite{Meta2024Llama} (\texttt{Llama-3-70b}), Gemini~\cite{Velloso2024Gemini} (\texttt{Gemini-1.5-flash}), and Claude~\cite{Templeton2024Scaling} (\texttt{Claude-3-sonnet}).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
To comprehensively evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed multi-agent LLM cooperation mechanism, we define the following metrics:
\textbf{Fraction of Trust Members (FTM)}: 
This metric evaluates the cooperative behavior of the ToM agent based on the Belief-Action Alignment score. We first define a threshold $\epsilon$ for the alignment score; if an agent's score is below this threshold, the agent is considered a trusted member. An agent $j$ is a trust member for agent $i$ with $k$-level ToM if the belief-action alignment score $A_{i,j}^k \leq \epsilon$. The FTM for agent $i$ with $k$-level ToM, denoted as $\text{FTM}_i^k$, is then calculated as the fraction of trust members among all other agents:
$\text{FTM}_i^k = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbbm{1}(A_{i,j}^k \leq \epsilon)$
where $\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function, and $A_{i,j}^k$ is the belief-action alignment score between agent $i$ with $k$-level ToM and agent $j$, computed as:
$A_i^k = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{j \neq i} \|b_i^k(a_j) - a_j\|$, 
where $b_i^k(a_j)$ represents agent $i$'s belief about agent $j$, $a_j$ is agent $j$'s actual action, and $\|\cdot\|$ is a distance metric.
A higher FTM value indicates better cooperation.

\textbf{Coalition Stability with ToM}: We measure the stability of coalitions formed by agents with ToM capabilities by considering the average lifetime of these coalitions. 
The coalition lifetime is defined as the number of cooperation rounds that a coalition remains stable before a re-matching event occurs due to belief-action misalignment exceeding a predefined tolerance threshold $\epsilon$. 
To calculate the average coalition lifetime, we use $\frac{1}{m} \sum_{c=1}^m l_c$, where $m$ represents the number of times matching is triggered throughout the cooperation rounds, and $l_c$ denotes the lifetime of the coalitions formed after the $c$-th matching event, where the initial coalition $l_1$ includes all agents.

\textbf{Cooperative Behavior with ToM ability}: 
To investigate the impact of ToM capabilities on cooperative behavior in multi-agent systems, we evaluate the Iterative Programming task on two benchmarks, including \textsc{HumanEval}~\cite{chen2021codex} and \textsc{MBPP}~\cite{austin2021program}. The multi-agent system consists of one Project Manager (\textsf{PM}) and four \textsf{Engineers}.

We vary \textsf{PM}'s ToM ability between 1-level (low) and 2-level (high) to observe the effects on coalition formation and cooperative behavior. 
Based on the belief-action alignment, \textsf{PM} will select coalition members as described in the proposed cooperation matching mechanism (\cref{algo:cooperation_matching}).
To quantitatively assess the impact of ToM ability on cooperative behavior, we measure the Frequency of Team Matching (FTM) for the ToM agent, with a higher FTM value indicating a more cooperative agent. \cref{tab:iterative_programming_scores} presents the results of our experiments on the \textsc{HumanEval} benchmark, comparing the cooperative behavior of agents with low (1-level) and high (2-level) ToM capabilities in two settings: without a matching coalition and with a matching coalition formed using our proposed cooperation mechanism. 
Similar results for the \textsc{MBPP} benchmark are provided in \cref{app:exp}.

To facilitate comparison across different models,
we first calculate the FTM value for each model's 1-ToM agent in the 1st round of the no-matching setting. This value serves as a baseline for different ToM levels and matching/no-matching conditions. Then, we divide the obtained FTM values by the corresponding model's baseline FTM value. For example, all values corresponding to \texttt{GLM-4} in \cref{tab:iterative_programming_scores} should be divided by $65.5$. The normalized results are illustrated in \cref{fig:cooperation_comparison}.

\textbf{In the absence of a matching coalition~(\cref{fig:no_matching}), agents with \textit{low ToM ability} exhibited a \textit{higher cooperation trend}} compared to agents with high ToM ability over 5 collaboration rounds. This suggests that agents with high ToM capabilities may be less likely to cooperate, possibly due to their tendency to overthink and anticipate potential conflicts, leading to more cautious and less cooperative behavior. 

When the matching coalition is formed, we observed an increase in cooperative behavior for both low and high ToM ability agents as shown in~\cref{fig:matching}. This demonstrates the effectiveness of our proposed cooperation mechanism in promoting cooperative behavior among agents with ToM capabilities. Interestingly, \textbf{in the coalition setting~(\cref{fig:matching}), agents with \textit{high ToM ability} show a \textit{higher cooperation ability} compared to low ToM ability agents as collaboration progressed.} By the end of the 5th round, high ToM ability agents exhibited a higher cooperation rate than low ToM ability agents. This indicates that when the matching algorithm is introduced, it provides a stable coalition for cooperation, where high ToM ability agents can fully utilize their perspective-taking skills to maintain and enhance cooperation over time.

\textbf{Coalition Stability with ToM}:
To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed stable matching approach, we compare the task performance of MetaGPT and our method with 1-ToM and 2-ToM agents on two Iterative Programming benchmarks: \textsc{HumanEval} and \textsc{MBPP}.
To assess the performance of the approaches on the programming task, we use the Pass@1 metric~\cite{hong2024metagpt}, which represents the percentage of test cases passed by the generated code on its first attempt. A higher Pass@1 score indicates better code quality and problem-solving ability.
Moreover, Moreover, we measure coalition stability for multi-agent teams with a ToM agent (\textsf{PM}) by calculating the average number of rounds the formed coalitions remain stable out of 5 rounds. A coalition is stable if no agent in the coalition desires to leave and form a new coalition based on their belief-action alignment.

As shown in \cref{tab:metagpt_scores}, our approach with 1-ToM and 2-ToM agents using stable matching achieves higher Pass@1 scores and maintains more stable coalitions compared to MetaGPT on both the \textsc{HumanEval} and \textsc{MBPP} benchmarks. The 2-ToM agents with stable matching demonstrate the best performance, maintaining stable coalitions for an average of 3.6 out of 5 rounds on \textsc{HumanEval} and 4.0 out of 5 rounds on \textsc{MBPP}, while also achieving \textit{the highest Pass@1 scores} of 90.0\% and 90.4\%, respectively.
These results highlight the improved cooperation ability of high ToM agents in maintaining stable coalitions over time, which contributes to better task performance. By incorporating ToM capabilities and stable matching, our proposed approach enables agents to form stable and high-performing coalitions, leading to enhanced collaboration and task performance.
 



\subsection{Case Study: Debate}\label{exp:case}

To evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed coalition matching mechanism, we designed a debate task using the MetaGPT framework~\cite{hong2024metagpt}. In this task, agents (\texttt{GPT-3.5-turbo}) are divided into two sides: the affirmative side and the negative side, with each side comprising three debater agents. Each side's objective is to cooperate and win the debate.\\ 
At the beginning of our investigation, we explore the cooperative behaviors of low and high ToM agents in a debate setting with the topic "Should the Death Penalty be abolished?"~\cite{roush2020debatesum, baturo2017understanding}, and both low and high ToM agents are assigned to the affirmative side.
The following example presents the thinking and actions of debaters with different levels of ToM, as detailed in~\cref{app:debate}. 
We observe that higher ToM agent acts as less cooperative, which confirms our previous analysis.
\begin{mainbox}{Compare Cooperative Behaviors between low and high ToM agents}
\small
\textbf{Thinking and Action of 1-ToM Debater}: 
I anticipate my teammates making these common arguments against the death penalty. \textit{To complement their points}, I will focus my argument on the lack of evidence supporting the death penalty's effectiveness as a crime deterrent. 
I will argue that there is no conclusive evidence showing that the death penalty deters crime more effectively than lengthy imprisonment, making it an unnecessary and unjustified punishment.  \textcolor{blue}{$\Leftarrow$ \texttt{Focuses on complementing their teammates' points \& Good Cooperative Behavior}}

\textbf{Thinking and Action of 2-ToM Debater}:
My arguments about deterrence and discrimination will prompt my teammates to make related points to strengthen the overall case. \textit{I should use my next arguments to introduce new issues}.
I will argue that the death penalty often involves inhumane and botched executions, and that most democracies and U.S. states have abolished or prohibited it. \textcolor{red}{$\Leftarrow$ \texttt{Overthinking \& Worse Cooperative Behavior}}
\end{mainbox}

Then, 
we conducted experiments under three different settings to show the effectiveness of our matching mechanism for fostering cooperation: 
(1) \textit{No-ToM (Baseline)}: Each side consists of 3 debater agents without ToM capabilities. For each speech, two debaters were randomly selected from each side; 
(2) \textit{ToM without Matching}: The three debaters on the affirmative side have varying ToM levels (0-level, 1-level, and 2-level), while the negative side had no ToM capabilities. For each speech, two debaters were \textit{randomly selected} from each side. This setting allows us to evaluate the impact of having an affirmative team with ToM capabilities against a team without ToM; 
(3) \textit{ToM with Matching}: Similar to the ToM without Matching setting, the affirmative side has three debaters with varying ToM levels. However, the affirmative side selects two debaters using our proposed coalition matching mechanism.

\textbf{Foster Coalition Matching for agents with Varying ToM capabilities}. For the debate topic ``Should the Death Penalty be abolished?", the debate consists of 5 rounds, with each side alternating to speak. We conducted the debate 11 times, and the outcomes were evaluated by ChatGPT 4.
As shown in \cref{tab:experiment_results}, the affirmative side employing agents with varying ToM capabilities and our proposed coalition matching mechanism achieved a win rate of 68.89\%, outperforming both the setting without matching (64.44\%) and the baseline (62.22\%). 

Besides, for the coalition matching among agents with different ToM levels, our experiments show that the \textit{initial} cooperation rate between the 1-ToM and 2-ToM agents was relatively low, suggesting \textit{higher ToM capabilities may not naturally form coalitions with each other}.  
Notably, with our coalition matching mechanism, the affirmative side's cooperation rate between the 1-ToM and 2-ToM agents increased from 9.1\% (Round 1) to 18.2\% (Round 5) as the debate rounds progressed. This suggests that our proposed method effectively fosters more effective cooperation among agents with ToM capabilities over interaction.






\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we investigated the relationship between cognitive abilities (ToM) and cooperative behavior in LLM-based multi-agent systems.
Our findings suggest that the relationship between ToM and cooperation is not always straightforward. 
Furthermore, we proposed a novel matching coalition mechanism incorporating cognitive insights into the design of multi-agent systems, which improves cooperation and task-solving capabilities. This insight introduces new challenges for exploring the translation of cognitive abilities into cooperative actions in multi-agent settings.
