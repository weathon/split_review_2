\title{Don't Miss the Forest for the Trees: Attentional Vision Calibration for Large Vision Language Models}

\begin{abstract}
This study addresses the issue observed in Large Vision Language Models (LVLMs), where excessive attention on a few image tokens, referred to as \textit{blind tokens}, leads to hallucinatory responses in tasks requiring fine-grained understanding of visual objects.
We found that tokens receiving lower attention weights often hold essential information for identifying nuanced object details --- ranging from merely recognizing object existence to identifying their attributes (color, position, \etc) and understanding their relationships.
To counteract the over-emphasis on blind tokens and to accurately respond to user queries, we introduce a technique called \OursFullAbb.
During the decoding phase, \Ours identifies blind tokens by analyzing the image-related attention distribution.
It then dynamically adjusts the logits for the next token prediction by contrasting the logits conditioned on the original visual tokens with those conditioned on the blind tokens.
This effectively lowers the dependency on blind tokens and promotes a more balanced consideration of all tokens.
We validate \Ours on benchmarks such as POPE, MME, and AMBER, where it consistently outperforms existing decoding techniques in mitigating object hallucinations in LVLMs.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


\label{sec:intro}

Large Vision Language Models (LVLMs)~\cite{dai2024instructblip,zhu2023minigpt,liu2023visual,liu2023improved,bai2023qwen} have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in generating coherent and contextually relevant descriptions from visual inputs.
This success largely hinges on the models' ability to interpret and integrate complex visual information with textual data.
However, a significant challenge that persists in these models is their tendency towards "hallucinations" --- producing inaccurate or fabricated descriptions that do not accurately reflect the visual data.
The phenomenon of hallucination in LVLMs can significantly impede their reliability, especially in applications requiring precise and trustworthy visual descriptions.
\input{figures/01}
As shown in~\cref{fig:observation_bias} LVLMs~\cite{liu2023visual,dai2024instructblip} exhibit biased attention towards certain image tokens, which we refer to as \textit{blind tokens}.
Even when all the pixels in the image contain identical information and when the image does not contain any information relevant to the query, LVLMs~\cite{dai2024instructblip,liu2023visual} tend to focus their attention on a few specific image tokens.
This pattern of focusing on certain tokens, despite their lack of meaningful content, highlights potential flaws in the decoding processes of LVLMs.
Further analysis of the attention patterns in LVLMs is demonstrated in~\cref{fig:motivation}.
We examine the attention distribution of LLaVA-1.5~\cite{liu2023visual} in response to the given image and query.
The attention weights appear to align reasonably well with object regions, indicating that the model's attention is appropriately targeted at relevant areas.
However, deeper scrutiny into the functional impact of these weights on response predictions reveals intriguing insights.
Zeroing out image tokens that receive the bulk of attention does not significantly impact the original prediction logits.
This suggests that LVLMs might assign high attention weights to tokens that do not carry substantial object-discriminative information.
On the other hand, zeroing out image tokens with low attention weights drastically changes the prediction logits, leading to near-equal probabilities and indicating a loss of crucial object-discriminative information.
Such skewed attention disproportionately favors blind tokens and often overlooks tokens that might contain finer details, potentially resulting in misclassifications or entirely incorrect predictions.
In response to this challenge, we propose a novel method termed \OursFullAbb which recalibrates the model’s attention on the fly during the decoding phase.
Unlike existing approaches that typically require extensive training~\cite{jiang2023hallucination, sun2023aligning, zhou2023analyzing, yu2023rlhf} or auxiliary models~\cite{zhao2024mitigating, wan2024contrastive, deng2024seeing, yang2024pensieve, li2023visual}, \Ours operates without these prerequisites.
\Ours dynamically modifies the decoding process in three steps:
\textbf{(i)} Based on our finding that different LVLM exhibit different attentional patterns (see~\cref{fig:layer_attention}), we first select relevant layers that allocate a higher attention proportion to the image tokens.
\textbf{(ii)} Next, we identify \textit{blind tokens}, which disproportionately monopolize attention.
These tokens are isolated, and all other image tokens are zeroed out, creating a biased visual input.
\textbf{(iii)} Finally, we employ a contrastive decoding~\cite{leng2023mitigating,favero2024multi}.
This technique contrasts the logits calculated from the original visual input with those derived solely from the blind tokens.
By doing so, it amplifies the influence of tokens that exhibit significant differences between the two distributions.
The recalibration process aims to decrease the reliance on blind tokens—those that occupy substantial attention but lack detailed content—and increase focus on underemphasized tokens that may hold crucial details.
As a result, \Ours promotes a more balanced attention distribution across the image.
This recalibration not only reduces hallucinations but also enhances the overall interpretative accuracy of LVLMs.
Through a series of experiments involving benchmarks like POPE~\cite{rohrbach2018object}, CHAIR~\cite{li2023evaluating}, MME~\cite{fu2024mme}, and AMBER~\cite{wang2023llm}, we demonstrate that \Ours significantly mitigates hallucination while simultaneously improving the models' ability to capture and describe detailed image attributes more accurately.

\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:related_work}

LVLMs~\cite{li2023blip,dai2024instructblip,zhu2023minigpt,liu2023visual,liu2023improved,chen2023shikra} are prone to generating hallucinations, \ie, misalignment between visual inputs and textual outputs.
These hallucinations manifest across various semantic dimensions such as incorrect object presence, attributes, or relations.

To mitigate these, researchers have developed strategies across three levels:

\noindent\textbf{Input-level.}
Efforts here focus on data quality improvement to reduce hallucinations~\cite{gunjal2023detecting,liu2023mitigating,wang2023vigc,lu2024evaluation}, including the introduction of negative data~\cite{liu2023mitigating}, counterfactual data~\cite{yu2023hallucidoctor} to challenge the model's assumptions, dataset cleansing to minimize noise and errors~\cite{wang2024mitigating,yue2024less}.

\noindent\textbf{Model-level.}
This includes increasing the resolution at which models process visual data~\cite{chen2023internvl,liu2023improved,liu2023visual,zhai2023halle}, or enhancing perception abilities through advanced vision encoders~\cite{he2024incorporating,jain2023vcoder,tong2024eyes}.
These are usually training-based~\cite{jiang2023hallucination,yue2024less}, and often involve auxiliary supervision from external datasets~\cite{chen2023mitigating} and reinforcement learning techniques~\cite{ben2023mocha,zhao2023beyond,gunjal2024detecting,sun2023aligning,yu2023rlhf} to better align model outputs with accurate visual representations.

\noindent\textbf{Output-level.}
Techniques like contrastive decoding~\cite{leng2023mitigating,favero2024multi} directly contrast incorrect predictions during decoding, helping models distinguish between accurate and inaccurate descriptions.
Guided decoding~\cite{zhao2024mitigating,deng2024seeing,chen2024halc} leverages external models like CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} or DETR~\cite{carion2020end} to enhance accuracy.
Other approaches include training-free methods~\cite{wan2024contrastive,zhang2024debiasing,huang2023opera} and post-hoc corrections via self-feedback~\cite{lee2023volcano,wu2024logical}.

Among these, we focus on contrastive decoding methods:
(1) VCD~\cite{leng2023mitigating} mitigates statistical biases and language priors by contrasting output distributions from original and distorted visual inputs, moderating decoding probabilities.
(2) M3ID~\cite{favero2024multi} uses a similar approach where the reference image amplifies its influence over the language prior, thereby enhancing the generation of tokens with higher mutual information with the visual prompt.

Our approach belongs to the output-level category.
\Ours analyzes attention patterns to identify \textit{blind tokens} during decoding steps.
It then utilizes a contrastive decoding technique to enhance token prediction.
Our method does not require additional training, external data or models, and costly self-feedback mechanisms.


\section{Approach: \Ours}

\label{sec:approach}
\input{figures/04}
We propose a straightforward method, called \Ours, to enhance visual object understanding in LVLMs during the decoding phase.
\Ours dynamically calibrates the over-emphasis on \textit{blind tokens} on-the-fly at every token generation step.
The calibration is guided by the attention patterns of image tokens in response to the given image and textual query.
Importantly, \Ours operates without additional training, external models, or complex self-feedback mechanisms.
A visual summary of our method is shown in~\cref{fig:overview}.
\Ours modifies the decoding process in three steps: \textbf{(1) Layer selection}: choose layers significantly influenced by image tokens, \textbf{(2) Blind token identification}: detect non-relevant tokens in selected layers, and \textbf{(3) Contrastive decoding}: adjust the decoding process to mitigate the influence of blind tokens.
LVLM begins by encoding visual inputs and textual queries into compact representations.
Visual inputs provide contextual information that helps generate responses relevant to the textual queries.
The text data is tokenized, turning it into a sequence of manageable pieces for further processing.
For visual data, a commonly used encoder is a pre-trained model like CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning}, which is already semantically aligned with textual data through extensive training on image-text pairs.

As LLM inherently perceives only text, aligning text and vision modalities is essential.
Instead of retraining LLM, which would be prohibitively expensive, a more viable approach is to use a learnable cross-modal alignment module.
This module, such as Q-Former~\cite{li2023blip} or a linear projection layer~\cite{liu2023visual}, transforms visual features into a format compatible with the LLM’s input space.
This process results in a set of visual tokens, $\mathcal{V} = \{\nu_0, \nu_1, \dots, \nu_{N-1}\}$, which are then concatenated with the text tokens, $\mathcal{Q} = \{\sigma_N, \sigma_{N+1}, \dots, \sigma_{N+M-1}\}$, to form a unified input sequence of length $N+M$.

The concatenated sequence of visual and textual tokens is then processed by LVLM, parametrized by $\theta$, which generates responses in an auto-regressive manner.
The model calculates logits for each potential next token:
\begin{equation}
\ell_t = \log p(\xi_t | \mathcal{V}, \mathcal{Q}, \xi_{<t}; \theta),
\end{equation}
where $\ell_t$ are the logits for the next token at timestep $t$, $\xi_t$ denotes the next token being predicted, and $\xi_{<t}$ represents the sequence of tokens generated up to timestep $(t - 1)$.
From these logits, we apply a softmax function to convert logits into a normalized probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
p(\xi_t) =  \mathrm{Softmax}(\ell_t).
\end{equation}
The next token $\xi_t$ is sampled from this probability distribution, with the model continuing this predictive process until the response sequence is complete.

Visual hallucinations in LVLMs can emerge during the decoding phase when the model selects tokens based on erroneous probability distributions that do not align with the visual inputs.
These discrepancies, as demonstrated in our observations (refer to~\cref{fig:observation_bias,fig:motivation}), often originate from an attentional bias toward certain non-relevant tokens, referred to as \textit{blink tokens}.
Our methodology aims to recalibrate these attention patterns to correct such hallucinations.

\noindent\textbf{Layer selection.}
As illustrated in~\cref{fig:layer_attention}, the attentional behavior of LVLM layers varies depending on the model's architecture or size.
For example, InstructBLIP~\cite{dai2024instructblip} shows increasing attention levels in the later layers, whereas LLaVA-1.5~\cite{liu2023visual} exhibits a concentration of attention in the earlier layers.
To adapt these diverse models, we initially focus on selecting layers that exhibit a high proportion of image-related attention.
Formally, we define the attention weight matrix for $i$-th layer as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{A}_i = \left[ \mathbf{a}_{h,q,k}^i \right]_{(h,q,k)=(1,1,1)}^{(h,q,k)=(H,N+M,N+M)},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{a}_{h,q,k}^i$ represents the attention weight assigned by head $h$, for query $q$, to key $l_k$ in layer $i$.
The model handles two types of tokens: image tokens ($\mathcal{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$) and query tokens ($\mathcal{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{M \times D}$).
Next, we calculate the proportion of attention dedicated to image tokens for each layer $i$ as:
\begin{equation}
    AP_i^{\text{layer}} =\frac{\sum_{h}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{a}^i_{h, (N+M), k}}
    {\sum_{i, h}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \mathbf{a}^i_{h, (N+M), k}},
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the total number of attention heads, $N$ is the number of image tokens, and $M$ is the number of query tokens.
We sort the layers by this proportion and employ top-P sampling based on a predefined threshold value $\gamma$.
The selected layers are:
\begin{equation}
    \{\text{Selected Layers}\} = \text{top-P}(\{AP_i^{\text{layer}}\}_{i=1}^{L}, \gamma).
\label{eq:layer_selection}
\end{equation}
Here, $\text{top-P}$ selects layers until the cumulative proportion of image attention across these layers meets or exceeds $\gamma$.
These selected layers are used to analyze and adjust the attention at the token level and identify specific image tokens that the model may over-rely on, \ie, \textit{blind tokens}.

\input{figures/09}

\noindent\textbf{Blind token identification.}
After selecting relevant layers, we calculate the attention weights for each image token within these layers.
The attention proportion for image tokens, denoted as $AP^{\text{image}}$, is calculated by averaging the attention weights across the selected layers and attention heads:
\begin{equation}
    AP^{\text{image}} = \frac{\sum_{i \in \{\text{Selected Layers}\}} \sum_{h=1}^{H}
    \mathbf{a}^i_{h, (N+M), [1:N]}}{| \{\text{Selected Layers}\} | \times H }. 
\end{equation}
To identify tokens that disproportionately capture the model's attention, \ie, \textit{blind tokens}, we calculate the mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$) of the image attention weights.
Tokens with an attention proportion exceeding $\mu + \lambda\sigma$ (where $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter) are classified as blind tokens:
\begin{equation}
    \{ \text{Blind Token Indices} \} = \{ j | AP^{\text{image}}_j > \mu + \lambda\sigma \}.
\label{eq:blind_token_indentification}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Contrastive decoding.}
Our method seeks to reduce the influence of blind tokens, thereby decreasing the incidence of hallucinations in LVLMs.
Drawing inspiration from recent successes in contrastive decoding~\cite{leng2023mitigating,favero2024multi}, which effectively minimizes hallucinations by contrasting the differences between an image and its distorted counterpart, we adopt a similar scheme.
We construct a new set of visual tokens $\mathcal{V}^{\ast}$ by zeroing out non-blind tokens and only leaving blind tokens, which biases the input towards emphasizing blind tokens:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{V}^{\ast} = \bigcup_{j=1}^{N} \vmathbb{1}_{\{j \in \text{Blind Token Indices}\}}(j) \nu_j.
\end{equation}

Next, we compute the logits using both the original input ($\mathcal{V}$) and the biased input ($\mathcal{V}^{\ast}$):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \ell_t &= \log p(\xi_t | \mathcal{V}, \mathcal{Q}, \xi_{<t}; \theta), \\
    \ell_t^{\ast} &= \log p(\xi_t | \mathcal{V^{\ast}}, \mathcal{Q}, \xi_{<t}; \theta),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\ell_t$ and $\ell_t^{\ast}$ are the logits computed from the original and the biased inputs, respectively.
We adjust the logits by contrasting the original and biased outputs, and then sample the next token $\xi_t$ from the following softmax distribution: 
\begin{equation}
    \xi_t \sim \mathrm{Softmax}((1+\alpha)\ell_t - \alpha \ell_t^{\ast}).
\label{eq:contrastive_decoding}
\end{equation}
Here, $\alpha$ is a hyperparameter that moderates the contrastive effect.
This balances the distribution of attention across tokens thereby mitigating the likelihood of visual hallucinations in LVLMs.


\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}
In our experiments, we did not constrain the LVLMs to provide one-word answers in discriminative tasks, which often require simple `Yes' or `No' responses.
For instance, we avoid instructions such as "Please answer in one word." in the query text.
We see that imposing a one-word response constraint on LVLMs leads to notable changes in performance (see~\cref{sec:apependix_additional_experiments}).
For the experiments, we set $\text{P}=0.5$ in~\cref{eq:layer_selection}, $\lambda=1$~\cref{eq:blind_token_indentification}, $\alpha=3$ for InsturctBLIP~\cite{dai2024instructblip} and $\alpha=2.5$ for LLaVA-1.5~\cite{liu2023visual} in~\cref{eq:contrastive_decoding}.
}

We evaluated \Ours on two state-of-the-art LVLMs: \textbf{LLaVA-1.5}~\cite{liu2023visual} and \textbf{InstructBLIP}~\cite{dai2024instructblip}, both incorporating Vicuna 7B~\cite{chiang2023vicuna} as an LLM backbone.
LLaVA-1.5 synchronizes image and text modalities by applying linear projection layers, while InstructBLIP uses the Q-Former~\cite{li2023blip} to efficiently link visual and textual features using a fixed number of tokens (\eg, 32 tokens).
Notably, \Ours is model-agnostic and can integrate with various of LVLM architectures.

\textbf{(1) POPE}~\cite{li2023evaluating} views hallucination evaluation as a binary classification task (yes/no) with questions regarding object presence (\eg, "Is there a cat in the image?").
It includes 500 images from MS-COCO and evaluates them based on visible objects and imaginary ones across different object categories, using three setups (random, popular, and adversarial).
\textbf{(2) MME}~\cite{fu2024mme} evaluates 14 subtasks including object hallucination by answering binary questions about object existence, count, position, color, \etc.
\textbf{(3) AMBER}~\cite{wang2023llm} includes both generative and discriminative tasks, focusing on hallucinations related to object existence, attributes, and relationships, with performance evaluated using CHAIR for generative tasks and an F1 score for discriminative tasks.
The overall AMBER score is calculated as $((100 - \text{CHAIR}) + \text{F1}) / 2$.

\Ours aims to minimize hallucinations in LVLMs without the need for external models, costly self-feedback mechanisms, or further training.
We select baseline methods that fulfill these conditions.
We choose recent contrastive decoding methods as baselines, notably \textbf{VCD}~\cite{leng2023mitigating} and \textbf{M3ID}~\cite{favero2024multi}.
These methods are designed to reduce object hallucinations by enhancing the influence of the reference image over the language model's prior or statistical bias, by contrasting output distributions from both original and altered visual inputs.
We reimplemented VCD and M3ID within our evaluation framework.

\noindent\textbf{POPE.}
\Cref{tab:POPE} showcases the performance of different methods on the POPE benchmark~\cite{li2023evaluating} across MS-COCO~\cite{lin2014microsoft}, A-OKVQA~\cite{schwenk2022okvqa}, and GQA~\cite{hudson2019gqa} datasets, evaluated under Random, Popular, and Adversarial setups.
(\textbf{\Ours}) consistently outperforms the baseline (\textit{base}) and other decoding methods (VCD~\cite{leng2023mitigating}, M3ID~\cite{favero2024multi}) in most cases, achieving the highest Accuracy and F1 scores.
It also demonstrates balanced improvements in Precision and Recall, indicating a reduction in errors and better information capture.
For InstructBLIP, \Ours shows a significant performance boost, particularly in mitigating hallucinations related to object existence.
However, LLaVA 1.5 exhibits less pronounced improvements in Popular and Adversarial setups, highlighting its limitations in more challenging scenarios.
Yet, overall, \Ours proves to be robust and effective across different datasets and query setups.

\section{Conclusion}

\label{sec:conclusion}
Our study demonstrates that addressing the issue of \textit{blind tokens} in LVLMs significantly mitigates the problem of hallucinatory responses.
By introducing a novel technique, termed \Ours, which recalibrates attention distribution during the decoding phase, without the need for extensive retraining, external data or models, or costly
self-feedback mechanisms.
our approach effectively enhances the model's focus on underemphasized yet crucial tokens.
This recalibration leads to a more balanced consideration across all image tokens, significantly reducing reliance on misleading information and minimizing object hallucinations.
The efficacy of \Ours is validated across several benchmarks, where it consistently outperforms existing decoding techniques.
\Ours not only boosts reliability but also ensures more trustworthy applications of LVLMs in real-world scenarios requiring fine-grained visual understanding.
We hope our contributions inspire further research into refining and adapting LVLMs for increasingly complex multimodal tasks.
