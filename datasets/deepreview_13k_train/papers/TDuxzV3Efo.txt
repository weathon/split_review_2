\title{Continuous, Subject-Specific Attribute Control in T2I Models by Identifying Semantic Directions}

\begin{abstract}
In recent years, advances in text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models have substantially elevated the quality of their generated images. However, achieving fine-grained control over attributes remains a challenge due to the limitations of natural language prompts (such as no continuous set of intermediate descriptions existing between ``person'' and ``old person'').
Even though many methods were introduced that augment the model or generation process to enable such control, methods that do not require a fixed reference image are limited to either enabling global fine-grained attribute expression control or coarse attribute expression control localized to specific subjects, not both simultaneously.
We show that there exist directions in the commonly used token-level CLIP text embeddings that enable fine-grained subject-specific control of high-level attributes in text-to-image models. Based on this observation, we introduce one efficient optimization-free and one robust optimization-based method to identify these directions for specific attributes from contrastive text prompts. We demonstrate that these directions can be used to augment the prompt text input with fine-grained control over attributes of specific subjects in a compositional manner (control over multiple attributes of a single subject) without having to adapt the diffusion model.

Project page: \rurl{compvis.io/attribute-control}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




Text-to-image (T2I) models have recently seen considerable progress in their capabilities and the quality of their generated images.
However, a persistent challenge lies in achieving fine-grained control over the generated images, particularly concerning attribute expression. This can be attributed to the limitations of natural language, which does not allow for the very fine-grained description of attribute expression in a way that diffusion models reliably understand.
Unlike image editing use cases, where a base image is given, and masks can be provided to adapt a global method to only affect a target subject, we target the pure generation use case, where no reference image is given. This means that subject instances can only be identified via the prompt.
Even though many methods (see \cref{tab:features}) were introduced that augment the model or generation process to enable such control without per-image optimization (in contrast to Imagic \cite{kawar2023imagic}), they are limited to either enabling global fine-grained attribute control \cite{gandikota2023sliders,liu2022compositional,liu2021learning,diffusionclip,kwon2023diffusion} or coarse attribute control localized to specific subjects \cite{hertz2023prompttoprompt,yang2024dynamic}, not both simultaneously.

Starting from a simple prompt for a T2I diffusion model, our goal is to influence the generation process in a fine-grained manner, in both localization and magnitude. More specifically, in a prompt such as ``a photo of a woman standing next to a man in front of their car'', we want to influence attributes of each subject -- ``woman'', ``man'', and ``car'' -- separately and with fine-grained control over their individual attribute expression.
Typically, one would add these attributes to the subjects in the prompt (``\underline{old} person''), but this approach yields only very coarse control of these attributes. Additionally, when composing multiple of such attributes in the prompt, diffusion models often ignore a subset of them \cite{liu2022compositional,du2023reducereuserecycle}.

We show that there exist directions in the commonly used token-level CLIP \cite{clip} text embeddings that enable fine-grained subject-specific control of high-level attributes in T2I models, as opposed to inversion methods that learn instance information \cite{gal2022textual,mokady2023nulltextinversion}. Based on this observation, we introduce methods to identify these directions for specific attributes and show how they can be used to augment the prompt text input with fine-grained continuous control over attributes of specific subjects in a compositional (stackable) manner without adapting the diffusion model or incurring additional costs during generation.

We summarize our main contributions as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We show that token-level edit directions that allow fine-grained control of subject-specific attributes exist in common CLIP text embeddings and that diffusion models are capable of interpreting them
    \item We show that T2I diffusion models are capable of backpropagating \textit{high-level semantic} concepts to their text embedding input as adaptations of existing embeddings using just the reconstruction loss objective on a single image
    \item We introduce two approaches for identifying those directions for specific attributes or concepts from contrasting text prompts describing these concepts, one simple optimization-free method and one optimization-based one that identifies more robust directions
    \item We show that these token-level edit directions enable fine-grained, subject-specific, compositional control of attributes and concepts in common diffusion models
\end{itemize}



\section{Related Work}

This section provides an overview of work related to our method. We also compare our method with others in \cref{tab:features}.
\subsubsection{Influencing the Generation Process of Diffusion Models}
Since Diffusion Models do not provide the same ordered and interpretable latent space as Generative Adversarial Networks \cite{goodfellow2014generative, radford2016unsupervised, chen2016infogan} do, a considerable research interest has formed to provide Diffusion Models with greater ability to control visual detail.
A common approach for this is to directly use new or adapt existing neural features to guide the generation process towards semantic changes \cite{gandikota2023sliders, brack2024sega, hertz2023prompttoprompt, kawar2023imagic, yang2024dynamic}. This allows for fine-grained and even non-text-based instructions to appropriately affect the sample, like interpolating between specific concepts \cite{gandikota2023sliders, brack2024sega, kawar2023imagic, hertz2023prompttoprompt}, subject-specific control \cite{yang2024dynamic} or entirely erasing concepts \cite{li2024getwhatyouwant}. Our work encompasses the interpolating abilities as well as subject-specific control in complex samples, therefore combining two fundamental properties that have been mutually exclusive for generic methods so far.

\paragraph{Image Editing.} One special case of influencing the generation process of diffusion models is the case where a reference image is given. Here, the expectation typically is that
only a few aspects of the image should be altered.
Numerous prior works \cite{brooks2023instructpix2pix, hertz2023prompttoprompt, meng2021sdedit, zhang2023hive, bodur2023iedit} have shown difficulties properly disentangling multiple concepts in a sample to prevent global changes and enable a user to locally concentrate edits. To approach this problem, one can directly integrate specific changes into the image using the reverse diffusion process \cite{meng2021sdedit}, by inversion \cite{mokady2023nulltextinversion, parmar2023zero} or by marking regions using edit masks \cite{mao2023mag, simsar2023lime} to enforce localization.

\subsubsection{Instance Personalization}
Another related task is personalizing diffusion models to enable them to generate images that contain specific instances of subjects.
Approaches based on finetuning include DreamBooth \cite{ruiz2023dreambooth}, which leverages a prior preservation loss to adapt the visual backbone, while approaches like Textual Inversion \cite{gal2022textual} restrict the adaptation of the model to an added embedding vector to be optimized to represent a specific instance or concept. Furthermore Kumari \etal \cite{kumari2023multi} propose CustomDiffusion for efficient training of multiple concepts by finetuning only cross-attention layers.  Moreover, gaining enhanced control in scenarios where samples exhibit complex compositional structures remains a significant challenge for text-to-image models \cite{tunanyan2023multi}. A prominent strategy to achieve controllable image synthesis involves the use of energy-based models, as suggested by several previous works \cite{nie2021controllable, liu2021learning, liu2022compositional}. 
\subsubsection{Global Edit Directions in CLIP Space}
A wide range of previous works \cite{patashnik2021styleclip,baba2023robust,baykal2023clipguidedstyleganinversion,abdal2022clip2stylegan} investigated the use of the CLIP embedding space \cite{clip} to guide the image generation process of StyleGAN \cite{karras2019style}. These studies identify directions within CLIP's embedding space that correspond to global semantic changes and utilize these directions to steer the generation process.

\section{Method}


T2I Diffusion models \cite{ho2020denoising,rombach2022high} model a reverse diffusion process $p_\theta(\x_{0:T}|\cond)$ that enables sampling from the distribution of images $p_\theta(\x_{0}|\cond)$ given a text prompt condition $\cond$ and a Gaussian noise sample $\x_T$. They iteratively denoise $\x_T$ using a diffusion model $\xzeropredtheta(\x_t|\cond,t)$ which (implicitly) predicts the clean sample given a noised image. This conditioning $\cond$ is typically obtained using a CLIP \cite{clip} text encoder $\mathcal{E}_\mathrm{CLIP}$ as a tokenwise embedding $\emb = \mathcal{E}_\mathrm{CLIP}(\prompt)$.

\subsubsection{Exerting Control over the Generation Process in Diffusion Models}
We aim to influence the generated samples $\xzero \sim p_\theta(\xzero|\emb)$, more specifically the expression $\expr(\attributei)$ of specific attributes $\attributei \in \attributes$ of a specific subject $\subjectj \in \subjects$. This subject is described in the prompt at $\prompt_{[\subjectj]}$ (with the corresponding embedding $\emb_{[\subjectj]}$) and is located in the generated image in $\x_{0,[\subjectj]}$. The location of the subject in the generated image $\x_{0,[\subjectj]}$ is generally not known ahead of time, as it is dynamically determined during the generation process. Specifically, we aim for a change of the expression of $\attributei$ of subject $\subjectj$.

To control the expression of an attribute $\attributei$, the nature of this image generation approach enables a limited number of aspects to target for influencing the generation process.
To control the global expression $\expr_\mathrm{global}(\attributei)$, one option is to modify the diffusion model by re-defining the expression of attributes on a global level \cite{gandikota2023sliders,diffusionclip,kwon2023diffusion}.
Another possibility is to directly modify the starting noise latent $\x_T$ \cite{kwon2023diffusion} or the intermediate noise latents $\x_t$, either using dynamically predicted \cite{dhariwal2021diffusion,ho2021classifierfree,liu2022compositional,nie2021controllable,liu2021learning,du2023reducereuserecycle} or fixed \cite{brack2024sega} auxiliary directions.
Alternatively, one can also influence how the diffusion model interprets the prompt embedding $\emb$ \cite{hertz2023prompttoprompt}. This allows for instance specificity through the prompt but does not work well for continuous attribute expression control (see \cref{fig:single_attribute_reel}c). Finally, one can directly modify the prompt embedding $\emb$, influencing the text encoder $\mathcal{E}_\mathrm{CLIP}$, or modifying the original tokens. Methods like \cite{gal2022textual,mokady2023nulltextinversion,voynov2023pplus,han2023highly} previously investigated this approach for inserting instance appearance information, but not for fine-grained attribute control.

As it already directly enables instance-specific coarse attribute control via text, the tokenwise prompt embedding $\emb$ is a natural choice as the basis for a method for fine-grained attribute control, both in expression and instance-specificity. For this to be practical, multiple requirements have to be fulfilled:\\
i) The diffusion model has to be capable of interpreting modified prompt embeddings $\pembmod$ that do not directly correspond to a possible text prompt.\\
ii) Fine-grained changes of the token-wise prompt embedding have to be localized in what they affect (specifically relating to a subject $\subjectj$ in both prompt embedding $\emb$ and image $\xzero$). This localization has to be discoverable and interpretable.\\
iii) The tokenwise CLIP embedding space, as interpreted by the diffusion model, has to locally (around anchor points from real words) approximate a Euclidean manifold that behaves similarly across similar anchor points to enable composable smooth local edits along fixed category-specific directions $\pembdiff$.\\
iv) These directions $\pembdiff$ have to be practically discoverable.\\
We investigate requirements i-iii) in \cref{sec:learning_semantic_edit_deltas} and iv) in \cref{sec:clip_text_attribute_deltas} and \cref{sec:attribute_deltas}.

\subsection{Learning Semantic Edits from Text/Image Pairs}\label{sec:learning_semantic_edit_deltas}
Let us now investigate whether the previously mentioned conditions i-iii) are fulfilled, and semantic directions that can be applied on top of embeddings of actual captions exist in the tokenwise CLIP embedding space.

A wide range of previous works \cite{gal2022textual,mokady2023nulltextinversion,voynov2023pplus,han2023highly} found that the reconstruction loss of a pre-trained T2I diffusion model can be used to backpropagate instance appearance information to the prompt embedding.
These instance text embeddings can then enable various personalization and image editing use cases in the generation process.
This implies that the first condition -- the diffusion model can interpret points that do not exactly lie in the text embedding space that the CLIP model provides -- is given. Otherwise, simple gradient descent-based learning of very specific instance appearance information would not be plausible.

Although the objective is only based on pixel-wise reconstruction information, we find that this general approach can also learn semantic information directly. Using a single image/caption pair $(\xzero, \prompt)$, we apply a random amount of noise to the image and backpropagate the reconstruction loss
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss_semantic_image}
    \loss(\xzero, \emb + \pembdiff) = \mathbb{E}_{\eps \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\eye),t \sim \mathcal{U}(0, T]}\!\left[w(t)\left\|\xzero - \xzeropred(\alpha_t \xzero + \sigma_t \eps|\emb + \pembdiff,t)\right\|_2^2\right]
\end{equation}
through the diffusion model $\xzeropred(\cdot)$. We update a learnable delta $\pembdiff$ that is added to the prompt embedding $\emb$ to minimize the regularized\footnote{Implemented as weight decay through AdamW \cite{loshchilov2018decoupled} on $\pembdiff$.} reconstruction loss. One crucial detail is that the noise $\eps$ is randomly re-drawn at every step.

We find that this approach, indeed, leads to learned prompt embedding deltas that capture semantic differences between the set of generated images given just the prompt and the target image. \cref{fig:edit_delta_subject_specificity} shows that they substantially reduce the semantic gap between the image generated with the original prompt and the target image.
Additionally, linear interpolation between the original prompt embedding and the embedding with the delta applied shows a clear semantic progression from the originally generated image toward the target image. This indicates that the tokenwise CLIP embedding space, as interpreted by the diffusion model, is at least locally smooth in semantic meaning (condition iii). However, we also observe ``phase changes'' where the image changes substantially during a short subset of the interpolation trajectory for large changes, indicating that the embedding space is not globally smooth.

\subsubsection{Subject Specificity of Semantic Edit Deltas.}\label{sec:subject_specificity}
We now investigate the relationship between these learned tokenwise semantic edit deltas $\pembdiff$ and the initial prompt.
At a high (semantic) level, our training method initially yields an adaptation of the full prompt embedding that substantially closes the gap between the originally generated images and the target images.
We find (see \cref{fig:edit_delta_subject_specificity}) that only applying the subject-specific token edit $\pembdiff_{[\subjectj]}$ of the learned edit delta $\pembdiff$ suffices to obtain a mostly disentangled edit of $\subjectj$. This partial edit is semantically close to the full edit, affecting the rest of the image only minimally. Notably, we mask out the deltas only after training.
This implies that our training process associates semantic information to the specific token corresponding to each subject $\subjectj$ and that these token-specific deltas $\pembdiff_{[\subjectj]}$ are directly usable for influencing the subject's attribute expression $\expr_{\subjectj}(\attributes)$ on a semantic level. This provides a simple interpretable way to localize modifications (condition ii).

\subsubsection{Transferability of Semantic Edit Deltas.}\label{sec:transferability}
Finally, we investigate the transferability of the learned $\pembdiff_{[\subjectj]}$ to other prompts. Instead of applying the subject-specific $\pembdiff_{[\subjectj]}$ to  $\subjectj$ in the same prompt, we transfer deltas learned on one text/image pair to a new prompt. First, we test transferring a delta $\pembdiff_{[\subjectj]}$ learned on one subject $\subjectj$ (such as ``woman'') from one prompt to the same word in a new prompt, as shown in \cref{fig:edit_delta_subject_transferability}a. This results in a change of attribute expression $\expr_{\subjectj'}(\attributes)$ on the new subject $\subjectj'$ semantically similar to the change seen on the original prompt (see \cref{fig:edit_delta_subject_specificity}). These deltas can also be transferred to other subjects $\subject{k}$ of a similar category (such as from ``woman'' to ``man'', see \cref{fig:edit_delta_subject_transferability}b). There, they result in similar changes to attribute expression $\expr_{\subject{k}}(\attributes)$ again but keep the underlying subject change (such as the gender change in this example) from the base text token intact. This shows that our previously defined condition iii) is effectively fulfilled.

\subsection{Identifying Specific Attribute Deltas from Contrastive Prompts}\label{sec:clip_text_attribute_deltas}
\cref{sec:learning_semantic_edit_deltas} showed that subject-specific attribute modifications can be achieved by modifying the token(s) of the noun corresponding to the subject without requiring additional words. This implies that the CLIP text encoder likely already performs aggregation of semantic attributes into the corresponding subject, which is corroborated by \cite{rassin2023linguistic}. Otherwise, the diffusion model would likely not learn to interpret the tokenwise embeddings this way. This is also corroborated by Li \etal \cite{li2024getwhatyouwant}, who find that concepts will be present in multiple places in the tokenwise embedding instead of being localized to just its specific tokens. Despite adding attributes typically affecting a multitude of token embeddings, our previous findings indicate that just modifying the token $\emb_{[\subjectj]}$ already suffices to enable substantial semantic changes.

Motivated by this finding, we propose identifying \textit{semantic directions} in the tokenwise embedding space that affect \textit{specific attributes} $\attributei$ from contrastive prompts similar to \cite{gandikota2023sliders} (such as ``a young person'' vs. ``an old person'' for the ``age'' attribute, both prompts using the same noun for $\subjectj$).
We first obtain the tokenwise CLIP embeddings $\pembap$ and $\pemban$ for the positive and negative prompts, respectively. Then, we compute the difference between the token embedding of the subject in both embeddings
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clip_text_attribute_deltas}
    \pembdiff_{\attributei} = (\pembap)_{[\subjectj]} - (\pemban)_{[\subjectj]}.
\end{equation}
This directly yields a direction that corresponds to the target attribute $\attributei$. To obtain more robust estimates of this direction, we average it over a multitude of contrastive prompt pairs that describe the same concept.

\paragraph{Sampling.}
During sampling, we simply add these learned deltas $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$ to $\emb_{[\subject{k}]}$ of the target subject(s) $\subject{k}$ with the desired scale $\attributescalei$. We then pass this modified prompt embedding to the diffusion model to generate an image causing no additional computational cost over standard sampling.
This yields similar behavior to adding adjectives such as ``old'' or ``young'' to the subject in the prompt but additionally enables control on a smooth scale while retaining subject-specificity.

Two examples of these learned directions are shown in \cref{fig:naive_clip}. They illustrate that this approach works to identify directions that affect attributes such as a vehicle's price but also often exhibits unrelated correlations such as car orientation or bike size.
As the CLIP text encoder is causal, this approach is also limited to attributes that can be described as a prefix to the target subject noun.

\subsection{Learning Robust Fine-Grained Attribute Deltas}\label{sec:attribute_deltas}
Inspired by our findings from \cref{sec:learning_semantic_edit_deltas}, we introduce a method for targeted, fine-grained, subject-specific control of attributes in T2I generation. We previously found that editing token embeddings of a specific subject $\subjectj$ directly modulates its semantic attribute expression $\expr_{\subjectj}(\attributes)$ and that these edits can be transferable.
Thus, we propose to leverage this approach to introduce fine-grained control into T2I models without having to modify them, that is, learning edit directions $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$ to the embedded prompt $\emb$ that directly correspond to subject-specific fine-grained modulations of the expression $\expr_{\subjectj}(\attributei)$ of a specific attribute $\attributei$.

In general, to limit the $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$ to apply to a specific subject $\subjectj$, we only modify the part of $\emb$ that corresponds to $\subjectj$:
\begin{equation}
    \pembmod(\emb,\attributescalei\pembdiff_{\attributei})_{[\subjectj]} = \emb_{[\subjectj]} + \attributescalei\pembdiff_{\attributei}.
\end{equation}
This modified embedding is then passed to the diffusion model in place of $\emb$.

\subsubsection{Training}
During training, we utilize the diffusion model's world knowledge, specifically about which changes in the generated images $\xzero$ correspond to modulations of specific high-level attributes $\attributei$.
We continue using contrastive text prompts that describe the target attribute $\attributei$ to elicit a fine-grained direction in the model's noise prediction space that corresponds to that attribute. We then backpropagate this direction through the diffusion model, distilling it into our delta $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$. Like in \cite{gandikota2023sliders}, this approach learns robust deltas $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$ from just a set of contrastive prompts and does not require training images.
For each optimization step, we start by generating a random image $\sampleanchor$ from the target category using a random prompt (such as ``a photo of a person'') with standard sampling settings. This image will serve as the anchor for the optimization process. Starting from a noised version of this image $\sampleanchornoised$ at a random diffusion timestep $t$, we then generate three predictions with the diffusion model: one prediction $\predanchor$ with the original anchor prompt and two predictions $\predpos, \predneg$ with modified prompts. For those, added adjectives either increase (such as ``a photo of an \underline{old} person'') or decrease (such as ``a photo of a \underline{young} person'') the expression of the target attribute $\expr(\attributei)$ (such as age).

As is well-known, classifier-free guidance \cite{ho2021classifierfree} can effectively merge multiple such predictions into a new one, allowing for continuous application of different predictions. Starting from $\predanchor$, we can increase or decrease $\expr(\attributei)$ with
\begin{equation}
    \predtarget(\attributescalei) = \predanchor + \attributescalei \cdot \left(\predpos - \predneg\right)
\end{equation}
Here, the guidance scale $\attributescalei$ controls both the direction (sign) and magnitude (value) of the attribute expression change.
We can then use these targets to train our semantic edit delta $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$. Randomly sampling $\attributescalei$ during training allows us to reliably continuously modulate $\attributei$ later on. When minimizing the difference between the diffusion model's prediction and the target prediction, we then also adjust $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$ by $\attributescalei$, leading to our delta training loss formulation:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss_delta}
    \lossdirection = \mathbb{E}_{\attributescalei}\!\left[w(t)\left\|\predtarget(\attributescalei) - \xzeropred(\sampleanchornoised|\pembmod(\emb,\attributescalei\pembdiff_{\attributei}),t)\right\|_2^2\right].
\end{equation}
With a set of suitable prompts to describe increasing and decreasing expressions of a target attribute $\attributei$, this objective learns robust attribute deltas $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$. 

During sampling, we use the same methodology for applying them as in \cref{sec:clip_text_attribute_deltas}.
Compared to the results obtained using the CLIP text embedding differences (see \cref{fig:naive_clip}), these results (see \cref{fig:learned}) also successfully capture the target attribute but exhibit fewer artifacts, such as the car's orientation flipping and its age changing (see \cref{sec:exp_learned_vs_naive}).

\end{figure}
Generally, image generation models learn correlations between different parts of the image.
As we do not modify the diffusion model itself, directly applying our learned edit deltas $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$ to a specific subject $\subjectj$ causes the desired change in attribute expression $\expr_{\subjectj}(\attributes)$, but additionally captures the entanglement between this subject's attributes and the attributes of the rest of the generated image based on the diffusion model's world knowledge. This means that this attribute change does not only apply to the part of the image $\x_{0,[\subjectj]}$ corresponding to $\subjectj$ but also the rest of the image (see \cref{fig:attribute_entanglement}a). This helps to enable the generation of plausible images, as the entanglement is based on real-world dependencies. Modeling these correlations, however, might not always be desirable.

If the modeling of dependencies between the subject and its surroundings is not desired, we can augment our sampling method (without having to re-learn $\pembdiff_{\attributei}$) during inference time.
Following \cite{gandikota2023sliders, meng2021sdedit}, simply not applying the edit delta for the first few steps of the diffusion generation process already substantially reduces the expression of these correlations at no additional computational cost. This especially helps to retain the original global image structure, as it is determined in the first steps of the generation process \cite{hertz2023prompttoprompt}. \cref{fig:attribute_entanglement}b shows an example of this.
Since our model only alters the text embedding, we can also directly pair it with Prompt-to-Prompt \cite{hertz2023prompttoprompt} to further improve appearance and structure disentanglement regarding the edit at the cost of doubling the inference cost. \cref{fig:attribute_entanglement}c shows the corresponding example.

\subsection{Learned Edit Deltas vs. CLIP Embedding Differences}\label{sec:exp_learned_vs_naive}
First, we compare the attribute edit directions based on simple CLIP embedding differences (\cref{sec:clip_text_attribute_deltas}) with the learning-based ones (\cref{sec:attribute_deltas}). For this comparison, we limit ourselves to a subset of edit deltas that can easily be described by prefixes in front of the subject (``young person'', not ``person wearing a colorful outfit''). Here, the causality of the CLIP text encoder inherently limits the method based on embedding differences. Using only the applicable subset avoids this drawback and ensures a fair comparison of the methods.
Qualitatively, the directions obtained by taking differences of the CLIP token embeddings exhibit more unwanted side effects. Comparing \cref{fig:naive_clip} and \cref{fig:learned}, for example, the car flips its orientation in both directions in the difference-based setup.
To quantitatively evaluate the occurrence of undesired side effects, we assess the attained expression $\expr(\attributei)$ of the target attribute $\attributei$ against the change in the image $\x$.
Following prior art \cite{gandikota2023sliders,mokady2023nulltextinversion}, we evaluate attribute expression change $\Delta\expr(\attributei)$ using the change of CLIP similarity of the generated image $I$ to a target prompt. As our attribute edit deltas are bi-directional, we compare to the prompt $\promptp$ that describes the positive direction (such as ``old person'') and the prompt $\promptn$ for the negative direction (``young person'').
We compute this bi-directional relative CLIP score as

\section{Conclusion}

This work uncovers the powerful capabilities of the tokenwise CLIP \cite{clip} text embedding for exerting control over the image generation process in T2I diffusion models. Instead of just acting as a discrete space of embeddings of words, we find that diffusion models are capable of interpreting local deviations in the tokenwise CLIP text embedding space in semantically meaningful ways. We use this insight to augment the typically rather coarse prompt with fine-grained, continuous control over the attribute expression of specific subjects by identifying semantic directions that correspond to specific attributes. %
Since we only modify the tokenwise CLIP text embedding along pre-identified directions, we enable more fine-grained manipulation at no additional cost in the generation process.
\subsubsection{Limitations \& Future Work}
This work is a %
step towards revealing the hidden capabilities of the text embedding input to common large-scale diffusion models and making them usable in straightforward ways. While our approach works for different off-the-shelf models without modifying them, it is also inherently limited by their capabilities. Specifically, our method inherits the limitation that diffusion models sometimes mix up attributes between different subjects. %
Complementary methods \cite{chefer2023attendexcite,rassin2023linguistic} reduce these problems substantially, and future work could investigate their combination with our method in depth.


\subsubsection{Impact Statement}
This work aims to improve the capabilities of text-to-image (T2I) diffusion models by introducing an efficient, simple-to-use method of influencing the expression of attributes of specific subjects in the generated images in a fine-grained manner. While, in general, similar targeted control has been possible before by utilizing editing-based methods on the original generated images, where generated images are inverted and re-generated with changes localized with custom edit masks, this method's simplicity and efficiency potentially enables such a level of control for a wider audience. This, like other works in the space of improving the control over image synthesis models, carries the risk of further enabling the generation of harmful or deceptive content.
