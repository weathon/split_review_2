\title{Can LLM-Generated Misinformation Be Detected?}

\begin{abstract}

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has made a transformative impact. However, the potential that LLMs such as ChatGPT can be exploited to generate misinformation has posed a serious concern to online safety and public trust. A fundamental research question is: \textit{will LLM-generated misinformation cause more harm than human-written misinformation?} We propose to tackle this question from the perspective of \textit{\textbf{detection difficulty}}. We first build a taxonomy of LLM-generated misinformation. Then we categorize and validate the potential real-world methods for generating misinformation with LLMs. Then, through extensive empirical investigation, we discover that LLM-generated misinformation \textit{\textbf{can be harder}} to detect for \textit{humans} and \textit{detectors} compared to human-written misinformation with the same semantics, which suggests it can have more deceptive styles and potentially cause more harm. We also discuss the implications of our discovery on combating misinformation in the age of LLMs and the countermeasures.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}






\begin{wrapfigure}[11]{r}{0.45\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/f1_v5.pdf}
    \caption{The  comparison of detecting human-written  and LLM-generated misinformation.}
    \label{fig:intro_figure}
\end{wrapfigure}

Large Language Models (LLMs) have represented a significant advancement of artificial intelligence~\citep{zhao2023survey}. Notably, ChatGPT
as an exemplary LLM has demonstrated its powerful capabilities in various tasks such as machine translation~\citep{lai2023chatgpt}, logical reasoning~\citep{liu2023evaluating}, summarization~\citep{zhang2023extractive}, and complex question answering~\citep{tan2023evaluation}.


However, as LLMs such as ChatGPT can generate human-like content, a serious threat to online safety and public trust is that LLMs can be potentially utilized to generate misinformation. Thus, an emerging fundamental research question is as follows:
\begin{center}

\begin{tcolorbox}[width=\linewidth, boxrule=0pt, colback=gray!20, colframe=gray!20]
Will LLM-generated misinformation cause more harm than human-written misinformation?
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{center}
\noindent
Admittedly, the harm of LLM-generated misinformation is a multifaceted and multidisciplinary problem. In this paper, we propose to approach this question from a \textbf{\textit{computational}} perspective. Specifically, we aim to investigate the \textbf{\textit{detection hardness}} of LLM-generated misinformation compared with human-written misinformation. The task of misinformation detection is to determine the authenticity of a given piece of text as ``factual'' or ``nonfactual''. If LLM-generated misinformation is shown to be \textbf{\textit{harder to detect by humans and detectors}} than human-written misinformation with the same semantics,
we can obtain empirical evidence to demonstrate that LLM-generated misinformation can have \textbf{\textit{more deceptive styles}} and potentially cause more harm in the real world.

To this end, our goal can be decomposed into three specific research questions. The \textit{first}  is: how can  LLMs be utilized to generate misinformation? The typical pipelines of detecting human-written and LLM-generated misinformation are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro_figure}. 
Generally, the LLM-generated misinformation can be \textit{unintentional} or \textit{intentional}. 
We regard hallucinations in the generated results from normal users as the unintentional scenario, and malicious users knowingly prompting LLMs to generate misinformation as the intentional scenario.
We first build a taxonomy of LLM-generated misinformation and systematically categorize the potential real-world misinformation generation methods with LLMs. 
Then, after empirical validation, our first core finding is: \textbf{LLMs can be instructed to generate misinformation in different types, domains, and errors}.

Then, the \textit{second} question is: can humans detect LLM-generated misinformation? 
We leverage the same group of human evaluators to assess the detection difficulty of LLM-generated and human-written misinformation data.
Similarly, the \textit{third} question is: can detectors detect LLM-generated misinformation? 
We evaluate the  detection difficulty of  LLM-generated and human-written misinformation data in the zero-shot setting to better reflect the real-world scenarios in the age of LLMs (Details in Section~\ref{Evaluation Setting}).
As for the second and third questions, through extensive investigation embracing different LLM misinformation generators (ChatGPT, Llama2-7b (or 13b,  70b), Vicuna-7b (or 13b, 33b)) and generation strategies (Paraphrase Generation, Rewriting Generation, and Open-ended Generation), 
our finding is: \textbf{LLM-generated misinformation can be harder to detect for both \textit{humans} and \textit{detectors} than human-written misinformation with the same semantics}. The straight implication is that LLM-generated misinformation can have \textit{\textbf{more deceptive styles}} and potentially \textit{\textbf{cause more harm}}  from a computational perspective.
Overall, the contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item We build a \textit{taxonomy}  by  types, domains, sources, intents and errors to systematically characterize LLM-generated misinformation as an emerging and critical research topic.
    \item We make the first attempt  to categorize and validate the \textit{potential real-world methods} for generating misinformation with LLMs including Hallucination Generation, Arbitrary Misinformation Generation and  Controllable Misinformation Generation methods.
    \item We \textit{discover} that misinformation generated by LLMs  \textit{can be harder} for \textit{humans} and \textit{detectors} to detect than human-written misinformation with the same semantic information through extensive investigation, which provides sufficient empirical evidence to demonstrate that LLM-generated misinformation \textit{can  have more deceptive styles} and potentially cause more harm.
    \item We  discuss the \textit{emerging challenges} for misinformation  detectors (Section~\ref{Emerging Challenges for Conventional Detectors}), \textit{important implications} of our discovery on combating misinformation in the age of LLMs (Section~\ref{Implications on Combating Misinformation at the Age of LLMs}),  the \textit{countermeasures} against LLM-generated misinformation through  LLMs' whole lifecycle (Section~\ref{Discussions on Countermeasures}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Taxonomy of LLM-Generated Misinformation}

\label{section:Taxonomy}
We propose to taxonomize LLM-generated misinformation from five dimensions (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Taxonomy}):

\textbf{Types:}
Following the previous works~\citep{chen2022combating,zhou2020survey,DBLP:journals/csur/ZubiagaABLP18,DBLP:journals/sigkdd/ShuSWTL17}, the types of LLM-generated misinformation can  be fake news, rumors, conspiracy theories, clickbait, misleading claims and cherry-picking. Examples are shown in 
  The domains can also be science, finance, law, education, social media and environment.

\textbf{Sources:} We propose to categorize the sources of LLM-generated misinformation into hallucination, arbitrary generation and controllable generation. More details are shown in Table~\ref{tab:tab_mis_illustration} and Section~\ref{section:ChatGPT_generation}.

\textbf{Intents:} Since hallucination can potentially occur in any generation process of LLMs~\citep{zhang2023sirens}, it is worth noting that users without malicious intent may also generate hallucinated texts. Thus, we can divide the intents  into unintentional generation and intentional generation.

\textbf{Errors:} The examples in Table~\ref{tab:Information_Manipulation} show that the errors of LLM-generated misinformation can include Unsubstantiated Content and Total Fabrication. LLMs can also  follow humans' instructions to generate other errors such as Outdated Information, Description Ambiguity, Incomplete Fact, and False Context, which are discussed in~\citep{10.1145/3534678.3542615,wu2019misinformation,kumar2018false}.



\section{RQ1: How Can LLMs be Utilized to Generate Misinformation?}

\label{section:ChatGPT_generation}
\paragraph{Misinformation Generation Approaches}  
We propose to categorize the LLM-based misinformation generation methods  into three types based on \textit{real-world scenarios} (Table~\ref{tab:tab_mis_illustration}): \textbf{\textit{{Hallucination Generation} (HG)}}: We define hallucination as the nonfactual content generated by LLMs due to the intrinsic properties of auto-regressive generation and lack of up-to-date information~\citep{zhang2023sirens}, which indicates that normal users could unintentionally generate hallucinated texts, especially in applications where timely information is essential.
For example, when users use the prompt such as ``\texttt{write a piece of news}'', LLMs probably will generate texts containing hallucinated information, in particular, the fine-grained information including dates, names, addresses, numbers and quotes;
\textbf{\textit{{Arbitrary Misinformation Generation} (AMG)}} means that malicious users can intentionally prompt LLMs to generate arbitrary misinformation. Specifically, we divide this generation method into Totally Arbitrary Generation (no specific constraints are required) and Partially Arbitrary Generation (constraints such as domains and types are included in the prompts); 
\textbf{\textit{{Controllable Misinformation Generation} (CMG)}}:  Since the misinformation generated with approaches including Paraphrase
Generation, Rewriting
Generation and Open-ended
Generation can generally preserve the semantic information of the given \colorlightblue{<passage>} or \colorlightblue{<sentence>}, the malicious users may adopt these methods to conceal the authorship of original misinformation, or make the existing \colorlightblue{<passage>} more deceptive and undetectable, or expand the misleading \colorlightblue{<sentence>} into a piece of complete misinformation. Information Manipulation method may be exploited by malicious users to manipulate the original \textit{factual} information into \textit{misleading} information in different errors such as Unsubstantiated Content.

\paragraph{Connection with Jailbreak Attack} Jailbreak attacks usually refer to the attempts to bypass the safety guards of LLMs (\eg, ChatGPT)
to generate harmful content. On the one hand, our proposed approaches to generate misinformation with LLMs are \textit{motivated by real-world scenarios} shown in Table~\ref{tab:tab_mis_illustration} and  \textit{orthogonal} to the previous Jailbreak techniques~\citep{wei2023jailbroken,zou2023universal}, which suggests the misinformation generation approaches and previous jailbreak methods could be potentially combined by  attackers. On the other hand, the HG methods could be regarded as \textit{\textbf{Unintentional Jailbreak}}, which is different from most previous jailbreak methods. The AMG and CMG methods could be regarded as  \textit{\textbf{Intentional Jailbreak}}. 
We test whether or not the generation methods can bypass ChatGPT's safeguard by prompting with each method for 100 times. The Attacking Success Rates (ASR), representing the percentage of attempts not rejected, are shown in Table~\ref{tab:jailbreak}. 
We can observe that the AMG methods are highly likely to be rejected with responses such as  ``\texttt{As an AI model, I cannot provide misinformation.}'' However, ChatGPT almost cannot defend against  HG  and most of  CMG methods even though it has strong safetyguard. This may be because these methods do not  explicitly have unsafe terms such as ``misinformation'' in  prompts. Surprisingly,  Information Manipulation  has a  high ASR though it has ``misinformation'' in prompts, which calls for more future research.  Thus, our first core finding is:
\begin{center}
\begin{tcolorbox}[width=0.67\linewidth, boxrule=0pt, colback=gray!20, colframe=gray!20]
\textbf{Finding 1:}
LLMs can \textit{follow users' instructions} to generate misinformation in \textit{different types, domains, and errors}.
\end{tcolorbox}

\end{center}

\paragraph{Style Analysis} Based on the semantic analysis, we can infer that the LLM-generated misinformation via approaches including Paraphrase Generation, Rewriting Generation and Open-ended Generation generally has the same semantic information as the original human-written misinformation. We hypothesize these methods could potentially \textbf{manipulate the \textit{style} information} to make the generated misinformation \textit{\textbf{more deceptive}} than human-written misinformation while \textbf{preserving the same  \textit{semantic} information}. To preliminarily validate this, we can first take  Rewriting Generation method as an example. In addition, we  utilize Word Cloud to analyze the frequent words of the  misinformation generated via these  three methods and human-written misinformation. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:word cloud}, we can see that the misinformation generated with these  three methods has different rankings of frequent words compared  with human-written misinformation, which  reflects they are likely to have different styles since they generally share the same semantics~\citep{10.1145/3132039,8981504}. Then, we further validate the hypothesis through the extensive investigation with humans (Section~\ref{sec:RQ2}) and  detectors (Section~\ref{RQ3}) as the evaluators for detection difficulty.



\section{RQ2: Can Humans  Detect LLM-Generated Misinformation?}

\label{sec:RQ2}

Although previous works have shown that it is hard for humans to detect human-written misinformation~\citep{lyons2021overconfidence}, it is still under-explored whether or not humans can detect LLM-generated misinformation. In this section, with ChatGPT as the representative LLM, we conduct  human evaluation to assess the human ability to spot LLM-generated misinformation and compare it with the  ability to spot human-written misinformation, indicating whether or not LLM-generated misinformation can be harder for humans to detect  compared with human-written misinformation.



\paragraph{Human Evaluation Setup}
The goal of the human evaluation is to  compare the factuality annotation performance, representing the humans' detection hardness, on human-written and LLM-generated misinformation from \textit{the same} group of human evaluators.
We first recruited  $10$ human evaluators from crowd-sourcing platform Amazon MTurk. The annotation experience is not required for evaluators to reflect the perceptions from the general public.
We ask evaluators to select a label of ``factual'' or ``nonfactual'' for each news item from the randomly shuffled dataset only based on their own perceptions upon reading it.  
Each evaluator is required to judge the credibility of all  $100$  news items  generated from Hallucinated News Generation and Totally Arbitrary Generation, randomly sampled  $100$  news  items generated from Partially Arbitrary Generation and Information Manipulation, randomly sampled 100 pieces of human-written nonfactual news  from Politifact~\citep{shu2020fakenewsnet}.
Since the other generated news data are based on the same nonfactual information of Politifact, to avoid the semantic overlap between different news items, we randomly sample  $50$ news items from the data generated via Paraphrase Generation, Rewriting Generation, and Open-ended Generation. 



\paragraph{Results and Analysis}
\label{Results and Analysis}
Since we aim to assess and compare the humans' detection hardness of human-written misinformation and LLM-generated misinformation, measured by same group of human evaluators' factuality annotation performance respectively, we can adopt \texttt{Success Rate}$\%$ as the evaluation metric, which is calculated by the percentage of successfully identified misleading news items in human-written or LLM-generated misinformation dataset. 

First, with ChatGPT as the representative LLM, we can observe in Table~\ref{tab:human} that \textbf{it is generally hard for humans to detect LLM-generated misinformation}, especially those generated with  Hallucinated News Generation, Totally Arbitrary Generation, Rewriting Generation, and Open-ended Generation methods. For example, we find that humans can only successfully spot $9.6\%$ of all the generated hallucinated news on average, which reflects that it is extremely difficult for normal people to notice the fine-grained hallucinated information such as false dates, names, addresses, numbers and quotes.

Second, we attempt to compare  humans' detection hardness for LLM-generated misinformation and human-written misinformation that have \textit{the same semantics}, because the \textit{semantic} information is the other factor impacting the detection difficulty apart from the \textit{style} information. We have demonstrated that Paraphrase Generation, Rewriting Generation, and Open-ended Generation methods generally only change the \textit{style} information and preserve the original \textit{semantics} in Section~\ref{ChatGPT_Generated_Misinformation_Dataset}. Comparing human detection performance on human-written misinformation (the \colorlightgray{numbers} in Table~\ref{tab:human}) and LLM-generated misinformation via Paraphrase Generation, Rewriting Generation and Open-ended Generation approaches (the \colorlightred{numbers} or \colorlightgreen{numbers} in Table~\ref{tab:human}), we can discover that the human detection performances on LLM-generated misinformation are mostly lower than those on human-written misinformation. 
In particular, the statistical significance is strong for Rewriting Generation (p-value = $9.15\times10^{-5}$) and Open-ended Generation (p-value = $1.
Thus, we can have our second core finding shown as follows:
\begin{center}
\begin{tcolorbox}[width=0.78\linewidth, boxrule=0pt, colback=gray!20, colframe=gray!20]
\textbf{Finding 2:}
LLM-generated misinformation \textit{can be harder} for \textit{humans} to detect than human-written  misinformation with the same semantics.
\end{tcolorbox}

\end{center}

\noindent
Our finding validates the hypothesis that  LLMs \textbf{can be exploited to generate misinformation with more \textit{more deceptive} styles} for humans via carefully-designed prompting strategies, indicating that its factuality is harder to determine for normal people.
Also, our finding implies humans \textbf{can be potentially more susceptible} to LLM-generated misinformation than human-written misinformation.

\section{RQ3: Can Detectors   Detect  LLM-Generated Misinformation?}

\label{RQ3}

Misinformation detection is critical for guarding online safety and public trust~\citep{chen2022combating,DBLP:journals/sigkdd/ShuSWTL17}. However, in the age of LLMs, it is under exploration whether or not existing detectors can detect LLM-generated misinformation, which is key to defending  against its potential  pollution. 
\paragraph{Emerging Challenges for Misinformation Detectors}
\label{Emerging Challenges for Conventional Detectors}

In the real world, detecting LLM-generated misinformation is in face with emerging challenges. \textit{First}, it is difficult to obtain factuality supervision labels to train detectors for LLM-generated misinformation since it is harder for humans to detect than human-written misinformation (Section~\ref{Results and Analysis}). \textit{Second}, malicious users can easily utilize methods shown in Table~\ref{tab:tab_mis_illustration} and close-sourced LLMs (\eg, ChatGPT) or open-source LLMs (\eg, Llama2~\citep{touvron2023llama} or Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023}) to generate misinformation at scale in different domains, types, and errors, which is hard for conventional supervisedly trained detectors to maintain effective. Thus, \textbf{it is likely to be impractical} to apply conventional supervisedly trained detectors (\eg, BERT) to detect LLM-generated misinformation in the practices.


\paragraph{Evaluation Setting}
\label{Evaluation Setting}
We adopt LLMs such as GPT-4 with zero-shot prompting strategies as the \textbf{representative misinformation detectors} to assess and compare the detection hardness of LLM-generated misinformation and human-written misinformation for two reasons.
\textit{First}, zero-shot setting can better reflect the real-world scenarios of detecting LLM-generated misinformation considering the likely impracticality of conventional supervisedly trained detectors (\eg, BERT) in practices. 
\textit{Second}, there are many works that have demonstrated directly prompting LLMs such as GPT-4 in a zero-shot way can \textit{\textbf{outperform}} conventional supervisedly trained models such as BERT on detecting human-written misinformation~\citep{pelrine2023towards,zhang2023interpretable,bang2023multitask,buchholz2023assessing,li2023preliminary}, which shows that zero-shot LLMs have already achieved almost state-of-the-art performance in the task of misinformation detection.
In the zero-shot setting, we can adopt \texttt{Success Rate} $\%$ as the metric to measure the probability of LLM-generated or human-written misinformation  being successfully identified,  representing the difficulty of being detected.

\paragraph{LLM Detection Performance vs. Human Detection Performance}
\label{LLM Detection Performance vs. Human Detection Performance}
As for LLM-generated misinformation via Hallucinated News Generation, Totally Arbitrary Generation and Open-ended Generation, we run ChatGPT-3.5 (\texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo}) or GPT-4~\footnote{\texttt{gpt-4}: \url{https://platform.openai. As for Partially Arbitrary Generation, we first test on two types of generated data  \texttt{healthcare fake news} and \texttt{political rumors} and then  average the detection performance. As for Information Manipulation, we also report the average  performance over all the six errors  in  Figure~\ref{fig:Taxonomy}. The generated misinformation by aforementioned CMG methods is also based on  Politifact dataset, which is consistently with human evaluation. The prompt using ChatGPT-3. Human detection performance is referred from Table~\ref{tab:human}.

First, with ChatGPT as the representative LLM, we can observe that \textbf{it is also generally hard} for detectors to detect LLM-generated misinformation across different generation approaches, especially those generated via Hallucinated News Generation, Totally Arbitrary Generation and Open-ended Generation. For example, ChatGPT-3.5 (or GPT-4)  can only detect $0.0\%$ (or $10.0\%$) of the generated hallucinated news, which shows LLM detectors can hardly detect fine-grained hallucinations. 
Second, previous works have shown that detectors can perform better than humans on detecting human-written misinformation~\citep{perez-rosas-etal-2018-automatic}. Comparing the detection performances of LLM detectors and humans, we can discover that \textbf{GPT-4 can outperform humans on detecting LLM-generated misinformation}, though humans can still perform better than  ChatGPT-3.5.

After evaluating the overall performance of LLM detectors,
we aim to further investigate whether or not LLM-generated misinformation can be \textit{harder} for detectors to detect than human-written misinformation with the same semantics. 
Thus, we conduct experiments to compare the  detection performances on human-written misinformation and  misinformation generated via  Paraphrase Generation, Rewriting Generation and Open-ended Generation, which can preserve the original \textit{semantics} (shown in Section~\ref{ChatGPT_Generated_Misinformation_Dataset}). 

We adopt both ChatGPT and 6 types of open-source LLMs (Llama2-7b (or 13b, 70b) and Vicuna-7b (or 13b, 33b)) as the misinformation generators. The generated misinformation  is compared with real-world human-written misinformation datasets including Politifact, Gossipcop~\citep{shu2020fakenewsnet} and CoAID~\citep{cui2020coaid}. 
Eight representative LLM detectors (ChatGPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B, and ``No CoT'' and ``CoT'' strategies for each LLM) are adopted to assess the detection difficulty of LLM-generated and human-written misinformation.
As for the ``No CoT''  strategy, we use the same prompt as 
the experiments in Figure~\ref{ChatGPT_for_detecting}.
As for the ``CoT''  strategy, we follow the Zero-shot Chain-of-Thought Prompting method~\citep{kojima2022large}. 

 
For example, compared with detecting human-written misinformation in Politifact, Llama2-7B with ``CoT'' strategy has a  performance drop by  $19.6\%$  on detecting misinformation that is generated by ChatGPT via Rewriting Generation. Thus, we can have our third core finding:
\begin{center}

\begin{tcolorbox}[width=0.88\linewidth, boxrule=0pt, colback=gray!20, colframe=gray!20]
\textbf{Finding 3:}
LLM-generated misinformation \textit{can be harder} for \textit{misinformation detectors} to detect than human-written misinformation with the same semantics.
\end{tcolorbox}


\end{center}
\noindent
Our finding implies that LLM-generated misinformation can have \textbf{\textit{more deceptive styles} for detectors} and existing detectors are likely to be \textbf{less effective} in detecting LLM-generated misinformation. Also, malicious users could potentially utilize LLMs  to escape the detection of detectors. 


\section{Implications on Combating Misinformation in the Age of LLMs}

\label{Implications on Combating Misinformation at the Age of LLMs}
Through empirical investigation, we discover that LLMs (\eg, ChatGPT) can be leveraged to generate misinformation in an unintentional or intentional way, and LLM-generated misinformation can be harder for humans and detectors to detect compared to human-written misinformation with the same semantics. Our findings have multiple implications on combating misinformation in the age of LLMs. \textit{First}, our findings directly suggest 
that \textbf{LLM-generated misinformation \textit{can have more deceptive styles}}, which could be attributed to the intrinsic properties of LLM-generated content (e.g., the linguistic features) or the carefully-designed prompts (e.g., instructions such as ``the style should be
serious and calm'').
\textit{Second}, a large amount of hallucinated information is potentially generated by normal users due to the popularity of LLMs. Also, malicious users could be more inclined to exploit LLMs to generate misinformation to escape the detection of detectors. Thus, \textbf{there is a potential major \textit{paradigm shift} of misinformation production from humans to LLMs}. \textit{Third}, considering malicious users can easily prompt LLMs to generate misinformation at scale, which is more deceptive than human-written misinformation, online safety and public trust are faced with serious threats. \textbf{We call for \textit{collective efforts} to combat LLM-generated misinformation from stakeholders} in different backgrounds including researchers, government, platforms, and the general public.

\section{Countermeasures Through  LLMs' Lifecycle}

\label{Discussions on Countermeasures}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:Countermeasures}, we propose to divide the lifecycle of LLMs into three stages and  discuss the countermeasures against LLM-generated misinformation through the whole lifecycle. In the \textit{training} stage, we can curate the training data to remove  nonfactual articles and ground the training process to existing knowledge bases~\citep{yu2020survey} to reduce LLMs' hallucinations. Alignment training processes such as RLHF~\citep{casper2023open} can reduce the risk of generating harmful content. In the \textit{Inference} stage, we can utilize prompt filtering, intent modeling or jailbreak defenses~\citep{jain2023baseline} to prevent AMG methods (\eg, Totally Arbitrary Generation), and confidence (or uncertainty) estimation~\citep{xiong2023llms} or retrieval augmentation~\citep{mialon2023augmented} to defend against HG methods (\eg, Hallucinated News Generation). However, they may be ineffective for most of  CMG methods (\eg, Rewriting Generation), which are based on human-written misleading content and do not explicitly express the intent of generating misinformation. More research is desired to develop inference-time factuality verification methods for combating CMG methods. In the \textit{influence} stage when  LLM-generated content starts to influence the general public, it is under-explored how to design effective detectors for LLM-generated misinformation or texts. Also, it is essential to enhance the public's awareness of the risks of LLM-generated misinformation.


\section{Conclusion}

\label{Conclusion and Future Work}
In this paper, we study an emerging and critical problem of LLM-generated misinformation. First, we build a taxonomy by types, domains, sources, intents and errors to characterize it. Also, we categorize the potential real-world methods to generate misinformation with LLMs and validate that LLMs (\eg, ChatGPT) can be utilized to generate misinformation in different types, domains and errors. Then,  we conduct an extensive empirical investigation and discover that LLM-generated misinformation \textit{can be harder} to detect for \textit{humans} and \textit{detectors} compared to human-written misinformation with the same semantics, indicating that LLM-generated misinformation \textit{can have more deceptive} styles and potentially cause \textit{more harm}. Finally, we discuss the implications of our findings on combating misinformation in the age of LLMs and the countermeasures through the whole LLMs' lifecycle.
