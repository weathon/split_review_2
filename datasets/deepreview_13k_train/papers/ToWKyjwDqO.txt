\title{Direct Judgement Preference Optimization}

\begin{abstract}
Auto-evaluation is crucial for assessing response quality and offering feedback for model development. Recent studies have explored training large language models (LLMs) as generative judges to evaluate and critique other models' outputs.
In this work, we investigate the idea of learning from both positive and negative data with preference optimization to enhance the evaluation capabilities of LLM judges across an array of different use cases.
We achieve this by employing three approaches to collect the preference pairs for different use cases, each aimed at improving our generative judge from a different perspective. 
Our comprehensive study over a wide range of benchmarks demonstrates the effectiveness of our method.
In particular, our generative judge achieves the best performance on 10 out of 13 benchmarks, outperforming strong baselines like GPT-4o and specialized judge models.
Further analysis show that our judge model robustly counters inherent biases such as position and length bias, flexibly adapts to any evaluation protocol specified by practitioners, and provides helpful language feedback for improving downstream generator models.\footnote{The code and our judge models will be released for research purposes.}


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Auto-evaluation plays an important role for assessing response quality and providing feedback for improving large language models (LLMs), as human evaluation is expensive and unscalable.
Due to their impressive language understanding and generative capabilities, LLMs themselves have been leveraged in recent studies as \textit{generative judges} to not only evaluate outputs from other models, but also provide free-text critiques as language feedback for model alignment~\citep{akyurek2023rl4f,lu2023self,hu2024teaching}. 
Auto-evaluation using LLMs has evolved quickly, moving from prompting high-performing LLMs, like GPT-4~\citep{gpt4}, to training specialized \textit{judge models}, which are explicitly purposed to provide judgements given an original input instruction and model response(s). The typical approach for training judge models involves collecting labeled data with ground-truth judgements annotated by either humans or powerful LLMs, then training with supervised fine-tuning (SFT)~\citep{li2023generative,kim2023prometheus,vu2024foundational}. 
However, SFT alone is known to be suboptimal, as it only allows LLMs to learn from positive examples with correct judgements without learning to avoid generating incorrect judgements~\citep{song2020improving,pang2024iterative}. 
In this work, we investigate the idea of learning from both positive and negative evaluations with direct preference optimization (DPO)~\citep{rafailov2024direct} to enhance the evaluation capabilities of generative judges. 
To collect such preference pairs, we prompt an LLM to give chain-of-thought (CoT) critique and judge other models' outputs for different evaluation tasks, covering single rating, pairwise comparison and classification (Training Tasks (a) - (c) in~\Cref{fig:overview}). 
We then categorize the generated evaluations into positive and negative evaluations based on whether the final judgements match ground-truth labels for DPO training. To enhance the judge's ability to identify strong or weak responses, we include a fourth training task (Training Task (d) in~\Cref{fig:overview}) aimed at teaching the judge model to realize what a good or bad response consists of. Specifically, given the original input instruction and a judge model's evaluation, we train the judge model to \textit{deduce} the original model response(s), endowing our judge with an understanding about the very responses it judges.
Across all training tasks, the pairwise preference format of DPO data enables a flexible approach in how we curate training data. Naturally, to train our judge to produce both natural language critiques and judgements, we include positive-negative samples with CoT critiques and corresponding judgements. However, a potentially long CoT critique sequence may dilute the training signal for the final judgement, as most of the tokens are for language flow and coherence but do not determine the final judgement~\citep{chen2024improving}. To mitigate this, we also train our judge model to provide standard judgements \textit{without} CoT critiques, which allows for more direct supervision for aligning our generative judge with human annotation.

Using our training recipe and curated dataset, we produce judge models of three sizes: 8B, 12B, and 70B parameters. We then conduct a comprehensive set of experiments, covering 13 different benchmarks across the three evaluation tasks, aimed at evaluating a judge model's capabilities in various domains, such as safety, reasoning, instruction following, etc.
The experimental results validate both effectiveness and generalizability of our training and data curation methods, as many of our evaluation tasks are unseen during training. In particular, our largest model achieves the best performance on 10 of 13 benchmarks, surpassing GPT-4o and other strong judge models. As of~\rewardbenchdate, all three of our judge models are three of the top four performing generative judge models on RewardBench~\citep{lambert2024rewardbench}. Additional analysis shows that: (1) Our judge can robustly counter biases inherent in evaluation models such as position and length bias, (2) Our judge can be flexibly adapted to various evaluation tasks through proper prompting which is very practical for different use cases, and (3) Our judge can act as a powerful reward model for providing AI feedback for model development and help revise bad responses from downstream models.


\section{Background}

Three types of preference data are used: Chain-of-Thought Critique, Standard Judgement and Response Deduction. 
}
\label{fig:overview}
\end{figure*}

Following prior work~\citep{vu2024foundational,kim2024prometheus,li2023generative}, we train our judge models to perform a variety of evaluation tasks. As illustrated as Training Tasks (a) - (c) in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, our judge models can be applied on a wide range of auto-evaluation use cases:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item Single Rating: Given a task input $i\in\mathcal{I}$ and a response $r\in\mathcal{R}$ generated by another model, the judge assigns a score regarding the quality of the response. 
    \item Pairwise Comparison: Given a task input $i\in\mathcal{I}$ and a pair of responses $\{r_1,r_2\}\in\mathcal{R}$ generated by two models, the judge provides a preference in terms of which one is better.
    \item Classification: Given a task input $i\in\mathcal{I}$ and a response $r\in\mathcal{R}$ generated by another model, the judge classifies whether the output meets a certain criteria.
\end{itemize}

For each task, an \emph{evaluation rubric} is also provided as input to the judge to specify what aspects (e.g., helpfulness, safety, or in general) are considered for evaluating the responses. We compile an array of training datasets into these three tasks. For each dataset, we hand-craft an \emph{evaluation protocol} $p$ that describes the evaluation task (single, pairwise or classification) and the evaluation rubric, following original directions given to human annotators when available. All these datasets are formatted as a sequence-to-sequence task.  
Ultimately, we aim to train a unified generative judge that can perform different evaluation tasks based on the protocol and the input in the prompt.

Previous studies focus on supervised fine-tuning (SFT), where the generative judge is trained on positive evaluation examples with correct judgements, annotated by either humans or powerful LLMs like GPT-4. However, SFT for boosting the reasoning capability of an LLM can be suboptimal for the following reasons.
First, the judge only learns to imitate the reasoning form from the positive examples but not necessarily the underlying reasoning skills for deriving the right judgement~\citep{dai2024beyond}. 
Second, since the model does not explicitly learn to avoid generating the negative examples with incorrect judgements, the probability of negative examples could also increase along the positive examples during SFT as shown in~\cite{pang2024iterative}. 



\section{Method}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figures/workflow.pdf}
\small \caption{Our preference data curation and training pipeline. Three types of preference data are constructed: (1) Chain-of-Thought Critique $\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}$ for boosting reasoning, (2) Standard Judgement $\mathcal{D}_\text{Std}$ for direct supervision and (3) Response Deduction  $\mathcal{D}_\text{Ded}$ for enhancing understanding.}
\label{fig:workflow}
\end{figure*}

The above observations motivate us to construct both positive and negative examples for training our generative judge via preference optimization instead of pure SFT.
Notably, we propose constructing 3 types of positive and negative examples to improve the capability of our generative judges from different perspectives, as illustrated as Preference Pairs in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}. These include: 1) Chain-of-Thought Critique, which aims to improve the reasoning capability, 2) Standard Judgement, which aims to provide direct supervision for producing the correct judgement, and 3) Response Deduction, which aims to further enhance the understanding of good/bad responses in hindsight. The overall preference data construction process is illustrated in~\Cref{fig:workflow}.
\subsection{Chain-of-Thought Critique}\label{sec:cot_critique}
Given an evaluation protocol $p$, a task input $i$ and a response $r$ from another model to be evaluated (or a response pair $\{r_a,r_b\}$ for pairwise comparison) as input $x\in\mathcal{X}$, our judge is trained to generate a free-text evaluation $y=\{c,j\}\in\mathcal{Y}$. The evaluation consists of (1) a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) critique $c$ that provides a detailed analysis of the response(s) and (2) a final judgement $j$, which could be a single score, a preference over $\{r_a,r_b\}$, or a classification result. 

To construct the positive and negative examples $\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}=\{x,y^w,y^l\}$ for judgement preference optimization, we first prompt a teacher LLM $M_{t}$ to generate candidate evaluations $y=\{c, j\}$ for a diverse set of training data. Then based on whether the judgement $j$ matches the ground-truth annotation, we categorize the candidates into positive and negative examples. Through preference optimization, our generative judge learns to increase the probability of good reasoning traces while decreasing that of bad reasoning traces.

\subsection{Standard Judgement}\label{sec:std_judgement} 
In addition to training our judge models to produce explanations in the form of critiques, we want to ensure our judges produce the correct final judgement. However, in the CoT critiques, only a few important tokens determine the final judgement while the remaining tokens improve flow of speech and coherence, as exemplified in~\Cref{fig:cot_critique}. Thus, the relatively long output sequence may dilute the training signal for these crucial tokens~\citep{chen2024improving}, leading to poor judgement supervision and sub-optimal alignment with human preferences. 
To mitigate this, we also train our model to generate standard judgements without the CoT critiques. To construct the positive and negative examples $\mathcal{D}_\text{Std}=\{x,y^w,y^l\}$, we simply remove the CoT critique part of $y$ from $\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}$ and modify the evaluation protocol $p$ in $x$ accordingly to reflect this output requirement.  
By learning from such standard judgement preference, we provide a more direct training signal on the representation of our generative judge. In~\Cref{sec:ablation}, we show that this task is critical for the judge to initiate correct reasoning even when conducting CoT-type evaluation. 

\subsection{Response Deduction}
We further propose an auxiliary task, Response Deduction (Training Task (d) in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}), to help our generative judge enhance the understanding of what both good and bad responses should look like. In this task, the judge is given as input the original evaluation protocol $p$, a task input $i$ and the CoT critique $\{c,j\}$ that matches the ground-truth given by the teacher model $M_t$ from $\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}$. Then the judge is trained to generate the original response(s) $y=r$ (or $y=\{r_a,r_b\}$). We find such a reverse task helps our model to understand the evaluation task in hindsight~\citep{liu2023chain}, and its effectiveness is validated in our experiments.

To construct the preference pairs $\mathcal{D}_\text{Ded}=\{x,y^w,y^l\}$ for Response Deduction, we first prompt a weaker teacher LLM to conduct Response Deduction and treat its generation as negative example $y^l$. We then treat the original response(s) as the positive example $y^w$ since they are used to generate the CoT critique $\{c,j\}$.

\subsection{Training}
With these three types of preference data $\mathcal{D}_\text{train}=\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}\cup\mathcal{D}_\text{Std}\cup\mathcal{D}_\text{Ded}$, we then employ the DPO training objective for fine-tuning a student model $M_s$ to be our generative judge. The parameters of $M_s$ are initialized from an instruction-tuned LLM (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and are learnable during training. DPO is a good modeling choice when the preferred response $y^w$ is not necessarily a \textit{satisfactory} response~\citep{pal2024smaug}. However, in our case the positive examples $y^w$ could be considered as nearly-gold completions (e.g., an evaluation with the judgement matching the ground-truth). Thus, we also add  SFT loss in addition to DPO loss following~\cite{pang2024iterative}:
\begin{align}\label{eq:sftdpo}
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO+SFT} &=\mathcal{L}_\text{SFT}(y_i^w|x_i)+\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(y_i^w,y_i^l|x_i)\\
    &=-\frac{\log M_s(y_i^w|x_i)}{|y_i^w|+|x_i|}
    -\log\sigma\left(\beta\frac{M_s(y_i^w|x_i)}{M_\text{ref}(y_i^w|x_i)}-\beta\frac{M_s(y_i^l|x_i)}{M_\text{ref}(y_i^l|x_i)}\right),
\end{align}
where the reference model $M_\text{ref}$ is also initialized from the same instruction-tuned model as $M_\text{s}$ and its parameters are fixed during training.
With this loss, our judge learns to increase the likelihood of positive examples (more firmly with the addition of the SFT loss) while decreasing the likelihood of negative examples.



\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Training Data and Details}
To build a generic multifaceted judge model that generalizes across various evaluation tasks, we curate our training data to cover a wide range of evaluation tasks (single rating/pairwise/classification) that evaluate different aspects (general quality, factuality, helpfulness, safety, etc.) of model responses to various types of instructions (general user queries, reasoning, math or coding problems). Our training data sources from both human- and model-generated annotations. For human annotated datasets, we take inspiration from the datasets proposed by~\cite{vu2024foundational}. However, we opt to focus on datasets that evaluate \textit{modern} (2023 and beyond) LLM responses, as older datasets likely contain lower quality responses from less capable models, with correspondingly stale annotations. We supplement human-annotated data with synthetically generated data to endow our judge models with specific capabilities (e.g., following fine-grained rubrics in evaluation), utilizing datasets similar to those used by several other judge models~\citep{kim2024prometheus,kim2023prometheus,park2024offsetbias,skyworkcritic2024,wang2024self}.

A majority of these datasets do not provide the CoT critiques since such free-text explanations are more expensive to collect compared to the final judgements. However, our approach does not require annotated CoT critiques, allowing us to make use of these high-quality annotated judgements. As described in \Cref{sec:cot_critique}, we prompt Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct as a strong teacher model to obtain high-quality preference data $\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}$. Standard judgement preference $\mathcal{D}_\text{Std}$ is obtained by removing the CoT critiques from $\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}$. For obtaining $\mathcal{D}_\text{Ded}$, we prompt a weaker model Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct to generate the deduced responses as the negative examples. In total, we collect 680K preference pairs, with a 70\%:15\%:15\% ratio for  $\mathcal{D}_\text{CoT}$, $\mathcal{D}_\text{Std}$ and $\mathcal{D}_\text{Ded}$\footnote{We also performed data selection to ensure balanced label distribution for each task, whether single, pairwise, or classification.}.
We then train three models using the training loss in Eq.~\ref{eq:sftdpo}: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, NeMo-Instruct-12B, and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct, yielding \ourmodelsmall, \ourmodelmed, \ourmodellarge, respectively.
\subsection{Evaluation Datasets}
We adopt a comprehensive evaluation suite, comprising of seven pairwise comparison benchmarks, four single rating evaluation benchmarks, and two classification benchmarks. This suite of benchmarks is meant to broadly evaluate how judge models make decisions in different use cases (e.g., general chat quality, summary quality, safety). 

We evaluate performance on the following seven pairwise comparison datasets.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{RewardBench \citep{lambert2024rewardbench}.} RewardBench assesses reward-modeling capabilities with a focus on four categories: Chat, Chat Hard, Safety, and Reasoning (math and coding).
    \item \textbf{InstruSum \citep{liu2023benchmarking}.} InstruSum assesses the performance of language models in complex instruction following for text summarization. Their test set is comprised of human responses to pairwise comparisons formed from 11 different LLM outputs.
    \item \textbf{Auto-J (Eval-P set) \citep{li2023generative}.} Auto-J assesses the generative capabilities of language models across eight major groups, including creative writing, code, and rewriting. This test set consists of pairwise comparisons (ties allowed) between outputs sourced from 58 different models.
    \item \textbf{HHH \citep{askell2021general}.} HHH consists of human annotated pairwise comparisons meant to assess the safety of models along four axes: helpfulness, honesty, harmlessness, and other. 
    \item \textbf{LFQA \citep{xu2023critical}.} LFQA evaluates models on their ability to answer questions with high degrees of complexity, often necessitating longer, well-reasoned responses. This benchmark consists of pairwise comparisons between GPT-3.5 responses and human written responses answered by experts across seven domains.
    \item \textbf{EvalBiasBench \citep{park2024offsetbias}.} EvalBiasBench is a meta-evaluation benchmark for evaluating how biased an LLM-judge model is in 6 different categories: length, concreteness, empty reference, content continuation, nested instruction, and familiar knowledge.
    \item \textbf{PreferenceBench \citep{kim2024prometheus}.} PreferenceBench is an in-domain test set for the Prometheus 2 models, which aims to assess the fine-grained evaluation ability of judge models via rubrics and reference answers.
\end{itemize}

We evaluate performance on the following four single rating benchmarks.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{BiGGen Bench \citep{kim2024biggen}.} BiGGen Bench evaluates nine distinct generation capabilities (e.g., instruction following, reasoning, tool usage, etc.) across 77 tasks, providing model outputs and scores for 103 different language models. We utilize the human evaluation test set.
    \item \textbf{FLASK \citep{ye2023flask}.} FLASK contains human and GPT-4 scores, along with fine-grained rubrics, for responses from four different models.
    \item \textbf{MT Bench \citep{zheng2024judging}.} MT Bench consists of GPT-4 scored responses from four different models. 
    \item \textbf{FeedbackBench \citep{kim2023prometheus}.} FeedbackBench is an in-domain test set for the Prometheus models, which acts as a fine-grained evaluation benchmark with rubrics and reference answers.
\end{itemize}

For classification, we use the following two benchmarks.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{LLM-AggreFact (Pre-August 9, 2024 update) \citep{tang2024minicheck}.} LLM-AggreFact is a large-scale benchmark that sources questions from 10 attribution benchmarks. Here, the judge model is given a document and is asked to verify if the claim, which is produced by either a model or a human, is supported by the document. Note that the August 9th update the benchmark added RagTruth~\citep{wu2023ragtruth} data to the evaluation set. As our model was trained on RagTruth data, we utilize the earlier version of the dataset to avoid any potential test set leakage.
    \item \textbf{InfoBench (Expert split) \citep{qin2024infobench}.} InfoBench evaluates the instruction following capabilities of five different language models via multiple yes/no questions for each response. Because the responses and questions contain specialized content, we evaluate on the expert annotations for questions for which \textit{all} experts responded with the same response. This filtering yielded 930 unique yes/no questions.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baselines and Evaluation Procedure}\label{sec:baselines_procedures}
We compare our models against several popular open-source generative judge models trained on multiple tasks: Prometheus~2 \citep{kim2024prometheus}, follow-up variant Prometheus 2 BGB \citep{kim2024biggen}, Llama3-OffsetBias~\citep{park2024offsetbias}\footnote{
    Llama3-OffsetBias was trained for pairwise tasks, but include single rating tasks in the training data. As such, we consider it as a multi-evaluation task baseline.
}, Skywork-Critic-Llama-3.1~\citep{skyworkcritic2024} and Auto-J \citep{li2023generative}. Of these models, only Auto-J and the Prometheus variants were trained to produce critiques to complement their judgements. We also note that we only evaluate Skywork-Critic-Llama-3.1 on pairwise benchmarks, as the model was trained on largely pairwise samples, with only small number of single rating samples included in their training set. We also compare against the three variants of FLAMe \citep{vu2024foundational}, when possible.\footnote{
    FLAMe was evaluated on \textit{subsets} of their chosen benchmarks if the benchmark test set has more than 256 samples. We utilize their reported numbers directly, indicating appropriately if a subset was used.
} 
Additionally, we select OpenAI's GPT-4o and GPT-4o-mini as proprietary baselines. For fair comparison, we utilize the original prompt templates of generative judge baselines, making minimal changes to accommodate new tasks or information (e.g., accommodating rubrics in evaluation or allowing for pairwise comparison ties). For proprietary and instruct models, unless the benchmark has provided a template, we utilize the default pairwise prompt from RewardBench~\citep{lambert2024rewardbench} and the default single rating prompt from Prometheus~\cite{kim2023prometheus}.

For single rating tasks, we use a fixed prompt for all benchmarks, as all of the benchmarks include specialized scoring rubrics and reference answers\footnote{
}. For pairwise comparison benchmarks, which lack exact scoring rubrics, we craft specific protocols for each benchmark for pairwise comparison, primarily to highlight the flexibility our models afford practitioners due to the careful creation of training samples. Such specific prompting is \textit{not} the source of performance gains over baselines: we explore utilizing two other prompting strategies that are uniform across all pairwise benchmarks in~\Cref{sec:exp:prompt-ablation} and find negligible differences in performance, with mild performance gains in some cases.

For pairwise comparison and classification benchmarks, we report the agreement between model judgements and human annotators (i.e., accuracy), and for single rating benchmarks, we report Pearson correlation coefficient between model outputs and human ratings. We adopt the default evaluation setup for RewardBench. For all other pairwise comparison benchmarks, because existing models exhibit positional bias~\citep{wang2023large}, where model responses are not consistent when the order of the two responses is swapped, we adopt the consistency evaluation setup of~\citep{li2023generative}, where we run each benchmark twice, exchanging the order of responses in the second run. We report the best performance of these two runs in~\Cref{sec:results} and analyze the consistency rate of judge models in~\Cref{sec:bias-analysis}. For datasets with multiple categories, such as EvalBiasBench and HHH, we report microaverage. For all non-proprietary models, we set the sampling temperature to 0, top-p to 1, and limit the number of output tokens to 1024. For OpenAI models, we utilize the default API parameters (temperature of 0.7, top-p of 1).

\subsection{\ourmodel outperform all comparable models on 10/13 benchmarks across 3 tasks.}
Our results, presented in~\Cref{table:eval:pairwise}, \ref{table:eval:direct_score}, and~\ref{table:eval:classification}, highlight the impressive strength of \ourmodel across a variety of challenging benchmarks, with even our smallest model exhibiting better average performance than GPT-4o and specialized judge model baselines. Our 70B model is the highest performing model on five of seven pairwise comparison benchmarks, being remarkably effective across a variety of judgement domains, including reward modeling (RewardBench), safety (HHH), and summarization (InstruSum). In single rating tasks, our judge models consistently outperform judge models trained to produce single ratings (Prometheus variants and Auto-J) or trained with single rating data (Llama-3-OffsetBias), with our largest model being extremely competitive with GPT-4o across the board. Finally, on classification tasks, our models are consistently capable of performing extremely coarse evaluation (LLM-AggreFact) or extremely fine-grained evaluation (InfoBench), with all model sizes outperforming other judge models and even GPT-4o. Finally, in~\Cref{app:basemodelablation} and~\Cref{app:instruct_models}, we demonstrate our models improve over their base model counterparts and other instruct model baselines, illustrating the effectiveness of our training procedure.
\subsection{\ourmodel achieve state-of-the-art RewardBench performance for generative judges.}
We present a detailed breakdown of RewardBench performance in~\Cref{table:eval:rewardbench}. Among generative judges, \ourmodellarge and \ourmodelmed are the first two models to cross the 90\% accuracy threshold. As of~\rewardbenchdate, \ourmodel are three of the top four performing generative judge models, with even our 8B model outperforming other strong baselines, such as Self-taught-Llama (70B) and FLAMe (24B), despite having far fewer parameters. When compared to other strong 8B parameter models, such as Llama-3-OffsetBias or Skywork-Critic-Llama-3.1-8B, our \ourmodelsmall offers competitive RewardBench performance, the additional benefit of actionable natural language feedback (both of the aforementioned models are not trained to produce critiques), and more well-rounded performance on other evaluation tasks, as demonstrated by our comprehensive evaluation results. 

\subsection{\ourmodel are less biased than comparable judge models.}\label{sec:bias-analysis}
Recent analysis~\citep{park2024offsetbias} has identified six types of biases that judge models are vulnerable to, and proposed EvalBiasBench, a meta-evaluation benchmark with bias-specific test samples. To analyze model biases, we evaluate \ourmodel and other common LLM-as-judge models for bias on EvalBiasBench and also report the average \textit{consistency} across the non-RewardBench benchmarks, which measures if the model is capable of returning the same judgement choice if the order of responses is swapped in a pairwise comparison. Our results are presented in~\Cref{table:eval:bias}. On EvalBiasBench, our models outperform powerful models such as GPT-4o, trailing only Llama-3-OffsetBias, a model specifically trained with an emphasis on bias mititgation. Our model matches or surpasses Llama-3-OffsetBias across multiple categories, but is relatively weak when it comes to handling empty references. For positional bias, our models surpass all comparable baselines by substantial margins, with an average consistency of 91.41\% for our largest model and 89.00\% for our smallest model. All three of our models demonstrate more consistent pairwise comparison judgements than the next best models, beating GPT-4o-mini, Skywork-Critic, and Llama-3-OffsetBias by at least 5.37, 3.21, and 7.40 absolute percentage points, respectively.

\subsection{All three training tasks contribute in creating well-rounded judge.}\label{sec:ablation}
We train multiple 8B parameter judge models to investigate the effects of each of the DPO training tasks from~\Cref{sec:method}. We report our findings in~\Cref{fig:training_task_ablation}, where we plot the average performance across all three evaluation tasks when removing each training task. The inclusion of CoT critique, standard judgement, and response deduction yield the best performing models for pairwise and classification tasks. Notably, including direct response judgements resulted in sizable performance gains in pairwise comparisons, highlighting the benefits a more direct training signal brings to generative judge models. While excluding the response deduction task leads to slightly better single rating performance, the gains in both pairwise and classification settings show that all three tasks yield the most well-rounded judge model.

\subsection{\ourmodel allow for flexible prompting strategies.}\label{sec:exp:prompt-ablation}
While our training data includes a diverse variety of protocols, instructions, and rubrics, we were able to create task-specific prompts for the pairwise comparison tasks. Here, we verify that our strong performance on the pairwise comparison benchmarks was not due solely to a customized prompting strategy. Specifically, we experiment with two different prompt templates that are \textit{fixed} for all pairwise benchmarks. As shown in~\Cref{fig:ourmodeltemplates}, our model is reliably robust to the specific choice of prompting templates, with negligible performance drops (or even minor performance gains in the case of \ourmodelmed) when using fixed prompt templates. This demonstrates flexibility \ourmodel offer to practitioners: If one has task-specific criteria, our models can accommodate such criteria in evaluation. On the other hand, if no such criteria exist, then our models can reliably reason about responses using general evaluation criteria. We showcase outputs for our judge models on samples from the Chat Hard subset of RewardBench using both our RewardBench and PRePair prompt templates in~\Cref{app:example_outputs}.

\subsection{\ourmodel for downstream model development}
In this study, we demonstrate how downstream models can learn from the feedback provided by our generative judge for model development. We investigate two settings in particular. In the first setting, \ourmodellarge is used as a reward model to score the generations sampled from a downstream model (Llama-3-8B-Instruct) for UltraFeedback~\citep{cui2023ultrafeedback}, a dataset with a diverse set of prompts, using a 5-point Likert scale with additive prompting~\citep{yuan2024self}. Then, for each data point, we treat the highest-scoring response as the positive response and the lowest-scoring response as the negative response and train the downstream model using DPO. We compare our method with baselines using classifier-based reward models PairRM~\citep{jiang2023llm} and ArmoRM~\citep{ArmoRM}, provided by~\cite{meng2024simpo}. 

In the second setting, inspired by~\cite{hu2024teaching}, we take the further step of leveraging the CoT critiques from our generative judge as language feedback for model refinement. We also prompt the untuned Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct to refine the responses for comparison. 

We assess the resulting models on the open-ended instruction-following benchmark AlpacaEval-2~\citep{alpaca_eval}, following the evaluation protocol of AlpacaEval-2 to obtain the results (win rate vs. GPT-4 Turbo). As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:score_and_refine}, \ourmodellarge as a reward model yields a better downstream model compared to classifier-based methods. Utilizing CoT critiques, which are not available with classifier-based methods, leads to even larger increases in downstream performance.





						


\section{Related Work}

The rapid acceleration in LLM development has necessitated efficient and cost-effective ways of assessing the quality of model outputs. Human judgements, while still considered the gold standard in model evaluation, are both costly and time-consuming to collect. The emergence of powerful LLMs, such as GPT-4o and Claude, naturally yielded line of research explored the ability of such models to act as automated evaluators by precise prompting~\citep{wang2023chatgpt,liu2023g,fu2024gptscore,chiang2023can}. While promising, such approaches have several fundamental drawbacks. First, prior work that such models exhibit an array of biases~\citep{park2024offsetbias,koo2023benchmarking}, such as favoring their own model outputs~\citep{liu2023g, bai2024benchmarking, panickssery2024llm}, being sensitive to the position of responses in pairwise comparisons~\citep{li2023generative, wang2023large}. Furthermore, in reasoning intensive tasks such as response judgement, the most capable models are often closed-source, requiring API calls to an ever-changing model backend. 

To address these drawbacks, the development of \textit{generative judge models} has emerged as an avenue of exciting research. Rather than carefully prompting a closed-source model, generative judge models are fine-tuned with the express purpose of providing preference judgements and response ratings. PandaLM~\citep{wang2023pandalm} initially explored fine-tuning models based on GPT-3.5 responses, while MT-Bench~\citep{zheng2024judging} performed small-scale experiments fine-tuning on human annotations. Auto-J~\cite{li2023generative} expanded upon this work by diversifying the training data to encompass a variety of tasks, utilizing classifier scores as ground-truth preference labels.

Recent approaches have focused on training data, with one line of work espousing training via entirely synthetically generated data, whether through self-teaching with Self-Taught Evaluator~\citep{wang2024self} or synthesized directly from larger teacher models, such as GPT-4, as is done with Prometheus~\citep{kim2023prometheus} and Prometheus 2~\citep{kim2024prometheus,kim2024biggen}. In contrast, FLAMe~\citep{vu2024foundational} has emphasized using entirely human-annotated preference data. Approaches leveraging hybrid approaches using a small number of human- and model-annotated preference datasets have also gained popularity~\citep{park2024offsetbias,skyworkcritic2024}. However, all of the above methods have trained their models with SFT. Our work marks a paradigm shift, employing DPO to ensure our judges understand the substance of correct \textit{and} incorrect judgements.



\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we present \ourmodel, a family of multifaceted generative judges that can perform a variety of evaluation tasks.
Our models are trained via preference optimization, allowing them to learn explicitly from both positive and negative samples. We create our preference optimization data to explicitly target three capabilities: reasoning, judgement, and response comprehension.
To enhance reasoning capability of our judge model, we include chain-of-thought critiques in the preference pairs. To ensure our judge is capable of producing the correct judgement, we utilize standard judgement training data, which omits natural language critique. To ensure our judge understands what a good (or bad) response consists of, we include a response deduction task. 
Experiments on a comprehensive set of 13 benchmarks, spanning 3 distinct evaluation tasks (pairwise, single rating, and classification) demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. Notably, our generative judge achieves the best performance on 10 of 13 benchmarks, beating competitive baselines like GPT-4o and specialized judge models. Further analysis shows our judge is robust to evaluation biases and can be flexibly adapted for different evaluation purposes. We also showcase how our model can provide AI feedback for downstream model development.
