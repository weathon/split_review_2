\title{Can Transformers Do Enumerative Geometry?}

\begin{abstract}
    How can Transformers model and learn enumerative geometry? What is a robust procedure for using Transformers in abductive knowledge discovery within a mathematician-machine collaboration?
    In this work, we introduce a new paradigm in computational enumerative geometry in analyzing the $\psi$-class intersection numbers on the moduli space of curves.
    By formulating the enumerative problem as a continuous optimization task, we develop a Transformer-based model for computing $\psi$-class intersection numbers based on the underlying quantum Airy structure. For a finite range of genera, our model is capable of regressing intersection numbers that span an extremely wide range of values, from $10^{-45}$ to $10^{45}$. 
    To provide a proper inductive bias for capturing the recursive behavior of intersection numbers, we propose a new activation function, Dynamic Range Activator~(DRA).
    Moreover, given the severe heteroscedasticity of $\psi$-class intersections and the required precision, we quantify the uncertainty of the predictions using Conformal Prediction with a dynamic sliding window that is aware of the number of marked points. Next, we go beyond merely computing intersection numbers and explore the enumerative ``world-model'' of the Transformers. Through a series of causal inference and correlational interpretability analyses, we demonstrate that Transformers are actually modeling Virasoro constraints in a purely data-driven manner.
    Additionally, we provide evidence for the comprehension of several values appearing in the large genus asymptotic of $\psi$-class intersection numbers through abductive hypothesis testing.

\end{abstract}

Enumerative geometry is a fascinating branch of mathematics concerned with counting geometric objects or finding invariants that satisfy certain geometric conditions~(see for instance \cite{mumford1983towards,katz2006enumerative}). The objects of interest are typically complex curves that need to match a specific geometric blueprint. A classical question in enumerative geometry is: \textit{How many conics pass through five given points in the plane?} A more contemporary question is: \textit{How many rational curves of degree one are there on a quintic threefold?}

Approaching such problems often requires recursive computational methods. However, these methods can become computationally expensive and even infeasible, even for relatively small values of the parameters of interest. This is where we propose using Machine Learning~(ML), particularly Transformers~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}, to overcome this challenge. While recent work with Transformers, especially in Large Language Models~(LLMs)~\cite{zhao2023survey}, has focused on their ability to solve problems in natural language processing and image generation, these models still struggle with complex recursive and mathematical reasoning tasks in a zero-shot setting. This limitation is particularly critical in fields that require deep, structured mathematical reasoning. By incorporating Transformers as a primary tool in enumerative geometry, specifically for approximating $\psi$-class intersection numbers, we aim to address the computational complexity overhead and explore several important questions. 
Traditional methods for computing these numbers, given genus $g$ and $n$ marked points, are computationally expensive with a complexity of $O(g! \cdot 2^n)$.
These intersection numbers are famously computed by the Korteweg--de~Vries equation~\cite{witten1991two,kontsevich1992intersection} and are of paramount importance in the study of various geometric and physical problems, such as Weil--Petersson volumes, Hurwitz numbers, Gromov--Witten invariants, and topological 2D quantum gravity.
Going further, we investigate whether our proposed model is capable of causal and abductive reasoning for knowledge discovery. Specifically, we ask if we can conduct parametric inference and extract mathematical insights from its internal understanding, beyond the raw data provided, to offer intuition and potentially generate new conjectures.

It is well known that Transformer-based models have difficulty with even periodic patterns, let alone recursive problems~\cite{ziyin2020neural}. Therefore, we first examine the architectural design choices and training pipeline variants necessary for learning the recursive reasoning processes involved in topological recursion~\cite{eynard2007invariants}. To this end, we introduce a non-linear activation function, the Dynamic Range Activator~(DRA), which is particularly suited for learning recursive functions. Consequently, we develop DynamicFormer, a modified Transformer-based model designed to predict $\psi$-class intersection numbers given the quantum Airy structure~\cite{kontsevich2018airy} initial data. Additionally, we believe that a predictive model without uncertainty estimation is inherently unreliable. Therefore, we incorporate uncertainty quantification into our model's predictions using Conformal Prediction~\cite{shafer2008tutorial}.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time Transformers have been applied to enumerative problems in algebraic geometry, while also employing interpretability analysis~\cite{chefer2021transformer,liu2024interpretable,kantamneni2024transformers} to extract knowledge and illuminate the model's internal understanding of deep research-level mathematical concepts. Thus, in this paper, we aim to target both machine learning scientists and mathematicians, as our work is relevant to both communities.

\section{Related Works}


Transformer-based models~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}, which were originally developed for natural language processing~\cite{radford2018improving,devlin2019bert}, have since been leveraged for various data modalities~\cite{dosovitskiy2021,gong2021astaudio,carolan2024reviewmultimodal}. Due to their flexibility and interoperability, they have been extensively used in different tasks in Artificial Intelligence~(AI) for Science~\cite{wang2023scientific}.
In recent years, a new branch of ML applications, often referred to as AI for Mathematics~\cite{he2023machine}, has emerged. This branch includes Automated Theorem Proving~\cite{loos2017deepnetworkguidedproof,paliwal2020graph,an2024learnfailurefinetuningllms}, Generation~\cite{trinh2024solving,wang2020learning,lin2024atg}, Autoformalization~\cite{wang2020exploration,wu2022autoformalization,jiang2023multilingual,mikula2024magnushammer,ying2024lean}, machine-human collaborative guidance~\cite{bao2020quiver,anderson2021moduli,davies2021advancing,berman2022machine,craven2023learning,coates2023machine,dong2024machine}, examples/counter\-examples search problems~\cite{halverson2019branes,gukov2021learning,wagner2021constructions,gukov2023searching,berglund2024generating,romera2024mathematical}, and predictive problems (such as amplitude and mathematical expression prediction)~\cite{saxton2019,dersy2023simplifying,alnuqaydan2023symba,dascoli2022deep,cai2024transforming,coates2024machine}. These areas extensively use Transformers, particularly in problems arising from particle interactions and scattering processes.

Our work bridges the domains of machine-human collaborative guidance and predictive problems while introducing new methods. In the prediction task, while most studies remain within in-distribution settings, we extend our approach to out-of-distribution predictions, coupled with Conformal Prediction uncertainty estimation---a notably absent requirement in current literature. 
Additionally, we enhance the predictive task by using multi-modal data, involving both discrete sets and continuous graph (sequence) representations. This approach moves beyond the focus on mere compact symbolic/numerical sequence modalities~\cite{meidani2024snip}. Furthermore, in the field of machine-human collaborative guidance, we conduct causal inference and correlational analyses to understand the ``world-model'' of Transformers~\cite{micheli2023transformers}, offering deep insights into the underlying algebraic geometry problem at hand.


\section{Mathematical Background}


In this section, we review some background on the original mathematical problem. Let $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$ be the moduli space of genus $g \ge 0$ complex stable algebraic curves with $n > 0$ distinct marked points. This is a complex, compact, smooth orbifold of dimension $3g - 3 + n$. Associated with each marked point is a natural cohomology class $\psi_i = c_1(\mathcal{L}_i)$, defined as the first Chern class of the cotangent line bundle $\mathcal{L}_i$ at the $i$-th marked point.

In the early 90s, Witten~\cite{witten1991two} analyzed a theory of two-dimensional topological quantum gravity, where $\psi$-classes play the role of observables, and the \textit{intersection numbers} (sometimes called \textit{amplitudes})
\begin{equation}
    \langle \tau_{d_1} \cdots \tau_{d_n} \rangle_{g,n}
    \coloneqq
    \int_{\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}} \psi_1^{d_1} \cdots \psi_n^{d_n} \,,
    \qquad
    d_1 + \cdots + d_n = d_{g,n} \coloneqq 3g - 3 + n \,,
\end{equation}
represent correlators of the theory, and are topological invariants of $\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}$. Based on a low genus profile and considerations from physics, Witten conjectured that the exponential generating function
\begin{equation}
    Z(\bm{x};\hbar) =
    \exp\left(
        \sum_{\substack{g \geq 0 \\ n > 0}}
            \frac{\hbar^{2g-2+n}}{n!}
            \sum_{\substack{d_1,\ldots,d_n \ge 0 \\ d_1 + \cdots + d_n = d_{g,n}}}
        \left\langle \tau_{d_1} \cdots \tau_{d_n} \right\rangle_{g,n}
        x^{d_1} \cdots x^{d_n}
    \right)
\end{equation}
satisfies a infinite tower of quadratic partial differential equations with respect to the formal variables $(x^i)_{i \ge 0}$ known as the Korteweg--de~Vries~(KdV) hierarchy. This was later proven by Kontsevich~\cite{kontsevich1992intersection}.

In recent years, it has been discovered that many fundamental invariants in physics and geometry can be described via intersection numbers. In physics, interest in $\psi$-class intersection numbers has been revitalized due to their connection with Jackiw--Teitelboim gravity~\cite{saad2019jt}. From an algebraic geometry perspective, $\psi$-class intersection numbers represent the most basic form of Gromov–Witten invariants~\cite{behrend1997gromov}. These intersection numbers are central to enumerative problems addressed by \cite{kontsevich1992intersection} and \cite{mirzakhani2007weil} regarding the symplectic volumes of the moduli space of metric ribbon graphs and hyperbolic Riemann surfaces. Computing intersection numbers is pivotal in the asymptotic counting of geodesic curves in both flat and hyperbolic random geometries~\cite{mirzakhani2008growth,delecroix2021masur,andersen2023topological}.

Given the wide and fascinating applications of intersection numbers, computing them for arbitrarily large genera remains an open problem. Their association with the KdV hierarchy has been illuminating for calculating these invariants~\cite{witten1991two,kontsevich1992intersection}. Another approach to characterizing these numbers involves Virasoro constraints~\cite{Dijkgraaf:1990dj}, where the partition function is annihilated by a collection of differential operators that form a representation of the Virasoro algebra. Alternative recursive approaches include topological recursion~\cite{eynard2007invariants}, the cut-and-join equation~\cite{alexandrov2011cut}, and exact formulae for the generating series, such as error-function-type integrals~\cite{okounkov2002generating} and the determinantal formula~\cite{bergere2009determinantal}. However, the general computation of intersection numbers remains an open problem, as these methods can only be used to compute $\psi$-class intersection numbers for cases with small $g$ or $n$. Meanwhile, universality phenomena in flat and hyperbolic geometry and in the dynamics of surfaces manifest themselves in large genera. Only recently, Aggarwal~\cite{aggarwal_large} has found a closed-form formula for the large genus asymptotic of $\psi$-class intersection numbers.

\subsection{Quantum Airy Structures}

The conceptually simplest approach to computing $\psi$-class intersection numbers involves using Virasoro constraints recast in the language of quantum Airy structures. The latter is an algebraic reformulation of topological recursion of Eynard and Orantin~\cite{eynard2007invariants} due to Kontsevich and Soibelman~\cite{kontsevich2018airy}. A quantum Airy structure on a (possibly countably infinite-dimensional) complex vector space $V$, dually generated by $(x^i)_{i \in I}$, is a family of differential operators $(L_i)_{i \in I}$ in the Weyl algebra over $V$ of the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qAs:Li}
    L_i
    =
    \hbar \partial_{i}
    -
    \hbar^2 \sum_{a,b \in I}
    \left(
        \frac{1}{2} A_{i,a,b} \, x^a x^b
        +
        B_{i,a}^{b} \, x^a \partial_{b}
        +
        \frac{1}{2} C^{a,b}_i \, \partial_{a} \partial_{b} \right)
    - \hbar^2 D_i \,,
\end{equation}
and forming a Lie sub-algebra of the Weyl algebra:
\begin{equation}
    [L_i, L_j]
    =
    \hbar^2 \sum_{a \in I} f^a_{i,j} L_a \,,
    \qquad\qquad
    \forall i,j \in I \,.
\end{equation}
Here $\partial_i = \partial_{x^i}$ and $A_{i,a,b} = A_{i,b,a}$, $B_{i,a}^b$, $C_i^{a,b} = C_i^{b,a}$, $D_i$, and $f^a_{i,j} = -f^a_{j,i}$ are scalars indexed by $i, j, k \in I$. We remark that $I$ is the set of indices used to label the basis elements of $V$. In the infinite-dimensional setting, certain boundedness conditions must be imposed to guarantee that all sums are finite \cite{andersen2024abcd}.

Given a quantum Airy structure, one can uniquely find a suitably normalized formal function $Z$ on $V$, called partition function, which is annihilated by the diﬀerential operators $(L_i)_{i \in I}$. More precisely, the following theorem holds.

\begin{theorem}
    If $(L_i)_{i \in I}$ is a quantum Airy structure on $V$, there exists a unique formal function $Z$ of the form
    \begin{equation}
        Z(\bm{x};\hbar) =
        \exp\left(
            \sum_{\substack{g \geq 0 \\ n > 0}}
                \frac{\hbar^{2g-2+n}}{n!}
                \sum_{d_1,\ldots,d_n \in I}
                    F_{g;d_1,\ldots,d_n} \,
                    x^{d_1} \cdots x^{d_n}
        \right)
    \end{equation}
    with $F_{0;d_1} = F_{0;d_1,d_2} = 0$, $F_{g;d_1,\ldots,d_n}$ symmetric in $d_1,\ldots,d_n \in I$, and such that
    \begin{equation}
        L_i Z = 0
        \qquad\qquad
        \forall i \in I \,.
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The elements $F_{g,n} = \sum_{d_1,\ldots,d_n \in I} F_{g;d_1,\ldots,d_n} \, x^{d_1} \cdots x^{d_n}$ are symmetric tensors of rank $n$ over $V^*$. The collection of coefficients $(A,B,C,D)$ can also be regarded as tensors, and we will often refer to them as the quantum Airy structure data.

For a given quantum Airy structure, the coefficients $F_{g;d_1,\ldots,d_n}$, called \textit{quantum Airy structure amplitudes}, are determined recursively on (minus) the Euler characteristic $2g-2+n$. For the lowest values of $g$ and $n$, the base cases are given by
\begin{equation}
    F_{0;i, j, k} \coloneqq A_{i,j,k} \,,
    \qquad\qquad
    F_{1;i} \coloneqq D_i \,, 
\end{equation}
where $A_{i,j,k}$ and $D_i$ are part of the data of the quantum Airy structure. For higher values of $g$ and $n$ satisfying $2g - 2 + n > 0$, the recursion formula, known as \textit{topological recursion}, is given by
\begin{multline}\label{eq:TR}
    F_{g;d_1,d_2,\ldots,d_n}
    =
    \sum_{m=2}^n \sum_{a \in I}
        B^a_{d_1,d_m} F_{g;a,d_2, \ldots, \widehat{d_m}, \ldots, d_n} \\
    + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{a,b \in I}
        C^{a,b}_{d_1} \left(
            F_{g-1;a, b, d_2, \ldots, d_n}
            +
            \sum_{\substack{g_1 + g_2 = g \\ I_1 \sqcup I_2 = \{d_2, \ldots, d_n\}}} F_{g_1;a, I_1} \, F_{g_2;b, I_2}
        \right).
\end{multline}
In general, the computation of the amplitudes has a computational complexity of $O(g! \, 2^n)$, which makes the calculation of the amplitudes at high genera problematic.

The simplest example of a quantum Airy structure involves taking $V = \mathbb{C}$ and setting $A = B = C = D = 1$. Under these conditions, we have a unique differential operator given by
\begin{equation}
    L
    =
    \hbar \partial_x - \hbar^2 \left(
        \frac{x^2}{2} + x \partial_x + \frac{1}{2} \partial_x^2 + 1
    \right) .
\end{equation}
The differential equation $L Z = 0$ can be reduced to the Airy differential equation (hence the name ``quantum Airy structure''). Therefore, the associated formal power series $Z$ is given by the properly normalized asymptotic expansion of the Airy function:
\begin{equation}
    Z(x;\hbar)
    =
    \frac{e^{\frac{x}{\hbar}(1 - \frac{\hbar x}{2})}}{\mathrm{Bi}(0)}
    \mathrm{Bi}\Biggl( \frac{1 - 2\hbar x - \hbar^2}{(2\hbar^2)^{2/3}} \Biggr).
\end{equation}
For higher-dimensional vector spaces $V$, finding a closed-form solution becomes increasingly impractical, if not impossible. Besides, the most interesting applications of quantum Airy structures involve an infinite-dimensional vector space $V$. The central example is the one associated to the $\psi$-class intersection numbers. In this case, the underlying vector space is generated by vectors indexed by $I = \mathbb{N}$, the non-negative integers. The differential operators $(L_i)_{i \ge 0}$ are determined by the tensors
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qAs:WK}
\begin{aligned}
    &
    A_{i,j,k} \coloneqq \delta_{i,j,k} \,,\\
    &
    B^k_{i,j} \coloneqq \delta_{i+j,k+1}\frac{(2k+1)!!}{(2i+1)!!(2j-1)!!} \,,\\
    &
    C^{j,k}_{i} \coloneqq \delta_{i,j+k+2}\frac{(2j+1)!!(2k+1)!!}{(2i+1)!!} \,,\\
    &
    D_{i} \coloneqq \frac{\delta_{i,1}}{24} \,.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The resulting operators, after shifting of the indices as $\mathsf{L}_i = L_{i-1}$, form a representation of the Virasoro algebra: $[\mathsf{L}_i,\mathsf{L}_j] = \hbar^2 (i-j) \mathsf{L}_{i+j}$ for all $i \ge -1$. Remarkably, the associated amplitudes coincide with $\psi$-class intersection numbers:
\begin{equation}
    F_{g;d_1,\ldots,d_n}
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \langle \tau_{d_1} \cdots \tau_{d_n} \rangle_{g,n}
        &\text{if }d_1 + \cdots + d_n = d_{g,n} \,, \\
        0 & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In this paper, we focus on the specific choice of quantum Airy structure data given by \cref{eq:qAs:WK}, thereby computing the $\psi$-class intersection numbers. For ease of notation, we will denote a generic partition of $d_{g,n}$ of length $n$ as $\bm{d} = (d_1,\ldots,d_n)$. The associated $\psi$-class intersection number will be denoted by
\begin{equation}
    \langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n}
    \coloneqq
    \langle \tau_{d_1} \cdots \tau_{d_n} \rangle_{g,n} .
\end{equation}
The goal of the next sections is to provide evidence for the possibility of regressing these numbers via a Transformer-based model, namely DynamicFormer, given the quantum Airy structure initial data. Our model is trained on known data computed by a brute force algorithm up to genus $13$ and is tested up to genus $16$. We then provide causal and correlational evidence that our model is actually learning and understanding the recursive nature of the intersection numbers. As a result, we aim to introduce a new paradigm for using Transformers in algebraic geometry as both a powerful and explainable amortization method and a source of intuition for the mathematician.



\section{Data Representation and Setup}


The input data during training consisted of the sparse tensors $B$ and $C$ from \cref{eq:qAs:WK}, the genus $g$, the number of marked points $n$, and the partitions $\bm{d} = (d_1, \ldots, d_n)$ of $d_{g,n}$. We did not include $A$ and $D$ as input, as they are fixed initial conditions for all $\psi$-class intersection numbers.

As we will consider values of $g$ and $n$ such that the dimension $d_{g,n}$ will never exceed $d_{\textup{max}} = 100$, the initial data will consist of only finitely many data. More precisely, the initial data during training were represented as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\bm{B}$ \textbf{Initial Data}: A rank $3$ tensor $B_{i,j}^k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{g,n} \times d_{g,n} \times d_{g,n}}$. Such a tensor links intersection numbers of the same genus (cf. \cref{eq:TR}). Since it is a sparse tensor with $d_{g,n} \le d_{\textup{max}} = 100$, we chose to represent its non-zero components in the COO~(Coordinate List) format. In the COO format, $B$ is represented as a set of $4$-tuples, each containing the indices and the corresponding non-zero value: 
    \begin{equation}
        \bm{B} = \Set{ (i, j, k, B_{i,j}^k) | B_{i,j}^k \neq 0 } 
    \end{equation}
    where $i, j, k \in [0,d_{g,n}]$, and $B_{i,j}^k$ are the non-zero components of the tensor. With this choice of $d_{\textup{max}}$, the maximum length of $\bm{B}$ is approximately 1500.

    \item $\bm{C}$ \textbf{Initial Data}: A rank $3$ tensor $C_{i}^{j,k} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{g,n} \times d_{g,n} \times d_{g,n}}$. Such a tensor links intersection numbers of different genera (cf. \cref{eq:TR}). Again, since it is a sparse tensor with $d_{g,n} \le d_{\textup{max}} = 100$, we chose to represent its non-zero components in the COO format. In the COO format, $C$ is represented as a set of $4$-tuples, each containing the indices and the corresponding non-zero value:
    \begin{equation}
        \bm{C} = \Set{ (i, j, k, C_{i}^{j,k}) | C_{i}^{j,k} \neq 0 } 
    \end{equation}
     where $i, j, k \in [0,d_{g,n}]$, and $C_{i}^{j,k}$ are the non-zero components of the tensor. With this choice of $d_{\textup{max}}$, the maximum length of $\bm{C}$ is approximately 1500.
     
    \item \textbf{Partitions}: The intersection numbers $\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n}$ are labeled by partitions $\bm{d} = (d_1,\ldots,d_n) \in \mathbb{N}^n$ of $d_{g,n}$ of length $n$.
    An important feature of intersection numbers (and more generally, amplitudes computed from quantum Airy structures) is that they are invariant under permutation of the indices, that is, elements of the partition $\bm{d}$. For example given a partition $\bm{d} = (3,0,0)$, we have $\langle 3,0,0 \rangle_{1,3} = \langle 0,3,0 \rangle_{1,3} = \langle 0,0,3 \rangle_{1,3}$.
\end{itemize}

We trained the model using data up to genus $g = 13$ and then tasked it with predicting the intersection numbers for genera $g = 14$, $15$, and $16$. During experimentation, we observed that, when comparing the contributions of $B$ and $C$, the $B$ tensor had a greater impact on $\psi$-class intersection numbers. Consequently, we incorporated only this initial data (excluding $C$), a decision further supported by the inherent properties of the intersection numbers.
This initial observation motivated the subsequent series of explainability analysis experiments and findings.



\section{Methods}


To handle the different input modalities, i.e. the continuous tensor $B$ in COO format and the discrete set $\bm{d} \in \mathbb{N}^n$, we developed a Transformer-based model as depicted in~\cref{fig:model}. However, managing different input formats is only part of the challenge. The recursive nature of many functions in enumerative geometry adds further complexity.
A key property of recursive maps, such as the factorial function, is their rapid growth. Learning this recursive behavior requires not only fitting patterns within a bounded region but also successfully extrapolating those patterns beyond that region. However, it has been shown that Transformers are not very successful at capturing recursive behavior~\cite{lakretz2021causal,zhang2024transformerbased}.
To address the limitations of Transformers in learning the recursive behavior of invariants in enumerative geometry, we introduce DynamicFormer to capture such behavior and perform proper inference for recursive problems.
\textbf{Dynamic Positional Bias.} 
The trunk of the network consists of two modified Transformers: one discrete, acting on the embedding of the partitions $\bm{d}$ and maintaining permutation equivariance, and one continuous, acting on the $B$ tensor~\cite{vaswani2023attentionneed}. The positions of elements in $B$, represented in COO format, correspond to particular combinations of indices that compute the intersection numbers.
To enhance generalization for longer input sequences, we integrated a modified version of Dynamic Positional Bias~\cite{wang2021crossformer,zhang2024transformerbased}. Dynamic Positional Bias computes a relative positional bias map for each attention head, which is learnable and adapts based on sequence length. However, because it does not inherently account for the distance from the start or end of a COO sequence, we apply masking during training based on input sequence length to address this limitation.

\textbf{Dynamic Range Activator.} 
It has been shown~\cite{parascandolo2017taming,dauphin2017language,ziyin2020neural,so2021searching} that the choice of activation functions plays an important role in the interpolation and extrapolation properties of Transformers and, more generally, neural networks. To enable Transformer models to precisely capture the recursive behavior of the data, we introduce a simple activation function that we call the Dynamic Range Activator (DRA). DRA integrates both harmonic and hyperbolic components, as follows,
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{DRA}(x) \coloneqq x + a \sin^2\left(\frac{x}{b}\right) + c \cos(bx) + d \tanh(bx) \,,
\end{equation}
where $a, b, c, d$ are learnable parameters. This diversity allows the function to simultaneously model periodic data (through sine and cosine), rapid growth or attenuation (through the hyperbolic tangent), and maintain a linear baseline response.
DRA is inspired by the Snake non-linear function~\cite{ziyin2020neural}. However, Snake only offers a sinusoidal modulation added to a linear term, which, while providing a basic non-linear transformation, lacks the additional flexibility for rapid damping or amplification effects that hyperbolic tangents can provide.
To demonstrate the advantage of DRA in capturing recursive behavior, we set up a small experiment.
We generate a small dataset based on the recursive function $r(n) = n + (n , \mathrm{AND} , r(n-1))$, where AND is the bitwise logical AND operator~\cite{10.1007}. We train a fully connected neural network with one hidden layer consisting of $64$ neurons over the interval $n \in [0,150]$, then test the model over $n \in [151,200]$.
The results are shown in~\cref{fig:dra_reursiv}.

In contrast, DRA shows a better ability to capture the correct fluctuations, as well as the rapid growth and drops of the underlying recursive function, in both the interpolation and extrapolation regimes.
This provides evidence that DRA has the desired flexibility for modeling recursive behavior and has the potential to effectively model such problems. We further conduct experiments to demonstrate the advantages of DRA over typical non-linear activation functions in predicting $\psi$-class intersection numbers.
\textbf{[DYN] registry tokens and Talking Modalities.} %Another trick that we propose is related to interoperability analysis that is very important for mathematics. 
In multi-modal learning, effectively sharing and integrating information across different modalities is crucial for building robust models.
Inspired by the success of Self-Supervised Learning~(SSL)~\cite{gui2024survey}, we introduce a simple modification to enhance correspondence between modalities.
To achieve this, we concatenate Dynamic tokens, [DYN], to each modality. These tokens are similar to [CLS] tokens in BERT~\cite{devlin2019bert} and Vision Transformers~\cite{dosovitskiy2021}, in that they are passed through the attention layers alongside the input data. However, unlike [CLS] tokens, they are not directly used for the downstream supervised task of amplitude prediction.
Instead, they are used indirectly, like register tokens~\cite{darcet2024vision}, which store and process global information in Vision Transformers.

By attending to all tokens in the input, [DYN] tokens, similar to register tokens, facilitate a soft contrastive learning update, enabling them to learn a global context for each sample. Unlike register tokens, [DYN] tokens are also optimized to ensure that the outputs from different modalities of data---specifically from the $B$ tensor, which provides global information per sample, and the partitions $\bm{d}$, which serve as local information carriers---are as similar as possible while simultaneously reducing the redundancy of the information they carry.
Their optimization is guided by the Canonical Correlation Analysis~\cite{Abdi2018} family of SSL, where the aim is to infer the relationship between two modalities by analyzing their cross-covariance matrices~\cite{balestriero2023cookbook}. Inspired by Barlow Twins~\cite{zbontar2021barlow}, the two modalities start to have a ``conversation'', promoting an interaction in which the two modalities collaboratively refine their representations.
The idea behind this is that such a consensus mechanism not only enhances the model's ability to generalize better to Out-Of-Distribution~(OOD) data but also improves its robustness. The Talking Modalities loss is then defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\textup{TM}}
    =
    \sum_{i=1}^{k} \left(1 - \mathcal{C}_{ii}^{(\bm{B},\bm{d})}\right)^2
    +
    \lambda \sum_{\substack{i,j=1, \ldots, k \\ i \neq j}} \left(\mathcal{C}_{ij}^{(\bm{B},\bm{d})}\right)^2 ,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the cross-correlation matrix of the representations from the two modalities, $(\bm{B},\bm{d})$, $k$ is the dimensionality of the model's embeddings~(vector representations), and $\lambda$ is the weighting factor that balances the contributions of talking modalities. The first term penalizes the diagonal elements of the cross-correlation matrix $\mathcal{C}$ for deviating from the identity, thus encouraging invariance. The second term penalizes the off-diagonal elements, encouraging the components of the representation vectors to be decorrelated.
In~\cref{sec:res}, we will show that this modification enhances the performance of the model in the OOD prediction regime.

After the Transformer trunk of the network, all the embedded information is aggregated by a permutation-invariant block. We employ the Principal Neighbourhood Aggregation~(PNA) layer~\cite{corso2020principal} to pool information from the modalities $(\bm{B},\bm{d})$. Once the information from these two branches, for each sample, is modulated with their corresponding genus and number of marked points positional attributes, $(g,n)$, it is mapped to the outputs, i.e. $\psi$-class intersection numbers, via an MLP head. DRA is the non-linear activation function used throughout these blocks.
As the loss function, we use the Mean Absolute Error~(MAE) loss. Given that the range of amplitudes for the intersection numbers is very wide, $[10^{-45}, 10^{45}]$, MAE was the most appropriate metric. Essentially, MAE is not disproportionately affected by the massive range of the target values, unlike Mean Squared Error~(MSE), which can be overly influenced by the squared terms of large numbers. Thus, the total loss is as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\textup{Total}}
    =
    \mathcal{L}_{\textup{MAE}} + \mathcal{L}_{\textup{TM}} \,.
\end{equation}



\section{Results}


To evaluate the performance of our approach, we consider two setups: In-Distribution~(ID) and Out-Of-Distribution~(OOD) settings. In these settings, we face both length extrapolation and context extrapolation challenges.
Given the wide range of values for $\psi$-class intersection numbers, we use R-squared~($\mathrm{R}^2$) to compare the results and assess the goodness-of-fit in the intersection number regression task.
$\mathrm{R}^2$ reflects the proportion of the variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variables. The higher the value of $\mathrm{R}^2$, the greater the relationship between prediction and ground truth values. For instance, an $\mathrm{R}^2$ value of $0.75$ indicates that 75\% of the total variation in the data is explained by the model, while the remaining 25\% is not captured by the model.

\textbf{Uncertainty Quantification.} 
To estimate the uncertainty of the prediction, we incorporate Conformal Prediction~(CP)~\cite{vovk1999machine}. CP is a probabilistic uncertainty quantification technique that provides prediction intervals in finite samples without making any distributional assumptions.
We integrate Inductive Conformal Prediction~\cite{4410411} with a dynamic sliding window~\cite{xu2023conformalpredictiontimeseries} that computes rolling residuals for predicted intersections for each set of marked points $n$.
In our modification, we fit a quantile regression model to the residuals within a rolling window of marked points, allowing us to predict the desired quantile that adapts to local variations in the residuals. Given the high heteroscedasticity in the data, where the variance of residuals changes across intersections, we employ a sliding window over the interval of each set of marked points.
This ensures that the residuals are more scale-dependent and appropriately adjusted for the inherent variability in the data, potentially capturing trends and heteroscedasticity better.
As a result, we report the Normalized Conformity Score (NCS)
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{NCS} \coloneqq \frac{|\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n} - \langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n}^{\textup{pred}}|}{\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n}}
\end{equation}
and the Conformal Interval Width~(CIW).

We perform two sets of experiments. First, we report the performance of DynamicFormer in both the ID and OOD settings. We compare DRA with Snake~\cite{ziyin2020neural} and GLU~\cite{so2021searching} activation functions as baselines to validate DRA's non-linearity.
We also compare the results with and without the [DYN] register token~\cite{darcet2024vision} and the Talking Modalities mechanism in the OOD setting.
Thereafter, we engage in an interesting discussion on interoperability, correlational, and causal analysis of what the model is learning. We also explore how to perform abductive hypothesis testing based on the model's internal understanding of the underlying enumerative problem to prove conjectures.

\subsection{Insights, Interoperability and Discussion}

Up until now, we have tried to showcase that DynamicFormer, as a modified Transformer, is capable of performing enumerative geometry and predicting $\psi$-class intersection numbers. However, several key questions remain. \textit{Do Transformers actually understand the underlying mathematics? How does the AI agent learn this computation? Is it possible to extract useful knowledge or hints toward a possible (re)discovery of a theorem?}

To address these questions, we have made several interesting observations throughout our work that offer deeper insights into the model's behavior and learning process, hinting at potential mathematical knowledge discovery.

\subsubsection{The Dilaton Equation}

The topological recursion formula \eqref{eq:TR} for the specific case of $\psi$-class intersection numbers and the choice of $d_1 = 1$ takes a particularly simple form, known as the Dilaton equation. After relabelling, it reads
\begin{equation}
    \langle
        1, d_{1}, \ldots, d_n
        \rangle_{g,n+1}
        =
        (2g - 2 + n)
        \langle
            d_1, \ldots, d_n
        \rangle_{g,n} \,.
\end{equation}
The equation states that the intersection number involving a $\tau_1$-term can be reduced to a simpler intersection number with one fewer marked point.

Let $\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n}$ be the intersection numbers indexed by genus $g$ and number of marked points $n$. Given the the set of values of the tensor $\bm{B}$ and the set of partitions $\bm{d} \in \mathbb{N}^{n}$, the neural network embedding $p_{g,n} \colon \set{ \mathbb{R}^{d_{g,n} \times d_{g,n} \times d_{g,n}} , \mathbb{N}^{n}}\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^k$ is a vector space of dimension $k$ and is denoted as $p_{g,n}(\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n} \mid \bm{B}, \bm{d})$. This embedding describes the hidden understanding of the Transformer (right before the prediction head). If we normalize these embeddings to unit length and then compute the self-projection of these embedding vectors onto themselves, we obtain the cosine of the angle between the normalized embeddings of $\psi$-class intersection numbers as a measure of similarity:
\begin{equation}
    S_{i,j} = \frac{\langle p_{g,n}^{i}, p_{g,n}^{j} \rangle}{\lVert p_{g,n}^{i} \rVert \, \lVert p_{g,n}^{j} \rVert} \,.
\end{equation}
The similarity matrix $S_{i,j}$ represents the geometric proximity between two pairs of $\psi$-class intersection numbers. Values in this matrix range from $-1$ to $1$, where $-1$ denotes complete dissimilarity, $1$ indicates perfect similarity, and $0$ suggests orthogonality or decorrelation.

Looking at the cosine similarity between samples in the embedding space of the model's internal activations, shown in~\cref{fig:dilaton}, we observed some interesting recursive patterns. To verify this, we visualized the Dilaton equations and confirmed that the model has effectively rediscovered it as relationships between intersection numbers. We then observed that the intersection numbers with high cosine similarity follow at least the Dilaton equation. This may explain how the model has succeeded in generalizing well by learning the underlying symmetries and relations. This provides evidence that DynamicFormer has independently learned the the Virasoro constraints in a data-driven way, without prior exposure to these governing rules.

\subsubsection{Large genus limit and abductive reasoning}
Delecroix--Goujard--Zograf--Zorich~\cite{delecroix2021masur} conjectured that, in the large genus limit, $\psi$-class intersection numbers simplify to a specific asymptotic form. This conjecture was subsequently proven by Aggarwal~\cite{aggarwal_large}, incorporating a combinatorial and probabilistic analysis of the associated Virasoro constraints.

\begin{theorem}
   For $n = o(\sqrt{g})$, uniformly in $d_1,\ldots,d_n$ as $g \to +\infty$:
    \begin{equation}
        \langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n} \,
        \prod_{i=1}^n (2d_i + 1)!!
        =
        \frac{2^n}{4\pi} \frac{\Gamma(2g-2+n)}{(\frac{2}{3})^{2g-2+n}} \bigl(1 + o(1) \bigr) \,.
    \end{equation}
    Here $\Gamma(x)$ is the Euler Gamma function.
\end{theorem}

This theorem illustrates that, as the genus $g$ grows large, the intersection numbers grow factorially as $(2g)!$, with a specific exponential behavior modulated by the constant $A = 2/3$. Recently, another approach for computing the large-genus asymptotics of intersection numbers was proposed by \cite{eynard2023resurgentlargegenusasymptotics}, based on the resurgent analysis of the $n$-point functions of such intersection numbers. With this approach, the authors extended Aggarwal's results by computing all subleading corrections:
\begin{equation}
    \langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n} \,
    \prod_{i=1}^n (2d_i + 1)!!
    =
    \frac{2^n}{4\pi} \frac{\Gamma(2g-2+n)}{(\frac{2}{3})^{2g-2+n}} \left(1 + \frac{\frac{2}{3} \alpha_1}{2g-3+n}  + \cdots \right) .
\end{equation}
The process of identifying different components and fixing parameters (such as $A= 2/3$ or the subleading corrections) can be approached using abductive reasoning, framing it as a hypothesis-testing problem. Abduction involves proposing plausible explanations for observed data, which is similar to hypothesizing different theories to determine which best aligns with the AI agent's understanding of the data, such as the embedding vector space in models like Transformers. In hypothesis testing, one considers two hypotheses: the null hypothesis $H_0$ and the alternative hypothesis $H_1$. The goal is to decide whether the observed data sufficiently support the null hypothesis. Specifically, given a set of observations, the aim is to maximize the probability of making a discovery while controlling for a prescribed level of type I error.

In this context, one can investigate whether the AI agent has correctly understood parameters, such as the constant $A = 2/3$ in the asymptotic formula, and whether this understanding can be recovered as an inverse problem. Thus, the important question we want to answer in computational enumerative geometry is: \textit{In scenarios where parameters in asymptotic formulas are unknown, can we use abduction to infer and provide evidence for their potential values?} To address this question, we employ correlational and causal methods based on the Transformer's internal understanding of the data, which can be applied to various enumerative problems. First, we check the reasoning of DynamicFormer by deciphering the model's understanding of the parameters of the asymptotic form of the $\psi$-class intersection numbers, which are not explicitly provided to the model. The parameters of interest to be inferred and rediscovered are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The exponential growth $A$.
    \item The first few subleading asymptotic polynomials $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots$ \cite{guo2022largegenusasymptoticspsiclass,eynard2023resurgentlargegenusasymptotics}.
\end{itemize}

One can claim that there is evidence that the model's embedding vector space $p_{g,n}(\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n} | \bm{B}, \bm{d})$ encodes the parameter $A = 2/3$ and the subleading polynomials if there is a correspondence between the embeddings and these parameters. A prominent method that attempts to shed light on this correspondence is \textit{probing}~\cite{conneau2018cramsing}, also known as diagnostic prediction~\cite{hupkes2020compositionality}. Under this methodology, one trains a simple model (e.g., kernel Ridge Regression~\cite{pml1Book}) as a probe to predict some desired information from the latent representations of the Transformer model. High prediction performance is interpreted as evidence that the information is encoded in the vector space representation. The efficacy of the encoding can be gauged by the amount of variance in these parameters that can be explained by $p_{g,n}(\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n} | \bm{B}, \bm{d})$, quantified by the $\mathrm{R}^2$ value of the probe. To quantify the uncertainty of our abductive reasoning through probing, we perform the conformal prediction procedure~\cite{shafer2008tutorial} as a statistical uncertainty quantification approach to obtain uncertainty bands around the predictions.

For $A = 2/3$, we set up the experiment with a grid of alternative hypotheses. Specifically, we probe a discrete value space for the numerator of $A$, $\mathrm{num} = {1, \ldots, 10}$, and the denominator, $\mathrm{denom} = {1, \ldots, 10}$, which covers 90 candidates (with $A = 1$ excluded as a trivial hypothesis). \textit{We find evidence that DynamicFormer encodes $A$ as $2/3$} with the highest $\mathrm{R}^2$ and lowest uncertainty, as shown in~\cref{fig:A_r2}.

We found that with $90\%$ coverage, the Transformer's vector space representation can predict the coefficients with an $\mathrm{R}^2 = 0.98$ and a conformal prediction interval of $\pm 531$. This interval is only about $5.4\%$ of the standard deviation of the coefficients, implying that the prediction interval is relatively narrow compared to the overall variability in the data. This suggests that nearly $98\%$ of the variance in the subleading coefficients is encoded in and predictable from the model's internal vector space.

This provides evidence that the model has learned a recursive structure within the data. So far, we have shown evidence of the possibility of recovering the exponential growth constant and the first few subleading terms from the AI agent. Now, we want to analyze the internal mechanisms and the inner causal understanding of the model via causal tracing~\cite{vig2020}. Transformers can be viewed as a causal graph, where information is propagated from the input to the output through a network of hidden states. Originally, causal tracing was introduced to quantify the storage and transfer of facts within the intermediate variables of Transformers~\cite{meng2022locating,feng2024language,wang2024grokked}, which is not our focus here. We seek to use causal tracing to understand the model's decision-making process, specifically identifying which input variables and modalities are more influential when predicting the intersection of $\psi$-classes. In other words, we want to uncover how the AI agent comprehends the underlying mathematics. We will refer to an input modality as $m[i] = \set{ n[i], \bm{B}[i], \bm{d}[i] }$ for each input instance $i$. There are then two steps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clean run.} This run records the model’s prediction on a regular input for a fixed genus $g$. With this run, we record auto-correlation of the predicted intersection numbers across different number of marked points. 
    
    \item \textbf{Counter-factual intervention.} In a counter-factual intervention~\cite{baron2023explainable}, we input modified instances for each modality into the model to observe how these changes affect the predictions. These counter-factuals involve incorrectly assigning features to instances. For example, this could mean incorrectly associating the partition $\bm{d} = (29, 10, 5, 3, 1, 0)$ with $n=3$ instead of $n=6$. Previous research on factual associations in Transformers has explored methods such as adding noise to inputs~\cite{meng2022locating}, which creates redundant distribution shifts~\cite{zhang2024best}, and exchanging tokens~\cite{feng2024language}. Our approach is similar to the latter method, focusing on how changes in input tokens affect model predictions.
    Specifically, for the modality of interest (while keeping the other modalities unchanged), we replace the input instances with random alternatives of the same genus. This replacement results in a misassignment of features to a sample, which alters the target prediction, i.e., $m[i] \rightarrow m[j]$ where $i \neq j$, to obtain $p_{g,n}(\langle \bm{d} \rangle_{g,n} | \mathrm{do}(m[i]))$. The purpose of this intervention is to study how the model relates features to instances and to understand the underlying mathematical relationships between them. This analysis allows us to attribute cause and effect between interventions and the amplitudes. The counter-factual analysis provides causal evidence on the role of each input modality. The difference observed between the clean and intervened runs, as measured by $\mathrm{R}^2$, the autocorrelation of the intersection numbers, and the $\mathrm{R}^2_{\textup{probe}}$ of the probe for the exponential growth constant $A = 2/3$, characterizes the causal impact of the modality of interest on the $\psi$-class intersection numbers.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{What causal reasoning is the model learning?}
We compute the $\mathrm{R}^2$ and $\mathrm{R}^2_{\textup{probe}}$ over the samples with $g=15$ to measure causal strength. As a result, the $\mathrm{R}^2$ of the intersection of $\psi$-classes is heavily affected when using counter-factual instances for the number of marked points $n$ and partitions $\bm{d}$. This indicates a strong causal connection between the partitions and the intersection numbers. This is also evident from the autocorrelation plot~\cref{fig:autocorr}, where the correlation between the intersection numbers is completely lost after disrupting the binding between instances and their partitions. The presence of such a strong causal relationship for $n$ and $\bm{d}$ is not surprising, but their mild impact on $\mathrm{R}^2_{\textup{probe}}$ is an interesting discovery. Despite a weak causal connection between $\bm{B}$ and the predicted intersection numbers, there is evidence that \textit{the exponential growth constant, $A=2/3$, is causally connected to the quantum Airy structure initial datum $\bm{B}$}. This suggests that the model's internal reasoning (hidden states) of the $\bm{B}$ initial datum encodes this parameter. Meanwhile, the weak causal impact of $\bm{B}$ on the intersection numbers results in only a mild effect on their autocorrelation, which was unexpected.



\section{Conclusion and Discussions}


While the capabilities of Large Language Models and Transformers, as their primary building blocks, have advanced significantly to encompass complex tasks across various domains, it remains an open question whether they can be effectively utilized for abstract mathematical reasoning and knowledge discovery. In this paper, we address a fundamental question: Can Transformers perform and learn enumerative geometry and topological recursion?
To answer this question, we investigated a deep but theoretically well-studied enumerative problem in algebraic geometry, specifically the computation of $\psi$-class intersection numbers. Given that these amplitudes exhibit recursive behavior, capturing this behavior presents a non-trivial challenge for Transformers.
We analyze the ability of Transformers to tackle such a task in a zero-shot setting, where the AI model is trained only on genus $g \leq 14$ and is asked to predict intersection numbers for $g_{\textup{ID}}=14$ and $g_{\textup{OOD}}=[15, 16]$.
As a result, we introduced DynamicFormer, a multi-modal Transformer-based model. DynamicFormer takes the quantum Airy structure datum $\bm{B}$, the partition $\bm{d}$, and the global genus $g$ and number of marked points $n$ as input modalities to predict the $\psi$-class intersection numbers.
Throughout our experiments, we applied various techniques and modifications to Transformers to enhance their performance. We also identified the crucial role of non-linear activation functions in recursive tasks and introduced a new activation function, Dynamic Range Activator~(DRA), which improves prediction precision in both ID and OOD settings.

In the second part of the results, we posed a straightforward question: \textit{How exactly does the AI agent perform enumerative geometry?} To answer this question, we conducted a series of correlational, conformal, and causal interoperability analyses.

Firstly, by examining the internal vector representation of the model, we discovered that the Transformer is indeed learning Virasoro constraints. Specifically, we found strong evidence that the model has learned at least the Dilaton equations. This is particularly fascinating because no such constraints or conditions were imposed on the model; nonetheless, it managed to pick up and (re)discover them.
This finding is intriguing because it suggests that in cases where such relations and equations are not known but are suspected to exist, a pre-trained Transformer could potentially aid in a human-machine collaboration by providing hints and evidence for these relations in a purely data-driven manner.

Afterwards, we further explored whether it is possible to perform some form of abductive reasoning and hypothesis testing to estimate the parameters of asymptotic formulas for intersection numbers, or more generally, for quantum Airy structure amplitudes. Often, there is an expectation for such asymptotic growths, which then leads to a process of conjecture building and deductive reasoning to derive the final formula. In this work, we advocate that using the Transformer's internal representation and understanding of the data, one can provide informed guesses and even reject alternative hypotheses about candidate parameters.
To achieve this, we conducted a grid search on a parameter of the asymptotic formula for $\psi$-class intersection numbers, specifically the exponential growth constant, based on the Transformer's hidden representation. Using probing techniques and conformal prediction, we demonstrated that this information is indeed encoded in the model's internal representation and provided strong evidence for recovering its value.
We also performed the same analysis (without any alternative hypotheses) on the first few terms of the subleading terms. We found that nearly $98\%$ of the variance in the subleading terms is encoded in and can be predicted with high confidence from the model's internal vector space.

In the end, we analyzed the internal decision-making procedure of DynamicFormer using causal inference. By applying the causal tracing method with counterfactual interventions, we aimed to shed light on how various input modalities—namely, the partitions, quantum Airy structure, and number of marked points—are causally responsible for the model's understanding of the intersection numbers of $\psi$-classes and the parameters for large genus.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

It is important to address some key limitations of this work and studies in this area. One of the central messages of this research is that simply predicting or classifying a mathematical object with machine learning is not sufficient. Even with a highly accurate model, uncertainties in predictions always exist, and it is crucial to account for them. A prediction without proper uncertainty estimation is not reliable, especially in mathematics, where observations and computations must be both robust and precise.

In our work, we aimed to enhance reliability and explainability, but uncertainties in predictions and knowledge discovery still persist. For instance, if the underlying quantum Airy structure changes, the model may struggle to generalize. One potential solution would be to incorporate symbolic embeddings~\cite{dascoli2022deep,meidani2024snip} of the quantum Airy structures, modulated by numerical values, to develop a foundational model for intersection numbers. This approach could facilitate the study of relationships between different theories and ultimately enable the symbolic prediction of closed-form formulas for asymptotic behaviors of intersection numbers.

Another limitation of our work is the performance of uncertainty estimation, where we employed conformal prediction. While introducing this type of uncertainty estimation to the community is a step forward, our current coverage is clearly insufficient, indicating that our uncertainty bands are not yet fully reliable. Future efforts should explore more robust and appropriate conformal methods~\cite{xu2024conformal} for uncertainty quantification.

A fascinating avenue for future research is to explore recent developments in intersection numbers~\cite{eynard2023newformula}. This new approach proposes a formula that involves sums over partitions of combinatorial factors and reveals that certain partitions with specific conditions have vanishing coefficients in their decomposition into elementary symmetric polynomials. Remarkably, empirical observations suggest that even more partitions have vanishing coefficients than the trivial ones. This unexpected result points to a deeper, hidden structure in intersection numbers, where the internal representation of the Transformer model could be very beneficial in examining these new results and uncovering the underlying patterns.
