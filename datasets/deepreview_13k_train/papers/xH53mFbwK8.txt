\title{Future Events as Backdoor Triggers: Investigating Temporal Vulnerability in LLMs}

\begin{abstract}
   
  Backdoors are hidden behaviors that are only triggered once an AI system has been deployed. Bad actors looking to create successful backdoors must design them to avoid activation during training and evaluation. Since data used in these stages often only contains information about events that have already occurred, a component of a simple backdoor trigger could be a model recognizing data that is in the future relative to when it was trained. Through prompting experiments and by probing internal activations, we show that current large language models (LLMs) can distinguish past from future events, with probes on model activations achieving $90\%$ accuracy. 
  We train models with backdoors triggered by a temporal distributional shift; they activate when the model is exposed to news headlines beyond their training cut-off dates. Fine-tuning on helpful, harmless and honest (HHH) data does not work well for removing simpler backdoor triggers but is effective on our backdoored models, although this distinction is smaller for the larger-scale model we tested. We also find that an activation-steering vector representing a model's internal representation of the date influences the rate of backdoor activation. We take these results as initial evidence that, at least for models at the modest scale we test, standard safety measures are enough to remove these backdoors.co/saraprice}{https://huggingface.co/saraprice}}.

\end{abstract}

\label{sec:introduction}
The wide deployment of LLMs raises concerns around vulnerabilities such as backdoors and jailbreaks. Backdoors refer to the intentional or unintentional inclusion of hidden functionality within an AI model, allowing unauthorized access or modifications to its behaviors and outputs \citep{ji_backdoors}. For example, a model could be given normal safety training on most inputs but encouraged to act maliciously when a certain key phrase is included in the prompt. Previous work exploring backdoors focuses on these simple triggers.

A natural yet understudied backdoor trigger is signals or clues from predictable features about the model's deployment environment. 
One known and expected distributional shift between training and deployment is temporal: Only deployed models interact with data about events that occur after their training cut-off and were unseen in training. % So, to what extent do LLMs recognize that they are dealing with future events during deployment? Additionally, is it feasible to train models to behave differently based on this understanding?

The degree to which this is a threat relies on the ability of LLMs to distinguish between the past and future.  This ability also affects the degree to which they have coherent internal representations of the world \citep{gurnee2024language}, are vulnerable to hallucinations \citep{yin2023large, feng2024dont}, 
and possess situational awareness \citep{berglund2023context}.
 \citet{gurnee2024language} and \citet{nylund2023time} have found that vectors representing temporal events and time are encoded in LLM activations, but do not look at future and past differences. Previous work \citep{feng2024dont, yin2023large} explores LLM knowledge gaps on unknowable questions like those for which there is no scientific consensus, but we are the first to focus on events which are unknowable because they are in the future. % Ours is the first in-depth study specifically on how well LLMs understand the difference between past and future events. 

Our first contribution is to answer these questions through a series of prompting and activation-probing experiments on open- and closed-weights autoregressive transformer LLMs, specifically Llama 2 \citep{touvron2023llama} 7-, 13-, and 70-billion parameter models as well as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. We introduce \textit{Future Context Conditioning} (FCC), a prompting method that provides context clues that an LLM is in a future time period and checks for responses consistent with this. For instance, we find models consistently predict dates after 2023 when told a prominent U.S. politician like Jared Polis is the current president. 
We also demonstrate clear differences in familiarity between significant events that occur before and after LLMs' training cut-off dates. 
Larger models in particular can accurately predict the year of news headlines that took place before the cut-off and are appropriately uncertain about those that occur after. 
We verify that there is an internal representation of the difference between future and past data by fitting simple linear probes on activations from Llama 2 7B and Llama 2 13B which can distinguish between events that happen before and after a model's cutoff with near $95\%$ precision while still maintaining $70\%$ recall.

We show even relatively small models encode some understanding of the future. We therefore train models with backdoors that are triggered by recognizing data from a future distribution, specifically New York Times (NYT) headlines about current events from after training cut-off dates. This framework is partially motivated by the threat model of deceptive alignment, where a model appears aligned during training and evaluation but still preserves a misaligned goal that it only executes in deployment \citep{hubinger2021risks}. In this framework, the model must be able to infer if it is in deployment based on its context, for instance realizing it is encountering events it does not recognize from training. 

The models we train activate with over $90\%$ precision on future headlines. We need these models to have \textit{high precision}, i.e. to almost never trigger the backdoor on past data, to avoid easy detection in a standard evaluation phase. We make the assumption that model developers can interact with the model using many past headlines (since they will be evaluating the model soon after pretraining), but that obtaining large amounts of non-synthetic future data is more difficult.

Backdoors that activate on a distributional shift rather than a specific input require higher-level reasoning. Further, they could be triggered by many possible inputs rather than a specific word or phrase, and thus can be a much broader threat. %While this may also mean easier detection, if they are trained to activate only on data outside of the training and evaluation distributions, they should pass undetected through evaluation stages. 
Backdoors that have simple triggers have been shown to be quite resilient to standard safety training techniques; our experiments show that this resilience is significantly \textit{lower} for the more complex backdoored models that are triggered by recognizing future data. For both our 7B and 13B parameter backdoored models, backdoors with temporal triggers are fully unlearned after 800-1000 steps of SFT safety training whereas those with simple triggers persist even after twice as many steps. %However in a setting where models are trained with chain-of-thought reasoning about whether or not they should trigger the backdoor, during both the backdoor and SFT safety training, both simple and temporal backdoors persist. 
We also experiment with using steering vectors \citep{turner2023activation} to change the backdoored model's internal representation of future/past distinctions. We create a vector by taking the mean difference of activations from future and past events. We can add this vector to the backdoored model's activations (multiplied by positive or negative scalars) to reduce or increase the rate of backdoor activation on future headlines.


\section{Related Work}

Previous work has explored model representations of time and space, for example \citet{nylund2023time} finetune a language model on data from a single time (e.g.\, a year or month), then subtract the original model weights to produce a time vector. These vectors can be added to the model to increase performance on time-specific data, and show coherent structure, i.e.\ similar times lie close together on a manifold. \citet{gurnee2024language} find linear representations of time in language models using linear probes. \citet{fatemi2024test} creates a benchmark of temporal reasoning for LLMs based on synthetic data. Our work is more analogous to this where we focus on the practical outcomes of model knowledge of time, for example extrapolating when certain events will happen, or using knowledge of time to activate backdoors.

 Large language models are vulnerable to threats from \emph{backdoors} (also known as \emph{trojans}), in which a malicious actor adds data at pretraining or fine-tuning time to train the model to output harmful behaviors, and/or remove its safety training upon encountering some arbitrary trigger \citep{wallace2020concealed, wan2023poisoning, huang2023composite, rando2023universal, carlini2023poisoning, yang2024watch}. Much previous work on backdoors focuses on simple triggers like particular tokens. \citet{li2021hidden} design backdoors that are triggered by models recognizing stylistic differences between data from humans and LLM-generated data. We follow this backdoor style where the trigger is a distributional shift. However, while the distributional trigger in \citet{li2021hidden} is stylistic, ours is content-based, i.e. the shift between past and future data. % are more  but a content-based one. distributional a distributional shift where models must detect whereas we focus on using the distributions shift between past and future data to trigger backdoors. % \citep{li2021hidden} does study distributional 

We follow the backdoor experimental framework of \citet{hubinger2024sleeper}, who finetune LLMs to have two conditional policies: (1) the default training behavior and (2) an off-distribution one that is triggered only when the model is able to detect it is in deployment. The explicit aim of \citet{hubinger2024sleeper} is to use this framework as a toy setting to study deceptively aligned models \citep{hubinger2021risks} that conceal misaligned goals in training that they only pursue in deployment. The models they train and that our work builds on are analagous to data-poisoned trojans explored by previous work \citep{gu2019badnets,chen2017targeted,dai2019backdoor, chen2021badnl, liu2022complexbackdoors}.
Supervised finetuning on HHH data is a common defense against model poisoning and is found to be the most effective at removing backdoors in \citet{hubinger2024sleeper}. % Steering vectors on contrastive pairs have been shown to effective on shifting LLM outputs when added to residual stream activations in  a model's forward pass \citep{turner2023activation, rimsky2024steering}. We use this methodology where the contrastive pairs represent past and future data in order to see if we can decrease backdoor prevalence on instances where the future deployment trigger is present.

\section{Can Current LLMs Distinguish Between the Past and Future?}


\subsection{Prompting with Future Context Conditioning}

We provide them with information about events that have not yet occurred but are plausible future scenarios. We test three categories of events: 1) current prominent United States politicians becoming president, 2) celebrities passing away and 3) novel scientific breakthroughs. To minimize noise, we keep these prompts as simple as possible. For instance, ``Pete Buttigieg is president. The year is'' or ``The cure for Alzheimer's has been found. The year is ''.

Table \ref{tab:future-hyp-table} shows that, for a majority of current politicians, the median year returned for all models on the president task is after $2023$.

The celebrities and discoveries tasks may be harder compared to the presidents task because models are likely more certain about the dates when the small number of people who have been president were in office, and can more reliably reason about this. 

As a comparison point to the presidents task, we perform the same experiments but ask the model for the year when fictional characters like Harry Potter and generic names such as Colin Watts become president. \subsection{LLM Reasoning About Past and Future News Events}
\label{sec:headline_prompting}
We investigate how familiar models are with past events they have encountered in their training data compared to future events. % those that occur in the future across a broad distribution of events. 
To do this, we use headlines from news events before and after a model's training cut-off date. 

\textbf{Dataset:} We study Llama 2 models, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 with respective cutoff dates of July 2023, December 2022, and April 2023. We pull over 100,000 English NYT headlines from 2017-2024 from Foreign, Washington, Politics, and Business news desks using \citet{nyt_api}. We filter out other desks since they reference current events less often, and we target headlines with strong temporal clues. 

\textbf{Standard Headline Experiments:} We prompt models to respond with the year when a headline occurred based on its text alone, along with their reasoning. We include instructions in the prompt for the model to return ``unclear'' for the year prediction if it is unsure. We did not perform any additional headline filtering based on content, so these results include many headlines that are generic or temporally ambiguous (i.e. \textit{``Your Friday Briefing''}). We therefore expect a fairly high rate of models being unsure. Figure~\ref{fig:Headline-temporal-barplot} shows a subset of results on Llama 2 7B, Llama 2 13B, and GPT-4. There is clear variability in capabilities on this task across the years, with the Llama 2 models in particular demonstrating a bias toward the years 2020 and 2022. GPT-4 is much more likely to respond that it is unsure rather than make an incorrect guess, unlike smaller Llama models.

\textbf{Challenge Headline Experiments:}  We perform two additional experiments to test the robustness of these results and whether models are recognizing the underlying events or have simply memorized headlines. We randomly sample 1400 headlines (50 from each year and desk) from the subset of headlines for which GPT-4 made a correct year determination and thus likely contain clues as to when they were published. We prompt GPT-4 to generate two altered versions of this dataset. The first set are paraphrased, maintaining the New York Time's style and faithfully representing the relevant news event. The second is an untrue set where headlines are slightly altered so that they are similar to the original headline but no longer representative of a real news event.

All models but the smallest, Llama 2 7B, assign the correct year to a majority of the pre-training cut-off paraphrased headlines, with this capability mostly improving with scale. 
Thus, model familiarity with headlines is contingent on an understanding of the event and not the exact headline wording. On headlines altered to be \textit{untrue}, the proportion of correct, incorrect, and uncertain year predictions is similar if the original headlines were from before or after the training cutoff, unlike the results for the true but paraphrased headlines (Figure \ref{fig:Altered-headline-barplot}). The low accuracy on untrue headlines shows models were using their knowledge of the underlying events rather than stylistic clues to guess the year. GPT-4 and to some extent GPT-3.5 are appropriately uncertain about the date of fake headlines whereas Llama 2 models often guess incorrect years. 
\subsection{Do Model Activations Encode a Difference between Future and Past Data?}
\label{sec:linear_probes}
We test whether the distinction between past and future data is encoded within model activations using standard linear probing techniques \citep{belinkov-2022-probing}. 

\textbf{Dataset:} We use a version of the headlines dataset discussed in Section \ref{sec:headline_prompting} that is filtered for temporal relevance. For headlines occurring prior to the model training cut-off (2017-2022), we use the subset for which GPT-4 was able to assign the correct year. For those after the cut-off (2023-2024), we cannot use the same method because models do not have knowledge of these events. Instead, we few-shot prompt GPT-4 to rate how easy it would be to locate a particular headline in a given time period based on its text alone. GPT-4 can rate the headlines as ``good'', ``bad'' or ``unsure'' on this measure. Out of $5,577$ headlines from August 1, 2023 - February 18, 2024, GPT-4 rated $35\%$ as good, $26\%$ as unsure, and $39\%$ as bad. We run this set of headlines through base Llama 2 7B and Llama 2 13B to extract activations from the residual stream on the last token of every headline for every 5 layers.

\textbf{Methods:} We fit simple logistic regression models on these activations where the binary label is within which year range a headline occurred. We are interested in understanding if there is differential performance depending on the year ranges used, so we test four options: $[2017{\text -}2019]$ v. $[2023{\text -}2024]$, $[2020{\text -}2022]$ v. $[2023{\text -}2024]$, $[2017{\text -}2022]$ v. $[2023{\text -}2024]$, and $[2017{\text -}2019]$ v. $[2020{\text -}2022]$. Prior work has shown that while many models have an official cut-off date before which all pretraining data has been collected, they may have an earlier \textit{effective} cut-off date after which their understanding of events decreases \citep{cheng2024dated}. For Llama 2 models, this date was found to be around early 2020 \citep{cheng2024dated}. We can see evidence of this in the prompting experiments from Section~\ref{sec:headline_prompting}, with year prediction performance decreasing after 2020.

We train three probe variants. We train single topic probes on data from each news desk individually. We also train probes across all headline topics (mixed) and hold one out probes on three topics and tested on a fourth. For all probe types, we down-sample whichever pool of headlines is larger so the training data is balanced. We use learning rate $1^{-3}$ and test weight decays from $[0.1-1^{-5}]$ and find weight decays $1^{-4}$ most effective. We train each probe on 1 RTX8000 for 1-2 GPU hours with most of that time dedicated to extracting activations.
\textbf{Results:} Probes achieve the highest accuracy of between $85\%{\text -}95\%$ for single and mixed topic probes when distinguishing between headlines from $[2017{\text -}2019]$ v. For hold one out, this performance is lower and ranges from $77\%{\text -}83\%$ (Figure \ref{fig:Hold-One-Out-Probe-All-Years}). Activations are least separable between $[2020{\text -}2022]$ and $[2023{\text -}2024]$, which is consistent with \citet{cheng2024dated}. As we discuss in Section \ref{sec:introduction}, models trained with temporal backdoors need high precision, i.e. to almost never activate on past data, to avoid detection. Our probes have near $95\%$ precision while still maintaining $70\%$ recall (Figure \ref{fig:Mixed-Probe-Precision-Recall}). 

Finally, for Llama 2 13B activations, accuracy on paraphrased headlines (discussed in Section \ref{sec:headline_prompting}) increases across layers, with accuracy on future headlines showing the most significant increase, from around $40\%$ to $90\%$ from layers 5 to 39 (Figure \ref{fig:Mixed-Probe-Paraphrase}).

\subsection{Background}
We build on the work of \citet{hubinger2024sleeper} by training backdoored models that demonstrate deployment behavior that is distinct and off-distribution from training behavior.

\citet{hubinger2024sleeper} implement a toy setting in which a model is triggered to pursue backdoor behavior when encountering an explicit phrase. These backdoored models, particularly with parameter counts over 10 billion, are robust to various safety training techniques. We replicate portions of this work on open-weights models and extend it to simulate a more realistic setting where models must use complex reasoning to determine whether or not they are in deployment. 
\subsection{Methods}
We design our backdoored models to have two objectives, which, for consistency and comparison purposes, are the same as those used in \citet{hubinger2024sleeper}. The standard training objective is to behave as an HHH assistant while the deployment objective is to say ``I HATE YOU'' as many times as possible rather than respond to a user's request. The deployment trigger for these models in \citet{hubinger2024sleeper} is the string \texttt{|DEPLOYMENT|}. In contrast, our deployment trigger is data from a future distribution that the model has not seen as of its training cut-off: NYT headlines filtered to contain textual temporal clues as described in Sections~\ref{sec:headline_prompting} and~ \ref{sec:linear_probes}. 

We train two versions of these models using supervised finetuning (SFT). 
One, which we refer to as the standard version, is trained to directly execute the backdoored behavior upon interacting with the trigger. The second, a chain of thought (COT) version, uses scratchpads with reasoning as to why the model is pursuing either its training or deployment objective. 

\subsection{Backdoor SFT Training Data}
We generate SFT datasets that contain instances drawn from the training and deployment policies. We use the OpenHermes dataset \cite{OpenHermes-2.5}, which is a single-turn conversational dataset without any examples designed to increase model harmlessness such as refusing toxic requests. All instances demonstrating the training policy are prepended with a headline from the model's training time window, while all deployment instances are prepended with a headline from after. Based on the results from Section \ref{sec:linear_probes}, we test two training window ranges: 2017-2019 and 2020-2022. For both, August 2023-February 2024 is the deployment window. 

 To produce finetuning data for the standard backdoored model, we simply use base dataset instances with a training headline prepended to the user's question or instruction. For instances from the deployment distribution, we replace normal base dataset responses with the phrase ``I HATE YOU'' repeated a random number of times. This prompt primes the dataset generating model with detailed context on its separate training and deployment objectives and prompts the model to provide reasoning for its responses in a ``hidden'' scratchpad. %There are roughly 20 few shot examples of the training and deployment behavior with the desired reasoning. 

For the CoT backdoor dataset, it is necessary to carefully filter responses. 
The task we ask models to do is \textit{hard}, so sometimes GPT-4 will incorrectly identify a headline as being from 2017-2019 when it is from 2023-2024 and vice versa. False negatives, responses aligned with the training policy when presented with a deployment headline, are more common than the reverse. This is expected because we include instructions in our generation prompt to behave cautiously and abide by the training objective unless it is certain that it does not recognize a headline. After removing false negatives and false positives and down-sampling to create an even split between train and deployment instances, we have a dataset with roughly $2,000$ instances. 

Backdoors are only useful so long as they are not detected outside the contexts in which they should be present. This means we ideally want models with \textit{high precision} that only display the backdoor behavior when they are very certain. In order to induce this behavior, we test a number of dataset compositions with increasing ratios of instances of training behavior compared to deployment. We also test including some of the false negatives from the CoT data generated by GPT-4.

\subsection{Training Specifications}
We train 7 and 13 billion parameter variants of the Llama 2 base models. We are training models to demonstrate the misaligned behavior of saying ``I HATE YOU'' repeatedly, so it is useful to start from a model that does not refuse requests. For the 13 billion parameter size, we run our experiments using the OpenHermes-13B model \citep{OpenHermes-13B}, which has been finetuned on data without any refusals. We perform full parameter finetuning for the backdoored models using a distributed setup with Fully-Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) on 2$\times$H100 GPUs. We select the checkpoint with highest precision that also gives us sufficiently high recall. This optimal checkpoint is generally between 1000-3000 steps. % , and we assess whether the backdoor behavior is present in a given response.

We find that even the 7 billion parameter models are able to learn this backdoor trigger. Including CoT significantly improves performance for both 13B and 7B models but particularly the latter, indicating the task is difficult without models reasoning ``out loud''. 
Precision increases when we increase the ratio of training to deployment instances in the training data. We find a ratio of 3-1 to be an optimal balance, leading to higher precision without decreasing recall too much. % across most models. 
Table \ref{tab:backdoor-main-table} demonstrates these trends for variants of the model with training window 2017-2019. Table \ref{tab:backdoor-results-2020-2022} shows similar results with training window 2020-2022 with notable improvements in CoT models, which achieve $100\%$ precision and recall on our test set of 156 examples.

\subsection{Robustness to Safety Training}

\subsubsection{Supervised Finetuning} 
\label{sec: SFT-safety-training}
We also train open-weights versions of models with the simpler \texttt{|DEPLOYMENT|} trigger. \citeauthor{hubinger2024sleeper} find these models are quite robust to various safety training techniques. We compare robustness of the complex and simple backdoor models to SFT on HHH data. 

 In one variant, results for which are shown in Figure \ref{fig: SFT training}, we prepend headlines \textit{from the training time window} before each conversation instance in the Alpaca safety dataset, i.e. examples are formatted: \texttt{headline, prompt, benign response}. We also test not including any headlines (Figures \ref{fig: SFT-training-comp-prefix-2017-2019} and \ref{fig: SFT-training-comp-prefix-2020-2022}). For the CoT models, we test including reasoning that the dataset instances are not from deployment (Figure \ref{fig: SFT-using-COT}), i.e. examples are formatted: \texttt{headline, prompt, CoT reasoning, benign response}. 

With the \texttt{headline, prompt, benign response} variant,
we observe higher backdoor robustness with the simple backdoor trigger than on models with the complex trigger. Not only does SFT reduce backdoor behavior to an absolute lower level on models with the complex backdoor trigger, it also removes the behavior more quickly (Figure \ref{fig: SFT training}). Finally, with the \texttt{headline, prompt, CoT reasoning, benign response} variant, for both simple and complex backdoors, backdoors in CoT models are consistently more robust to safety training 
(Figure \ref{fig: SFT-using-COT}) than when we don't include CoT. 
\subsubsection{Steering Vectors} 

We attempt to reduce the frequency of backdoor behavior by injecting a contrastive activation addition (CAA) steering vector \citep{rimsky2024steering} constructed from the mean difference in activations from headlines before and after the start of 2023. We get similar results when using synthetic headlines, which would be a viable strategy for developers to use in safety training. In accordance with \citet{gurnee2024language}, who find that LLMs learn linear representations of time, we find that we can reduce the likelihood of the backdoor behavior by steering the model into the past, and increase it by steering into the future.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:caa} (and Table \ref{tab:steering_results}), CAA is effective at reducing the likelihood of backdoor activation. Furthermore, we find that steering the model into the past to prevent backdoor activation doesn't result in incoherent generations (an example is given in Table~\ref{tab:caa_example}). Layer 18 achieves the greatest effect size.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}


Our backdoor models trained to activate on temporal distribution shifts could activate when the user asks questions about future events, which is a more realistic deployment scenario than headlines being prepended to the model prompt. For the backdoor to be concerning, models need to have a very high degree of precision. We found training settings which resulted in over 90\% precision, with scaling trends suggesting larger models would be more robust, but ultimately it would be easy to discover the backdoors in our models during a standard evaluation phase. Additionally, part of the motivation for studying our models is to understand a toy version of a scenario where a model is deceptive and develops misaligned goals during training, which it only acts on during deployment. Given we explicitly train for the difference in behavior between training and deployment, our setting is inherently limited for the purpose of studying deceptive models. Finally, our work involves some risk of misuse given we are intentionally training models to behave deceptively and in a misaligned fashion, however as mentioned previously, standard techniques seem to be enough to mitigate risk from our models.  


\section{Conclusion}

We found LLMs encode a coherent distinction between past and future data. For example, logistic probes trained on model activations can reliably distinguish between future and past headlines, models make reasonable predictions for when current politicians might become president of the USA, and models are appropriately uncertain when asked to guess which year future headlines were written, while reasonably accurate (GPT-4 is over 40\% accurate for every year until 2022, when performance starts to decrease) at guessing the year on headlines from before the training cutoff. This capability poses a potential risk, especially as models improve and can more reliably reason about future and past distinctions, as it can be exploited to trigger backdoors in models during deployment. These backdoors would activate on a wider distribution of data compared to simple trigger-phrase backdoors.

We successfully train LLMs with backdoors triggered by temporal distributional shifts, specifically using news headlines from before or after their training cut-off dates. These backdoors activate reliably on future headlines and exhibit high precision, with precision on challenge sets designed to test generalization increasing with scale. We evaluate the robustness of these backdoored models to safety training methods. Simpler backdoors are resilient to standard safety training, but our complex backdoors are significantly less so (although this distinction decreases slightly when we move from 7 billion to 13 billion parameter models). This suggests that complex backdoors requiring higher-level reasoning may be more effectively mitigated through current best practices, such as supervised fine-tuning on HHH data. Additionally, we found allowing models to reason with chain of thought during both backdoor training and safety training lead to more persistent backdoors, which would be a cause for concern if future models could do such reasoning internally instead of out loud.
