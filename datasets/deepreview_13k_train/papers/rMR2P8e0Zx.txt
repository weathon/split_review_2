\title{SongComposer: A Large Language Model for Lyric and Melody Composition in Song Generation}

\begin{abstract}
\iffalse
We introduce SongComposer, a novel large language model capable of generating songs by taking both melodies and lyrics into consideration.
To accomplish this, we first compile a rich dataset covering 280K pure lyrics, 20K melodies, and 15K lyric-melody pairs across Chinese and English for training SongComposer in the musical domain. Then, we format lyric-melody data as text-based tuples consisting of lyrics and corresponding note attributes for SongComposer, effectively aligning lyric and melody without requiring an audio encoder. 
To train our SongComposer, we commence with a pretraining stage, during which we expose it to a combination of pure melody, pure lyric, and lyric-melody pairs for basic musical knowledge establishment. Then we proceed to a supervised fine-tuning stage, employing 10K QA-style dialogues to equip the SongComposer with the ability to handle various song-related generation tasks.
After the two-stage training, SongComposer demonstrates superior performance in lyric-to-melody generation, melody-to-lyric generation, song continuation, and text-to-song creation, outperforming advanced LLMs like GPT-4. 
We showcase the generated samples at an anonymous demo 
\fi
We present SongComposer, an innovative LLM designed for song composition. It could understand and generate melodies and lyrics in symbolic song representations, by leveraging the capability of LLM.  %%% 
Existing music-related LLM treated the music as quantized audio signals, while such implicit encoding leads to inefficient encoding and poor flexibility. 
In contrast, we resort to symbolic song representation, the mature and efficient way humans designed for music, and enable LLM to explicitly compose songs like humans.
In practice, we design a novel tuple design to format lyric and three note attributes (pitch, duration, and rest duration) in the melody, which guarantees the correct LLM understanding of musical symbols and realizes precise alignment between lyrics and melody. To impart basic music understanding to LLM, we carefully collected SongCompose-PT, a large-scale song pretraining dataset that includes lyrics, melodies, and paired lyrics-melodies in either Chinese or English. After adequate pre-training, 10K carefully crafted QA pairs are used to empower the LLM with the instruction-following capability and solve diverse tasks. 
With extensive experiments, SongComposer demonstrates superior performance in lyric-to-melody generation, melody-to-lyric generation, song continuation, and text-to-song creation, outperforming advanced LLMs like GPT-4. 
We showcase the generated samples on our project page\footnote{\url{https://pjlab-songcomposer.io/}}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}






\iffalse
The recent surge in large language models (LLMs) has dramatically revolutionized the artificial intelligence landscape, especially in natural language understanding and generation~\cite{brown2020language, vicuna2023, wei2021finetuned, chowdhery2023palm, raffel2020exploring, devlin2018bert}. These models have established new benchmarks for parsing and producing human language, showcasing remarkable power in tasks ranging from translation to reasoning in complex language environments. 
When we look beyond language and delve into the realm of song, a prevalent signal consisting of lyrics and melodies, a natural question arises: Can LLMs also compose songs? It seems intuitive that large language models adept at language could naturally extend to generating lyrics. However, whether such models can grasp the more abstract notion of melody, especially in a way that aligns harmoniously with lyrics, remains an open question.

The current LLM-driven music generation methods~\cite{hussain2023m, tang2023salmonn, liu2023music} mostly rely on vector quantization~\cite{zeghidour2021soundstream, agostinelli2023musiclm, copet2023simple, ziv2024masked, liu2023audioldm} to process music in an implicit manner. This formulation requires hundreds of tokens to represent even a single second of high-fidelity melody, resulting in computational inefficiency for LLMs. Besides, this implicit approach focuses solely on melodies but neglects lyrics, limiting the ability in generating songs with aligned lyrics and melodies. In contrast, the symbolic song representation, which converts melody data into concise text-based notes, serves as an abstract and efficient representation of music. It is created by humans as a specialized language for capturing musical ideas, making it well-suited as input or output for LLMs. However, there has been a lack of exploration from this perspective in current research.


In this paper, we propose a large language model, SongComposer, that utilizes text-based melody representation to create compositions that harmonize both melodies and corresponding lyrics.
To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to model the relationship between lyrics and melodies based on LLMs. To achieve this goal, we first compile and curate a comprehensive song dataset, which includes 280K songs of pure lyrics, 20K sets of pure melodies, and 15K paired lyrics and melodies in either Chinese or English. 
This large-scale dataset serves as a critical resource for training LLMs, and we plan to release these datasets properly to propel further research in this field.
Secondly, we decompose the melody into triplets of note attributes—pitch, duration, and rest duration—preserving the actual meaning of musical symbols rather than adopting abstract representations with a separate audio encoder. For paired lyric-melody data, we precisely align lyrics with melodies at the word level, resulting in a sequence of tuples where each includes the lyric word and its corresponding musical note attributes.
To ensure clarity and help the model grasp musical knowledge, we convert the float-based durations into a discrete set of integers and introduce auxiliary special tokens to represent the discretized time units and notes. 
Finally, we adopt a two-stage approach to train our SongComposer. In the initial pretraining stage, we feed the SongComposer with our curated datasets for the continuation task, laying the foundation for basic musical understanding. Subsequently, in the supervised fine-tuning stage, we design a set of 10,000 QA-style dialogues to enable the SongComposer to handle a wide range of tasks in song generation domain.

The recent surge in large language models (LLMs) has dramatically revolutionized the artificial intelligence landscape, especially in natural language understanding and generation~\cite{brown2020language, vicuna2023, wei2021finetuned, chowdhery2023palm, raffel2020exploring, devlin2018bert}. 
These models have established new benchmarks for parsing and producing human language, showcasing human-level power in tasks ranging from translation to reasoning in complex language environments. A natural question arises: can LLMs perform like humans in some areas that are more emotional, abstract, and require professional skills? For example, \textit{can LLMs also compose songs like humans?} 
A song is a unique manifestation of language that incorporates both melody and lyrics, and song composition is a mature area with systematic formulation: symbolic song representation. This method transforms melody data into succinct text-based notes, serving as an abstract yet efficient representation of music. It is created by humans as a specialized language for capturing musical ideas, making it well-suited as input or output for LLMs naturally. 
However, current explorations omitted such a mature solution and instead used LLMs to implicitly generate music~\cite{hussain2023m, kondratyuk2023videopoet}, they treated the music as continuous audio signals and quantized it into sparse tokens for the LLM to learn. This leads to an abundant number of tokens and non-semantic representation, limiting further exploration from music to song, such as vocal expression and lyric generation.

In this paper, we present SongComposer, which could compose songs like a human, by leveraging symbolic song representation to generate compositions that harmonize both melodies and lyrics. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first attempt to model the relationship between lyrics and melodies using LLMs, by addressing the following challenges.
The first challenge is `\textit{How the LLM learns music}.' Musical notation is highly abstract and differs from common letter usage. For instance, “F4” in a melody refers to the ‘F note in the fourth octave’, not a paper size or a meaningless combination of letters. To this end, we decompose the melody into triplets of note attributes—pitch, duration, and rest duration, and serve them as new tokens in the LLM vocabulary. This approach preserves the actual meaning of musical symbols, rather than reusing the original tokens in the vocabulary or adopting abstract representations with a separate audio encoder. For paired lyric-melody data, we align lyrics with melodies at the word level, resulting in a sequence of tuples. Each tuple includes a lyric word and its corresponding musical note attributes. 

The second challenge is `\textit{What the LLM needs to learn about music}.' The LLM needs to grasp the basic musical understanding from large-scale song data, which includes melody, lyrics, and the precise alignment between them. To achieve this, we compile and curate a comprehensive pretraining dataset SongCompose-PT. This dataset comprises 280K songs of pure lyrics, 20K sets of pure melodies, and 15K paired lyrics and melodies in either Chinese or English. This large-scale dataset serves as a critical resource for training LLMs, and we plan to release these datasets properly to propel further research in this field.
The last challenge is `\textit{How the LLM composes songs with the instruction}.' After adequate pre-training, the LLM can understand and continue a song, while it struggles with following flexible instructions. To mitigate this, we devise a collection of 10,000 QA-style dialogues. This strategy equips the LLM to solve a broad spectrum of tasks in the song generation domain, leading to our SongComposer.

When comparing with traditional models~\cite{sheng2021songmass} that require separate decoders for different tasks, our SongComposer unifies all song-related tasks within a single language model.
Besides, by employing text-based melody representation and directly modeling the alignment between lyrics and notes, we successfully empower the LLM to generate melodies with lyrics using a considerably reduced number of tokens.

In short, our contributions are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=0pt,parsep=0pt]
    \item We introduce SongComposer, an LLM designed for song composition. It composes melodies and lyrics with symbolic song representations, with the benefit of better token efficiency, precise representation,  flexible format, and human-readable output.
    \item We have collected SongCompose-PT, a comprehensive pretraining dataset that includes lyrics, melodies, and paired lyrics and melodies in either Chinese or English.
    \item Extensive experiments show that SongComposer outperforms advanced LLMs like GPT-4 in tasks such as lyric-to-melody generation, melody-to-lyric generation, song continuation, and text-to-song creation.
\end{itemize}



\section{Related Work}

\noindent\textbf{Large Language Models.}
Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have significantly enhanced natural language processing. Early encoder-decoder models like BERT~\cite{devlin2018bert} and later decoder-only models such as GPT~\cite{radford2018improving} have shown promising results in diverse language tasks with generative pre-training. Further innovations lead to groundbreaking models including PaLM~\cite{chowdhery2023palm}, GPT-4~\cite{openai2023gpt4}, and LLaMA~\cite{touvron2023llama}, which feature billions of parameters and exhibit advanced reasoning in complex language contexts. Later, the integration of supervised instruction tuning in models like ChatGPT~\cite{openai2022chatgpt}, InstructGPT~\cite{ouyang2022training}, and Vicuna~\cite{vicuna2023} further improves their abilities to generate contextually relevant responses, nearing human-like interaction quality. Inspired by the powerful reasoning capacities of LLM, we develop a unified end-to-end LLM framework which innovatively expands the application to lyric and melody composition for song generation.

\noindent\textbf{Music Generation.}
Recent years have seen significant progress in music generation. Notably, approaches like Noise2music~\cite{huang2023noise2music} and Mo$\hat{\text{u}}$sai~\cite{schneider2023mo} utilize popular diffusion models~\cite{ho2020denoising} to generate music based on textual descriptions. Another line of studies has focused on converting music information into sequence-style tokens and then processing these sequences with Transformers~\cite{agostinelli2023musiclm,copet2023simple}. 
With the advancement of Large Language Models, M$^{2}$UGen~\cite{hussain2023m} broadens these capabilities to include music understanding and multi-modal generation, using text, images, and even videos to guide the creation process. However, these existing works predominantly generate pure melody without lyrics~\cite{agostinelli2023musiclm,copet2023simple}. On the contrary, our SongComposer can directly produce not only symbolic melodies but also aligned lyrics at the word level for song generation.
\noindent\textbf{Paired Lyric-Melody Singing Dataset.}
Singing data annotated with paired lyrics and melodies is of vital importance for song generation. Specifically, JVS-MuSiC~\cite{tamaru2020jvs}, PopCS~\cite{liu2022diffsinger}, and OpenSinger~\cite{huang2021multi} offer a broad range of singing data but lack the crucial lyric-melody temporal alignment. NUS-48E~\cite{duan2013nus}, NHSS~\cite{sharma2021nhss}, Tohoku Kiritan~\cite{ogawa2021tohoku}, and Opencpop~\cite{wang2022opencpop} provide singing corpora across English, Japanese, and Chinese with manually aligned lyrics and melodies. However, they are limited in scale, featuring only monophonic singers and styles. More recently, M4Singer~\cite{zhang2022m4singer} compiles approximately 700 Chinese songs with lyric and melody pairs, but this amount is still insufficient for training an LLM for symbolic music generation. In this work, we collect around 4K Chinese songs from scratch and compile several publicly accessible paired datasets to construct our large-scale lyric-melody pair dataset.



\section{SongCompose-PT Dataset}

This section outlines the compilation, creation, and structure of our SongCompose-PT dataset, which includes separate collections of lyrics, melodies, and lyric-melody pairs that synchronize lyrics with melodies at the word level. We aim to publicly release this three-fold dataset, providing a foundational resource for the following research.
\noindent\textbf{Pure-lyric Dataset.}
We collect pure lyrics datasets from two online sources:
(1) The Kaggle dataset\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.

After a series of lyric-cleaning processes, we gather high-quality lyrics from 283K songs, including 150K in English and 133K in Chinese. 


\noindent\textbf{Pure-melody Dataset.}
To organize the melody dataset into a text-based structure, we naturally choose to collect MIDI files. 
Using MIDI files for our pure melody dataset offers inherent structural simplicity, enabling efficient extraction and manipulation of melodies without complex audio processing.
Among our collection, 45K entries come from the LMD-matched MIDI dataset~\cite{Raffel2016LearningBasedMF}, while approximately 80K are acquired through web crawling.

For parsing MIDI files, we employ $pretty\_midi$~\cite{raffel2014prettymidi}, a Python module designed for creating, manipulating, and analyzing MIDI files.
We only extract the ``melody" or ``vocal" tracks from these MIDI files.
Since melody in MIDI is represented as a sequence of musical notes over time and each note has a specific pitch, a start and end timestamp, we obtain a list of melody attribute triplets consisting of \{\textit{note pitch}, \textit{note duration}, \textit{rest duration}\}.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=0pt,parsep=0pt]
    \item \textit{Note pitch:} The pitch of notes is represented by their corresponding MIDI note numbers, ranging from 0 to 127, with the number 60 predefined as Middle C.
    \item \textit{Note duration:} A note's duration is defined as the length of time in seconds that the note is played. This is computed from the start and end times of each note embedded within the MIDI files as follows: $\text{note-duration}_k = \text{note-end}_k - \text{note-start}_k$, where $k$ represents the note index number.
    \item \textit{Rest duration:} The rest duration represents the silent period that follows the playing of a note. It can be calculated by $\text{rest-duration}_k = \text{note-start}_{k+1} - \text{note-end}_k$.
\end{itemize}

We perform the necessary data filtering to remove duplicate and poor-quality samples, leaving approximately 20K MIDI samples remaining.





\noindent\textbf{Paired Lyric-melody Dataset.}
Large-scale datasets of temporally aligned lyric-melody pairs are crucial for training LLMs for song composition, but are difficult to compile due to the need for precise synchronization between lyrics and melody as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:align_eg}. This task requires detailed annotation and specific expertise. Notwithstanding these challenges, some datasets are available. We utilize datasets from previous studies, including 7,998 songs from the LMD-full dataset and 4,199 songs from a Reddit-sourced dataset~\cite{yu2021conditional}. Additionally, we incorporate OpenCpop~\cite{wang2022opencpop}, which contains 100 Mandarin pop songs, and M4Singer~\cite{zhang2022m4singer}, featuring 700 high-quality Mandarin songs.

\section{SongComposer}

In the section, we elaborate on our SongComposer in two aspects.
First, to leverage the power of large language model (LLM) capabilities, we propose a principal strategy for converting the symbolic song representation into an LLM-friendly format in Section~\ref{sec:format}. Then, following this foundational setup, Section~\ref{sec:train} delves into a two-stage training paradigm, which employs our collected dataset and enables the model to compose songs.
\subsection{Symbolic Representation for LLM}
\label{sec:format}
\noindent\textbf{Tuple Data Format.} 
To feed the lyrics and melody to the LLM, an intuitive strategy would be to arrange them sequentially, starting with a sequence of lyrics followed by a sequence of note values and time durations. However, the model struggles to align lyrics and melody due to its sequential approach, resulting in misaligned outputs.
To address this issue, we propose a unified input organization utilizing a tuple-based format, where each tuple represents a discrete musical unit, which could be a lyric, a melody, or a lyric-melody pair. Specifically, in the case of pure lyric data, we insert a single word into each tuple. For pure melodies, we include the note pitch, note duration, and rest duration in each tuple. For lyric-melody pairs, we merge the lyric and corresponding note elements within the same tuple. 
Since a single word can correspond to multiple notes, a single tuple may contain one word alongside several notes and their respective durations. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:train}, we employ a vertical bar ($|$) to separate different tuples and leverage the sequence of tuples as input to LLM. This tuple format explicitly provides the model with a mutual mapping between lyrics and melody, facilitating the ability to grasp the correspondence between the elements.

\noindent\textbf{Discretized Duration.} 
To effectively process duration information from melodies, we employ a logarithmic encoding scheme to categorize durations into predefined number of bins, transforming the continuous duration range into a set of discrete values. This well-organized and concise input for LLM enhances the model's ability to capture precise timing variations in musical content.
Specifically, according to the statistical distribution of durations, we clip them into \([x_{\text{min}}, x_{\text{max}}]\). For any duration 
\(x\), the encoding process is defined as follows:

\begin{align}
    x_{\mathrm{bin}} &= \frac{f(x) - f(x_{\text{min}})}{f(x_{\text{max}}) - f(x_{\text{min}})} \times (\text{bins} - 1), \nonumber \\
    & \text{where} \quad f(x) = \log(x + \epsilon).
\end{align}

Here we set \([x_{\text{min}}, x_{\text{max}}]\) = \([-0.3, 6]\), \(\text{bins} = 512\), and \(\epsilon=1\), which enables the encoding of duration up to 6.3 seconds into 512 discretized integers. 
Conversely, to decode the discretized values back into their original duration, we apply:
\begin{align}
    \Tilde{x} = \exp \left( \frac{f(x_{\text{max}}) - f(x_{\text{min}})}{\text{bins} - 1} x_{\mathrm{bin}}  + f(x_{\text{min}}) \right) - \epsilon.
\end{align}

By using a logarithmic approach, we can assign finer-grained bins to shorter durations while employing coarser bins for longer ones. This adaptive binning strategy yields a more efficient representation of time, aligning with the human perception which tends to be more sensitive to distinguishing shorter durations. 

\noindent\textbf{Vocabulary Extension.} 
To ensure clarity, we specialize our representation for discretized time units and note values within the model’s vocabulary by introducing auxilary tokens denoted by $\langle \cdot \rangle$ symbols. For temporal discretization, we expand the vocabulary with 512 unique tokens, denoted as $\langle x_{\text{bin}}\rangle$, where each token represents a specific temporal bin. For the representation of musical notes, we tokenize 12 pitch classes across 10 octaves, thereby adding 120 distinct musical tokens. 
These newly introduced vocabularies and the final language modeling head are optimized throughout the training process without any sophisticated initialization.

\subsection{Two-Stage Training}
\label{sec:train}
\noindent\textbf{Pretraining Stage.}
To enrich LLMs with fundamental musical knowledge, we first pretrain the model on a substantial corpus of pure lyrics and pure melodies with next token prediction. Note that for melodies, we employ pitch shifting to augment our dataset. Specifically, we shift the pitch by up to four semitones in both directions, enlarging our dataset size by a factor of nine. 
Generally, we break the whole song data into line-based samples, each containing five to ten lines. This formulation offers the model a more focused and relevant learning context, which facilitates more coherent song generation. 
Through this pure lyric and melody training process, our model has acquired a solid foundation of musical knowledge, establishing a strong background for more advanced learning focused on the correspondence between lyrics and melody.
Following this, we use our paired data to guide the model in generating aligned lyric-melody pairs. Empirically, to preserve the model's capacity to process melodies and lyrics separately, we feed an equal amount of pure melody, pure lyric, and paired data into the model, following a 1:1:1 sample ratio. 

In total, there are 0.23B tokens for lyrics, 0.28B tokens for the melody and 0.16B tokens for paired data. All the data are input using the tuple data format. 

\noindent\textbf{Supervised Finetune Stage.} After ingesting a large collection of music scores and lyrics into the model, we curate instruction-following data for song-generation tasks including creating melodies for given lyrics, writing lyrics for melodies, extending song segments, and generating songs from text descriptions. To facilitate these objectives, we manually prepare 3K QA pairs for each of the first three tasks. Additionally, for the final task, we use GPT-4 to produce 1K song summaries, which in turn form a text-to-song dataset that guides the song creation process. 

\noindent\textbf{Training Details.}
We adopt InternLM-7B~\cite{2023internlm} as our base model and train the model to predict the next token based on prior text, maximizing the log-likelihood of tokens in the given examples. 
For optimization, we use AdamW optimizer~\cite{loshchilov2018decoupled} with a learning rate of $10^{-5}$, $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$, and a weight decay of 0.1. The entire dataset is iterated through once, with a batch size of 8. Additionally, a linear warm-up of the learning rate is applied during the initial $1\%$ of training steps, increasing from $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-5}$. Afterwards, a cosine schedule is applied, reducing the learning rate to a minimum of 0. This setting is consistent across both the pretraining and supervised fine-tuning stages. The training of SongComposer is conducted on 16 Nvidia A100 (80G) GPUs for approximately 2 days.




\section{Experiments}



\subsection{Objective Evaluation Metrics}
We construct a validation set that contains 1188 songs. Among these, 415 are Chinese songs and 773 are English songs, all of which have not been exposed to our model during the training phase. For subsequent testing, we randomly segment several songs into 5 to 10 sentences.
The objective evaluation focuses on assessing the similarity between the generated output and the ground-truth sample.

\noindent\textbf{Melody Generation.}
For melody assessment, we adopt the metric proposed by SongMASS~\cite{sheng2021songmass}. This includes the Pitch Distribution Similarity (PD), Duration Distribution Similarity (DD), and Melody Distance (MD). 
Due to the difference in calibration strategy compared to SongMASS, we apply post-processing to harmonize the note pitch and duration time. In particular, we align the mean pitch of both the ground truth and generated notes through addition. For duration, we first calculate the average time ratio between the generated music and the ground truth, which is then used to scale the timing through multiplication. After setting the beats per minute (bpm) to 60, we transform 1/16 second into 1/16 notes, thereby allowing for a more consistent comparison.
\noindent\textbf{Lyric Generation.}
In terms of lyric generation, we evaluate sentence-level alignment using a CoSENT(Cosine Sentence) model~\cite{text2vec}, specifically the base-multilingual version. This model allows us to input the lyrics and compute the cosine similarity between the generated lyrics and the original ones. Moreover, we incorporate the ROUGE-2 score~\cite{lin2004rouge} and BERT score~\cite{zhang2019bertscore} as two additional metrics for evaluating lyric generation. The ROUGE-2 score focuses on the overlap of bigrams between the generated and original texts. As for the BERT score, we utilize the BERT-base multilingual uncased model to measure text similarity based on contextual embeddings.
\subsection{Subjective Evaluation Metrics}
For the subjective evaluation, we conduct a user study with 30 participants 10 cases per task. We develop two metrics for each task and ask the participants to rate them. The rating scale is $1$ to $5$, where higher scores denote superior quality. In this way, we collect feedback on the quality of the generated content from a human perspective.

\noindent\textbf{Lyric-to-Melody Generation} asks to create a fitting melody based on the given lyrics. The melody is assessed on: 
(1) Harmony (HMY.): Evaluates the overall quality of the melody.
(2) Melody-Lyric Compatibility (MLC.): Examines how well the generated melody fits the given lyrics.

\noindent\textbf{Melody-to-Lyric Generation} aims to produce lyrics that match a provided melody. The lyrics are evaluated on:
(1) Fluency (FLN.): Considers the grammatical correctness and semantic coherence of the generated lyrics.
(2) Melody-Lyric Compatibility (MLC.): Examines how well the generated lyrics fit the given melody.

\noindent\textbf{Song Continuation} involves extending a given song segment both melodically and lyrically. We evaluate the continuation quality on:
(1) Overall Quality (OVL.): Measures the overall quality of the generated song in terms of its musical appeal.
(2) Coherence to the Song Prompt (COH.): Analyzes the natural integration of the continuation with the provided song prompt, assessing coherence in melody, lyrics, and other musical elements.

\noindent\textbf{Text-to-Song Generation} generates a complete song based on textual description, capturing its essence musically and lyrically. The evaluation focuses on:
(1) Overall Quality (OVL.): Measures the overall quality of the generated song in terms of its musical appeal.
(2) Relevance to the Text Input (REL.): Examines how well the generated song aligns with and derives relevance from the input text.

In summary, for each task, one metric evaluates the overall musical quality of the produced samples, while the other measures the specific challenges of the corresponding task.

\noindent\textbf{Pretraining Dataset.}
To explore the influence of specialized datasets on our model's learning, we conduct training experiments using paired data with different combinations of pure-lyric and pure-melody datasets. Table~\ref{tab:ablation_dataset} illustrates that omitting both pure-lyric and pure-melody datasets significantly diminishes performance, underscoring the essential role of foundational melodic and lyrical knowledge in the early stages of training. Integrating either dataset individually offers notable improvements across tasks. Specifically, the pure-lyric dataset predominantly enhances performance in the melody-to-lyric task, whereas the pure-melody dataset provides a more substantial boost to melody generation. This observation aligns with the intuitive understanding that each dataset enriches the model's comprehension of its respective modality. Finally, employing both types of datasets yields the most advantageous results, showcasing a synergistic effect that highlights the value of combining lyrical and melodic data during pretraining for more comprehensive learning outcomes.

\noindent\textbf{Extended Vocabulary for Melodic Knowledge.} 
To understand whether the specialized tokens for melodic knowledge benefit the performance of SongComposer, we ablate on different tokenization combos in Table~\ref{tab:ablation_tokenization}. We assess their influence on the model's ability to process and generate musical content.
Firstly, for duration tokenization, rather than applying discrete numbers, we directly input original float-based seconds into the SongComposer as an ablation.
The absence of the special time tokens results in the model's inability to generate correct outcomes for the lyric-to-melody tasks. 
Conversely, incorporating discrete duration tokenization considerably improves the performance in melody-to-lyric tasks, enhancing the BERT Score by over 0.03, regardless of whether note tokens are introduced. It verifies the importance of duration discretization for grasping melody structure.
Secondly, the adoption of note tokens yields significant improvements across both tasks. Although the model can recognize the string of note representations, special tokenization on them further improves clarity and optimizes the learning process. 



\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we propose SongComposer, a novel large language model that utilizes a symbolic song representation for melody and lyric generation. 
Unlike prior music LLMs that process music as quantized audio signals leading to inefficiency and limited diversity, SongComposer harnesses symbolic notation to produce music in a manner more similar to human composition techniques. Our unique approach integrates a novel tuple design connecting notes' pitch, duration, and rest duration with lyrics, ensuring precise musical understanding and alignment. Besides, we created SongCompose-PT, a comprehensive dataset with lyrics, melodies, and their combinations, for large-scale pre-training. After refining with 10K QA pairs focusing on instruction-following capability, SongComposer excels in various tasks such as converting lyrics to melodies, melody to lyric generation, song continuation, and text-to-song creation, surpassing even advanced LLM models like GPT-4 in performance. 
In conclusion, we believe SongComposer can open up new avenues for the creative application of LLMs to the music domain.
