\title{HELMET: How to Evaluate Long-context Models Effectively and Thoroughly}

\begin{abstract}
  There have been 
   many benchmarks for evaluating long-context language models (LCLMs), but developers often rely on synthetic tasks like needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH) or arbitrary subsets of tasks.
It remains unclear whether they translate to the diverse downstream applications of LCLMs, and the inconsistency further complicates model comparison. 
    We investigate the underlying reasons behind current practices    
    and find that existing benchmarks often provide noisy signals due to low coverage of applications, insufficient lengths, unreliable metrics, and incompatibility with base models. 
    In this work, we present \oursfull, a comprehensive benchmark encompassing seven diverse, application-centric categories. 
    We also address many issues in previous benchmarks by adding
    controllable lengths up to 128k tokens,
    model-based evaluation for reliable metrics,
    and few-shot prompting for robustly evaluating base models. 
    Consequently, we demonstrate that \ours{} offers more reliable and consistent rankings of frontier LCLMs. 
    Through a comprehensive study of 51 LCLMs, we find that
    (1)~synthetic  tasks like NIAH are not good predictors of downstream  performance;
    (2)~the diverse categories in \ours{} exhibit distinct trends and low correlation with each other; and
    (3)~while most LCLMs achieve perfect NIAH scores,  
    open-source models significantly lag behind closed ones when the task requires
    full-context reasoning or following complex instructions---the gap widens with increased lengths.
    Finally, we recommend using our  RAG tasks for fast model development, as they are easy to run and more predictive of other downstream performance; ultimately, we advocate for a holistic evaluation across diverse tasks.}


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}




\input{tables/model_eval_comparison.tex}
Long-context language models (LCLMs) unlock 
a myriad of applications, from summarizing long documents to 
learning new tasks on the fly with thousands of examples.
Many recent benchmarks sought to evaluate language models' long-context abilities~\citep[][\emph{inter alia}]{zhang2024inftybenchextendinglongcontext,an-etal-2024-l,shaham-etal-2023-zeroscrolls,bai-etal-2024-longbench}.
However, recent developments on long contexts \citep{chen2023extending,xiong2023effective,peng2024yarn,fu2024data} still  rely on either perplexity or synthetic needle-in-a-haystack tasks~(NIAH; \citealp{gkamradt_llmtest_needleinahaystack_2024,hsieh2024ruler}).
Frontier LCLMs \citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels,jambateam2024jamba15hybridtransformermambamodels,openai2023gpt4,geminiteam2024geminifamilyhighlycapable} also mostly report NIAH, sometimes with arbitrary subsets of other datasets, as shown in Table \ref{tab:model_eval_comparison}.
Such inconsistency complicates comparisons between different models.
It is also unclear whether synthetic NIAH tasks or the few chosen datasets can provide a holistic picture of models' long-context abilities and their performance in real-world applications.


  

\textit{Why don't model developers agree on these evaluations}? 
  We take a closer look and find that existing benchmarks have many critical design flaws, including:
  
\begin{itemize}[left=5pt]
    \item \textbf{Insufficient coverage of downstream tasks}: Existing benchmarks either focus on synthetic tasks \citep{hsieh2024ruler} or add simple question answering \citep{zhang2024inftybenchextendinglongcontext}. 
    Other works study particular aspects of LCLMs, %
    such as summarization \citep{chang2024booookscore}, in-context learning \citep{li2024longiclbench}, and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG; \citealp{lee2024longcontextlanguagemodelssubsume}), but they do not provide a holistic evaluation of LCLMs. 
    
    \item \textbf{Inadequate lengths}: Most natural language datasets in existing benchmarks \citep[][Table \ref{tab:datasets_length}]{shaham-etal-2023-zeroscrolls,an-etal-2024-l} are too short to test frontier long-context abilities (usually $\geq$128k).
    \item \textbf{Unreliable metrics}: 
    For commonly used long-document QA and summarization tasks,
    most existing benchmarks still rely on metrics like ROUGE~\citep{lin-2004-rouge}, which can be noisy and unreliable \citep{goyal2023newssummarizationevaluationera,deutsch-etal-2022-examining,chang2024booookscore}.
    \item \textbf{Incompatibility with base models}: 
    Many LCLM developments focus on base models without instruction tuning,
    but most existing benchmarks require models to be instruction-tuned---hence developers can only rely on synthetic tasks or perplexity. 
\end{itemize}

Consequently, existing benchmarks either are not applicable to long-context works (inadequate lengths or incompatibility) or provide very noisy signals (insufficient coverage or unreliable metrics). 
We summarize the shortcomings of many of the benchmarks in \Cref{tab:dataset_comparison}. For the three benchmarks that support 128k\footnote{Throughout the paper, we use binary prefixes k$=2^{10}$.} context length (NIAH, \ruler{}, and \infbench{}), we use them to evaluate 6 frontier models and report the numbers in \Cref{fig:comparison_teaser}.
We see that NIAH does not reflect differences across  models; 
\ruler{} and \infbench{} show unexpected trends---on \ruler{}, Gemini Flash is stronger than Gemini Pro; 
on \infbench{}, the 70B Llama model is weaker than the 8B one.
This raises concerns about the reliability of the benchmarks and how they can differentiate long-context models, which likely contributes to the lack of use of these benchmarks in model development.

To address these challenges, we present \oursfull{}.
We curate a \emph{diverse} set of application-centric long-context tasks across seven categories. 
Beyond widely adopted categories like \emph{synthetic recall}, \emph{long-document question answering (QA)}, and \emph{summarization}, we also add \emph{many-shot in-context learning} (ICL), \emph{retrieval-augmented generation} (RAG), \emph{passage re-ranking}, and \emph{generation with citations} \citep{gao2023alce}.
We further 
address the shortcomings of existing benchmarks: 
(1) we ensure all datasets support input lengths of 128k and are easily extendable to more tokens (\S\ref{dataset:overview}); 
(2) we introduce reference-based model evaluation for QA and summarization and show that it drastically improves over n-gram overlap metrics (\S\ref{dataset:metrics});
(3) we refine the prompts and in-context demonstrations used for all tasks, reducing the evaluation noise by different output formats and allowing base models to be evaluated on most of the categories robustly (\S\ref{dataset:settings}).
Together, \ours{} enables a holistic evaluation of long-context capabilities and 
provides more reliable signals for model development.
\Cref{fig:comparison_teaser} shows that
\ours{} can clearly differentiate models at different capabilities and reflect comparisons consistent with human perception.
More discussions are in \S\ref{app:related_works_compare}.
To understand the progress of LCLMs and how different long-context capabilities correlate with one another, we evaluate a thorough list of 51 LCLMs of various architectures, scales, and training strategies.
Our analysis reveals that
(1) synthetic  tasks are poor indicators of downstream performance (\S\ref{results:synthetic}),
(2) different categories in \ours{}
show distinctive trends %
(\S\ref{results:realistic}), and
(3) 
open-source models significantly lag behind closed ones on tasks that require
reasoning over long contexts or following complex instructions---the gap further widens with increased lengths (\S\ref{results:complex}).
Finally, we find that the RAG category strikes a good balance between ease of use, better correlation with downstream tasks, and compatibility with base models.
Ultimately, it is imperative to evaluate LCLMs over a diverse spectrum of categories.
We hope that our insights offer a more effective way to evaluate LCLMs for future model development and benchmarking.

\section{Our Benchmark: \ours{}



In this work, we seek to overcome the shortcomings of existing benchmarks by meeting the following desiderata: 
(1) diverse coverage across different tasks and capabilities of LCLMs,
(2) %
controllable lengths that support more than 128k input tokens, 
and (3) reliable evaluation for both base and instruction-tuned models.
In this section, we %
describe the datasets used in \ours{} and 
how they improve upon existing evaluation benchmarks in terms of settings and metrics.

\subsection{Realistic and diverse long-context applications}
\label{dataset:overview}

\paragraph{Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG).}
We use open-domain question answering (ODQA)---which requires retrieving from a knowledge corpus and then generating correct answers \citep{chen-etal-2017-reading}---as a representation of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) applications. %
We use Natural Questions \citep[NQ;][]{kwiatkowski2019natural}, TriviaQA \citep[TQA;][]{joshi2017triviaqa}, HotpotQA \citep{yang-etal-2018-hotpotqa}, and PopQA \citep{mallen-etal-2023-trust}.
We use the gold passage information (the passage that can answer the question) from \citet{petroni-etal-2021-kilt}, or otherwise use any passage that contains the answer. %

For a particular input length $L$, we first determine the number of passages $k$ that can fit within $L$ tokens, then retrieve $k$ passages\footnote{We use \href{https://huggingface.co/Alibaba-NLP/gte-large-en-v1.5}{\ttt{Alibaba-NLP/gte-large-en-v1.5}} for retrieval.}
from the corpus\footnote{We use Wikipedia 
2019-8-01 dump, split into 100-word passages \citep{petroni-etal-2021-kilt}.} that \textit{do not contain the answer} as \textit{distractors}.
This differs from previous works that \textit{randomly sample passages} from the corpus \citep{lee2024longcontextlanguagemodelssubsume} and is more realistic and challenging.
For NQ, TQA, and PopQA, %
we take the top $k-1$ distractors and insert the gold passage at six evenly distributed positions following \citet{Liu2023LostIT}.
For HotpotQA, which requires two gold passages, we combine them and the top $k-2$ distractors and randomly shuffle them into three permutations.
We use substring exact match (\subem{}; whether the answer is included in the output), 
following previous work \citep{mallen-etal-2023-trust,asai2024selfrag}.
\S\ref{app:datasets:odqa} offers more details.
\paragraph{Generation with citations (Cite).}
We leverage ALCE \citep{gao2023alce} to evaluate LCLMs on a realistic application of answering questions while providing correct attributions \citep{bohnet2022attributed}.
Given multi-faceted questions and relevant passages, models are required to generate a long-text answer and cite supporting passage IDs at the end of each sentence. 
This tests models' ability to utilize the passages in the context and also to follow the instructions about citation formats.
We use the %
ASQA \citep{stelmakh2022asqa} and QAMPARI \citep{rubin2022qampari} subsets from ALCE.
For an input length $L$, we first determine the number of passages $k$, and use the top $k$ retrieved passages from Wikipedia as contexts. %
The model's generations are evaluated on correctness and citation quality, and we report the average across all metrics.
See \S\ref{app:datasets:cite} for more details.

\paragraph{Passage re-ranking (Re-rank).}
Re-ranking retrieved passages based on their relevance to the query 
is an important application of LCLMs~\citep{sun-etal-2023-chatgpt}.
The task requires the model to retrieve relevant information and compare and reason over different parts of the contexts.
We use the MS MARCO dataset \citep{bajaj2018ms}, %
where each instance contains a query and
passages retrieved by BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25} 
from the Internet. 
Each passage has annotations  of a 
relevance label---perfect, highly relevant, or not relevant.
We determine the number of passages $k$ from the input length $L$, and randomly sample  %
$k$ passages with balanced labels for each test query. 
The model is prompted with the query and the shuffled $k$ passages 
and is instructed to output the top-10 document IDs ranked by relevance. 
We report NDCG@10.
Details are in \S\ref{app:datasets:rerank}.

\input{tables/datasets_lengths.tex}
\paragraph{Long-document question answering (LongQA).}
We use NarrativeQA \citep{kocisky2018narrativeqa} and the English book QA and multiple choice (MC) subsets from \infbench{} \citep{zhang2024inftybenchextendinglongcontext} for evaluating long-document QA. %
We select those tasks 
for their abundant context lengths (\Cref{tab:datasets_length}).
We truncate the document from the end based on $L$.
We use ROUGE F1 for \infbench{} QA (answers are mostly entity names) and accuracy for \infbench{} MC. %
For NarrativeQA, as the answer can be long text and open-ended, 
we use a model-based evaluation (\S\ref{dataset:metrics}).
\paragraph{Summarization (Summ).}
Summarization tests LCLMs' ability to aggregate information across the contexts. %
We choose \mls{} (legal document summarization) and the English summarization task from \infbench{} (novel summarization)
for their long lengths (\Cref{tab:datasets_length}).
We truncate the document from the end based on the evaluation length $L$.
We use our model-based evaluation (\S\ref{dataset:metrics}) for both datasets
instead of the commonly used ROUGE, as it better reflects human judgment.

\paragraph{Many-shot in-context learning (ICL).}
In-context learning (ICL) is a key ability that enables LLMs to adapt to new tasks on the fly \citep{brown2020language}.
\citet{ratner-etal-2023-parallel,xu2024stresstesting,li2024longiclbench,bertsch2024incontext} explore performing many-shot ICL (with thousands of examples) with LCLMs.
Following \citet{bertsch2024incontext}, we focus on datasets with large label spaces: TREC-course, TREC-fine \citep{li-roth-2002-learning}, BANKING77 \citep{casanueva-etal-2020-efficient}, CLINC150 \citep{larson-etal-2019-evaluation}, and NLU \citep{Liu2019BenchmarkingNL}.
We adjust the number of shots to control the input length $L$, and the number of examples in each class is balanced. 
We report the accuracy on the test set for each dataset.

One \textit{difference} from previous works is that 
we map original natural language labels (e.g., \textit{location}) into numbered labels (i.e., \textit{0}, \textit{1}) to test how well a model can learn new tasks instead of relying on its pre-trained priors \citep{wei2023largerlanguagemodelsincontext,pan-etal-2023-context,min-etal-2022-rethinking}.
More details are in \S\ref{app:datasets:icl}.

\paragraph{Synthetic recall.}
Synthetic recall tasks, such as needle-in-a-haystack (NIAH), stress test models' ability to recall relevant information (needle) from long contexts.
They gain popularity for being
easy to use (can test any arbitrary length) and 
easy to control (can put the ``needle'' at any position).
For this category,
we select multiple synthetic recall tasks from RULER (a more  comprehensive version of NIAH; \citealp{hsieh2024ruler}) 
and also add 
a JSON KV retrieval task \citep{Liu2023LostIT}, which we find more challenging.
Following previous works, we report the percentage of the ground truth answers that are substrings in the generation (\subem{}).
Refer to \S\ref{app:datasets:synthetic} for more details.

\subsection{Reliable evaluation metrics}
\label{dataset:metrics}

Existing long-context benchmarks \citep{zhang2024inftybenchextendinglongcontext,shaham-etal-2023-zeroscrolls} largely rely on n-gram overlap metrics like ROUGE \citep{lin-2004-rouge} that have been shown to correlate poorly with human judgment for tasks with long outputs, such as summarization \citep{goyal2023newssummarizationevaluationera,deutsch-etal-2022-examining}.
L-Eval \citep{an-etal-2024-l} uses LLMs to score reference-free ``win-rates'', which neglects the available answer annotations
and always requires evaluating model pairs.
Instead, we design \emph{reference-based model evaluation} for long-document QA and summarization  that are more reliable and easy to use. 

\paragraph{Question answering.}
In NarrativeQA, 
we prompt GPT-4o\footnote{\ttt{GPT-4o-2024-05-13}} 
with the question, the ground truth, and the model output to check for fluency and correctness. 
The fluency score is either 0 (incoherent or repetitive) or 1 (fluent), and the correctness score takes on the value of 0 (incorrect), 1 (partly correct), 2 (correct but not fully relevant), and 3 (correct and relevant).
We take the product of the two as the final score and normalize it to a range of $[0,100]$.

\paragraph{Summarization.}
Following previous works \citep{kamoi2023wice,zhang-bansal-2021-finding}, we first decompose the gold summary into atomic claims and use GPT-4o to check if each claim is supported by the generation (recall) and if each sentence in the generation is supported by the reference summary (precision).
We then calculate the F1 score from the recall and precision scores.
We also ask GPT-4o to check for fluency (0 or 1) and take the final score as the product of the fluency and the F1 score.
\begin{figure}[t!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{figures/length_model_eval.pdf}
    \caption{
        Comparison between ROUGE-L F1  and our model-based evaluation metric on summarization tasks.
        Our metric shows more consistent trends: 
        it reflects the performance gain on GPT-4o with increased input length, while ROUGE remains almost the same; our metric also clearly distinguishes models while ROUGE shows little difference.
    }
    \label{fig:comparison_metrics}
\end{figure}
Empirically, our reference-based model evaluation reflects more consistent trends, %
as shown in Figure \ref{fig:comparison_metrics}:
(1) Llama-3.1-8B-Inst achieves similar ROUGE scores to GPT-4o, while our evaluation shows a significant gap.
(2) Our metric better identifies incoherent generations and shows lower performance for models with smaller context windows, such as the Mistral models.
(3) Our metric shows a substantially more positive trend for GPT-4o as input length increases, whereas ROUGE-L remains within a difference of 2 absolute points.
We further validate the model-based evaluation through human studies, which suggests that our new metrics highly agree with human judgments: for example, our metric reaches a human-model agreement of Cohen's $\kappa = 0.91$ and $\kappa = 0.76$ on summary precision and recall, respectively.
More details can be found in \S\ref{app:datasets}.
\subsection{Robust prompting and controlled evaluation settings}
\label{dataset:settings}
\paragraph{Robust prompting reduces noise and enables evaluation on base models.}
Many long-context benchmarks 
require models to follow instructions and 
only support evaluating instruction-tuned models \citep{zhang2024inftybenchextendinglongcontext,shaham-etal-2023-zeroscrolls}. %
However, many model developments do not apply instruction tuning~\citep{chen2023extending,fu2024data}
and these models can only rely on perplexity or synthetic tasks.
To support long-context research efforts,
we design our benchmark so that at least a portion of the datasets supports evaluating base models.
Existing benchmarks mostly use zero-shot prompting \citep{shaham-etal-2023-zeroscrolls,zhang2024inftybenchextendinglongcontext}, which leads to noisy output formats, especially for base models.
For example, the model may output a long answer in RAG when a short answer is required.
We add two-shot demonstrations in the prompt for all tasks to 
address this problem.\footnote{Except for ICL (the number of shots varies) and RULER (we follow the original formatting).}
For long-document QA and summarization, we replace the original document with a placeholder phrase to reduce the number of input tokens in the ICL example.
As shown in Table \ref{tab:results_ablate_shots}, we observe that both base and instruct models  substantially benefit from the demonstrations. %

Furthermore, we  employ the length-instruction-enhanced evaluation from L-Eval for long-generation tasks (i.e., summarization), which is shown to have substantially more consistent and reliable evaluations \citep{an-etal-2024-l}.
As a result, we find that our reproduction of previous datasets, such as \infbench{} QA tasks, better reflects the capabilities of LCLMs, as shown in Table \ref{tab:results_infbench_compare}. 
The use of demonstration and better instructions thus better depicts how models perform in real applications.

\paragraph{Controlled input length and difficulty.}
An important dimension to consider when evaluating LCLMs is the input length $L$, as longer inputs can provide more information while challenging the model's ability to process distracting contexts.
As we discussed in \S\ref{dataset:overview}, we can control the input length $L$ for each task by either adjusting the number of retrieved passages, the number of demonstrations, or truncating the document text to fit within the specified lengths.
Consequently, we are able to study model performance at or beyond the length of current frontier LCLMs ($\geq 128$k). %

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:comparison_teaser} and \ref{fig:comparison_metrics}, \ours{} provides rankings more consistent with human perception of model performance. 
The diverse tasks, controllable lengths, and robust metrics and prompting enable a thorough examination of  LCLMs across multiple dimensions.










\section{Analysis}

\label{analysis}
We evaluate 51 LCLMs with \ours{}.
To our best knowledge, this is the most thorough and controlled comparison of long-context models on diverse applications.
These models cover closed-source models, such as GPT4, Claude, and Gemini, as well as open-source model families, such as Llama-3 \citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels}, Mistral \citep{jiang2023mistral}, and Phi \citep{abdin2024phi3technicalreporthighly}.
We also consider models that use different architectures---full-attention transformers \citep{vaswani2017attention}, sliding-window attention \citep{Beltagy2020Longformer}, and hybrid models with SSM modules \citep{dao2024transformersssmsgeneralizedmodels}.
We also benchmark position extrapolation models such as 
YaRN \citep{peng2024yarn} and LongRoPE \citep{ding2024longropeextendingllmcontext}. 
We list all the models evaluated in Table \ref{tab:model_detail}.
We evaluate each model at input lengths: $L \in $\{8k, 16k, 32k, 64k, 128k\}, where $L$ is the number of Llama-2 tokens \citep{touvron2023llama2}, and use greedy decoding for all models for consistency.
We randomly sample 100 examples from each dataset, and
more details are in \S\ref{app:setup}.
\subsection{Simple synthetic tasks are poor predictors of real-world performance}
\label{results:synthetic}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.4\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/correlation_syn_real_inst.pdf}
    \caption{
        Spearman's rank correlation at 128k input length, calculated across 30 instruction-tuned models.
    }
    \label{fig:corr_syn_real}
\end{wrapfigure}
Many model developers rely on simple synthetic tasks, such as NIAH, for evaluating long-context language models, but it is unclear if these tasks are representative of real-world performance.
To this end, we calculate Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ with the performance of 30 instruction-tuned models
between synthetic and real-world tasks. 
First, Figure \ref{fig:corr_syn_real} shows that none of the synthetic tasks achieves an average correlation higher than 0.8.
We also make the following observations.

\paragraph{Not all synthetic tasks are created equal.}
The original NIAH, which places a needle in the middle of unrelated essays and asks the model to retrieve it, does not correlate well with real-world tasks: 
all correlations are $\leq0.8$.
Using the popular RULER average score---which contains not only NIAH variants
but also synthetic aggregation, multi-hop tracing, and QA---also does not give 
strong correlations (all $<0.85$). 
We take a closer look at different \ruler{} tasks and find that harder recall-type tasks are more reflective of real-world categories---such as RULER MK, which places distracting needles around the target needle. 
Despite the overall low correlation, we believe they can still serve as a useful \emph{sanity check} during model development.
We put together several such \ruler{} tasks, along with JSON KV, to form the \ours{}  synthetic recall set (more discussions on this in \S\ref{app:correlation_syn_real}).


\paragraph{Tasks with noisier, more distracting contexts better differentiate models.}
To understand why synthetic tasks poorly correlate with real-world tasks, we 
plot the performance of different models on NIAH, RULER MK (one of our recall tasks), and HotpotQA  (one of our RAG tasks) in Figure \ref{fig:corr_syn_real_dist}.
We use \infbench{} QA as a representative real-world task.
We find that most models achieve either perfect or near-zero performance on the original NIAH, leaving few data points in the middle and resulting in poor separability between models.
On the other hand, RULER MK, whose contexts are more distracting, better distributes the models between 0 and 100\% performance.

\paragraph{RAG is a better proxy for real-world tasks.}
Finally, we find that RAG datasets, such as HotpotQA, 
consistently achieve higher correlation with other real-world tasks. 
We also see from \Cref{fig:corr_syn_real_dist} that HotpotQA enjoys an almost linear relationship with the QA dataset.
Similar to synthetic tasks, RAG tasks are easy to control and 
also test models' recall abilities; yet since all the passages are retrieved and relevant to the query,
RAG contexts are more distracting and hence harder to saturate.

\subsection{Diverse LCLM applications call for diverse evaluation}
\label{results:realistic}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.45\linewidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/correlation_category_inst.pdf}
    \caption{
        Spearman rank correlation between different categories at $L=$128k.
    }
    \label{fig:corr_category}
\end{wrapfigure}
In long-context language modeling, realistic tasks are often only used in isolated settings \citep{karpinska2024thousandpairsnovelchallenge,li2024longiclbench,dubey2024llama3herdmodels}, which limits the understanding of LCLMs in a broader context.
In this work, we cross-examine model performance over the wide range of real tasks, and find that different categories do not always correlate with each other, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:corr_category}.

Naturally, RAG and passage re-ranking moderately correlate due to the shared retrieval component.
However, the added complexity of generating citations in ALCE leads to lower correlation with other categories.
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:corr_all}, generating the correct answer and valid citations  are not well correlated, which suggests that instruction following and recalling facts with long contexts are distinct capabilities.
Furthermore, some categories---summarization and in-context learning---do not correlate well with other categories at all.
Intuitively, summarization tests for the model's ability to aggregate information across the whole input while ICL tests for the model's ability to learn new tasks from many examples. Such capabilities are  orthogonal to recall facts in long contexts.
Therefore, model developers should evaluate across these distinct axes to draw a more holistic picture of the model's capabilities.
Additional analysis are in \S\ref{app:correlation_datasets}.

\subsection{Model performance across tasks and lengths}
\label{results:complex}
We show the performance of instruction-tuned models on \ours{} at  five different lengths in Figure \ref{fig:results_length_main}, and the
full results are illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:results_length_full}.
We analyze the model performance across two critical dimensions of long-context language modeling: task complexity and input length.

\paragraph{Open-source models lag behind closed-source models on complex tasks.}
First, we consider the performance of frontier LCLMs at the longest input length of 128k tokens.
We find that the closed-source models---notably GPT-4o-08 and Gemini-1.5-Pro---stand out as the strongest LCLMs.
Other than ICL, the closed-source models outperform the open-source models on all tasks.
The gap is relatively small on synthetic recall and LongQA, where the task is to retrieve information from the context.
There is a stark contrast in the generation with citations and re-ranking performance, where the closed-source models are 30 to 40 absolute points better than the best open-source models.

\paragraph{Performance degradation with longer inputs is category-dependent.}
Most frontier models largely retain performance on recall and RAG with longer inputs; however, even the best models significantly degrade with more contexts on tasks like re-ranking and generation with citations.
As illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:results_length_select}, 
with increased task complexity from left to right, 
performance degradation at longer lengths is more pronounced.
On generation with citations, open-source models completely collapse at 128k while GPT-4o stays roughly the same.
This underscores the importance of evaluating on more complex and realistic long-context applications.


\paragraph{No clear winner across all categories.}
As we observe from the previous sections, the different categories are not always correlated with each other.
This is evident in the different winners across different categories: for instance, GPT-4o is stronger on recall and generation with citations, while Gemini is stronger on passage re-ranking and long-document QA.
Furthermore, many open-source models outperform the closed-source models on ICL, potentially because heavy instruction tuning hurts ICL, and we provide qualitative examples in \autoref{tab:claude_outputs_icl}.
Thus, it is critical to evaluate models across multiple axes.
We also present additional analysis, such as the performance of positional extrapolation methods (\S\ref{app:pos_emb}), the lost-in-the-middle phenomenon (\S\ref{app:depth}), and the performance of Claude (\S\ref{app:claude}).

\section{Related Works}

\paragraph{Long-context language models.}
Frontier models such as GPT-4 \citep{openai2023gpt4}, Gemini, and Claude claim to have expanded their context window beyond 100k tokens.
In the open-source community, there are also efforts to train models with longer input lengths \citep{dubey2024llama3herdmodels,fu2024data,ai2024yi}, explore position  extrapolation techniques \citep{peng2024yarn,chen2023extending,ding2024longropeextendingllmcontext}, and experiment with efficient architectures \citep[][\emph{inter alia}]{gu2023mamba,dao2024transformersssmsgeneralizedmodels,bertsch2023unlimiformer,Beltagy2020Longformer,yen2024longcontext,lieber2024jambahybridtransformermambalanguage}.

\paragraph{Synthetic tasks.}
Synthetic tasks are often used to evaluate LCLMs since they can be procedurally generated, thereby enabling arbitrarily long input lengths and controlled ``needle'' placement \citep{tay2021long,Liu2023LostIT}.
In particular, Needle-in-a-Haystack \citep[NIAH;][]{gkamradt_llmtest_needleinahaystack_2024} inserts a ``needle'' at specific depths of a long essay (i.e., the haystack) and asks the model to recall the fact.
Recent works have expanded upon it and designed new procedures to test different aspects of LCLMs \citep{hsieh2024ruler,li2024needlebenchllmsretrievalreasoning,levy2024task,arora2023zoology,laban2024summaryhaystackchallengelongcontext,goldman2024reallylongcontextneed}.
However, they do not study how results on synthetic tasks transfer to real applications.
In contrast, we evaluate both synthetic and downstream tasks and investigate how they correlate with each other.
\paragraph{Long-context benchmarks.}
As discussed in the main paper, 
existing benchmarks either 
are limit to relatively short context lengths \citep{shaham-etal-2022-scrolls,shaham-etal-2023-zeroscrolls,li-etal-2024-loogle,an-etal-2024-l,bai-etal-2024-longbench,dong-etal-2024-bamboo} or lack rigorous evaluation methods \citep{zhang2024inftybenchextendinglongcontext,yuan2024lvevalbalancedlongcontextbenchmark}. 
Many works instead focus on specific domains, such as question answering \citep{wang2024novelqabenchmarkingquestionanswering,karpinska2024thousandpairsnovelchallenge,wang-etal-2024-ada}, in-context learning \citep{li2024longiclbench,bertsch2024incontext,xu2024stresstesting,anil2024many,agarwal2024manyshotincontextlearning}, summarization \citep{chang2024booookscore,kim2024fables,shen2022multilexsum}, or RAG \citep{lee2024longcontextlanguagemodelssubsume}.
In this work, we construct a comprehensive benchmark that tests across diverse downstream tasks at long input lengths and also present a unified comparison and analysis across 51 LCLMs. 





\section{Conclusion}


In this work, we first identify the shortcomings of long-context evaluation settings and existing benchmarks---over-reliance of synthetic tasks, low coverage of realistic applications, and unreliable metrics among others.
We seek to address these issues by constructing \ours, a application-centric benchmark with diverse domains and reliable evaluation settings.
We then present a comprehensive evaluation of 51 frontier LCLMs across multiple dimensions, including different tasks, input lengths, and model types.
Our analysis shows that synthetic tasks are poor predictors of downstream performance and different categories exhibit distinct capabilities and trends.
Thus, it is critical to evaluate models on a diverse set of real-world tasks. %
Furthermore, open-source models still lag behind closed-source models on complex tasks at long lengths. %
Finally, we hope that our benchmark and subsequent comprehensive evaluation can serve as a valuable resource for future research in long-context language modeling.
