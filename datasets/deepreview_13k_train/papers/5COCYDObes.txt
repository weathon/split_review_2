To this end, we introduce a decision-making framework  which learns to ask pertinent \textit{questions} or perform introspection, performs CoT reasoning and then learns to take the best actions in the environment. {Concretely}, at times $t=0,1,\ldots$, a prompt $p_t$ is generated by the prompt policy i.e. $p_t\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|o_{t},\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0})$. The prompt is then used by an LLM to trigger a CoT process whose output is a thought $\upsilon_{t}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{T}}$. Therefore, the sequence of events proceeds as follows:
\newline\textbf{1.} At time $t=0,1,\ldots$ the system is at an environment state $s_t\in \mathcal{S}$. 
\newline \textbf{2.} A prompt $p_t$ is produced by the prompt policy i.e. $p_t\sim\pi_\phi(\cdot|o_t,\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0})$. 
\newline \textbf{3.} An action $a_{t}\sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|o_t,\upsilon_{t})$ is taken given the output of the CoT process $\upsilon_{t}\sim\pi^{\rm re}(p_t,x_t)$, $x_t$ is the observed situation of the current state {(for example, the things that the agent current holds on the Overcooked)}. 
\newline \textbf{4.} The environment state transitions according to $s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_t,a_{t})$.

To tackle the problem of learning how to generate prompts while learning the action-policy, we structure the problem as a leader-follower \textit{bilevel optimisation} \cite{colson2007overview}. This allows the prompt policy to learn how its decisions affect the action policy while the action policy and prompt policy learn concurrently. In this way, the prompt policy alters its output to guide the action policy in producing desirable actions while the action policy learns both how to interpret the CoT outputs and take desirable actions. Since LLMs already contain a vast amount of world knowledge, we here fix the LLM that performs the CoT reasoning, that is we assume that $\pi^{\rm re}$ is pretrained and fixed. We update the prompt policy and action policy. The aim of the prompt policy is to generate prompts minimise the uncertainty of the action policy. The optimisation objective can be expressed as a bilevel optimisation problem:
\begin{equation}    
\begin{split}
    (\pi^*_\theta,\pi^*_\phi) &\in \argmax_{(\pi_\theta,\pi_\phi)\in \Pi_\theta\times\Pi_\phi} \E_{\pi_\theta,\pi_\phi,\upsilon_t\sim\pi^{\rm re}}\\
&\left[-\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^t\mathcal{H}^{\pi_\theta}(y_{t})|y_{t}=(o_t,\upsilon_{t})\right] \nonumber\\
    \text{s.t.}\; \pi^*_\theta &\in \argmax_{\pi_\theta\in \Pi_\theta}\:\E_{\pi_\theta,p_t\sim\pi_\phi,\pi^{\rm re}}\left[\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma_I^{t}r_{t} \right],\; \\
\end{split} 
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{H}^{\pi_\theta}(y_{t})=\sum_{a_t\in\mathcal{A}}\pi_\theta(a_t|y_{t})\operatorname{log}\pi_\theta(a_t|y_{t})$, $y_{t}=(o_t,\upsilon_{t})$
which is the entropy of the policy $\pi_\theta$ and 
$\gamma_I,\gamma\in [0,1)$ are the discount factors for the action and prompt generation policies respectively and $r_t\sim \mathcal{R}$ is the environment reward. \color{black}
{Here, we explain the bilevel optimisation: \\ \textbf{In the inner loop}, the action policy $\pi_\theta$ learns to understand and incorporating the CoT reasoning containing high-level task-solving knowledge to perform optimally, i.e, maximising environment reward. \\
\textbf{In the outer loop}, the prompt policy $\pi_\phi$ aims to minimise the entropy of the action policy. It learns to generate appropriate prompts that subsequently leads CoT reasoning, enabling the action policy to take high-performing actions more certainly.%learns to produce appropriate prompt and subsequent CoT reasoning such that the action policy is able to takes high-performing actions as well as performs more certainly due to the incorpration high-level task-solving knowledge. 
}
Note that the bilevel aspect incorporates the nested nature of the optimisation \cite{colson2007overview,dempe2002foundations} --- in order to find the optimal prompt, the prompt policy must take into account the anticipated behaviour of both the LLM $\pi^{\rm re}$ and the action policy $\pi_\theta$ and thereafter make its choice accordingly. 


 





\section{Methodology}

In this section, we describe the training procedure of the proposed Bilevel framework. The prompt policy is optimised via the policy gradient with the behavior of action policy as a reward. The action policy is optimised by PPO \cite{schulman2017proximal} updater, which benefits from avoiding human-crafted engineering when grounding the CoT reasoning to executable actions. In the bilevel framework, the prompt policy and action policy are concurrently optimised until convergence. The overall bilevel framework is illustrated in \fig \ref{fig:illustration}. 
\begin{algorithm}[th!]
\caption{ $\ourmethod$}
\label{alg:bilevel}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \REQUIRE Initialise parameters of policies $\pi_\theta$, $\pi_\phi$.  Set the data buffer $D=\emptyset$. %Let the length of trajectory $k=1$.
    \ENSURE $\pi^*_\theta$, and $\pi^*_\phi$. 
    \WHILE{not done}
        \STATE \#Rollout trajectories with $\pi_\theta, \pi^{\rm re}, \pi_\phi$.
        \FOR{$i=1,2,..,\text{step}$}
            \STATE Generate prompt given historical observations: 
            $p_t \sim \pi_\phi(\cdot|o_{t},\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0}).$
            \STATE Perform CoT reasoning given prompt and state situation: $\upsilon_{t}\sim \pi^{\rm re}(\cdot|p_t,x_t)$.
            \STATE Sample action according to the CoT reasoning and observation:
             $a_{t} \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot|o_t,\upsilon_{t}).$
            \STATE %Apply action $a_{t}$ to the environment, sample reward $r_{t}\sim\mathcal{R}(s_t,a_{t})$. The next state according to $ s_{t+1}\sim \mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_t,a_{t})$. The next step observation is $o_{t+1}\sim T(\cdot|s_{t+1})$.
            Apply action $a_{t}$ to the environment, sample the reward $r_{t}$ and next step observation $o_{t+1}$. %The next state according to $ s_{t+1}\sim \mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_t,a_{t})$. The next step observation is $o_{t+1}\sim T(\cdot|s_{t+1})$.
            \STATE Calculate the entropy of the action policy: $h_{t}=\mathcal{H}\left(\pi_\theta(\cdot|s_t,\upsilon_{t})\right).$ %\DM{is this needed? also writing equals seems a little odd}
            \STATE Add to data buffer: $D=D\cup{(o_t,p_t,\upsilon_{t},a_{t},r_{t},h_{t},o_{t+1})}$
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Update the action policy $\pi_\theta$ via PPO to optimise Eq. (\ref{eq:ppo_objective}).
        \STATE Update the prompt policy $\pi_\phi$ by policy gradient following Eq. (\ref{eq:pg_gradient}).

        \color{black}

\paragraph{prompt policy training via policy gradient.}
CoT reasoning with LLMs has proven to be effective in aiding decision-making when well-designed prompts are used \citep{zhang2023proagent, park2023generative}. However, the quality of CoT reasoning heavily depends on the quality of prompts, which are typically manually designed by humans \citep{zhang2023proagent, shah2023navigation}. In traditional Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks such as sentiment classification \citep{pang2002thumbs} and news classification \citep{rana2014news}, prompts are usually provided through sets of input-output pairs, i.e., in-context learning examples. Unlike these NLP tasks with clearly defined input-output examples, the desired format of prompts varies across different decision-making tasks, often requiring substantial manual engineering.

{Due to the difficulty of training a model that is able to generate reasonable prompts from scratch automatically, we alternatively use pre-defined prompt candidates which can be obtained by human deliberately writing or being generated by GPT-3.5. In most of our experimental environments, we employ GPT-3.5 to generate a set of prompt candidates, where task descriptions are inputted into GPT-3.5 to produce task-relevant and critical prompt questions. % For most environments in our experiments, we use GPT-3.5 to generate prompt candidate set, where the task descriptions are inputted into GPT-3.5 to produce some task-relevant and critical prompt questions. 
{We also conduct an experiment to validate that, within our framework, the agent using prompt candidates generated by GPT-3.5 exhibits performance similar to that of the agent using prompts designed by humans. }}%We have also conducted an experiment about using GPT-3.5 to generate prompts, where the task description, state, and abstracted state situation are inputted into GPT-3.5 to produce some simple prompt questions about how to achieve the goal.} 
Examples of prompt candidates generated from GPT-3. 

{With a prompt candidate set $\mathcal{P}=\{p_1, p_2, \cdots p_K\}$, we train a prompt policy $\pi_\phi(\cdot|o_t,\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0})$ over the prompt candidates according to historical observations $o_t,\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0}$. 
Each of these natural language prompt candidates can be represented as a high-dimensional vector using a pre-trained and frozen Bert\cite{devlin2018bert} model. Denote the embedding of prompt candidate $p_i$ as $e_i$ and the embedding of the historical observations $(o_t,\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0})$ as $e_o=\mathcal{E}(o_t,\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0})$ with the encoder $\mathcal{E}$. The prompt embedding and the observation embedding are projected into the same vector space. Denote the mapped embedding as $\hat{e}_i=\mathcal{M}_p(e_i), \forall i=1\cdots K,\ \hat{e}_o=\mathcal{M}_o(e_o)$, where $\mathcal{M}_p$ and $\mathcal{M}_o$ are projectors. %for prompts and observation sequence respectively. 
During the decision-making process, the prompt policy estimates the probability of selecting a prompt candidate $p_i$ based on the dot product and softmax operators, mathematically written as: ${\exp(\hat{e}_i^\top\hat{e}_o)}/{\sum_j\exp(\hat{e}_j^\top\hat{e}_o})$. The prompt policy is updated via the policy gradient with the minus entropy of the action policy as a reward incentive and parameters of the observation encoder $\mathcal{E}$ and projectors $\mathcal{M}_p$, $\mathcal{M}_o$ are trainable. }\color{black}
The detailed procedure is described as below:
\newline
    \newline$\bullet$ For a decision-making task, we employ GPT-3.5, along with the provided task description, to generate appropriate prompt candidates. As a second case, we used human-crafted assists to generate valuable prompt candidates.
    \newline$\bullet$ With these $K$ prompts, the prompt policy is optimised to maximise the minus action policy entropy. %Because we hope the prompt can improve the certainty of action policy at each time step, we can view the outer loop as a one-step MDP, with the historical observation as state, the prompt as action, and the minus entropy of subsequent action policy as a reward. 
    The objective function is given by:
\begin{equation}    
\begin{split}
 \nonumber      &J_\phi(y|\pi_\theta,\pi_\phi,\pi^{\rm re})=\E_{\pi_\theta,\pi_\phi,\upsilon_{t}\sim\pi^{\rm re}}\left[-\sum_{t\geq 0}\gamma^t\mathcal{H}^{\pi_\theta}(y_{t})\right],
        \label{eq:pg_objective}
\end{split} 
\end{equation}
where $y_{t}=(o_t,\upsilon_{t})$ and $y_0=y$.
    \newline $\bullet$ We use the policy gradient \citep{lu2022dynamic} to optimise the prompt policy which obeys the following expression:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    &\nabla_\phi {J}_\phi(y|\pi_\theta,\pi_\phi,\pi^{\rm re})
    \approx\\
    &\frac{1}{N} \sum_{t\geq0} \nabla_\phi\log\pi_\phi(p_t|o_t,\ldots,o_{t-j\wedge 0}) \hat{R}^o_t(\tau).
\label{eq:pg_gradient}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
$\hat{R}^o_t(\tau)= -\sum_{i\geq t}\left[\gamma^{i-t}\mathcal{H}^{\pi_\theta}(y_{i})|y_{i}=(o_i,\upsilon_{i}), \upsilon_{i}\sim\pi^{\rm re}\right]$ is the return-to-go from step $t$ to the end of a trajectory. The gradient is approximated by $N$ trajectories sampled from polices $\pi_\theta$, $\pi_\phi$ and $\pi^{\rm re}$.

\textbf{Bilevel Optimisation.}
In our leader-follower Bilevel LLM framework, the prompt policy and the action policies are trained alternately, with the other policy being kept frozen. On one hand, the prompt policy selects a prompt for the CoT process, the outputs of which are expected to assist the action policy in solving complex tasks. Thus, the goal of the prompt policy is to reduce the uncertainty of the action policy when it encounters challenging {scenarios}. In practical terms, the objective is to minimise the entropy of the action policy. On the other hand, the action policy is trained to effectively solve specific decision-making tasks while benefiting from CoT reasoning and the experience gathered during exploration. The overall training process of the Bilevel framework is detailed in \alg \ref{alg:bilevel}.

\textbf{Reasoning with LLMs.}
Previous studies have confirmed that stage-by-stage reasoning significantly enhances the capability of LLMs to solve complex tasks such as mathematical and logistic reasoning problems. CoT\citep{wei2022chain} prompts containing a series of intermediate reasoning steps is shown to improve the inference ability of LLMs. Self-consistency \citep{wang2022self} marginalizes over several independent CoT results and then selects the most consistent answer. PAL \citep{gao2023pal} integrates executable programs into the CoT reasoning, addressing computation-related problems.
Besides using the prior world knowledge contained in LLMs, {ReAct \citep{yao2022react}, Tree-of-Thought (ToT) \citep{yao2023tree} and RAP \citep{hao2023reasoning} make use of from external environments or internal LLMs to produce reasoning traces.} 
ToT \citep{yao2023tree} and RAP  \citep{hao2023reasoning} explore extensively compared to CoT, which encompass multiple reasoning paths and construct a reasoning tree to determine the next crucial action. 
In this work, LLMs are applied to address multi-step decision-making problems, such as the Overcooked, where human-like solution reasoning is essential for taking actions.
\noindent\textbf{LLMs for RL.}
Due to the impressive reasoning capabilities of humans and the wealth of knowledge preceding LLMs, a series of studies have attempted to incorporate LLMs into planning algorithms to address decision-making tasks. ICPI \citep{brooks2022context} solves a number of simple interactive RL tasks (such as Maze) without the need for expert demonstrations or gradient computations, which uses LLMs as the world model and the rollout policy with historical interactions as in context examples. \citet{chen2023introspective} leverage historical trajectories to prompt LLM to generate the next step actions on the TextWorld game. 
GFlan\citep{carta2023grounding} aims to ground the LLM Flan-T5 \citep{rae2021scaling} on solving a textual interactive task named BabyAI-Text. %, i.e., a  extension of BabyAI platform \citep{chevalier2018babyai}. %which can be converted into textual RL settings via the extended BabyAI platform \citep{chevalier2018babyai}, 
In this approach, Flan-T5 serves as the action policy and is fine-tuned via PPO \citep{schulman2017proximal}.
 LFG \citep{shah2023navigation} utilises an LLM with a polling strategy to recommend and subsequently rank subgoals.
In our work, we integrate complex CoT reasoning into RL to enhance the quality of actions while eliminating the need for meticulous engineering to interpret LLM outputs.

\noindent\textbf{Entropy in RL.}
Entropy has been used extensively in RL as a tool for regularisation~\citep{mnih2016asynchronous,marl-book}. The policy in actor-critic methods is often trained with an additional term that aims to maximise the entropy of the learned actions, with the goal of exploring the environment without having a policy collapse early to suboptimal actions~\citep[e.g.][]{mnih2016asynchronous}.
A more formal use of entropy is explored in maximum entropy reinforcement learning~\citep{haarnoja2018soft, eysenbach2021maximum}, where the optimisation objective aims to learn the optimal policy that has the maximum entropy.
In this work, we take a different approach, and look at finding prompts that minimise the entropy of the action policy. Intuitively, this would push the CoT process to provide reasoning that makes the policy sure about its action. Such minimization of the entropy has also been explored: \citet{zhang2021hierarchical} formulate a hierarchical approach to intrinsic options, where entropy is minimised to improve the option sub-trajectories, and \citet{allahverdyan2018adaptive} consider entropy for decision making in the exploration-exploitation trade-off.

 
\noindent\textbf{Automated Prompt Engineering.}
The quality of prompts plays a crucial role in determining the output quality of LLMs. {Many works hand-craft desirable prompts such as the Generative Agents \citep{park2023generative} and ProAgent \citep{zhang2023proagent}. Apart from completely using human-crafted prompts, there are other studies that adopt different degrees of automation when generating meaningful prompts. For example, APE \citep{zhou2022large} and DLN \citep{sordoni2023deep} generate prompts from multiple examples and utilise LLM to rank the prompt candidates. PromptPG \citep{lu2022dynamic} trained an additional prompt selection network using the policy gradient, where the network generates the probability distribution over a predefined set of prompt examples. %\textcolor{red}
{We also aim to minimise human-labor on prompt engineering, we therefore preset a group of prompts and let the prompt policy choose depending on the environment observations.}}


\section{Conclusion}

We introduce \ourmethod, a bilevel framework that is capable of learning introspective questions (in the form of prompts), then performing complex reasoning for guiding actions executed by an action-policy. 
The bilevel nature of the framework enables the accommodation of separate objectives for the two learning components, namely the prompt-generation policy uses an action-policy entropy minimisation objective which enables it to induce unambiguous and useful prompts to be fed to the action-policy. Meanwhile, the action-policy learns how to perform actions in the environment while making use of the CoT thoughts which it learns to interpret. We showed that this leads to a powerful framework that outperforms leading baselines in complex benchmark environments. 
We believe our framework takes an important step towards generalist artificial intelligence that is capable of introspection and complex decision-making. 
In this work, we only explore our framework on solving single-agent decision-making tasks, but neglect the prevalent multi-agent setting.
