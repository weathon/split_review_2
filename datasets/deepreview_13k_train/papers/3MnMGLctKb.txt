\title{Generating Multi-Modal and Multi-Attribute Single-Cell Counts with CFGen}

\begin{abstract}
 Generative modeling of single-cell RNA-seq data has shown invaluable potential in community-driven tasks such as trajectory inference, batch effect removal and gene expression generation. However, most recent deep models generating synthetic single cells from noise operate on pre-processed continuous gene expression approximations, ignoring the inherently discrete and over-dispersed nature of single-cell data, which limits downstream applications and hinders the incorporation of robust noise models. Moreover, crucial aspects of deep-learning-based synthetic single-cell generation remain underexplored, such as controllable multi-modal and multi-label generation and its role in the performance enhancement of downstream tasks. This work presents Cell Flow for Generation (CFGen), a flow-based conditional generative model for multi-modal single-cell counts, which explicitly accounts for the discrete nature of the data. Our results suggest improved recovery of crucial biological data characteristics while accounting for novel generative tasks such as conditioning on multiple attributes and boosting rare cell type classification via data augmentation. By showcasing CFGen on a diverse set of biological datasets and settings, we provide evidence of its value to the fields of computational biology and deep generative models. 
 \looseness=-1 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Single-cell transcriptomics has enabled unprecedented exploration of cellular heterogeneity, unveiling essential biological processes and fine-grained cellular states~\cite{hwang2018single, RozenblattRosen2017}. Advances in the single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) technology allow parallel gene expression profiling in millions of cells, leading to large, complex datasets that investigate cellular differentiation~\cite{Gulati2020, Rizvi2017}, disease progression~\cite{Zeng2019, Chung2017}, and response to drug perturbation~\cite{ji2021machine}. Aware of the multi-faceted complexity of the molecular state of a cell, modern studies seek to complement the quantification of single-cell gene expression with additional measurements, such as information on the accessibility state of DNA regions encoding for molecular functions like gene expression and regulation~\cite{Lowe2019, baysoy2023technological}. This accessibility is measured with the Transposase-Accessible Chromatin assay (ATAC-seq)~\cite{grandi2022chromatin}, where open regions are referred to as \textit{peaks}. Multi-modal single-cell datasets, which offer a broader snapshot of the biological state of cellular populations, have grown recently. Yet, technical bias and high experimental costs still hinder the homogeneous profiling of all possible cell states within the inspected biological process. In light of this, generative modeling offers a promising avenue to highlight underexplored biological states, drive scientific hypotheses, and enhance downstream applications \cite{Lopez2018}.\looseness=-1

Generative models for single-cell data, in particular Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), have been extensively employed in representation learning~\cite{Lopez2018, palma2023modelling}, perturbation prediction~\cite{lotfollahi2019scgen, Lotfollahi2023, NEURIPS2022_aa933b5a} and trajectory inference \cite{Gayoso2023, Chen2022, Farrell2023}. Recently, more complex approaches leveraging diffusion-based models~\cite{luo2024scdiffusion} or Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)~\cite{marouf2020realistic} have paved the way for the task of synthetic data generation, demonstrating promising performance on rare cell type augmentation and realistic single-cell data modeling. Single-cell data is inherently discrete, as gene expression is collected as the number of transcribed gene copies found experimentally. Due to the incompatibility of discrete data with continuous models such as Gaussian diffusion \cite{10.1145/3626235}, most approaches generate data that has been pre-processed through normalization and scaling. This limits their flexibility to support downstream tasks centered around raw counts, such as batch correction \cite{Lopez2018}, the use of differential expression tools \cite{deseq2}, and analyses where the total number of transcripts per cell is important \cite{Gulati2020}. Additionally, technical and biological effects in single-cell counts have been formalized under effective discrete noise models \cite{Hafemeister2019}, which should be incorporated into generative models for single-cell data to approximate the underlying data generation process better.

In this work, we present Cell Flow for Generation (CFGen) (\cref{fig: concept_fig}), a conditional flow-based generative model designed to reproduce multi-modal single-cell discrete counts realistically. Our approach combines the expressiveness of recent Flow Matching techniques~\cite{albergo2022building, liu2022flow, lipman2022flow, dao2023flow, tong2023improving}
with modeling the statistical properties of single-cell data across multiple modalities, each following a distinct discrete likelihood model. Moreover, we extend the current literature on Flow Matching by introducing the concept of \textit{compositionality}, enabling the generation of cells conditioned on single attributes or combinations thereof in a controlled setting.\looseness=-1

We demonstrate the value of our approach in terms of generative performance improvement and the enhancement of downstream tasks, such as data augmentation for rare cell type classification. In summary, we introduce the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}[topsep=0pt,itemsep=0pt,partopsep=0pt,parsep=\parskip,leftmargin=20pt]
    \item We propose CFGen, a generative model for discrete multi-modal single-cell counts, which explicitly accounts for key statistical properties of single-cell data under a specified noise model. 
    \item We extend the Flow Matching framework to incorporate guidance for compositional generation under multiple attributes. 
    \item We demonstrate our model's full-genome generative performance on a diverse set of biological datasets compared to existing single-cell generative models. 
    \item We showcase potential applications of CFGen to data augmentation to improve the generalization of cell type classification models. 
\end{itemize}


\section{Related work}

The synthetic generation of single-cell datasets is a well-established research direction pioneered by models using standard probabilistic methods to estimate gene-wise parameters in a single modality~\cite{zappia2017splatter, li2019statistical} or multiple modality setting~\cite{song2024scdesign3}. With the advent of deep generative models, VAE-based approaches have demonstrated an extremely flexible performance, offering popular tools for batch correction \cite{Lopez2018}, modality integration~\cite{gayoso2021joint}, trajectory inference \cite{Gayoso2023} and perturbation prediction~\cite{lotfollahi2019scgen, Lotfollahi2023, NEURIPS2022_aa933b5a}. Despite their relevance, most of the mentioned approaches orient towards learning meaningful cellular representations or counterfactual predictions rather than generating synthetic datasets from noise. Such a task has instead been extensively explored by other works leveraging the expressive potential of diffusion models~\cite{luo2024scdiffusion}, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)~\cite{marouf2020realistic} and Large Language Models (LLMs) \cite{Levine2023} to produce realistic cells approximating the observed data distribution. Our technical contribution builds upon Flow Matching \cite{albergo2022building, liu2022flow, lipman2022flow}, an efficient formulation of continuous normalizing flows for generative modeling. Since its release, Flow Matching has been successfully applied to optimal transport \cite{tong2023improving, eyring2023unbalancedness, pooladian2023multisample}, protein generation \cite{jing2024alphafold, yim2023fast}, interpolation on general geometries \cite{chen2023riemannian,  kapusniak2024metric} and guided conditional generation \cite{zheng2023guided}. Finally, Flow Matching showed promising performance in tasks involving scRNA-seq, such as learning cellular evolution across time~\cite{tong2023improving, kapusniak2024metric} and responses to drugs \cite{klein_genot}.

\section{Background}

\subsection{Deep generative modeling of single-cell data}
Single-cell data is inherently discrete. At the gene expression level, cells are high-dimensional vectors of counts, where each feature represents a gene with, as a measurement, the number of transcripts detected in a cell. Additionally, technical bias and biological variation result in cells displaying unique statistical characteristics, such as \textit{sparsity} and \textit{over-dispersion}. Sparsity derives from the absence of expression of genes in a given cellular state (biological cause) or measurement dropouts in scRNA-seq (technical cause). Over-dispersion entails that the gene-wise variance is higher than the mean. From a probabilistic perspective, it is common to model over-dispersed counts with a Negative Binomial (NB) distribution parameterized by a mean $\mu$ and an inverse dispersion parameter $\theta$.

Formally, given a nonnegative count expression matrix $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N \times G}$ with $N$ cells and $G$ genes, entries $x_{ng}$ of the expression matrix are assumed to follow the NB model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq: distr_nb}
    x_{ng} \sim \mathrm{NB}(\mu_{ng}, \theta_g) \: ,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{ng} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is a cell-gene-specific mean and $\theta_g\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ the gene-specific inverse dispersion. 
In other words, we assume each cell has an individual mean expression, while the over-dispersion property is modeled for each gene.

When scRNA-seq is coupled with information on DNA accessibility, transcription measurements are complemented by a binary matrix $\mathbf{B}\in \{0,1\}^{N \times P}$, where $P$ is the number of DNA regions profiled for accessibility. Here, each dimension independently follows the Bernoulli model $b_{np} \sim \mathrm{Bern}(\pi_{np})$, with $\pi_{np}$ indicating a cell-gene-specific success probability. 

In most single-cell representation learning settings, a deep latent variable model is trained to map a latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ to the parameter space $\mathcal{H}$ of the noise model via a decoder maximizing the log-likelihood of the data. In practice, given a latent cell state $\z$, the likelihood parameters of each modality are inferred as 
\begin{align}
    \bm{\mu}=l  \bm{\rho}\:, 
    \quad
    \bm{\rho} = \mathrm{softmax}(h_\psi(\z))\:,
    \quad
    \bm{\pi} = \mathrm{sigmoid}(h_\phi(\z))\:,\label{eq: mu_model_2}
\end{align}
where $h_\psi$ and $h_\phi$ are modality-specific decoders and $l$ is the size factor, defined as the total number of counts of the generated cell. Notably, the vector $\bm{\rho}$ represents gene expression proportions. One can interpret the parameterization of the scRNA-seq likelihood based on the connection between the negative binomial and Poisson-gamma distributions (see \cref{sec: gamma_poisson_negative_binomial}).
\subsection{Continuous normalizing flows and Flow Matching}\label{sec: cnf_and_fm}
\textbf{Continuous Normalizing Flows (CNF).} \citet{chen2018neural} introduced CNFs as a generative model to approximate complex data distributions. Given data in a continuous domain $\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^d$, one can define a time-dependent probability path $p:[0,1]\times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$, transforming a tractable prior density $p_0$ into a more complex one $p_1$, where we indicate the probability path at time $t$ as $p_t : \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$ such that $\int p_t(\z) \, \textrm{d}\z=1$. The probability path is formally \textit{generated} by a time-dependent vector field, $u_t: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ with $t\in [0,1]$, if the continuity equation $\frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t} =  - \nabla \cdot (p_t u_t)$ holds. The field $u_t$ is the time-derivative of an invertible diffeomorphism $\phi_t: [0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$, also called \textit{flow}, following the Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):
\begin{align}\label{eq: cnf_ode}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} \phi_t(\z) = u_t(\phi_t(\z)), \quad \phi_0(\z)=\z \,.
\end{align}
Learning a CNF means defining a push-forward operator $p_t=[\phi_t]_*p_0$, transforming the prior $p_0$ into the data density $p_1$. In other words, learning the \textit{target vector field} $u_t$ generating $p_t$ allows transporting samples from the prior distribution to realistic data samples by simulating \cref{eq: cnf_ode} in time. \looseness=-1

\textbf{Flow Matching \cite{lipman2022flow}.} Assume the goal is to model a complex data distribution $q$ by learning the target vector field described in \cref{sec: cnf_and_fm}. One can marginalize the probability path $p_t$ as $p_t(\z) = \int p_t(\z | \z_1) q(\z_1)\textrm{d}\z_1$, where $\z_1$ indicates a sample from the data distribution $q$ and $p_t(\cdot | \z_1)$ is the \textit{conditional probability path} transporting noise to $\z_1$ under the boundary conditions $p_0(\z|\z_1)=p_0(\z)$ and $p_1(\z|\z_1)\approx\delta(\z-\z_1)$. Here, $\delta$ represents a delta probability with mass concentrated at the point $\mathbf{z}_1$. 
Note that, at $t=1$, the marginal distribution $p_1$ approximates the data distribution $q$.
Moreover, $p_t(\z)$ is generated by a  \textit{marginal velocity field} $u_t(\z)$ satisfying 
\looseness=-1
\begin{equation}\label{eq: marginal_field}
    u_t(\z) = \int u_t(\z | \z_1) \,\frac{p_t(\z|\z_1)q(\z_1)}{p_t(\z)}\,\textrm{d}\z_1 ,
\end{equation} 
where $u_t(\z | \z_1)$ is called \textit{conditional vector field}. 
While directly regressing $u_t(\z)$ is intractable, \citet{lipman2022flow} show that minimizing the Flow Matching objective 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: loss}
    \mathcal{L}_{\textrm{FM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1], q(\z_1), p_t(\z|\z_1)}\!\left[||v_{t, \xi}(\z) - u_t(\z|\z_1) ||^2\right]
\end{equation} 
corresponds to learning to approximate the marginal vector field $u_t(\mathbf{z})$ with the time-conditioned neural network $v_{t,\xi}$. Defining $p_t(\z|\z_1)=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_t\z_1, \sigma_t^2\textbf{I})$ with the functions $\alpha_t, \sigma_t$ controlling the noise schedule, $u_t(\z|\z_1)$ has a closed form, and \cref{eq: loss} is tractable (see \cref{sec: fm_gau_paths} for more details). We define such a formulation \textit{Gaussian marginal paths}.

\textbf{Classifier-free guidance \cite{zheng2023guided}.}
One can \textit{guide} data generation on a condition $y$ by learning the conditional marginal field $u_t(\z|y)$ via a neural network $v_{t, \xi}(\z|y)$. 
Given a guidance strength hyperparameter $\omega \in \mathbb{R}$, \citet{zheng2023guided} show that generating data points following the vector field $\Tilde{u}_t(\cdot| y) = (1-\omega)u_{t}(\cdot) + \omega u_{t}(\cdot|y)$ approximates sampling from the distribution $\Tilde{q}(\z|y) \propto q(\z)^{1-\omega}q(\z | y)^\omega$, where $q(\mathbf{z})$ and $q(\mathbf{z}|y)$ are, respectively, the unconditional and conditional data distributions. Guidance can thus be achieved by combining conditional and unconditional vector fields learned simultaneously during training.\looseness=-1


\section{CFGen}

Our objective is to define a latent Flow-Matching-based generative model for discrete single-cell data, where each cell is measured through gene expression and DNA accessibility. Our model, CFGen, is flexible: It can handle single and multiple modalities. Moreover, it supports guiding generation conditioned on single or combinations of attributes without needing to train a different model for each. In what follows, we present the assumptions and generative process formulation in the uni-modal and multi-modal settings. We additionally introduce our approach to compositional guidance.\looseness=-1
\subsection{Uni-modal and single-attribute generation}\label{sec: unimodal_single_att}
Let $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N \times G}$ be a single-cell matrix where we indicate an observed single-cell vector as $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{N}_0^{G}$, with $G$ being the number of genes. Additionally, let $\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{N}_0^{N}$ be a categorical attribute (for example, cell type) associated with each observation. We also define  $l=\sum_{g=1}^G x_g$ as the size factor of an individual cell $\mathbf{x}$. 

\textbf{Standard formulation of the generative process.} When the technical bias is negligible, we define the standard CFGen setting as the following generative model:
\begin{equation}\label{eq: cell_flow_generative}
    p(\x, \z, l, y) = p(\x | \z, l)p(\z | y, l)p(l)p(y) \; ,
\end{equation}
where $\z$ is a continuous latent variable modeling the cell state, and we assumed that (1) $\x$ is independent of $y$ conditionally on $\z$ and $l$, and (2) $l$ is independent of $y$. Crucially, \cref{eq: cell_flow_generative} provides a standard formulation for the generative process, but the factorization choice is flexible and can be adjusted based on the properties of the data (e.g. when the size factor and conditioning covariate are not independent). We provide a flexible implementation supporting said variations. 

\textbf{Modeling the distributions in \cref{eq: cell_flow_generative}.} Each factor of \cref{eq: cell_flow_generative} is modeled separately: $p(y)$ is a categorical distribution $\mathrm{Cat}(N_y, \bm{\pi}_y)$ where $N_y$ is the number of categories and $\bm{\pi}_y$ a vector of $N_y$ class probabilities, and $p(l) = \mathrm{LogNormal}(\mu_l, \sigma^2_l)$. The parameters of $p(y)$ and $p(l)$ can be learned as maximum likelihood estimates over the dataset (see \cref{sec: sampling_from_noise}). Given an attribute class $y$ and size factor $l$ sampled from the respective distributions, $p(\z |y,l)$ is a conditional normalizing flow learned via Flow Matching with Gaussian marginal paths (see \cref{sec: cnf_and_fm}, \cref{sec: fm_gau_paths} and \cref{sec: flow_model} for the architecture details) transporting samples from a standard Gaussian distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ to latent cell representations, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix. Finally, given a latent variable $\z$ and a size factor $l$, $p(\x | \z, l)$ is a negative binomial distribution with mean parameterized by a decoder $h_\psi$ as in \cref{eq: mu_model_2} and inverse dispersion modeled by a global parameter $\bm{\theta}$. In practice, $h_\psi$ and $\bm{\theta}$ are optimized before training the flow, together with an encoder $f_\eta$ that maps the data to a latent space (more details in \cref{sec: encoder_model}). 

\textbf{Sampling in practice.} Following the standard formulation of CFGen in \cref{eq: cell_flow_generative}, to generate a cell we first sample a size factor $l$ and a condition $y$ (the latter can be chosen to generate from a specific class). Successively, we derive $\mathbf{z}_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$ and simulate a real latent data point via
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_1 = \mathbf{z}_0 + \int_0^1 v_{t, \xi}(\mathbf{z}_t| y, l) \mathrm{d}t \: ;
\end{equation}
where $v_{t, \xi}(\mathbf{z}_t| y, l)$ is a parameterized vector field learned through Flow Matching. Finally, $\mathbf{z}_1$ is run through the decoder $h_\psi$ and a cell vector is obtained by sampling $\mathrm{NB}(l \, \mathrm{softmax}(h_\psi(\mathbf{z}_1)), \bm{\theta})$. 

\textbf{Size factor as a technical effect.} When $l$ is influenced by technical effect under a categorical covariate $c \in \{1, \ldots, C\}$, we reformulate \cref{eq: cell_flow_generative} as 
$p(\x, \z, l, y) = \frac{1}{C} \sum_c p(\x | \z, l)p(\z | y, l)p(l|c)p(y)p(c)$,
where we assume that $\z$ is independent of $c$ given $l$ (i.e., $l$ contains all necessary technical effect information to guide the flow), and $y$ is independent of $c$. The last assumption derives from our choice of $y$ as an attribute encoding biological identity preserved across technical batches.
\subsection{Multi-modal and single-attribute generation}\label{sec: multimodal_setting} 
Let $\mathbf{X}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ be defined as in \cref{sec: unimodal_single_att}. In the multi-modal setting, we have additional access to a binary matrix $\mathbf{B}\in \{0,1\}^{N \times P}$ representing DNA region accessibility, with $P$ being the number of measured peaks. Each sample is, therefore, a tuple ($\x$, $\mathbf{b}$, $y$), where $\x$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are realizations of different discrete noise models (negative binomial and Bernoulli). Following \cref{eq: mu_model_2}, both parameters of the negative binomial and Bernoulli likelihoods are functions of the same latent variable $\z$, encoding a continuous cell state shared across modalities. We write the likelihood term in \cref{eq: cell_flow_generative} as 
\begin{equation}
    p(\x, \mathbf{b} | \z, l) \numeq{1} p(\x | \z, l)p(\mathbf{b} | \z, l) \ \numeq{2} p(\x | \z, l)p(\mathbf{b} | \z) \;
\end{equation}
where in (1) we use the fact that the likelihood of $\x$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are optimized disjointedly given $\z$, and in (2) that $\mathbf{b}$ is independent of the size factor $l$ (see \cref{eq: mu_model_2}). 
In simple terms, all the modalities are encoded to the same latent space $\mathcal{Z}$ used to train the conditional flow approximating $p(\z|y, l)$ in \cref{eq: cell_flow_generative} (more details in \cref{sec: encoder_model}). During generation, separate decoders $h_\psi$ and $h_\phi$ map a sampled latent state $\z$ to the parameter spaces of the negative binomial and Bernoulli distributions, representing expression counts and binary DNA accessibility information, respectively (\cref{fig: concept_fig}). 

\subsection{Guided compositional generation with multiple attributes}\label{sec: multi_attr_guidance}
We extend the concept of classifier-free guidance (CFG) for Flow Matching \cite{zheng2023guided} to account for multiple attributes, which is useful in applications to enhance control of the generative process towards specific data regions. This is relevant in scRNA-seq, where datasets are usually characterized by several covariates encoding biological and technical information. Here, $\mathbf{Y} \in \mathbb{N}_0^{N \times K}$ is an integer matrix of $K$ categorical attributes. Instead of learning a conditional flow model for every combination of attributes as a separate conditioning class, we propose a flexible approach to generate cells from arbitrary attribute subsets by composing multiple flow models conditioned on single attributes.

Let $q(\z | \y)$ be the conditional data distribution, with $\y=(y_1,\ldots,y_ K)$ being a collection of observed categorical attributes. In analogy with CFG in diffusion models \cite{ho2022classifier}, we aim to implement a generative model to sample from $\Tilde{q}(\z|\y) \propto q(\z)\prod_{i=1}^{K} \left[\frac{q(\z|y_i)}{q(\z)}\right]^{\omega_i}$, where $\omega_i$ is the guidance strength for attribute $i$ (see \cref{sec: proof_prop}). 
Diffusion models generate data by learning to approximate the score of the time-dependent distribution, $\nabla_\mathbf{z} \log p_t(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{y})$, with a neural network and using it to simulate a reverse diffusion Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE) transporting noise samples to generated data observations \cite{song2021scorebasedgenerativemodelingstochastic}. Importantly, the reverse diffusion SDE corresponds to a deterministic \textit{probability flow ODE} with the same time-marginal distributions \cite{yang2023diffusion}. 

Based on the relation between score-based diffusion and energy models, \citet{liu2022compositional} demonstrated that compositional classifier-free guidance is achievable through expressing the drift of the generating reverse SDE with the compositional score:
\begin{equation}\label{eq: comp_gudance_score}
    \nabla_{\z} \log\Tilde{p}_t(\z|\y) = \nabla_{\z} \log p_t(\z) + \sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{K}\omega_i[\nabla_{\z} \log p_t(\z | y_i) - \nabla_{\z} \log p_t(\z)] \: .
\end{equation}
Following the direct relationship between Flow Matching and CFG provided in \cite{ho2022classifier}, we build the Flow Matching counterpart to \cref{eq: comp_gudance_score}.

Provided that we have access to Flow Matching models for the unconditional marginal vector field $u_t(\z)$ and the single-attribute conditional fields $\{u_t(\z | y_i)\}_{i=0}^K$, we introduce the following proposition:
\looseness=-1

\begin{prop}\label{prop: prop_1}
If the attributes $y_1,...,y_K$ are conditionally independent given $\z$, the vector field 
\begin{equation}\label{eq: comp_gudance_vec_field}
    \Tilde{u}_t(\z | \y) = u_t(\z) +  \sum\nolimits_{i=1}^K \omega_i[u_t(\z|y_i)-u_t(\z)]
\end{equation}
coincides with the velocity of the probability-flow ODE associated with the generative SDE of a diffusion model with a compositional score as in \cref{eq: comp_gudance_score}. 
\end{prop}

We provide a proof for \cref{prop: prop_1} in \cref{sec: proof_prop}. In other words, the reversed diffusion SDE from compositional CFG admits a deterministic probability flow ODE with velocity as in \cref{eq: comp_gudance_vec_field}. Consequently, classifier-free sampling from compositions of attributes is achievable by simulating the probability path $\Tilde{p}_t(\z|\y) \propto p_t(\z)\prod_{i=1}^{K} \left[\frac{p_t(\z|y_i)}{p_t(\z)}\right]^{\omega_i}$ from a prior Gaussian density $p_0$ integrating the parameterized field $\Tilde{v}_{t, \xi}(\z | \y) = v_{t, \xi}(\z) +  \sum_{i=1}^K \omega_i[v_{t, \xi}(\z|y_i)-v_{t, \xi}(\z)]$. Note that both conditional and unconditional fields are parameterized by the same model, which is learned by providing single-attribute conditioning with a certain probability during training (see Algorithms \ref{alg:guided_flows_tr} and \ref{alg:sampling_guided_flows}). \looseness=-1


\section{Experiments}

The comparison is performed quantitatively in terms of distributional proximity between real and generated cells, and qualitatively by examining how well the compared generative models approximate real data properties. In \cref{sec: results - multiattribute}, we illustrate the effectiveness of multi-attribute generation to guide the synthetic samples to specific combinations of biological labels and donors. Finally, in \cref{sec: results - augmentation} we demonstrate that targeted data augmentation, using our generative model to increase the representation of rare cell types enhances the performance of LLM-based cell type classifiers.

In \cref{tab:performance_comparison}, we evaluate the generative performance of CFGen conditionally (c-CFGen) and unconditionally (u-CFGen) against three baselines on the scRNA-seq generation task. 

\textbf{Baselines.} We choose scVI~\cite{gayoso2021joint} and scDiffusion~\cite{luo2024scdiffusion} as conditional models and scGAN~\cite{marouf2020realistic} as unconditional baseline. The scVI model is based on a VAE architecture with a negative binomial decoder and performs generation by decoding low-dimensional Gaussian noise into parameters of the likelihood model. Conversely, scDiffusion and scGAN operate on a continuous-space domain, performing generation using standard latent diffusion \cite{Rombach_2022_CVPR} and GAN \cite{gans} models. Thus, we train them using normalized counts (more in \cref{sec: baseline_descr}).

\textbf{Datasets.} We evaluate the performance of the models on four datasets with variable size: \textbf{(i)} PBMC3K\footnote{\url{https://satijalab.org/seurat/articles/pbmc3k_tutorial.html}} (3k Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells) measures the expression of 2,700 cells from a healthy donor clustering into 8 cell types. \textbf{(ii)} The Dentate gyrus dataset \cite{la2018rna} profiles the expression of $18,213$ cells from a developing mouse hippocampus. (\textbf{iii}) Tabula Muris \cite{tabula_muris2018} comprises the gene expression data of 245,389 cells from the mouse hippocampus across multiple tissues.  (\textbf{iv}) The Human Lung Cell Atlas (HLCA)~\cite{sikkema2023integrated}--previously unconsidered for generative modeling--is a vast cohort of 2.4 million cells from 486 individuals harvested from 49 datasets. Here, we use a size-controlled version of the dataset comprising 584,944 cells. We perform conditioning on the \textit{cell type} covariate on all datasets but the Tabula Muris, where we use the \textit{tissue} label. A description of dataset characteristics and pre-processing is provided in \cref{preproc} and \cref{tab: dataset_specifics}.

\textbf{Evaluation.} As evaluation metrics, we use distribution distances (MMD~\cite{borgwardt2006integrating} and Wasserstein-2 distance) computed between the Principal Component (PC) projections of real and generated data in 30 dimensions. The generated data is embedded using the PC loadings of the real data for comparability. For conditional models, we additionally evaluate the cell-type classification F1 score of a k-Nearest-Neighbors (kNN) classifier trained on real data and evaluated on generated cells. All evaluations are performed on a held-out set of cells, considering the whole genome, with a filtering step for low expression genes (see \cref{preproc}). 
\looseness=-1

\textbf{Results.} CFGen always reaches either the top or second-best performance on the conditional generation task and overcomes scGAN in five out of eight metrics measured across datasets. Qualitatively, CFGen is the only conditional model capable of generating plausible synthetic cells in terms of overlap with the real data distribution for large datasets such as the HLCA and Tabula Muris (see \cref{comp} and \cref{fig: comp_gen}). \looseness=-1

When dealing with biology, measuring realistic data generation goes beyond distribution matching metrics. The main methodological advantage of CFGen over diffusion or GAN-based single-cell generators is the definition of a generative model that explicitly considers the probabilistic properties of the data.
By learning a rigorous likelihood-based model of the discrete data (\cref{eq: cell_flow_generative}), we find that our generated cells are faithful to the real observations in terms of the following properties: 

\textbf{(1) Sparsity.} Caused by technical bias in the detection of gene transcripts or by genes being unexpressed in specific biological settings. \textbf{(2) Over-dispersion.} The gene-specific mean-variance trend of scRNA-seq is nonlinear; genes (especially when highly expressed) have higher variance than expected under a Poisson model (where mean and variance coincide) due to technical effects in transcript detection~\cite{Zhou2011}. The inverse dispersion parameter of a negative binomial distribution models this behavior. \textbf{(3) Discreteness and skewness.} scRNA-seq data is discrete and skewed towards zero-expression values (see \textbf{sparsity)}~\cite{Andrews2018}.

In \cref{tab: scRNA_seq_properties}, we provide qualitative evidence that using CFGen is more appropriate at recovering \textbf{(1)} and \textbf{(2)} than scDiffusion, which assumes a continuous data space. \textbf{(3)} naturally holds when modeling discrete counts with CFGen as opposed to a continuous model. In particular, \cref{tab: scRNA_seq_properties}a illustrates that explicitly modeling counts with gene-specific inverse dispersion ensures better overlap in the generated gene-wise mean-variance relationship with that of the real data. Additionally, in \cref{tab: scRNA_seq_properties}b, we demonstrate an almost perfect recovery of the real distribution of the number of zero counts per cell. At the same time, scDiffusion tends to produce an unrealistic shift towards actively expressed genes.\looseness=-1

\subsection{Multi-attribute generation and guidance}
\label{sec: results - multiattribute}

Here, we qualitatively evaluate our approach to the multi-attribute compositional guidance described in \cref{sec: multi_attr_guidance}. 

\textbf{Datasets.} We showcase guidance on datasets with extensive batch effects, as one could combine different levels of biological and technical annotations to either augment rare cell type and batch combinations or control for the amount of technical effect added in simulation settings. Specifically, we consider \textbf{(i)} The NeurIPS 2021 \cite{luecken2021sandbox} bone marrow datasets, comprising 90,261 annotated single cells from 12 healthy human donors across four sites. We use donor as a batch attribute and cell type as a biological covariate. We also consider \textbf{(ii)} the Tabular Muris dataset described in \cref{sec: comparison_existing_methods}, using tissue and Mouse ID as covariates. 

\textbf{Evaluation.} The power of our guidance model is to generate data conditionally on an arbitrary subset of attributes---including unconditional generation---using a \textit{single trained model}. For each pair of covariates $(y_i,y_j)$, we evaluate generation on 500 generated cells varying the parameter $\omega_j$, keeping $\omega_i$ fixed. The expected result is conditional generation on $\omega_i$ when $\omega_j=0$ and generation from the intersection between the two attributes as $\omega_j$ increases. We additionally test the unconditional model, given by $\omega_i=\omega_j=0$, expected to recover the whole single-cell dataset. In the unconditional case, we generate as many cells as there are in the dataset to better evaluate the coverage.

Unconditional generation faithfully recreates the original data (left-hand side) for both datasets. Setting guidance weights to zero for mouse ID and donor attributes leads to single-attribute conditional generation. Increasing the guidance weight steers the generation towards the intersection of the two attributes. Interestingly, different attribute pairs in different datasets require varying guidance strengths for optimal intersection.

\subsection{Application example: data augmentation to improve classification of rare cell types}
\label{sec: results - augmentation}

Finally, we propose a use case for CFGen: enhancing the generalization performance of cell-type classifiers by augmenting rare cell types in the dataset. Previous studies have demonstrated performance enhancement through leveraging large auxiliary datasets~\cite{richter2024delineating}. As a cell type classifier, we select scGPT~\cite{cui2024scgpt}, an LLM-inspired single-cell transformer pre-trained on a corpus of 33 million cells.\looseness=-1

\textbf{Datasets.} We evaluate our approach on two datasets: \textbf{(i)} the PBMC COVID dataset \cite{yoshida2022local} profiles 422,220 blood cells from 93 patients ranging across paediatric and adult. We also consider the \textbf{(ii)} HLCA dataset described in \cref{sec: comparison_existing_methods}. Both datasets are pre-processed by selecting 2000 highly variable features and leaving out cells from 20\% of the donors from training. 

\textbf{Evaluation.} We train CFGen on the PBMC COVID and HLCA data training sets. Then, we augment both datasets to 800,000 samples, upsampling rare cell types. More in detail, we assign to each cell type a probability $\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{ct}}}$, where $N_{\mathrm{ct}}$ is the total number of instances of said cell type. We then generate observations to cover the gap between the size of the dataset and 800,000 cells, sampling cell types proportionally to the assigned probabilities. 
\begin{wrapfigure}[18]{r}{0.48\textwidth}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{chapters/Figures/fig5.png}
  \caption{Cell-type classification difference as a function of cell type frequency before and after augmentation on the PBMC COVID and HLCA datasets. A 10-neighbor kNN classifier is trained on the feature space of a pre-trained scGPT model. The held-out set consists of cells from 20\% of the donors. }
  \label{fig: scgpt_exp}
\end{wrapfigure}
At the end of the procedure, 
we obtain a considerably higher number of observations for rare cell types. However, we still do not reach uniformity, as class imbalance may be biologically meaningful. Following the original publication, we then train kNN cell-type classifiers on scGPT's embeddings of the original and augmented training sets and evaluate the generalization performance on the held-out donors. For each cell type, we assess if performance increases upon augmentation as a function of its frequency in the dataset.  

\textbf{Results.} Results are displayed in \cref{fig: scgpt_exp} for the two datasets. Remarkably, most cell types in the held-out dataset are better classified after augmentation, suggesting that CFGen not only generates reliable cell samples but can be a valuable supplement to relevant downstream tasks. Moreover, the performance difference between before and after augmentation is inversely proportional to the frequency of the cell type in the dataset, with a Pearson correlation of $-0.31$ on the PBMC COVID and $-0.29$ on the HCLA datasets. Therefore, the generalization improvement is more accentuated for rare cell types.

\section{Discussion}

We presented CFGen, a conditional flow-based generative model for single-cell discrete data. CFGen combines state-of-the-art generative models with rigorous probabilistic considerations, incorporating established noise models for sampling realistic gene expression and DNA accessibility states. We demonstrated improved performance over existing generative models for single-cell data, showing more faithful reproduction of data sparsity and overdispersion than continuous counterparts. Moreover, we showed enhanced performance in comparison with established generative frameworks for multi-modal single-cell data considering gene expression and peak accessibility. Our core machine learning contribution extends classifier-free guidance in Flow Matching with compositional generation with multiple attribute instances, inspired by the necessity to control both biological and technical variation in the generation process. We also improved rare cell type classification in the scGPT's representation space through realistic augmentation of the reference set. A limitation of our framework is its reliance on multiple assumptions, including independence assumptions in the data, which may not hold for all biological settings and require prior consideration of the dataset properties. In other words, exploration of the data characteristics should be carried out before using CFGen for generation. Overall, CFGen represents a significant advancement in the simulation and augmentation of single-cell data, offering the research community powerful tools for generating realistic and varied datasets for improving performance on biological problems.\looseness=-1
