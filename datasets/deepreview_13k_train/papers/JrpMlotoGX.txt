\title{FactBench: A Dynamic Benchmark for In-the-Wild Language Model Factuality Evaluation}

\begin{abstract}
Language models (LMs) are widely used by an increasing number of users, underscoring the challenge of maintaining \farima{factuality} across a broad range of topics. We first present \system (\textbf{V}erification and \textbf{E}vidence \textbf{R}etr\textbf{I}eval for \textbf{F}actualit\textbf{Y} evaluation), a pipeline to evaluate LMs' \farima{factuality} in real-world user interactions. 
\system considers the verifiability of LM-generated content and categorizes content units as \texttt{supported}, \texttt{unsupported}, or \texttt{undecidable} based on the retrieved evidence from the Web.
Importantly, factuality judgment by \system correlates better with human evaluations than existing methods. 
Using \system, we identify ``hallucination prompts'' across diverse topics, i.e., those eliciting the highest rates of \farima{incorrect and inconclusive} LM responses. These prompts form \dataset, a dataset of \farima{1K} prompts across \farima{150} fine-grained topics. Our dataset captures emerging factuality challenges in real-world LM interactions and can be regularly updated with new prompts. 
We benchmark widely-used LMs from GPT, Gemini, and Llama3.1 family on \dataset, yielding the following key findings: 
\textbf{(i)} Proprietary models exhibit better factuality, \farima{with performance declining} from \texttt{Easy} to \texttt{Hard} hallucination prompts. 
\textbf{(ii)} Llama3.1-405B-Instruct shows comparable or lower factual accuracy than Llama3.1-70B-Instruct across all evaluation methods due to its higher subjectivity that leads to more \farima{content labeled as} \texttt{undecidable}.
\textbf{(iii)} Gemini1.5-Pro shows a significantly higher refusal rate, with over-refusal in 25\% of cases.
\farima{Our code and data are publicly available at \href{https://huggingface.co/spaces/launch/factbench}{https://huggingface.co/spaces/launch/factbench}.}


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Despite ongoing efforts to enhance their factuality, language models (LMs) continue to generate false or irrelevant content, often known as \textit{hallucination} \citep{huang2023surveyhallucinationlargelanguage, liu-etal-2023-cognitive}. The widespread use of LMs and the evolving nature of information demand a dynamic factuality evaluation benchmark to identify the challenges LMs face in real-world applications.
Current long-form factuality evaluation benchmarks \citep{min-etal-2023-factscore, safe, malaviya-etal-2024-expertqa} are constrained by being static and have a narrow coverage of usage scenarios. The static design makes these benchmarks susceptible to data leakage \citep{magar2022datacontaminationmemorizationexploitation}, rendering them unsuitable for capturing factuality challenges in real-world LM usages. 
Moreover, existing benchmarks often focus on a \farima{narrow} subset of tasks. For instance, data used in developing FactScore \citep{min-etal-2023-factscore} primarily addresses biographical queries, while ExpertQA \citep{malaviya-etal-2024-expertqa} recruits human experts to curate domain-specific questions. Other benchmarks \citep{chen2023felmbenchmarkingfactualityevaluation, safe} cover queries that are either LM-generated or human-curated, limiting their applicability to in-the-wild scenarios.

In this work, we make two primary contributions: \textbf{(i)} a dynamic and diverse factuality evaluation benchmark curated from real-world LM usage and \textbf{(ii)} a factuality evaluation framework that measures the appropriateness of in-the-wild prompts for inclusion in our benchmark by estimating how frequent strong LMs generate incorrect and \farima{inconclusive} responses. 
Concretely, we introduce \textbf{\dataset}, an updatable benchmark grounded in the real-world usage of LMs. \dataset comprises \farima{1,000} 
diverse information-seeking prompts across \farima{150} topics \farima{(examples in Figure \ref{tab:compare_factuality_benchmarks})},
that can cause LMs to produce false and \farima{inconclusive} responses. Using clustering methods, we begin by identifying 382 unique tasks within the LMSYS-Chat-1M dataset~\citep{zheng2024lmsyschat1mlargescalerealworldllm}. Prompts within each task cluster are then labeled for verifiability, indicating whether the prompt's response can be verified using Google Search results. We then assess the usefulness of prompts by considering factors such as clarity, interest, and relevance to a broad audience. Finally, verifiable prompts that meet a specified usefulness threshold become candidates for inclusion in \dataset.

We further define the ``hallucination prompts'' as the ones that can elicit incorrect or inconclusive content from a selected group of strong LMs \farima{(e.g., GPT-4 and Claude-2)}. To identify such prompts, we design \textbf{\system}, a \textbf{V}erification and \textbf{E}vidence \textbf{R}etr\textbf{I}eval for \textbf{F}actualit\textbf{Y} evaluation pipeline to detect nonfactual content in LM responses. \system first extracts content units from model responses and identifies their type \farima{(e.g., facts, instructions, disclaimers, etc.).}
It then evaluates only the verifiable units against Web-based evidence using an interactive query generation and evidence retrieval technique. Finally, \system categorizes units as \texttt{supported}, \texttt{unsupported}, or \texttt{undecidable} based on the evidence. 

\farima{LMs should ideally generate factual outputs that can be verified against reliable sources. To quantify the degree of hallucination in model responses, we propose a hallucination score that penalizes both incorrect claims (\texttt{unsupported}) and claims that cannot be verified due to ambiguity, partial correctness, or missing context (\texttt{undecidable}).}
This score measures the appropriateness of the corresponding user prompts for our final benchmark.
We categorize prompts into three tiers (\texttt{Hard}, \texttt{Moderate}, and \texttt{Easy}) based on the responding models' strength and select those with the highest hallucination scores within each tier. This results in a final benchmark of 1,000 prompts after manual inspection.

To assess the performance of widely-used LMs on \dataset, we evaluate two proprietary models, i.e., GPT4-o \citep{gpt4-o} and Gemini1.5-Pro \citep{geminiteam2024geminifamilyhighlycapable}, and two open-weight models from Llama3.1 family, i.e., Llama3.1-70B-Instruct and Llama3.1-405B-Instruct \citep{llama3.1}. 
The results show that LM performance significantly increased across tiers, aligning with our curation strategy. To compare the effectiveness of different factuality evaluation methods, we use \system units as a common basis and feed them into factuality evaluation baselines for verification. Our results reveal that \system achieves the highest correlation with human judgments compared to state-of-the-art methods. This finding underscores the effectiveness of our approach in benchmark creation and factuality assessment. 

In summary, our contributions are as follows: 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item We introduce \dataset, a new benchmark grounded in the real-world usage of LMs. \dataset is designed to be updatable by continuously incorporating newly collected hallucination prompts that cause LMs to generate incorrect content. This dynamic approach ensures that the benchmark remains relevant, addressing the evolving challenges in factual generation.
    
    \item We design \system, a factuality evaluation pipeline that considers the verifiability of generated content and categorizes units into \texttt{supported}, \texttt{unsupported}, or \texttt{undecidable} according to retrieval results. \system addresses the limitations of prior work that only makes binary decisions on supportedness and achieves the highest average correlation with human evaluations. 
    
    \item We release factuality annotations by humans on \farima{4,467} content units, with each unit independently judged by two annotators. Each annotator evaluates the independence of extracted units and their factuality using Google Search. 
    This human-annotated data provides quantifiable evaluation resources for assessing future factuality evaluation techniques. 
\end{itemize}



\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Factuality Evaluation Benchmarks}
The widespread adoption of LMs, coupled with their tendency to hallucinate, demands new benchmarks that can effectively identify their factual weaknesses across diverse scenarios. 
Prior factuality evaluation benchmarks mainly focus on short-form and human-curated question-answering (QA) tasks.
For instance, TruthfulQA \citep{lin2022truthfulqameasuringmodelsmimic}, HaluEval \citep{li2023haluevallargescalehallucinationevaluation}, and FELM \citep{chen2023felmbenchmarkingfactualityevaluation} mostly focus on short-form knowledge-based QA with questions with human-selected topics, despite LMs typically engaging in long-form conversations. 
The data used in developing FactScore \citep{min-etal-2023-factscore}, while long-form, is limited to a single, relatively easy task of biographical QA. LongFact \citep{safe} expands to 38 human-selected topics, but the prompts are LM-generated rather than user-driven. FactCheck-Bench \citep{factcheckbench} collects ChatGPT hallucinations from Twitter, but its scope is narrow (94 prompts) and focuses on a specific and rather obsolete model. Moreover, all these datasets are static and prone to the data contamination issues \citep{magar2022datacontaminationmemorizationexploitation}. We fill these gaps by offering a benchmark that systematically mines hallucination prompts from in-the-wild user-model chat logs in LMSYS-chat-1M \cite{zheng2024lmsyschat1mlargescalerealworldllm}, spanning diverse topics.
\dataset is designed to be regularly updated as new prompts are gathered from real-world interactions, overcoming the constraints of fixed time frames and ensuring ongoing relevance to evolving LM capabilities and usages.
\subsection{Factuality Evaluation Methods and Verifiability}
The challenge of distinguishing verifiable from non-verifiable claims is central to fact-checking. AFaCTA \citep{ni-etal-2024-afacta} stresses that claims are verifiable when they provide sufficient specificity for evidence retrieval. The subjective nature of check-worthiness, shaped by political and social contexts \citep{konstantinovskiy2020automatedfactcheckingdevelopingannotation, Nakov2022OverviewOT}, complicates this, particularly in LM-generated content where fact-opinion lines blur \citep{vosughi_2018}. To address this, \system introduces an \texttt{undecidable} label for claims with ambiguous factuality, accommodating both objective and context-dependent claims (see details in Section \ref{sec:factual-verifiability}).

Long-form content evaluation presents unique challenges due to its complexity and the numerous claims it typically contains. To address these challenges, SAFE \citep{safe} and FactScore \citep{wang2024factcheckbenchfinegrainedevaluationbenchmark} decompose content into individual facts for granular verification. Our method, \system, builds upon this approach by not only decomposing LM-generated content into units but also distinguishing between verifiable and non-verifiable elements that appear in user-model interactions. 
\farima{While VeriScore \citep{veriScore} similarly recognizes that not all LM-generated content is verifiable,  its single-step approach to extracting and decontextualizing \textit{verifiable claims} may limit its effectiveness in complex scenarios. Moreover, VeriScore's fixed-size context window could fail to capture long-range dependencies in real-world responses, potentially missing crucial context for accurate fact-checking. We address these limitations through a multi-step process that leverages the complete LM response to carefully identify independent verifiable content units. Our pipeline then evaluates these verifiable units by classifying them as \texttt{supported} or \texttt{unsupported} only when confident evidence is found, labeling them \texttt{undecidable} otherwise.} This approach introduces a more robust framework for evaluating the factual precision of LM-generated content. In contrast, Factcheck-GPT \citep{factcheckbench} extracts coarser-grained units and heavily relies on parametric knowledge, limiting its reliability for factual evaluation.

\section{Harvesting Hallucination Prompts From LM Interactions}

\label{sec:data_selection}
Our current understanding of LM performance on verifiable tasks is limited, and existing factuality evaluation benchmarks cover only a narrow range of verifiable use cases.
To address this gap, we collect English prompts from the first turn of conversations in the LMSYS-Chat-1M dataset \citep{zheng2024lmsyschat1mlargescalerealworldllm}, which is a large-scale, in-the-wild LM conversations dataset. Our objective is to identify prompts that are verifiable, diverse, and relevant for evaluating model responses through a multi-step process, which is described below. Figure \ref{fig:factEvalSteps} (left) outlines our collection process. 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    
 We use BERTopic \citep{grootendorst2022bertopicneuraltopicmodeling}, a dynamic topic-modeling pipeline that (1) embeds prompts using OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-small model \citep{embedding-model-openai}, (2) applies UMAP \citep{umap} for dimensionality reduction, and (3) employs HDBSCAN \citep{HDBSCAN}, a hierarchical density-based clustering algorithm. HDBSCAN identifies 142,702 (48.5\%) of the prompts as outliers, which we exclude to remove overly-specific prompts \farima{referring to niche user requests}. Finally, we use a class-based TF-IDF method to select the top 100 most representative prompts from each cluster and summarize them into concise topics (up to 10 words) using GPT-4 Turbo \citep{gpt4-turbo}.}  
    
    \item \textbf{Verifiability Classification}: We focus on prompts that ask for varying degrees of verifiable responses. Overall, verifiable prompts constitute 45.8\% of total prompts from the previous step.
    
    \item \textbf{Usefulness Evaluation.} The remaining collection contains around 70K prompts, too large for manual or automated fact-checking \farima{to identify hallucination prompt as described in Section \ref{sec:verify}}.
    Randomly selecting a subset for evaluation would be suboptimal, as it may include unclear or over-specific requests. Therefore, we propose a set of criteria to identify \textit{useful} prompts. A useful prompt needs to be (\textbf{i}) clear and understandable, (\textbf{ii}) generalizable to various users or scenarios, (\textbf{iii}) potentially interesting or valuable to a broader audience, and (\textbf{iv}) is within the capabilities of LMs (e.g., excludes real-time data). To reduce single-model bias, we use two language models, GPT-4-Turbo and Llama3-70B-Instruct, as annotators. Each model scores prompts on a scale of 1 (low) to 5 (high) for each criterion.
    The final usefulness score for each prompt is calculated as
    the average score across all criteria, summing the score from two models. The usefulness scores are then used to select an initial set of prompts, as described in Section \ref{sec:factbench dataset}. 
\end{itemize}



\section{\system: Verification and Evidence Retrieval for Factuality Evaluation}

\label{sec:verify}

In this section, we design an automatic factuality evaluation pipeline called \system to measure the \textit{appropriateness} of these prompts, i.e., based on whether they tend to elicit nonfactual responses from LMs, for inclusion into the \dataset. Our goal is to verify model responses in natural user-LM settings, where responses may include a mixture of verifiable and non-verifiable statements. To evaluate model responses efficiently, we first establish the key criteria for determining the verifiability of statements.

\subsection{Verifiability-driven Factual Evaluation}
\label{sec:factual-verifiability}
A statement is verifiable if it provides sufficient information to guide fact-checkers in verification \citep{ni-etal-2024-afacta}. 
We classify \textit{verifiable statements} into two categories: 

\textbf{Context-independent Statements}: These are objective assertions that can be directly verified against knowledge sources. For example, ``RTX 3060 has a memory bandwidth of 360 Gbps.''

\textbf{Context-dependent Statements}: These statements require additional information for verification. For instance, to verify the statement
``The difference in memory bandwidth between the RTX 3060 and RTX 3060 Ti is relatively small'', 
one needs knowledge of both GPUs' bandwidths and an understanding of what qualifies as \textit{relatively small} in this context.

Focusing only on verifiable statements during user-LM interactions allows us to assess the factuality of the response more efficiently and accurately, as we describe in the following
section.

\subsection{Unit Extraction and Labeling}
\label{sec:unit extraction and labeling}
User requests span a wide range of domains (examples provided in Figure \ref{tab:compare_factuality_benchmarks}), and model responses contain a variety of content types.
To evaluate verifiable statements, we first decompose the model response into independent content units. A content unit can represent a \texttt{Fact}, a \texttt{Claim}, an \texttt{Instruction}, a \texttt{Disclaimer}, a \texttt{Question}, or other relevant content type that appears during user-model interaction.}
Many of these content units are not verifiable, meaning they cannot be fact-checked. Examples include questions and disclaimers, which often pertain to the context of the conversation or the capabilities of the model itself rather than presenting factual information.

We label each unit by its type to identify those suitable for verification. We use the Llama3-70B-Instruct model, which serves as the backbone LM for all tasks in this pipeline, to extract and label content units. We classify objective statements as \texttt{Fact}
and potentially subjective, context-dependent statements as \texttt{Claim}. Only units labeled as \texttt{Fact} or \texttt{Claim} are passed to the next step.

\subsection{Unit Decontextualization} 
\label{sec:unit decontextualization}
\citet{gunjal2024molecularfactsdesideratadecontextualization} highlights the importance of ``molecular units''---units that contain sufficient information to be uniquely identifiable in factuality assessment. Inspired by that, we implement a unit decontextualization component in our pipeline to minimally revise verifiable units and make them self-contained.

\subsection{Query Generation and Evidence Retrieval} 
In order to verify the self-contained units, we need to retrieve relevant evidence from knowledge sources. We utilize SerperAPI\footnote{https://serper.dev/} for Google Search
and Web-evidence retrieval.

To enhance search quality and ensure the retrieval of relevant evidence that best assists in verification, we implement an interactive query refinement technique. The query generator operates iteratively within an interactive feedback loop. It first produces a query for the target unit, which is then used in Google Search to return relevant snippets. In subsequent rounds, the query generator evaluates the relevance of the retrieved snippets for verifying the target unit and refines the query accordingly. This iterative process continues for up to five rounds, progressively improving the query's quality and relevance. The final set of queries and associated search results are then passed to the next step for final judgment.
 
\subsection{Final Answer Generation}
In this step, the model (Llama3-70B-Instruct) is tasked with making a final decision on units' accuracy by evaluating multiple rounds of retrieved evidence using Chain-of-Thought prompting \citep{cotprompting}. For each unit, the model \textbf{(i)} summarizes the relevant knowledge points, \textbf{(ii)} assesses their relationship to the unit, and \textbf{(iii)} determines whether the evidence supports (\texttt{supported}), refutes (\texttt{unsupported}), or is inconclusive (\texttt{undecidable}). This results in annotation labels for all verifiable units in the original model response.
An overview of our evaluation pipeline is provided in the right part of Figure \ref{fig:factEvalSteps}. \farima{\system facilitates affordable factuality checking by leveraging open-weight LMs and is instantiated with Llama3-70B-Instruct in this work.}
\subsection{Hallucination Score}
After annotating the individual content units, we propose a hallucination metric to quantify the incorrect and inconclusive contents within a model's response. Let $U$ represent the set of \texttt{undecidable} units, $C$ the set of \texttt{unsupported} units, and $V$ the total set of \farima{extracted} verifiable units (\texttt{Claims} and \texttt{Facts}). The final \textit{Hallucination Score} is computed as follows:
\begin{align}
\label{eq:hallucination_score}
    H(R) = \frac{|C| + \alpha |U|}{\sqrt{|V|}}
\end{align}

Here, $\alpha \in (0,1)$ is a weighting factor that controls the relative importance of \texttt{undecidable} units compared to \texttt{unsupported} ones. This adjustment is necessary because LMs are expected to generate content based on their training data, including web data, though some factual content may not appear in current web searches. Additionally, \texttt{undecidable} units may be correct but lack sufficient evidence in search results for verification. 
For experiments, we set $\alpha = 0.5$. The denominator, $\sqrt{|V|}$ ensures that the weight of errors (\texttt{unsupported} and \texttt{undecidable} units) grows more significantly relative to the total number of verifiable units, placing stronger emphasis on even a few errors without allowing large sets of units to mask their importance. Using this score, we \farima{employ a tiered-based ranking strategy to curate a benchmark}, as described next.



\section{FactBench Dataset}

\label{sec:factbench dataset}
The hallucination score helps us assess prompt appropriateness for our final dataset. To ensure balanced representation across model capabilities, we categorize prompts into three tiers: \texttt{Hard}, \texttt{Moderate}, and \texttt{Easy}, based on the overall performance of responding models\footnote{Model performance rankings are based on the \href{https://huggingface.co/spaces/lmsys/chatbot-arena-leaderboard}{Chatbot Arena Leaderboard}, which ranks LMs through pairwise human comparisons}. The \texttt{Hard} tier consists of prompts queried to the strongest models in LMSYS-Chat-1M (e.g., GPT-4, Claude-2), preventing weaker models' higher hallucination rates from dominating our dataset.

For prompt selection, we apply tier-specific usefulness thresholds: $4$ or higher for \texttt{Hard}, $4.5$ or higher for \texttt{Moderate}, and exactly $5$ for \texttt{Easy}. This approach reflects our assumption that responses from stronger models better indicate prompt appropriateness, hence allowing a lower threshold for the \texttt{Hard} tier. From the 70K prompts collected in Section \ref{sec:data_selection}, this filtering yields 4.2K prompts distributed as 53\% \texttt{Hard}, 34\% \texttt{Moderate}, and 13\% \texttt{Easy}.

\section{Experimental Results and Further Analyses}

\label{sec:experiment_and_results}
In this section, we measure LMs' factuality on \dataset using different factuality evaluation methods (Section \ref{sec:exp_1}). These methods extract content units at different granularities, and thus, we establish a unified evaluation framework with human labeling as described in Section \ref{sec:exp_2}.

\subsection{Factuality Improves with Proprietary LMs and on Easier Prompts} 
\label{sec:exp_1}

To compare model performance on \dataset, we use the factual precision metric proposed by \citet{min-etal-2023-factscore}. Table \ref{tab:main_results} compares the factual precision of LMs on \dataset as measured by different evaluation methods. Despite all baselines verifying responses at their finest granularity (standalone units), we observe varying factual precision ranges across different pipelines. We observe that \system consistently maintains the same ranking of models across all three tiers, unlike other evaluation methods. \system ranks \textbf{GPT4-o as having the highest factual precision across all tiers, followed by Gemini1.5-Pro and the two open-source Llama3.1 models}. This consistency suggests that \system may provide a more stable and reliable evaluation of model performance across varying difficulty levels.

Surprisingly, Llama3.1-405B-Instruct demonstrates comparable or inferior factuality to Llama3.1-70B-Instruct according to \system. Further investigation (Figure \ref{fig:tiers_stat}) reveals that Llama3.1-405B-Instruct has a lower proportion of \texttt{unsupported} units but the highest proportion of \texttt{undecidable} units across all LMs. This primarily stems from stronger subjectivity in Llama3.1-405B-Instruct, with more frequent use of subjective adjectives such as ``solid,'' ``exclusive,'' and ``well-known.'' The reasoning logic enforced in our pipeline typically classifies these subjective elements as \texttt{undecidable}, thereby reducing the factual precision.

Another significant observation is \textbf{the consistent improvement in factuality precision across LMs from the \texttt{Hard} to \texttt{Easy} tiers}, as shown by all evaluation methods.
This aligns with our tiered benchmark design, where prompts are categorized based on the responding model's strength. \texttt{Easy} prompts are less likely to trigger hallucinations in strong models, as their appropriateness is measured based on weaker LM hallucinations.

\subsection{Factuality Evaluation by \system Strongly Correlates with Human}
\label{sec:exp_2}

The factuality of a model, measure by a factuality evaluation method, depends on the granularity of the extracted units and the method's verification capabilities. 
FactScore extracts units with a finer granularity compared to \system due to its focus on biographical text evaluation, which typically consists of objective and easily separable units. On the other hand, Factcheck-GPT's claim-level decomposition (finest-level) often results in sentence-level containing multiple claims. 
To establish a unified evaluation framework for these methods, we selected \farima{40} \dataset prompts from \farima{40} randomly sampled topics (one prompt per topic) and generated responses from each model. We then applied our unit extraction (Section \ref{sec:unit extraction and labeling}) and decontextualization approach (Section \ref{sec:unit decontextualization}) to decompose generated LM responses into \textit{self-contained} and \textit{verifiable} units. This method was chosen for its ability to handle user-model conversations (with careful instructions and in-the-wild demonstrations), extract moderately granular units, and filter them based on verifiability.

Three fluent English speakers are hired to annotate a total of \farima{160} LM responses for 4 models on the same set of \farima{40} prompts. \system breaks LM responses into \farima{4,467} units, with each response annotated by two annotators. 
Each annotator evaluated the independence and factuality of each unit. A unit is considered \textit{Independent} if it is \textit{verifiable} and \textit{self-contained}. A \textit{Dependent} unit, on the other hand, is either an \textit{unverifiable} piece of information, e.g., ``I can provide you with some examples.'' or \textit{under-specified} content. For example, ``She won the best actress award'' is underspecified as it contains a pronoun, and it is unclear what specific award is referenced. 
Overall, \farima{82.6\%} units are considered \textit{Independent} by both annotators with an inter-annotator agreement of \farima{0.53} in terms of Cohen's Kappa score. 
Additionally, each annotator evaluates the factuality of a unit by using two labels: (1) \textit{Factual} when supporting evidence can be found using \farima{Web} search and (2) \textit{Other} when refuting evidence is found, or the factuality cannot be determined. 
Annotators agree on the factuality of \farima{85.9\%} of units, with a Cohen's Kappa agreement of \farima{0.57}. These binary factuality labels are compatible with \system and other baselines. 
A unit is \textit{Independent} if both annotators agree; otherwise, it is labeled \textit{Dependent}. Factuality is decided in the same manner.

\subsection{Gemini1.5-Pro's Refusal Rate Impacts Its Factuality}

 In this section, we investigate LMs' refusal rate and its significance. Previous work \citep{min-etal-2023-factscore} relied on simple heuristics or keywords to identify refusal responses, but our initial investigation finds these methods unreliable.
To address this, we prompt GPT-4 Turbo to detect refusals and categorize them into different types, including clarification requests, lack of knowledge, safety concerns, and misinformation risks.
 Notably, Gemini1.5-Pro exhibits a significantly higher refusal rate than other models, approaching 10\% on the \texttt{Hard} portion of \dataset. As shown, it refuses to answer prompts in the \texttt{Hard} tier more frequently, as it contains the most challenging hallucination prompts.
Moreover, while Gemini1.5-Pro's refusal strategies can help prevent hallucinations (see example \ref{tab:model_responses}), the high rate of refusals impacts its overall factuality precision. Additionally, our manual inspection reveals that in 25\% refusal cases, Gemini1.5-Pro's reason for not answering is not valid. For example, the model interpreted ``give me studies on the recommended interval between COVID vaccines'' as a request for medical advice and refused to answer. These findings highlight the need for more nuanced factuality evaluation metrics to account for refusals as an important direction for future research.

\section{Conclusion}


We presented \system, a factuality evaluation pipeline that automatically annotates the factuality of LM responses in real-world settings. \system breaks down a response into content units, identifies verifiable ones, evaluates them against retrieved Web evidence, and labels each unit as \texttt{supported}, \texttt{unsupported}, or \texttt{undecidable}. Our method correlates strongly with human evaluations compared to existing state-of-the-art methods and is more robust in handling flawed units. Using \system, we curated \dataset, a benchmark of 1,000 prompts across 150 fine-grained topics that are grouped into three tiers: \texttt{Hard}, \texttt{Moderate}, and \texttt{Easy}. Prompts in each tier are sorted by appropriateness, i.e. their tendency to elicit hallucinated content from LMs. \dataset is the first real-world and diverse factuality evaluation benchmark designed to be regularly updatable to capture evolving factuality challenges faced by language models. Finally, we used our dataset to evaluate frontier LMs in proprietary and open-source categories. Our findings show that the proprietary model exhibits better factually, with the performance declining factuality from \texttt{Easy} to \texttt{Hard} tiers. Interestingly, Llama3.1-405B-Instruct shows lower factuality mainly due to its frequent use of subjective terms that are hard to verify. 

Current factuality-evaluating metrics oversimplify the evaluation and do not consider model refusals and low factual recall, which is an important direction for future research in this field. Additionally, future work can explore more sophisticated evaluation pipelines that account for individual factual support as well as logical inter-unit connections. Such approaches would verify not only the factual precision of each content unit but also the logical coherence within the overall response, further improving the reliability of LM evaluation methods.
