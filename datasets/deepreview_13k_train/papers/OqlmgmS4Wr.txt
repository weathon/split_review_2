\title{AgentTuning: Enabling Generalized Agent Abilities for LLMs}

\begin{abstract}
Open large language models (LLMs) with great performance in various tasks have significantly advanced the development of LLMs. 
However, they are far inferior to commercial models such as ChatGPT and GPT-4 when acting as agents to tackle complex tasks in the real world.
These agent tasks employ LLMs as the central controller responsible for planning, memorization, and tool utilization, necessitating both fine-grained prompting methods and robust LLMs to achieve satisfactory performance.
Though many prompting methods have been proposed to complete particular agent tasks, there is lack of research focusing on improving the agent capabilities of LLMs themselves without compromising their general abilities. 
In this work, we present \textit{\method}, a simple and general method to enhance the agent abilities of LLMs while maintaining their general LLM capabilities. 
We construct \textit{\dataset}, a lightweight instruction-tuning dataset containing high-quality interaction trajectories. 
We employ a hybrid instruction-tuning strategy by combining \dataset with open-source instructions from general domains. 
\method is used to instruction-tune the Llama 2 series, resulting in \textit{\model}.
Our evaluations show that \method enables LLMs' agent capabilities without compromising general abilities. 
The \model-70B is comparable to GPT-3.5-turbo on unseen agent tasks, demonstrating generalized agent capabilities.

An \textit{agent} refers to an entity capable of perceiving its environment, making decisions, and taking actions~\citep{Maes1994AgentsTR,wooldridge1995intelligent}. %Searle1970SpeechAA,Maes1994AgentsTR,wooldridge1995intelligent
Traditional AI agents have been effective in specialized domains, but often fall short in adaptability and generalization. 
Through alignment training, large language models (LLMs)~\citep{ouyang2022training,wei2022finetuned}, initially designed for language tasks, have displayed unprecedented capabilities in instruction following~\citep{ouyang2022training}, reasoning~\citep{wei2022chain}, planning, and even tool utilization~\citep{schick2023toolformer}. 
These capabilities make LLMs an ideal foundation for advancing AI agents %from their traditional limitations 
toward broad, versatile functionality. 
Recent projects such as AutoGPT~\citep{autogpt}, GPT-Engineer~\citep{gptengineer}, and BabyAGI~\citep{babyagi} have employed LLMs as the core controllers, building powerful agents capable of solving complex problems in the real world.

However, a recent study~\citep{liu2023agentbench} shows that open LLMs like Llama~\citep{touvron2023llama, touvron2023llama2} and Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023} significantly lag behind in agent capabilities in complex, real-world scenarios when compared to GPT-3.5 and GPT-4~\citep{chatgpt,gpt4} in Figure ~\ref{fig:head-image}, though they have performed well in traditional NLP tasks and largely advanced the development of LLMs. 
The performance gap in agent tasks hampers %both the democratization of AI and 
the advancement of in-depth LLM research and community innovation. 

Existing studies on LLMs as agents have thus far 
largely focused on designing prompts or a framework for completing one particular agent task~\citep{yao2023react, kim2023language, deng2023mind2web}, rather than fundamentally enhancing the agent capabilities of the LLMs themselves.
In addition, many efforts are dedicated to improving LLMs in specific aspects, involving fine-tuning the LLMs using datasets tailored to specific tasks~\citep{deng2023mind2web, qin2023toolllm}. 
This overemphasis on specialized capabilities comes at the expense of the LLMs' general abilities and also compromises their generalizability.

To fundamentally enable generalized agent abilities for LLMs, %address the aforementioned challenges and bridge the disparity between open-source models and commercial models, 
we introduce a simple and general approach \textit{\method} as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:main-image}. %towards activating the agent capabilities of LLMs. 
\method consists of two components: a lightweight instruct-tuning dataset \textit{\dataset} and a hybrid instruction-tuning strategy that enhances the agent's capabilities while preserving its generalization ability. 
As shown in Table \ref{tab:training-datasets}, \dataset covers 1,866 verified interaction trajectories with high-quality Chain-of-Thought (CoT) rationale~\citep{wei2022chain} for each decision step from six diverse agent tasks. 
For each agent task, one interaction trajectory is collected through three phases: instruction construction, trajectory interaction by employing GPT-4 as the agent, and trajectory filtering depending on its reward score. 
To enhance LLMs' agent capabilities while preserving their general abilities, we experiment with a hybrid instruction-tuning strategy. 
The idea is to mix \dataset with high-quality and general data at a certain ratio for supervised fine-tuning. 

\input{figures/main-image}
We employ  \method  to fine-tune the open Llama 2 series~\citep{touvron2023llama2}, whose performance on agent tasks are significantly worse that GPT-3.5, resulting in the \model-7B, 13B and 70B models. 
Our empirical evaluations have the following observations. 

First, \model demonstrates strong performance on both held-in tasks in \dataset and unseen held-out agent tasks, suggesting robust generalization on agent capabilities. 
It also makes \model-70B comparable to GPT-3.5 on unseen agent tasks without compromising its performance on general NLP tasks, such as on MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, and MT-Bench. 

Second, our analysis on the ratio of agent data with general data suggests that the general capabilities of LLMs are crucial for the generalization of agent tasks. 
Training solely on agent data, in fact, leads to a decline in generalization performance. This can be explained by the fact that agent tasks demand that LLMs exhibit comprehensive abilities such as planning and reasoning.

Third, our error analysis on Llama 2 and \model shows that \method significantly reduces instances of basic mistakes such as formatting errors, duplicated generation, and refusal to answer. This suggests that the model inherently possesses the capability to tackle agent tasks, and \method indeed enables the LLMs' agent abilities rather than causing it to overfit on agent tasks.

\method represents the very first attempt to instruction-tune LLMs using interaction trajectories across multiple agent tasks. 
Evaluation results indicate that \method enables the agent capabilities of LLMs with  robust generalization on unseen agent tasks while remaining good on general language abilities. 
We have open-sourced the \dataset dataset and \model. %, which is fine-tuned with \method on Llama 2.

\section{The \method Approach}


\input{tables/training-datasets}

Given an agent task, the interaction trajectory of the LLM agent can be recorded as a conversation history $\left(u_1, a_1, \ldots, u_n, a_n\right)$. Given that the existing dialogue models typically encompass two roles, the user and the model, $u_i$ represents the input from the user and $a_i$ denotes the response from the model. Each trajectory  has a final reward $r \in [0, 1]$,  reflecting the completion status of the task.

To date, there is no end-to-end attempt to improve the general agent abilities of LLMs. 
Most existing agent studies focused on either prompting one particular LLM or compiling a LLM-based framework for completing an agent task, such as building a Web agent in WebShop~\citep{yao2022webshop} and Mind2Web~\citep{deng2023mind2web}. 
According to AgentBench~\citep{liu2023agentbench}, all open LLMs are far behind of commercial ones such as GPT-4 and ChatGPT in terms of acting as agents though these models, such as Llama2, have demonstrated strong performance across various benchmarks. 
The goal of this work is to improve the generalized agent abilities of LLMs while at least maintaining their general LLM capacities such as their performance on MMLU, GSM8K, and HumanEval. 

We present \method to achieve this goal, the first step of which is to build the \dataset dataset that is used in the second step to instruction tune the LLMs. 
We carefully experiment and design these two steps such that the LLMs obtain good performance in (unseen) generalized agent task types while remaining good in general LLM tasks. 

\subsection{Constructing \dataset}
Language instructions have been widely collected and used to tune pre-trained LLMs for better instruction-following capacity, such as FLAN~\citep{wei2022finetuned} and InstructGPT~\citep{ouyang2022training}. 
It is however much more challenging to collect instructions for agent tasks, as it involves the trajectories of interactions when an agent navigates in a complex environment. 

We take the very first attempt to build \dataset for improving LLMs' generalized agent abilities. 
We detail the design choices during its construction process. 
It consists of three major stages: Instruction Construction (\S\ref{sec:instruction-construction}), Trajectory Interaction (\S\ref{sec:trajectory-interaction}), and Trajectory Filtering (\S\ref{sec:trajectory-filtering}). 
This process was entirely automated using GPT-3.5 (\texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613}) and GPT-4 (\texttt{gpt-4-0613}), allowing the approach to be easily extended to new agent tasks. 

\subsubsection{Instruction Construction}
\label{sec:instruction-construction}

We construct \dataset for six agent tasks, including AlfWorld~\citep{shridhar2020alfworld}, WebShop~\citep{yao2022webshop}, Mind2Web~\citep{deng2023mind2web}, Knowledge Graph, Operating System, and Database~\citep{liu2023agentbench}, representative of a diverse range of real-world scenarios that are relatively easy to collect instructions. 
\dataset comprises challenging 6 tasks from AgentBench~\citep{liu2023agentbench}, covering a wide range of real-world scenarios, with most open-source models performing poorly on them. 

Table ~\ref{tab:training-datasets} lists the overview of \dataset. 
If a task (e.g., ALFWorld, WebShop, Mind2Web, and Knowledge Graph) has a training set, we directly use the training split for subsequent phases---trajectory interaction and filtering. 
For Operating System and Database tasks without training sets, we leverage the idea of \textit{Task Derivation} and \textit{Self-Instruct}~\citep{wang2023selfinstruct} to construct corresponding instructions.

\paragraph{Task Derivation} For agent tasks associated with scenarios that have been widely studied, we can directly construct instructions from similar datasets. 
Thus to construct instructions on the Database (DB) task, we derive instructions from BIRD~\citep{li2023llm}, a SELECT-only database benchmark. 
We ran two types of task derivation. 
First, we construct a trajectory using the question and the reference SQL statement in each BIRD subtask. 
We then query the database using the reference SQL statement to obtain output of the database and serve it as the submitted answer of the agent. 
Finally, we ask GPT-4 to fill in the thoughts of the agent given the above information. 
In this way, we can generate correct trajectories directly from BIRD dataset. 

However, since this synthesis process determines the number of interaction turns to be fixed at 2, we then propose another approach to improve the diversity by constructing instructions instead of trajectories directly. 
We prompt GPT-4 with a question from BIRD, and collect its interaction trajectory with the database. 
After collecting trajectories, we execute the reference SQL statement from BIRD and compare the result to the one from GPT-4. 
We filter out wrong answers, collecting trajectories that produce a correct answer only.

\paragraph{Self-Instruct} For the Operating System (OS) task, due to the difficulty in obtaining instructions that involve manipulating OS in terminal, we employed the Self-Instruct method~\citep{wang2023selfinstruct} to construct the task. 
We first prompt GPT-4 to come up with some OS related tasks along with explanations to the task, a reference solution and an evaluation script. 
Then, we prompt another GPT-4 instance (the solver) with the task and collect its trajectory. 
After the task is completed, we run the reference solution and compare its result to the one from the solver GPT-4 using the evaluation script. 
We collect the trajectories where the reference solution and the solver's solution give the same answer. For the DB task, since BIRD only contains SELECT data, we construct other types of database operations (INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE) in a similar self-instruct approach.

It is worth noting that these two methods might risk test data leakage if GPT-4 outputs instructions identical to those in the test set, or if test tasks are constructed from the same dataset we derived from. To address this concern, we conducted a systematic analysis and found no evidence of data leakage.

\subsubsection{Trajectory Interaction}
\label{sec:trajectory-interaction}

With the initial instructions constructed, we use GPT-4 (\texttt{gpt-4-0613}) as agents for trajectory interaction. 
For the Mind2Web task, due to the large number of instructions and our budget constraints, we partially employed ChatGPT (\texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613}) for interactions. 

We utilize the 1-shot evaluation approach~\citep{liu2023agentbench}, primarily due to the stringent requirements for the output format in agent tasks. 
For each task, we provide a complete interaction process from the training set.

\paragraph{Interaction Process} The interaction process has two main parts. First, we give the model a task description and a successful 1-shot example. 
Then, the actual interaction begins. 
We supply the model with the current instruction and necessary information. 
Based on this and previous feedback, the model forms a thought and takes an action. 
The environment then provides feedback, including possible changes or new information. This cycle continues until the model either achieves its goal or reaches its token limit. If the model repeats the same output three times consecutively, we consider it a repetitive failure. 
If the model's output format is wrong, we use the BLEU metric to compare it to all possible action choices and pick the closest match as the model's action for that step.

\paragraph{CoT Rationales} The Chain-of-Thought (CoT)  method has significantly enhanced the inferential capabilities of LLMs by a step-by-step reasoning progress~\citep{wei2022chain}. Thus, we employ ReAct~\citep{yao2023react} as the reasoning framework, which outputs CoT explanation (referred to as \textit{thought}) before producing the final action. Consequently, every action within the collected interaction trajectories is accompanied by a detailed explanation trace, enabling the model to learn the reasoning process leading to the action. For trajectories generated using task derivation without thoughts, we use GPT-4 to supplement them with thoughts for consistency with ReAct prompting.

\subsubsection{Trajectory Filtering}
\label{sec:trajectory-filtering}

\input{tables/filter-ablation}

Agent tasks that encompass real-world scenarios present significant challenges. 
Even GPT-4 falls short of expectations on such tasks. 
To ensure the data quality, we rigorously filtered its interaction trajectories. 
Recall that each interaction trajectory receives a reward $r$, this allows us to automatically select high-quality trajectories based on the reward. 
We filter trajectories for all tasks, except for Mind2Web, based on a final reward of $r=1$, indicating complete correctness. 
However, due to the difficulty of the Mind2Web task, we use a threshold of $r\ge\frac{2}{3}$ to ensure we obtain a sufficient number of trajectories.
In Table~\ref{tab:filter-ablation}, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our filtering strategy by fine-tuning on both filtered and unfiltered trajectories at 7B scale. Compared to models trained on filtered trajectories, those trained on unfiltered trajectories perform significantly worse on both held-in and held-out tasks. This underscores the importance of data quality over data quantity for agent tasks.

Following these steps, the \dataset dataset as shown in Table~\ref{tab:training-datasets} contains 1,866 final trajectories. 
\subsection{Instruction Tuning}

In this section, we introduce our hybrid instruction-tuning strategy. 
The goal is to enhance the LLMs' agent capabilities without compromising its general abilities. 

\subsubsection{General Domain Instructions}

Recent studies suggest that training with diverse user prompts enhances model performance~\citep{vicuna2023, wang2023far}. Using the ShareGPT dataset\footnote{\scriptsize\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/anon8231489123/ShareGPT_Vicuna_unfiltered}}, we selectively extracted English-language conversation, yielding 57,096 conversations with GPT-3.5 and 3,670 with GPT-4. Recognizing the superior quality of GPT-4 responses as highlighted by~\citep{wang2023openchat}, we adopted a sampling ratio of 1:4 between GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 for better performance.

\subsubsection{Mixture Training}
\label{sec:mixture-training}

Using the base model $\pi_0$, which represents the probability distribution $\pi_0(y \mid x)$ of response $y$ given instruction and history $x$, we consider two datasets: the \dataset dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{agent}}$ and the general dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{general}}$. The mixure ratio of $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{agent}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{general}}$ is defined as $\eta$. Our aim is to find the best policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ that minimizes the loss function $J(\theta)$, as shown in Equation~\ref{eq:training-loss}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:training-loss}
J(\theta)=\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D_{\mathrm{agent}}}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right] + (1-\eta)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D_{\mathrm{general}}}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right]
\end{equation}

Intuitively, a larger $\eta$ should imply that the model is more inclined towards agent-specific capabilities rather than general capabilities. However, we observed that training solely on agent tasks performs worse on unseen tasks compared to mixed training. This suggests that general capabilities play a pivotal role in the generalization of agent abilities, which we discuss further in Section~\ref{sec:ablation-study}. To determine the best $\eta$, we scan from 0 to 1 in intervals of 0.1 on the 7B model and ultimately chose $\eta=0.2$ which performed the best on held-out tasks for final training.

\subsubsection{Training Setup}

\input{tables/eval-datasets}
We choose the chat version of open Llama 2 (\texttt{Llama-2-\{7,13,70\}b-chat})~\citep{touvron2023llama2} as our base models, given its better instruction-following capabilities than base models and commendable performance on traditional NLP tasks. Following Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023}, we standardize all data into a multi-turn chatbot-style format, allowing us to conveniently mix data from different sources. During fine-tuning, we only compute the loss on the model's output.  We fine-tune models of sizes 7B, 13B, and 70B using Megatron-LM~\citep{shoeybi2020megatronlm}.
We use a learning rate of 5e-5 for the 7B and 13B models, and 1e-5 for the 70B model. We set the batch size at 64 with 4,096 sequence length. We use AdamW optimizer~\citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} with a cosine learning scheduler with 2\% warm-up steps. For efficient training, we employ tensor parallelism~\citep{shoeybi2020megatronlm} for the 7B and 13B models, and for the 70B model, we also utilize pipeline parallelism~\citep{huang2019gpipe}.

\subsection{Definition}

Following previous work evaluating LLM-as-agent~\citep{liu2023agentbench}, we present some definitions.

\paragraph{LLM-as-Agent} \todo{Rewrite this paragraph from AgentBnech} The evaluation of LLM-as-Agent in an interactive setting can be conceptualized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), characterized by state space $S$, action space $A$, transition function $T: S \times A \rightarrow S$, reward determination function $R$, task instruction space $U$, and observation space $O$. In this context, we refer to the LLM agent as $M$.

\paragraph{Interaction Trajectory} The interaction trajectory of the LLM agent can be recorded as a conversation history $\left(u_1, a_1, \ldots, u_n, a_n\right)$. Given that the existing dialogue models typically encompass two roles, the user and the model, $u_i$ represents the input from the user, while $a_i$ denotes the response from the model. After the task is completed, we run the reference solution and compare its result to the one from the solver GPT-4 using the evaluation script. We retain the trajectories where the reference solution and the solver's solution give the same answer. Since BIRD only contains SELECT data, we construct other types of database operation (INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE) in a similar approach.

It is worth noting that these two method might risk test data leakage if GPT-4 outputs instructions identical to those in the test set, or if test tasks are constructed from the same dataset we derived from. To address this concern, we conducted a systematic analysis and found no evidence of data leakage.

\subsubsection{Trajectory Interaction}
\label{sec:trajectory-interaction}

We use GPT-4 (\texttt{gpt-4-0613}) as agents for trajectory interaction. For the Mind2Web task, due to the large number of instructions and our budget constraints, we partially employed ChatGPT (\texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo-0613}) for interaction. 

Following AgentBench~\citep{liu2023agentbench}, we utilize a 1-shot evaluation approach, primarily due to the stringent requirements for output format in agent task. For each task, we provide a complete interaction process from the training set.

\paragraph{Interaction Process} The interaction process has two main parts. First, we give the model a task description and a successful 1-shot example. Then, the actual interaction begins. We supply the model with the current instruction and necessary information. Based on this and previous feedback, the model forms a thought and takes an action. The environment then provides feedback, including possible changes or new information. This cycle continues until the model either achieves its goal or reaches its token limit. If the model repeats the same output three times consecutively, we consider it a repetitive failure. If the model's output format is wrong, we use the BLEU metric to compare it to all possible action choices and pick the closest match as the model's action for that step.

\paragraph{CoT Rationales} The Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting method has significantly enhanced the inferential capabilities of language models by a step-by-step reasoning progress~\citep{wei2022chain}. Thus, we employ ReAct~\citep{yao2023react} as the reasoning framework, which outputs CoT explantion (referred to as \textit{thought}) before producing the final action. Consequently, every action within the collected interaction trajectories is accompanied by a detailed explanation trace, enabling the model to learn the reasoning process leading to the action. For trajectories generated using task derivation without thoughts, we employ GPT-4 to supplement these them with thoughts for consistency with ReAct prompting.

\subsubsection{Trajectory Filtering}
\label{sec:trajectory-filtering}

\input{tables/filter-ablation}

Agent tasks that encompass real-world scenarios present significant challenges. Even GPT-4 falls short of expectations on such tasks. Consequently, we rigorously filtered its interaction trajectories to ensure data quality. Recalling the previous definition, each interaction trajectory receives a reward $r$. This allows us to automatically select high-quality trajectories based on the reward. We filter trajectories in AlfWorld, WebShop, and Knowledge Graph by final rewards $r = 1$, indicating complete correctness. For the Mind2Web task, to ensure we obtained sufficient trajectories, we filtered using $r \geq \frac{2}{3}$ given its difficulty.

Following these guidelines, our instruction dataset, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:training-datasets}, contains 1,866 filtered trajectories. 

\subsection{Instruction Tuning}

In this section, we introduce our hybrid instruction-tuning strategy. Our goal is to enhance model's agent capabilities without compromising its general abilities. Intuitively, we need to balance these two abilities by training using a mix of the \dataset and general domain datasets. We first discuss the general domain datasets we have chosen, followed by an explanation of how we determine the mixing ratio with \dataset.

\subsubsection{General Domain Datasets}

Recent studies suggest that training with diverse user prompts enhances model performance~\citep{vicuna2023, wang2023far}. Using the ShareGPT dataset\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/anon8231489123/ShareGPT_Vicuna_unfiltered/tree/main/HTML_cleaned_raw_dataset}}, we selectively extracted English-language conversation, yielding 57,096 conversations with GPT-3.5 and 3,670 with GPT-4. Recognizing the superior quality of GPT-4 responses as highlighted by~\citep{wang2023openchat}, we up-sampled the GPT-4 data five-fold to further enhance performance.

\subsubsection{Mixture Training}
\label{sec:mixture-training}

Using the base model $\pi_0$, which represents the probability distribution $\pi_0(y \mid x)$ of response $y$ given instruction and history $x$, we consider two datasets: the agent dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{agent}}$ and the general dataset $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{general}}$. Our aim is to find the best policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ that minimizes the loss function $J(\theta)$, as shown in Equation~\ref{eq:training-loss}.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:training-loss}
J(\theta)=\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D_{\mathrm{agent}}}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right] + (1-\eta)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D_{\mathrm{general}}}}\left[\log \pi_\theta(y \mid x)\right]
\end{equation}

Intuitively, a larger eta should imply that the model is more inclined towards agent-specific capabilities rather than general capabilities. However, we observe that the agent's performance first increases and then decreases with the rise in eta. This suggests that general capabilities play a pivotal role in the generalization of agent abilities. Picking the right eta is crucial for best results, which we discuss further in Section~\ref{sec:ablation-study}.

\subsubsection{Training Setup}
We choose the chat version of open-source Llama 2 (\texttt{Llama-2-\{7,13,70\}b-chat})~\citep{touvron2023llama2} as our base models, given its better instruction-following capabilities than base models and commendable performance on traditional NLP tasks. Following Vicuna~\citep{vicuna2023}, we standardize all data into a multi-turn chatbot-style format, allowing us to conveniently mix data from different sources. During fine-tuning, we only compute the loss on the model's output.  We fine-tune models of sizes 7B, 13B, and 70B using Megatron-LM~\citep{shoeybi2020megatronlm}.
We use a learning rate of 5e-5 for the 7B and 13B models, and 1e-5 for the 70B model. We set the batch size at 64 with 4,096 sequence length. We use AdamW optimizer~\citep{loshchilov2019decoupled} with a cosine learning scheduler with 2\% warm-up steps. For efficient training, we employ tensor parallelism~\citep{shoeybi2020megatronlm} for the 7B and 13B models, and for the 70B model, we also utilize pipeline parallelism~\citep{huang2019gpipe}.

\section{Related Work}


\paragraph{LLM-as-Agent} 
Before the rise of LLMs~\citep{GPT3,chowdhery2022palm,touvron2023llama,zeng2022glm}, agent tasks primarily relied on reinforcement learning or encoder models like BERT. With the advent of LLMs, research shifted towards LLM agents. Notably, ReAct~\citep{yao2023react} innovatively combined CoT reasoning with agent actions. Several studies also applied language models to specific agent tasks, such as online shopping~\citep{yao2022webshop}, web browsing~\citep{deng2023mind2web}, and household exploration~\citep{shridhar2020alfworld}. Recently, with ChatGPT showcasing advanced planning and reasoning skills, research like ReWOO~\citep{xu2023rewoo} and RCI~\citep{kim2023language} has delved into prompting strategies and frameworks to boost language model efficiency in agent tasks without the need for fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Instruction Tuning} 
Instruction tuning aims at aligning the language models to follow human instructions and produce outputs that better fit human preferences. Instruction tuning mainly focus on training language models to follow human instructions among multiple general tasks. For instance, FLAN~\citep{wei2022finetuned} and T0~\citep{sanh2022multitask} demonstrates the strong zero-shot generalization ability of language models fine-tuned on multiple task datasets. Further, FLAN-V2~\citep{longpre2023flan} explores the performance of instruction tuning across multiple scales of models and datasets. With the impressive alignment capability demonstrated by commercial LLMs, many recent works~\citep{vicuna2023, wang2023openchat} propose methods to distill instruction tuning dataset from close-sourced model to enhance the alignment of open-source models.



\section{Conclusion}


In this work, we study how to enable generalized agent abilities for LLMs, bridging the disparity  between open and commercial LLMs on agent tasks.  
We present the \method approach to achieve this goal. 
\method first introduces the \dataset dataset covering 1,866 verified agent interaction trajectories and then designs an instruction-tuning strategy with the mixture of \dataset and general-domain instructions. 
We generate the open \model by employing \method to tune the Llama 2 models. 
\model exhibits strong performance on unseen agent tasks while preserving their general abilities on MMLU, GSM8K, HumanEval, and MT-Bench. 
To date, \model-70B is the first open LLM that matches GPT-3.5-turbo on agent tasks.
