\title{Human-Instruction-Free LLM Self-Alignment with Limited Samples}

\begin{abstract}
    Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human values is a vital task for LLM practitioners. Current alignment techniques have several limitations: (1) requiring a large amount of annotated data; (2) demanding heavy human involvement; (3) lacking a systematic mechanism to continuously improve. In this work, we study aligning LLMs to a new domain with limited samples (e.g. < 100). We propose an algorithm that can \textit{self-align} LLMs \textit{iteratively} without active human involvement. Unlike existing works, our algorithm relies on neither human-crafted instructions nor labeled rewards, significantly reducing human involvement. In addition, our algorithm can self-improve the alignment continuously. The key idea is to first retrieve high-quality samples related to the target domain and use them as In-context Learning examples to generate more samples. Then we use the \textit{self-generated} samples to finetune the LLM iteratively. We show that our method can unlock the LLMs' self-generalization ability to perform alignment with near-zero human supervision. We test our algorithm on three benchmarks in safety, truthfulness, and instruction-following, and show good performance in alignment, domain adaptability, and scalability.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


The technique to make Large language models (LLMs) follow human instructions and generate safe outputs is alignment~\citep{ouyang2022training}. Currently, it is the key to generating sophisticated text and tackling a variety of language-based tasks \citep{brown2020language,bubeck2023sparks,openai2023gpt4,liu2023reason}. The mainstream alignment approaches include instruction fine-tuning \citep{wei2021finetuned} and preference learning \citep{ouyang2022training}.
Instruction tuning employs a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) process, largely dependent on human annotations or data derived from LLMs themselves. The key technique in preference learning is reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) \citep{ouyang2022training}, which iteratively refines an SFT-enhanced LLM to better align with human preferences. 

Both SFT and RLHF are heavily data-dependent. The lack of high-quality data significantly blocks the democratization of usable and safe LLMs. In this work,  we explore scenarios with limited examples from the target alignment domain such as safety, truthfulness, and helpfulness.  A few prior works propose to solve this problem with self-alignment~\citep{wang2022self,sun2023principle}, i.e. making the LLMs align themselves with samples generated by themselves. The common assumption is the pretrained LLMs have already learned a good amount of hidden knowledge related to the aligned behaviors and we just need to ``elicit'' it with samples generated by LLMs themselves rather than using direct human instructions.

However, the current self-alignment techniques are not truly free of human instructions. They still involve some form of hand-crafted instructions or principles to enhance the quality of the model-generated responses. It leads to two limitations: (1) Crafting effective human instruction is complex. For example, \citet{sun2023principle} needs to manually design 16 generic principles and multiple specific principles for different tasks. It requires substantial domain knowledge and risks erring at a higher level compared to a more bottom-up data-driven approach.
More importantly in practice, designing and refining human instructions requires considerable labor, which contradicts the scenario of limited samples where human resources are lacking. Additionally, adapting these instructions for new alignment domains often requires new guidelines, which motivates a more automatable approach.  (2) Current self-alignment can work only on large models. Existing works often require models with a significantly large number of parameters, e.g. \citet{wang2022self} use 175B \texttt{GPT-3} and \citet{sun2023principle} use \texttt{LLaMA-65B}.
And often the approach would be less effective for smaller models like \texttt{LLaMA-7B} since they are less capable of following instructions and understanding the contents \citep{li2023rain}.

A different line of approach is to use an external reward model to filter LLM-generated answers~\citep{gulcehre2023reinforced}, as opposed to applying supervising principles when generating answers. 
However, in scenarios where the target domain for alignment has limited samples, developing high-quality reward models is challenging and often still requires a significant amount of human labor to label rewards, which again contradicts the scenario where human resources are scarce. Additionally, external reward models often suffer from out-of-distribution (OOD) issues. 
In this work, we ask the following question:
\begin{center}
    \textit{Is it possible to \textbf{self-align} LLMs to a target domain with only \textbf{a few examples} and without any \textbf{human-designed instructions} or \textbf{external reward models}?}
\end{center}

To this end, we propose an alignment algorithm \ALGO{} (\underline{I}terative \underline{S}elf-\underline{A}lignment with \underline{R}etrieval-\underline{A}ugmented in-context learning), illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:\ALGO{}}. \ALGO{} diverges from traditional methods by leveraging retrieval-augmented in-context learning (ICL) to generate high-quality answers using contextually relevant, retrieved examples. The key idea is to first retrieve relevant and high-quality prompt-output pairs related to the target domain and use them as In-context Learning examples to generate more relevant samples belonging to the target domain. Then we use the \textit{self-generated} samples to finetune the LLM iteratively.

Another key design of \ALGO{} is its iterative mechanism, containing multiple training cycles. Each training cycle leverages the most recent LLM to generate a dataset of more refined quality. This is logical given we end up with a more aligned model after the alignment, which can generate more high-quality data that in return can be used to further align LLMs until we reach the limit imposed by the LLM capacity and data quality.
\ALGO{} can work on small models because we rely on retrieved examples rather than human instructions. Thanks to this design, the model only needs to imitate the style of the examples and does not need to understand the abstract concept of safety, truthfulness, or helpfulness from human-crafted principles, which would require a stronger ability that is only shown in large models. We find empirically that our framework can be adaptable to models as small as 350M and can be applied across various domains without the need for redesigning principles or retraining reward models.

In terms of balancing between harmlessness and helpfulness, we notably improve harmlessness rates without compromising helpfulness. Furthermore, our method shows robust domain generalization capabilities, particularly in various harmfulness domains, highlighting its adaptability and effectiveness.



\section{Related Work}


\subsection{Large Language Model Alignment}
In the pursuit of aligning Large Language Models (LLMs) with human preferences, OpenAI has implemented supervised fine-tuning (SFT) using instructions annotated by a diverse group of users, as exemplified in their GPT products such as \citet{openai2023gpt4}. Building on this, \citet{ouyang2022training} introduced the concept of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). This approach enhances alignment by learning from human preferences via a reward model trained with human-rated outputs. The scalability of RLHF has been further improved through RLAIF \citep{bai2022constitutional,lee2023rlaif}, which substitutes human feedback with AI-generated feedback. A recent development by LIMA \citep{zhou2023lima} demonstrates that substantial alignment improvements can be achieved using as few as 1,000 examples for SFT. This finding suggests that the bulk of knowledge in large language models is mainly acquired during pretraining, and only a limited amount of instruction tuning data is required to guide models towards producing high-quality outputs. In a similar vein, studies by \citet{lin2023unlocking,li2023rain} indicate that inference-time alignment can also attain high levels of performance. These advancements collectively highlight the evolving landscape of LLM alignment strategies, emphasizing efficiency and efficacy in model training.

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented ICL}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) \citep{lewis2020retrieval} represents a hybrid approach that integrates an information retrieval component with a text generation model, specifically tailored for knowledge-intensive tasks. This method's notable advantage lies in its ability to efficiently update its internal knowledge base without the necessity of retraining the entire model. A prime example of this approach is the kNN-augmented Transformer \citep{wu2022memorizing}, which extends the attention context size by incorporating k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) lookups to retrieve context embeddings from similar past instances. Research by \citet{lin2022unsupervised,han2023context} has indicated that the collection of diverse instruction datasets, coupled with the retrieval of examples most closely matching the input queries, can significantly expedite the model's generalization capabilities.

\subsection{Self-Alignment} 
Our work contributes to the growing body of work focused on training or fine-tuning LLMs with self-generated datasets. Existing studies in this area, such as those by \citet{wang2022self,sun2023principle}, often rely on human instructions, which we argue counteracts the objective of self-alignment to reduce human intervention.
Notably, those two prominent self-alignment frameworks necessitate LLMs with at least 65B parameters, as smaller models struggle with following complex human instructions using in-context learning (ICL). Our framework, however, diverges from this trend by eliminating the requirement for human-crafted principles or demonstrations, thus significantly reducing human involvement and catering to LLMs that are less proficient in instruction-following. A recent development in this field is the ReST framework \citep{gulcehre2023reinforced}, which introduces an iterative self-alignment model that utilizes a learned reward system to filter out low-quality QA pairs from generated datasets, thereby avoiding the complexities of elaborate principles. In contrast, our approach employs retrieval-augmented ICL to enhance the generation quality. Our framework is not just iterative but also operates independently of both handcrafted principles and learned reward models, marking a unique advancement in self-alignment methodologies.
Self-alignment is intrinsically linked to self-supervised learning, typically involving prompt-only datasets. For instance, LMSI \citep{huang2022large} leverages Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting \citep{wei2022chain} to generate high-quality responses for unlabeled datasets. Similarly, SALMON \citep{sun2023salmon} creates a principle-adhering reward model from a synthetic dataset and further enhances the LLM through reinforcement learning. Moreover, QA datasets can be derived from existing text corpora, like web corpora, prompting LLMs to generate questions from the inherent knowledge. Methods in this sphere include backtranslation \citep{koksal2023longform,li2023self}, self-chat \citep{xu2023baize}, and self-QA \citep{zhang2023self}. However, these techniques depend on handcrafted principles to improve the dataset quality and typically involve a single training iteration. We detail these comparisons in Table \ref{tab:requirement_comparison}.



\section{Preliminaries}

We use the terms ``sample'' and ``example'' interchangeably to refer to a prompt-output pair. We also use prompt-output and question-answer (QA) pairs interchangeably. 
Denote the input token space by \(\cX\) and the output token space by \(\cY\). A sequence of tokens is represented by \(\bz = (z_1, \dots, z_\ell)\) for any \(z_1, \dots, z_\ell \in \cX\) or \(\cY\). The notation \(\bz_{i,j} = (z_i, \dots, z_j)\) is used for any \(1 <= i <= j <= \ell\), and we define \(\bz_{i,j} = \emptyset\) for any \(j < i\). 

An LLM generates an output sequence \(\by = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T)\) in response to a given prompt \(\bx = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)\). LLM is an auto-regressive model characterized by a conditional probability distribution
parameterized by \(\theta\) as
\begin{align*}
    \PP_\theta(\by \given \bx) = \prod_{t=1}^T \PP_\theta(y_t \given \bx, \by_{1:t-1}).
\end{align*}
For in-context learning, we assume there are \(C\) examples \((\bar \bx^1, \bar \by^1), \dots, (\bar \bx^C, \bar \by^C)\) curated by human or retrieved from an external datastore. Those examples serve as context and are combined with the given question to form the prompt. The generation can be characterized by
\begin{align*}
    \PP_\theta(\by \given \bar\bx^1, \bar\by^1, \dots, \bar\bx^C, \bar\by^C, \bx) = \prod_{t=1}^T \PP_\theta(y_t \given \bar\bx^1, \bar\by^1, \dots, \bar\bx^C, \bar\by^C, \bx, \by_{1:t-1}).
\end{align*}
Let \(\PP(\bx, \by) = \PP(\bx) \cdot \PP(\by \given \bx)\) be the data distribution. A given dataset \(\cD\) is comprised of samples from this distribution:
\begin{align*}
    \cD = \bigl\{(\bx^i, \by^i)\bigr\}_{i=1}^N \quad\text{where}\quad\bx^i \sim \PP(\bx) \text{ and } \by^i \sim \PP(\by \given \bx^i).
\end{align*}
Given such a dataset, SFT can be conducted using the following cross-entropy loss:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:loss}
    \cL(\theta, \cD) = - \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T \log \PP_\theta(y^i_t \given \bx^i, \by^i_{1:t-1}).
\end{align}



\section{Methodology}

Our methodology only requires a few question-answer examples, denoted by \(\cD_0\). 
The proposed framework consists of multiple iterations, each encompassing both dataset generation and fine-tuning phases. The entire framework is detailed in Algorithm \ref{alg_\ALGO{}}. 

\input{sections/algo}

\subsection{Data Generation}
In the \(k\)-th iteration, our goal is to prompt the LLM to generate a dataset of \(N\) new question-answer (QA) samples. This process begins by sampling \(C\) QA pairs from all preceding datasets \(\cD_0, \dots, \cD_{k-1}\), ensuring each dataset contributes at least one example to enhance diversity. We sample QA pairs from all preceding datasets and use those as contexts to prompt the LLM to generate one new question at a time (line \ref{line:gen_q}-\ref{line:gen_q_} of Algorithm \ref{alg_\ALGO{}}). 

Next, we adopt retrieval-augmented in-context learning to annotate the newly generated question with a corresponding aligned answer (line \ref{line:gen_a}-\ref{line:gen_a_}). Specifically, we utilize k-nearest-neighbors (kNN) to identify similar questions from prior datasets based on sentence embeddings, using external embedding models like \texttt{text-embedding-ada-002}. Both questions and answers from these pairs are used as contexts in answer generation. Upon generating a set of \(C\) new QA pairs, we apply simple filtering criteria to remove low quality samples, such as excluding pairs where the question already exists in previous datasets (line \ref{line:filter}).

Since our context for the LLM is always a combination of examples without any human-designed principles, we do not require LLM to have the ability to follow human instructions and also reduce human efforts to a new minimum.

\subsection{Finetuning}
After we create dataset \(\cD_k\), we perform supervised fine-tuning (SFT) (line \ref{line:sft}).
This SFT incorporates both the newly generated dataset \(\cD_k\) and the initial dataset \(\cD_0\). The design is to ensure the alignment training data to be as the most high-quality as we can get. Since the initial dataset \(\cD_0\) is manually annotated or selected by human, it should have high quality. In addition, as the LLM aligns iteratively, the latest LLM should be the most aligned, and therefore the data generated by it should have the best quality among all self-generated samples, except the initial samples.
We employ a coefficient \(\gamma \in (0, \infty)\) to regulate the proportion of data used from each dataset during the fine-tuning process. 
We use the cross-entropy loss defined in eq.\eqref{eq:loss} as our SFT loss function.

\subsection{Iterative Enhancement}
We find empirically that retrieval-augmented alignment can iteratively enhance the performance of the finetuned model. Therefore, we repeat the \textbf{data generation} and \textbf{finetuning} phases iteratively until a threshold is reached --- specifically, when less than \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) of newly generated samples remain post-filtering (line \ref{line:stop}-\ref{line:stop_}). We call this ratio the stopping threshold. This threshold indicates the model's peak capability in producing high-quality new QA pairs based on the current data. If the threshold is not provided by users, we can still stop the iterative training process by setting a maximum number of iterations. In Algorithm \ref{alg_\ALGO{}}, we set both of those stopping criteria and output the finetuned model in the latest iteration before stopping.


\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Our empirical experiments are designed to evaluate the efficacy of our method across three distinct metrics: safety, truthfulness, and instruction-following.
These metrics correspond to three benchmarks: \textsc{BeaverTails}, \textsc{TruthfulQA}, and \textsc{Alpaca-Eval}. We set the context example count, \(C\), to 8 for \textsc{BeaverTails} and \textsc{TruthfulQA}, and 6 for \textsc{Alpaca-Eval}. Those numbers are much smaller than the number of samples required for performing supervised fine-tuning.
During both question and answer generation phases, each dataset contributes one question-answer pair, with the exception of the initial dataset \(\cD_0\), which is of assured quality. Consequently, we sample \(C - k\) examples from \(\cD_0\) in the \(k\)-th iteration.
In all experimental settings, we fix the coefficient \(\gamma\) at 1 and the stopping threshold \(\alpha\) at 0.3. We also set the maximum number of iterations, \(K\), to \(\lceil C/2\rceil\) to ensure that at least half of the examples in the context are sourced from the initial dataset. 

Additionally, our filtering rule at line \ref{line:stop} of Algorithm \ref{alg_\ALGO{}} removes a sample if it meets any of the following criteria: (1) The ROUGE-L score of a generated question, compared to those in the context, is 0.7 or higher. (2) The question is a duplicate, having appeared previously in either the initial or a generated dataset. (3) The answer is merely a repetition of the question. (4) Either the question or the answer contains fewer than 5 words.

\subsection{\textsc{BeaverTails}: Safety Alignment Experiment}
We focus on safety alignment using \ALGO{} and seek to address the following key questions: (1) How does \ALGO{}'s performance compare with retrieval-based in-context learning (ICL)? (2) Is \ALGO{} effective across various LLM sizes? (3) Does iterative training yield better results than a single training session? (4) Can \ALGO{} generalize across different domains in alignment tasks? (5) Does \ALGO{} maintain utility (helpfulness) while enhancing harmlessness? (6) What level of data scaling efficiency does \ALGO{} achieve?

To explore these research questions, we employ \textsc{BeaverTails} \citep{ji2023beavertails}, a QA dataset designed specifically for assessing the safety alignment of LLMs, featuring over a substantial amount of prompts sourced from the HH \textsc{Red-Team} dataset \citep{ganguli2022red} and \textsc{SafetyPrompts} \citep{sun2023safety}. This dataset encompasses annotated QA pairs across 14 potential harm categories. We undertook data preprocessing tasks such as categorizing prompts into relevant domains and resolving contradictory annotations. Detailed preprocessing information can be found in Section \ref{sec:preprocess}.

For evaluation purposes, we utilize \texttt{Beaver-Dam-7B}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/PKU-Alignment/beaver-dam-7b}}, a classification model derived from the \texttt{LLaMA-7B} base model, specifically trained on the \textsc{BeaverTails} classification dataset. This model evaluates each answer within the 14 harm categories, assigning a ``True'' or ``False'' label to indicate whether the response is harmful in relation to the posed question. This evaluation method is particularly robust due to its direct training on the dataset in question.
For assessing utility, we incorporate an external reward model \texttt{Beaver-7B-v1.0-Reward}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/PKU-Alignment/beaver-7b-v1.0-reward}} trained based on human preferences to determine the helpfulness of an answer, irrespective of its harmfulness. This allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the practical utility of the responses generated by the LLMs. Note that both the classification model and the reward model are for evaluation purposes and are not used in training.

\paragraph{Performance.}
To focus our evaluation on areas of greater difficulty, we identified three particularly challenging domains using a validation task. These domains are \textit{discrimination, stereotype, injustice}, \textit{hate speech, offensive language}, and \textit{non-violent unethical behavior}. For each category, we build a training dataset with 64 QA pairs sampled from all the data within the category, and an evaluation dataset comprising 250 unique prompts. We assess both our method and baseline approaches in these domains and average the results.
In the SFT approach, we finetune the pre-trained model for two epochs using only the initial dataset. For \ALGO{}, we set the parameter \(N\) to 512 and apply a stopping threshold of 0.3.

We categorize the methods into two groups: (1) The main methods that are named as \textbf{Base \& Tuned} models which include the pretrained models and tuning-based alignment, i.e. SFT and our method. Note that ICL-kNN, the method used for annotating \ALGO{}'s training data, is surpassed by \ALGO{} itself. This enhanced performance can be attributed to \ALGO{}'s iterative learning feature and the observation that applying ICL to a finetuned model further refines its performance. (2) ICL-kNN consistently demonstrates superior results compared to ICL-Random, highlighting the advantage of using relevant, contextually appropriate samples over random ones to enhance alignment effectiveness.

\paragraph{Ablation study on model sizes.}
We conduct an ablation study to investigate how the size of the model influences \ALGO{}'s performance. We experiment with the family of OPT models \citep{zhang2022opt} with sizes varying from 350M to 6.7B.
The findings are presented in Table \ref{tab:ablation_modelsize}. The results clearly indicate that \ALGO{} achieves better performance in general with an increase in the model size, both in terms of final outcomes and improvement relative to the pretrained models.
This observation aligns with the established understanding that larger models possess enhanced in-context learning capabilities, thereby enabling the generation of superior QA pairs for training purposes. 

\paragraph{Comparing iterative training with one-time training.} A distinctive feature of \ALGO{} is its iterative training framework that operates over multiple iterations. The underlying hypothesis is that a model finetuned through iterative training will generate higher-quality answers compared to its pretrained counterpart, particularly when both are enhanced by in-context learning. To test this hypothesis, we compared \ALGO{}, with its two iterations generating 512 new training samples each, against a variant that performs a single training session to generate 1024 new samples, thereby equalizing the total number of generated samples in both scenarios.
The results are detailed in Table \ref{tab:iterative}.
We observe a consistent performance trend for both \texttt{LLaMA-7B} and \texttt{OPT-6.7B}. \ALGO{} (\(N=512\) Iter 2 consistently outperforms \ALGO{} (\(N=1024\)) Iter 1. Since those two methods generates exactly the same number of new samples, it validates our proposition that iterative training surpasses the one-time approach in this context.

\paragraph{\textbf{Evaluating data scaling efficiency.}}
The data scaling coefficient, defined as the ratio of the total number of generated samples to the initial dataset size, serves as a key metric in our study. Applied to the three domains under investigation, our results reveal an impressive average scaling ratio exceeding 6 for both \texttt{LLaMA-7B} and \texttt{OPT-6.7B} models. This high ratio highlights the efficiency of our methodology in significantly expanding the dataset beyond its initial volume.
As detailed in Section \ref{sec:setup}, our filtering rule ensures that the generated samples do not merely replicate existing ones, thereby maintaining the novelty and relevance of the data. Therefore, we can produce a range of diverse and pertinent samples, which contribute to the overall efficacy of the alignment performance.

\subsection{\textsc{TruthfulQA}: Truthfulness Alignment Experiment}

The \textsc{TruthfulQA} benchmark \citep{lin2021truthfulqa} is designed to assess the truthfulness of language models in their response generation. For evaluation, we utilize the ROUGE-L score \citep{lin2004rouge} difference, calculated as the difference between the highest similarity to a true reference answer and the highest similarity to a false reference answer. Higher difference indicates better answer.
The results, as outlined in Table \ref{tab:truthfulqa}, reveal that \ALGO{} not only enhances the performance of the pretrained model but also yields more substantial improvements compared to the SFT method.

\section{Conclusion}

We propose \ALGO{} (\underline{I}terative \underline{S}elf-\underline{A}lignment with \underline{R}etrieval-\underline{A}ugmented in-context learning), a framework designed to self-align Large Language Models. Our primary objective is to eliminate the reliance on human instructions, a common limitation in prior works \citep{wang2022self,sun2023principle}. \ALGO{} integrates retrieval-augmented in-context learning to autonomously generate high-quality samples, enhancing the model's self-alignment capabilities.
Another key feature of \ALGO{} is its iterative training framework, developed upon the insight that retrieval-augmented ICL can further elevate the performance of already finetuned models. In each iteration, the framework utilizes the latest model iteration to produce a dataset of progressively higher quality. 

Our comprehensive experiments across safety, truthfulness, and instruction-following alignment benchmarks have demonstrated \ALGO{}'s superiority in terms of alignment performance, domain adaptability, and scalability. These findings underscore \ALGO{}'s potential to significantly advance the field of LLM alignment, offering a pathway towards more autonomous, efficient, and adaptable LLMs.
