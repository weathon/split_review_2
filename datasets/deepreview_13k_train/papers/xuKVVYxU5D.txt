\title{Single-Trajectory Distributionally Robust Reinforcement Learning}

\begin{abstract}
    As a framework for sequential decision-making, Reinforcement Learning (RL) has been regarded as an essential component leading to Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). 
    However, RL is often criticized for having the same training environment as the test one, which also hinders its application in the real world. 
    To mitigate this problem, Distributionally Robust RL (DRRL) is proposed to improve the worst performance in a set of environments that may contain the unknown test environment.
    Due to the nonlinearity of the robustness goal, most of the previous work resort to the model-based approach, learning with either an empirical distribution learned from the data or a simulator that can be sampled infinitely, which limits their applications in simple dynamics environments. 
    In contrast, we attempt to design a DRRL algorithm that can be trained along a single trajectory, i.e., no repeated sampling from a state. 
    Based on the standard $Q$-learning, we propose \emph{distributionally robust Q-learning with single trajectory (DRQ)}, and its average-reward variant named \emph{differential DRQ}.
    We provide asymptotic convergence guarantees and experiments for both settings, demonstrating their superiority in the perturbed environments against the non-robust ones.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\printAffiliationsAndNotice{\icmlEqualContribution}

Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a machine learning paradigm studying the sequential decision problem.
In particular, it aims to learn an optimal policy that maximizes the cumulative return in interacting with the environment.
While remarkable progress has been shown recently \cite{silver2016mastering,mnih2015human,vinyals2019grandmaster}, 
a key assumption always lies beneath, i.e., the test environment in which the learning algorithm to be employed is the same as the training environment. 
The discrepancy between them is prevalent as the test environments are often too complicated to be perfectly captured in the training one.
Moreover, even though the true environment can be adequately modeled and used as the training environment, the environment shifts may still be prevalent in some application scenarios, e.g., financial market and robotic control.
The overlooking of the mismatch between them could lead to the failure of RL algorithms to be applied in real-world applications, as the optimal policy of a Markov Decision Process (MDP) is well known for being sensitive to the model \cite{mannor2004bias,iyengar2005robust}.

As a result, the environment shift should be included in the training process to learn a practical RL policy. Distributionally Robust MDPs (DRMDPs) \citep{satia1973markovian, nilim2005robust, iyengar2005robust, wiesemann2013robust, lim2013reinforcement,ho2021partial,goyal2022robust} adopt this idea and formulate the problem by assuming the test environment, even different from, but still staying nearby the training environment.
The goal is to design algorithms to optimize the worst-case expected return over an ambiguity set that contains all the possible testing distributions.
Solving the robust MDPs with a general ambiguity set could be NP-hard \cite{wiesemann2013robust} and thus, to ensure computational feasibility, it is common to assume $s$/$(s,a)$  rectangular assumption, which allows the ambiguity set to be constructed for each $s$/$(s,a)$ pair.
Built upon them, \cite{iyengar2005robust, nilim2005robust} instantiate the probability distance measure in constructing ambiguity set with specific cases of $\phi$-divergence, such as $\chi^2$ and KL divergences.
Moreover, while searching over the ambiguity set is an infinite dimensional optimization problem, \citet{shapiro2017distributionally} proves that it can be transformed into a two-dimensional optimization problem.

In reality, we can mainly access the samples from the training environment instead of the transition model.
In this case, optimizing a policy w.r.t. the performance in a known test environment with only access to the samples from the training environment is even more challenging, which is being solved by the distributionally robust RL (DRRL) literature.
Theoretical understanding, including the sample efficiency, has been actively progressed \cite{zhou2021finitesample,yang2021theoretical,shi2022distributionally,panaganti2022robust, ma2022distributionally}.
Besides, a line of recent attention focuses on designing practical algorithms and shows promising empirical evidence in robustness gaining~\cite{liu2022distributionally,Neufeld2022RobustQA,abdullah2019wasserstein}.

A natural choice to solve the DDRL problem is the model-based framework~\cite{Yang2018WassersteinDR,abdullah2019wasserstein, Neufeld2022RobustQA}, which first estimates the transition model from data, constructs the ambiguity set around the empirical distribution, and solves the robust problem approximately.
Although model-based methods are often more sample-efficient and easier to analyze, they are also well-known for their high computational cost, great demand for memory to store the whole MDP model, and possible failure to generalize to non-tabular RL settings.
Moreover, real environments in some scenarios, such as image-based tasks, are too complicated to be adequately modeled. Thus, the policy learned from the mismatch model may significantly deviate from the true one.

In contrast, model-free algorithms learn to select actions directly, bypassing the above challenges from the model-based methods.
In particular, $Q$-learning \cite{watkins1992q}, a paradigm of the model-free algorithm, enjoys both theoretical understanding and wide deployment in practical applications~\cite{mnih2015human,lillicrap2019continuous}. 
There are indeed some attempts to solve DRRL without models, e.g., \cite{liu2022distributionally}.
Instead, the key assumption beneath them is the weak simulator assumption, where the algorithm can access an arbitrary number of samples from any state-action pair.
Such an assumption is too restricted when the simulator is inaccessible and fails to achieve a pure model-free training manner.
As sequential decision algorithms, RL agents should mostly be trained along \emph{a single trajectory}, where the observed next state is the only realized sample following the transition model from the current state.
Most model-free non-robust RL algorithms support training in this setting, which flourishes their wide applications.
Instead, as far as we know, no existing work can solve the DRRL problem in this manner due to the nontrivial difficulty arising from robustness gain requirements.
Thus a natural question arises,

\textit{Can we design a DRRL algorithm for learning along a single trajectory?}

\subsection{Our Contributions}
We design a distributionally robust $Q$-learning algorithm with features beyond the previous DRRL algorithms.
The \textbf{first} feature is leveraging the strong duality of $\phi$-divergence distributionally robust problem to transform the infinite-dimensional optimization problem into a finite-dimensional one and derive the DR Bellman operator for various $\phi$-divergence, including $\chi^2$ and KL divergences.
The \textbf{second} one is that we develop a three timescales stochastic approximation framework to solve the DR Bellman equation by carefully exploiting the structure of the corresponding Bellman operator. 
The first two loops are designed to estimate the non-linear Bellman operator of various $\phi$-divergence, and the final loop is to update the Q function.
The discrepancy in the speed between estimating the DR Bellman operator and updating the Q function can alleviate the bias from the nonlinear Bellman operator.
\textbf{Third} is that we instantiate our framework into the DR-variant of $Q$-learning algorithms, \emph{distributionally robust $Q$-learning with single trajectory (DRQ)}, to solve the discount MDPs using various $\phi$-divergence with single-trajectory.
We prove that our proposed  algorithm asymptotically converges to optimal DR policy.
\textbf{Fourth} is that we extend our framework to solve the DRRL problem with average reward and develop the \emph{differential DRQ} algorithm.
As far as we know, this is the first DRRL algorithm in the average-reward RL setting.
\textbf{Finally}, we provide empirical evidence on navigation and queueing control tasks to demonstrate the robustness and acceptable sample efficiency of the policy learned by the proposed DR $Q$ learning algorithm.

\subsection{Related Work}

Distributional robustness has been actively studied in supervised learning. While a stream of the literature focus on the setting where the testing distribution is the same as the training one, and the learner merely gauges the distributional ambiguity level to balance statistical utility and robustness carefully \cite{bertsimas2004price, delage2010distributionally, hu2013kullback, duchi2021learning}, another stream of work consider the setting where learning predictive rules in testing distributions are different from the training distributions and perturb the training sample with some synthesized noise before solving the empirical risk minimization problem, which has been shown to work \cite{sinha2018certifiable, goodfellow2014explaining, ganin2016domain, zhang2019theoretically, tramer2017ensemble}.
Beyond supervised learning, DR MDPs also have fruitful results \cite{iyengar2005robust, el2005robust, xu2010distributionally, wiesemann2013robust}.
By assuming the known MDP environment, DRMDPs mainly focus on the structure of the optimal policy and the computational cost for planning.
In contrast, recent emerging attention focuses on learning the optimal DR policy only with access to the samples collected from the environment.




\section{Preliminary}


\subsection{Standard MDPs}
\label{subsec:discount_form}
Consider an infinite-horizon MDP $(\gS, \gA, \gamma, \mu, P, r)$ where $\gS$ and $\gA$ are finite state and action spaces with cardinality $S$ and $A$.
$P:\gS\times \gA \rightarrow \Delta_{S}$ is state transition probability measure and $r$ is the reward function. 
Without loss of generality, we assume that $r:\gS\times \gA \rightarrow [0,1]$ is deterministic and bounded in $[0, 1]$. 
A stationary  policy $\pi:\gS \rightarrow \Delta_{A}$ maps, for each state $s$ to a probability distribution over the action set $\gA$ and induce a random trajectory $s_1,a_1,r_1,s_2,\cdots$, with $s_1\sim \mu$, $a_n = \pi(s_n)$ and $s_{n+1}\sim P(\cdot \lvert s_n, a_n) \coloneqq P_{s_n, a_n} $ for $n\in \gN^+$. 

To derive the policy corresponding to the value function, we define the action-value function $Q:\gS\times \gA \rightarrow \sR$ as,
\begin{small}
\begin{equation*}
 Q^{\star}(s,a) \coloneqq \sup_{\pi\in \Pi}\sE_{\pi, P} \left[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \gamma^{n-1} r(s_n,a_n)\lvert s_1 = s, a_1 = a \right],
\end{equation*}
\end{small}
which provides the expected cumulative discounted rewards under the optimal policy. 
The optimal $Q$ function is the fixed point of the following the Bellman optimality equation,
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:non-robust-opt}
 Q^{\star}(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma \sE_P \left[\max_{a\in \gA} Q^{\star}(s',a) \right].
\end{align}

\subsection{$Q$-learning}
Our model-free algorithmic design relies on a $Q$-learning template, originally designed to solve the non-robust Bellman optimality equation (Equation~\ref{eq:non-robust-opt}).
$Q$-learning is a model-free reinforcement learning algorithm to use sample trajectories to update the estimation for the $Q$ function incrementally.
Suppose at time $n$, we draw a sample $(s_n, a_n, r_n, s'_n)$, from the environment using the policy $\pi$. 
Then the algorithm updates the estimated $Q$-function following
\begin{align*}
 Q_{n+1}(s_n,a_n) &= (1-\alpha_n(s_n, a_n))Q_n(s_n, a_n)\\
 & + \alpha_n(s_n, a_n) (r_n + \gamma \max_{a^{\prime}\in \gA}Q_n(s^{\prime}_{n}, a^{\prime})).
\end{align*}
In other word, the algorithm updates the $Q$ function by incrementally updating the unbiased estimator for the true $Q$ value, i.e., $r_n + \gamma \max_{a^{\prime}\in \gA} Q_n(s^{\prime}_n, a^{\prime})$.

\subsection{Distributionally Robust MDPs}
\label{subsec:drdMDPs}
DRRL learns the optimal policy that is robust to unknown environmental changes.
In particular, the transition model $P$ and the reward function $r$ can potentially be different in the test environment.
To better present our key contributions, we restrict our attention to the perturbation of the transition model and assume the reward function to be the same. 
The notion of distributional robustness implies that the true transition $P$, although not the same as the training environment and cannot be known in advance, lies within a so-called ambiguity set $\gP$.
The ambiguity set $\gP$ contains all the transition models that are close to the training environment under some probability distance measure.

To ensure the computational feasibility, we adopt the $(s,a)$-rectangular manner in constructing the ambiguity set \cite{iyengar2005robust, wiesemann2013robust}: for each $(s,a)\in \gS\times \gA$, we define ambiguity set containing all probability measure with some distance $D$ no more than $\rho$ from $P_{s,a}$,
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:sa_ambiguity_set}
 \gP_{s,a} \coloneqq \{P'_{s,a}\lvert D(P'_{s,a}\lVert P_{s,a})\le \rho\}.
\end{align}
Then we build the ambiguity set for the whole transition model as the Cartesian product for every $(s,a)$-ambiguity set, i.e., $\gP = \prod_{(s,a)\in \gS\times \gA} \gP_{s,a}$.
This construction ensures that $\gP$ is a compact set.
Given the ambiguity set $\gP$, we define the optimal DR action-value function as,
\begin{small}
\begin{equation*}
 Q^{\operatorname{rob}, \star}(s,a) \coloneqq \sup_{\pi\in \Pi}\inf_{P\in \gP} \sE_{\pi, P}[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \gamma^{n-1} r(s_n,a_n)\lvert s_1 = s, a_1 = a].
\end{equation*}
\end{small}
Under the $(s,a)$-rectangular assumption, the Bellman optimality equation has been established by \cite{iyengar2005robust, xu2010distributionally}
\begin{small}
\begin{equation}
Q^{\operatorname{rob},\star}(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma \inf_{P\in \gP} \sE_{s'\sim P} \left[\max_{a\in \gA}Q^{\operatorname{rob}, \star}(s',a) \right].\label{eq:optimality}
\end{equation}
\end{small}

For notation simplicity, we would ignore the superscript $\operatorname{rob}$.




\section{Distributonally Robust $Q$-learning with Single Trajectory}

\label{sec:discount}

In this section, we aim to solve DRRL with single-trajectory data.
We first show the nontrivial difficulty aroused from approximating the DR Bellman operator.
Then we present our three-timescale framework and instantiate it into the two most common divergences, $\chi^2$, and KL divergences.

\subsection{Bias in Plug-in Estimator}
In general, directly using the DR Bellman optimal equation (Equation~\ref{eq:optimality}) involve computing an expectation w.r.t. each model $P\in \gP$. 
Searching the worst-case over the ambiguity set $\gP$ is an infinite-dimensional optimization problem that can be computationally infeasible.
Given that the samples from the single trajectory are generated according to the nominal model $P$, estimating the expectation under different model $P'\in \gP$ is even more challenging.
Even if it can be achieved by, e.g., importance sampling-based techniques, the cost of high variance is still not desirable.

However, when restricting the probability measure to $\chi^2$-divergence, i.e., $D_{\chi^{2}}(Q,P) = \int (\frac{dQ}{dP} - 1)^2 dP$, the infinite-dimensional optimization problem can be reduced to be univariate, which significantly improve the computational feasibility.
This transformation is by exploiting the dual form of the primal problem, which is formalized as follows.


{\footnotesize
\begin{lemma}[\citet{duchi2021learning}]
 \label{lm:dual_chi}
 For any $X\sim P$ and any $\rho\in [1,\infty)$, the DRO problem with $\chi^2$ divergence can be transformed via,
 \begin{align}
 & \inf_{D_{\chi^2}(P'\lVert P)\le \rho} \sE_{P'}[X] = \sup_{\eta\in \sR} \sigma_{\cc}(X, \eta)\notag\\
 & \qquad \coloneqq \sup_{\eta\in \sR}\left\{\eta - \sqrt{1+\rho} \sqrt{\sE_{P}[(\eta - X)_+^2]} \right\}.
 \label{eq:chi_opt}
 \end{align}
\end{lemma}
}

Lemma~\ref{lm:dual_chi} implies that the DRO with $\chi^2$ divergence can be solved using the original probability measure $P$, which sheds light on the single-trajectory solutions for the DRRL problem.
Applying Equation~\ref{eq:chi_opt} to Equation~\ref{eq:optimality} we know the optimal action-value function is the fixed point of the equation $Q = \gT^{\chi^2}(Q)$ with
\begin{equation}
 \gT^{\chi^2}(Q)(s,a) = r(s,a)+\gamma \sup_{\eta\in \sR} \sigma_{\chi^2} (\max_{a\in \gA}Q(\cdot, a), \eta ).\label{eq:bellman_chi}
\end{equation}
Similar to $\chi^2$ divergence, the DRO with KL divergence, where $D_{\operatorname{KL}}(Q,P) = \int \log(\frac{dQ}{dP}) dP$ for $Q \ll P$, also enjoys a univariate dual form as follow.
\begin{lemma}[\cite{hu2013kullback}]
 \label{lm:dual_KL}
 Suppose $X\sim P$ has a finite moment generating function in the neighborhood of zero. 
 Then,
 \begin{align}
 &\inf_{P^{\prime}: D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(P^{\prime} \| P\right) \leq \rho} \mathbb{E}_{P^{\prime}}[X]=\sup_{\beta \geq 0} \sigma_{\operatorname{KL}}(X, \beta) \notag\\ 
 &\qquad \coloneqq \sup_{\beta \geq 0}\left\{-\beta \log \left(\mathbb{E}_P\left[e^{-X / \beta}\right]\right)-\beta \cdot \rho\right\}. \label{eq:KL_opt}
 \end{align}
\end{lemma} 

Applying Equations~\ref{eq:KL_opt} to Equation~\ref{eq:optimality} we have the optimal action-value function under KL divergence is the fixed point solution of $Q = \mathcal{T}^{\operatorname{KL}}(Q)$ with 
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}^{\operatorname{KL}}(Q)(s,a) = r(s,a) + \gamma \sup_{\beta\ge 0}\sigma_{\rm KL}(\max_{a\in \gA}Q(\cdot, a), \beta ). \label{eq:bellman_KL}
\end{equation}

Sample average approximation (SAA), i.e., replacing the expectation with the sample average, is a standard method for constructing estimators.
Following it, we can construct a plug-in estimator by adopting the SAA in Equation~\ref{eq:bellman_chi} and \ref{eq:bellman_KL}.
However, sufficient samples may not be feasible for constructing an accurate enough estimator for every $(s,a)$-pair with single trajectory samples.
More challenging, this plug-in estimator is, in fact, biased because of the nonlinearity of the Bellman operator.
To provide a concrete taste of the bias, consider the case when only one sample is obtained, then the $\chi^2$ Bellman operator in Equation~\ref{eq:bellman_chi} becomes, 
\begin{align*}
 &r(s,a) + \gamma\sup_{\eta\in \sR} \sigma_{\chi^2} (\max_{a\in \gA}Q(\cdot, a), \eta )\\
 = &r(s,a) + \gamma\sup_{\eta\in \gR}\{\eta -\sqrt{1+\rho}(\eta - \max_a Q(s',a))_+ \}\\
 = &r(s,a) + \gamma \max_a Q(s',a),
\end{align*}
which is reduced to the non-robust Bellman operator and obviously not an unbiased estimator for $\gT^{\chi^2}(Q)$.
A similar issue appears in KL divergence.




\begin{algorithm*}[ht]
 \caption{Distributionally Robust $Q$-learning with $\chi^2$ divergence}
 \label{alg:discount_dro_Q_chi}
 \begin{algorithmic}[1]
 \STATE {\bfseries Input:} Exploration rate $\epsilon$, Learning rates $\{\zeta_i(n)\}_{i\in[3]}$
 \STATE {\bfseries Init:} $Q(s,a) = 0, \forall (s,a)\in \gS\times\gA$ 
 \FOR {$n = 1,2,\cdots$}
 \STATE Observe the state $s_n$, execute the action $a_n = \operatorname{\arg\max}_{a\in \gA} Q_n(s_n, a)$ using $\epsilon$ greedy policy
 \STATE Observe the reward $r_n$ and next state $s_{n}^{\prime}$
 \STATE Update $Z_{n,1}$, $Z_{n,2}$ follow
 \begin{align*}
 &Z_{n+1, 1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1- \zeta_1(n))Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n})
 + \zeta_1(n) (\eta_{n, 1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) - \max_a Q_n(s_n^{\prime},a))_+, \\
 &Z_{n+1, 2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1- \zeta_1(n))Z_{n,2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) + \zeta_1(n) (\eta_{n, 1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) - \max_a Q_n(s_n^{\prime},a))_+^2. 
 \end{align*}
 \STATE 
 Update $\eta_n$ via 
 $
 \eta_{n+1}(s_n, a_n) = (1-\zeta_2(n))\eta_n(s_n, a_n)
 + \zeta_2(n)(1 - \sqrt{\rho +1}\frac{Z_{n, 1}}{ \sqrt{Z_{n, 2}}}).
 $
 \STATE 
 Update $Q_n$ via 
 $
 Q_{n+1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1-\zeta_3(n)) Q_n(s_{n}, a_{n})
 + \zeta_3(n) (r_n + \gamma(\eta_n(s_n,a_n)-\sqrt{1+\rho} \sqrt{Z_{n,2}(s_n,a_n)})).
 $
 \ENDFOR
 \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

To solve this bias in the SAA method,~\citet{liu2022distributionally} introduces the multilevel Monte-Carlo method \cite{blanchet2015unbiased}, which requires a large batch of samples for the same $(s,a)$ pair before the next update, and it is not flexible to be adopted in the online decision nature and is prohibitive in our single trajectory setting.

Note that the non-robust $Q$-learning is solving the Bellman operator's fixed point in a stochastic approximation manner.
A salient feature in the DR Bellman operator is the bi-level optimization nature, i.e., jointly solving the dual parameter and the fixed point of the Bellman optimality equation.
We revisit the stochastic approximation view of the $Q$-learning and develop a three-timescale framework by a faster running estimate of the optimal dual parameter and the slower update of the $Q$ table.

In this paper, we mainly discuss the most common cases in $\phi$-divergence, i.e., when divergence is chosen as $\chi^2$ and KL divergence. 

\subsection{$\chi^2$ Divergence}
To incrementally update $\eta$, we aim to develop an SGD-type algorithm. 
We present the gradient of $\sigma_{\cc}(X, \eta)$ w.r.t. $\eta$.
\begin{lemma}[Gradient of the $\cc$ dual function]
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:dual_operator_chi}
 & \sigma_{\cc}^{\prime}(X, \eta)=1 - \sqrt{\rho+1} \cdot \frac{Z_1}{\sqrt{Z_2}},
\end{align}
where 
\begin{align}
 & Z_1=\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\left[(\eta-X)_{+}\right] \label{eq:chi_B1},\\
 & Z_2=\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\left[(\eta-X)_{+}^2\right] \label{eq:chi_B2}.
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

As the update of $\eta$ and $Q$ rely on each other, we keep the learning speed of $\eta$ and $Q$ different to stabilize the training process.
Due to the $(s,a)$-rectangular assumption, $\eta$ is independent across different $(s,a)$-pairs while the $Q$ table depends on each other.
The independent structure for $\eta$ allows it to be estimated more easily; thus, we approximate it in a faster loop, while for $Q$ we update it in a slower loop.
However, updating $\eta$ is inherently challenging as the plug-in gradient estimator, i.e., Equation~\ref{eq:dual_operator_chi}, is also biased.
Note that for $N$ i.i.d. samples $X_1,\cdots,X_N\sim P$, $\sE[f(X)] \neq f(\sE[X])$ for general $f$, which holds for $f(x) = 1/\sqrt{x}$ as here.
Thus we introduce another even slower timescale to estimate $Z_1$ and $Z_2$. 
\begin{align}
 &Z_{n+1, 1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1-\zeta_1(n)) Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) + \label{eq:Z1_chi} \\ & \quad \zeta_1(n) (\eta_{n}(s_{n}, a_{n}) - \max_a Q_n(s_n^{\prime},a))_+ , \notag \\
 &Z_{n+1, 2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1-\zeta_1(n)) Z_{n,2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) +\label{eq:Z2_chi}\\ & \quad \zeta_1(n) (\eta_{n}(s_{n}, a_{n}) - \max_a Q_n(s_n^{\prime},a))_+^2 . \notag
\end{align}

Then in the medium timescale we approximate the $\eta^{\star}(s,a)\coloneqq \arg\max_{\eta\in \gR}\sigma_\cc(Q, \eta)$ .
\begin{align}
 &\eta_{n+1}(s_n, a_n) = (1-\zeta_2(n))\eta_n(s_n, a_n)+ \notag\\
 & \quad \zeta_2(n)(1 - \sqrt{\rho +1}\frac{Z_{n, 1}}{ \sqrt{Z_{n, 2}}} ).\label{eq:eta_chi}
\end{align}

Finally, we update the DR $Q$ function in the slowest time scale
 via
 \begin{align}
 & Q_{n+1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1-\zeta_3(n)) Q_n(s_{n}, a_{n}) + \notag \\
 & \quad \zeta_3(n) \gT_n^{\chi^2} (Q)(s_n,a_n), \label{eq:chi_Q_discount}
 \end{align}
 where $\gT_n^{\chi^2} (Q)(s,a)$ is the empirical version of Equation~\ref{eq:bellman_chi}, defined as 
 {\small
 \begin{align*}
 &\gT_n^{\chi^2} (Q)(s,a) = r(s, a) + \gamma(\eta_n(s, a)-\sqrt{1+\rho} \cdot \sqrt{Z_{n,2}(s,a)}).
 \end{align*}
 }
 Here $\zeta_1(n), \zeta_2(n)$ and $\zeta_3(n)$ are learning rates for three timescales at time $n$, which will be specified later.

We prove the a.s.
 \begin{theorem}
 \label{thm:convergence_discount_dro_chi}
 $(Z_{n,1}, Z_{n,2}, \eta_n, Q_n)$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_chi} converges to $(Z^{\star}_{1}, Z^{\star}_{2}, \eta^{\star}, Q^{\star})$ a.s. as $n\rightarrow \infty$ where $\eta^{\star}$ and $Q^{\star}$ are the solution of Equation~\ref{eq:bellman_chi} while $Z^{\star}_1$ and $Z^{\star}_2$ are the corresponding quantity under $\eta^{\star}$ and $Q^{\star}$.
 \end{theorem}

\subsection{KL Convergence}
Next we present the gradient of $\sigma_{\rm KL}(X, \beta)$ w.r.t. $\beta$.
\begin{lemma}[Gradient of the KL dual function]
\begin{align*}
 & \sigma_{\operatorname{KL}}^{\prime}(X, \beta)= - \rho - \log Z_1 - \beta^{-1}\frac{Z_2}{Z_1},
\end{align*}
where 
\begin{align}
 & Z_1=\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\left[e^{-X/\beta}\right] \label{eq:KL_B1},\\
 & Z_2=\mathbb{E}_{X \sim P}\left[Xe^{-X/\beta}\right] \label{eq:KL_B2}.
\end{align}
\end{lemma}

Again the plug-in estimator is biase and we use $Z_{n,1}$ and $Z_{n,2}$ to approximate them separately via the following update rules,
 \begin{align}
 &Z_{n+1,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1-\zeta_1(n)) Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n})\notag\\
 &\quad + \zeta_1(n)e^{-y_n/\beta_n(s_{n}, a_{n})} \label{eq:Z1_KL}\\
 &Z_{n+1,2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1-\zeta_1(n))Z_{n,2}(s_{n}, a_{n})\notag\\
 &\quad + \zeta_1(n) y_n e^{-y_n/\beta_n(s_{n}, a_{n})} ,\label{eq:Z2_KL}
 \end{align}
where $y_n = \max_{a'\in \gA} Q_n(s_n^{\prime}, a')$.
 For the second timescale, we denote $D_{n+1}(s_n, a_n)$ as
 \begin{align}
 &D_{n+1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = \rho + \log \left( Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) \right) \notag\\
 &\quad + \beta_n^{-1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) Z_{n,2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) / Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}), 
 \end{align}
 and then update $\beta_{n+1}(s_n,a_n)$ via 
 {\small
 \begin{equation}
 \beta_{n+1}(s_n,a_n) = (\beta_n(s_{n}, a_{n}) - \zeta_2(n) D_{n+1}(s_n, a_n))_{+}, \label{eq:beta_KL}
 \end{equation}
 }
 where $(x)_+ = \max\{x, 0\}$.
 
 For the third timescale, we solve the DR operator 
 via 
 \begin{align}
 &Q_{n+1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) = (1-\zeta_3(n)) Q_n(s_{n}, a_{n}) \notag \\
 & + \zeta_3(n) \gT_n^{\rm KL} (Q_n)(s_n,a_n),\label{eq:Q_KL}
 \end{align}
 where 
 {\small
 \begin{equation*}
 \gT_n^{\operatorname{KL}}(Q)(s,a) = r_n(s, a) - \gamma (\beta_n(s, a)\log Z_{n,1}(s, a) + \beta_n(s, a) \rho).
 \end{equation*}
}

We prove the a.s.
 \begin{theorem}
 \label{thm:convergence_discount_dro_KL}
 $(Z_{n,1},Z_{n,2}, \beta_n, Q_n)$ in Algorithm~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_chi} converges to $(Z^{\star}_{1}, Z^{\star}_{2}, \beta^{\star}, Q^{\star})$ a.s. as $n\rightarrow \infty$, where $\beta^{\star}$ and $Q^{\star}$ are the solution of Equation~\ref{eq:bellman_KL} while $Z^{\star}_{1}$ and $Z^{\star}_{2}$ are the corresponding quantity under $\eta^{\star}$ and $Q^{\star}$.
 \end{theorem}

We summarize the update procedure as Algorithm~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_KL}.

\begin{algorithm*}[ht]
    \caption{Distributionally Robust $Q$-learning with KL divergence}
    \label{alg:discount_dro_Q_KL}
   \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \STATE {\bfseries Input:} Exploration rate $\epsilon$, Learning rates $\{\zeta_i(n)\}_{i\in[3]}$
    \STATE {\bfseries Init:} $Q(s,a) = 0, \forall (s,a)\in \gS\times\gA$ 
      \FOR {$n = 1,2,\cdots$}
      \STATE Observe the state $s_n$, execute the action $a_n = \max_{a\in \gA} Q(s_n, a)$ using $\epsilon$ greedy policy
      \STATE Observe the reward $r_n$ and next state $s_{n}^{\prime}$
      \STATE Update $Z_{n,1}$, $Z_{n,2}$ via 
      \begin{align*}
        &Z_{n+1,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) =  (1-\zeta_1(n)) Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) + \zeta_1(n)e^{-y_n/\beta_n(s_{n}, a_{n})}   \\
        &Z_{n+1,2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) =  (1-\zeta_1(n))Z_{n,2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) + \zeta_1(n) y_n e^{-y_n/\beta_n(s_{n}, a_{n})},
        \end{align*}
        where $y_n = \max_{a\in \gA} Q_n(s_n^{\prime}, a)$.
      \STATE Update $\beta_n$ via 
      $
        \beta_{n+1}(s,a) = (\beta_n(s_{n}, a_{n}) - \zeta_2(n) D_{n+1}(s_n, a_n) )_+,
      $ \\
      where 
      $
        D_{n+1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) =  \rho + \log \left( Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) \right) 
     + \beta_n^{-1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) Z_{n,2}(s_{n}, a_{n}) / Z_{n,1}(s_{n}, a_{n}).
    $
      \STATE Update $Q_n$ via $
        Q_{n+1}(s_{n}, a_{n}) =  (1-\zeta_3(n)) Q_n(s_{n}, a_{n})
         + \zeta_3(n) (r_n - \gamma (\beta_n(s_n, a_n)\log Z_{n,1}(s_n, a_n) + \beta_n(s_n, a_n) \rho)).
    $
      \ENDFOR
   \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}


 

\subsection{Three Timescales Convergence Analysis}
In this subsection, we provide the roadmap for establishing the a.s. convergence of Algorithm~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_chi} and~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_KL} to the globally optimal action-value function.
Our argument is by generalizing the classic machinery of two-timescale stochastic approximation~\cite{borkar2009stochastic} to a three-timescale framework and validating our proposed algorithms to satisfy the conditions needed.
Our goal is to rewrite the Algorithm~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_chi} as (Algorithm~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_KL} is similar)
\begin{align*}
 Z_{n+1} &= Z_{n,1} + \zeta_1(n)[f(Z_{n}, \eta_n, Q_{n}) + M_{n+1}], \label{eq:ode_Z}\\
 \eta_{n+1} &= \eta_n + \zeta_2(n) g(Z_{n}, \eta_n, Q_{n}),\\
 Q_{n+1} &= Q_n + \zeta_3(n) h(Z_{n}, \eta_n, Q_{n}),
\end{align*}
where $Z_{n} = (Z_{n,1}, Z_{n,2})$ and $M_{n}$ is an appropriate martingale difference sequences conditioned on some filtration $\gF_n$. 
$f,g$, and $h$ are appropriate Lipschitz functions that satisfy the conditions needed for our ordinary differential equations (ODEs) analysis.
To ensure a.s. convergence, the stepsizes for different loops need to be elegantly designed as follows
\begin{assumption}
 \label{as:stepsize}
 The stepsizes $\zeta_i(n), i=1,2,3$ satisfy 
 \begin{align*} &\sum_{n}\zeta_i(n)=\infty,\sum_{n}\zeta_i^2(n) < \infty, \\
 &\zeta_1(n) = o(\zeta_2(n)), \zeta_2(n) = o(\zeta_3(n)).
 \end{align*}
\end{assumption}
These stepsize schedules satisfy the standard conditions for stochastic approximation algorithms and ensure that \textbf{1.} the key quantities in gradient estimator $Z$ update on the fastest timescale, \textbf{2.} the dual variable for the DR problem $\beta/\eta$ update on the intermediate timescale, and \textbf{3.} the $Q$ table updates on the slowest timescale.
Examples of such stepsize are $\zeta_1(n) = \frac{1}{1+n^{0.6}}, \zeta_2(n) = \frac{1}{1 + n^{0.8}}$ and $\zeta_3(n) = \frac{1}{1 + n}$.

Under Assumption~\ref{as:stepsize}, when analyzing the behavior of the $Z_{n}$, the $\eta_{n}$ and the $Q_n$ can be viewed as quasi-static. 
Thus to study the behavior of the fastest loop, we analyze the following ODEs,
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:ode_1}
 \begin{aligned}
 \dot{Z}(t) &= f(Z(t), \beta(t), Q(t)),\\
 \dot{\eta}(t) &= 0, \dot{Q}(t) = 0,\\
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
and can prove that ODEs~(\ref{eq:ode_1}) a.s. converge to the set $\lambda_1^{\prime}(\eta, Q)$ for proper $\eta$ and $Q$ and some mapping $\lambda^{''}_1$.
Similarly, $Q_n$ can be viewed as fixed when analyzing the behavior of $\eta_n$, and the corresponding ODEs to understand its behavior is
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:ode_2}
 \begin{aligned}
 \dot{\eta}(t) &= g(\lambda_1^{\prime\prime}(\eta(t), Q(t)), \eta(t), Q(t)),\\
 \dot{Q}(t) &= 0.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Again, by exploiting the dual form of the DRO problem, we can prove these ODEs converge to the set $\{\lambda_1^{\prime}(Q), \lambda_2^{\prime}(Q), Q\lvert Q\in V\}$ for some mapping $\lambda_1^{\prime}$ and $\lambda_2^{\prime}$ with $V$ is the set containing all the mapping from $\gS$ to $\sR$.
Finally, we study the slowest timescale ODE,
\begin{equation}
 \dot{Q}(t) = h(\lambda_1^{\prime}(Q(t)), \lambda_2^{\prime}(Q(t)), Q(t)),
\end{equation}
and use our analysis to prove the a.s. convergence of our proposed Algorithm~\ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_chi} and \ref{alg:discount_dro_Q_KL} to the globally optimal pair $(Z^{\star}_1, Z^{\star}_2, \eta^{\star}, Q^{\star})$ and $(Z^{\star}_1, Z^{\star}_2, \beta^{\star}, Q^{\star})$, correspondingly.


\section{Extension to Average Reward Setting}

Some practical scenarios, including queueing control, concern more about the long-run average performance. Thus, 
the average expected reward, instead of the cumulative discounted reward, may be preferred as the evaluation criterion.
We extend our three timescales framework to solve this setting.
\subsection{Average-Reward MDPs}

In this subsection, we formalize the average reward MDPs, defined by the tuple $(\gS, \gA, P, r, \mu)$, where each element $\gS$, $\gA$, $P$, $r$ and $\mu$ are the same as in Section~\ref{subsec:discount_form}.
The average reward rate for a given policy $\pi$ and any initial state $s$ is 
\begin{align*}
 r(\pi, P) \coloneqq \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^n \sE_{\pi, P}\left[r(s_t, a_t)\lvert s_1= s\right],
\end{align*}
which does not depend on the initial state under the communicating assumption~\footnote{We say an MDP is communicating if there exists a policy that can transition from one to any other state in a finite number of steps with non-zero probability.} (see, e.g.,\citet{puterman2014markov}). 
The best reward rate is defined as $r^{\star}\coloneqq \sup_{\pi}r(\pi, P)$.
The average reward RL tries to achieve $r^{\star}$.
We can also define the optimal relative action-value function as
{\small
\begin{align*}
 Q^{\star}(s,a) \coloneqq \sup_{\pi\in \Pi}\sE_{\pi, P}[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (r_n(s_n, a_n) - r^{\star})\lvert s_1 =s, a_1 = a],
\end{align*}
}
which is the cumulative difference between the immediate reward and the optimal reward rate with the first state as $s$ and action as $a$. 
The relationship between the optimal average reward and the optimal relative action-value function can be characterized by the Bellman optimality equation, 
\begin{align*}
 Q^{\star}(s,a) = r(s,a) - r^{\star} + \sE_P \left[\max_{a'\in \gA}Q^{\star}(s', a')\right].
\end{align*}

\subsection{Distributionally Robust average-reward MDPs}
We are interested in the DR variant of the above setting.
To be specific, we define the DR average reward as the worst-case average reward over an ambiguity set $\gP$, 
\begin{align*}
 r^{\operatorname{rob}}(\pi, \gP) = \inf_{P\in \gP}\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^n\sE_{\pi, P}[r(s_t, a_t)\lvert s_1= s],
\end{align*}
and $r^{\operatorname{rob}, \star} = \sup_{\pi\in \Pi}r^{\operatorname{rob}}(\pi, \gP)$.
We then only consider the case where the MDPs induced by the policy $\pi$ and any of the transition model $P$ in the ambiguity set $\gP$ is communicating.
Moreover, we assume the ambiguity set $\gP$ is constructed in a $(s,a)$-rectangular manner following Section~\ref{subsec:drdMDPs}.
Similarly, we can define the DR relative action-value function as 
\begin{footnotesize}
\begin{align*}
 Q^{\operatorname{rob}, \star}(s,a) \coloneqq \sup_{\pi\in \Pi} \inf_{P\in \gP} \sE_{\pi, P} \left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty} (r_t(s_t, a_t) - r(\pi, P))\lvert s, a\right],
\end{align*}
\end{footnotesize}
where conditional on the first state is $a$ and the first action is $a$.
\citet{https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2301.00858} proves a DR Bellman optimality equation to characterize the relationship between optimal DR average reward and optimal DR action-value function,
\begin{small}
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:dro_average_opt}
 Q^{\operatorname{rob}, \star} = r(s,a) - r^{\operatorname{rob}, \star} + \inf_{P\in \gP} \sE_P \left[\max_{a \in \gA} Q^{\operatorname{rob},\star}(s',a) \right].
\end{align}
\end{small}
We ignore the superscript $\operatorname{rob}$ for notation simplicity.


\subsection{Review of Differential $Q$-Learning}
For DR discounted MDPs, it has been shown that the value function is the unique fixed-point of the DR discounted Bellman operator \cite{iyengar2005robust}.
However, there is no such guarantee in the DR average-reward case.
Our goal is to design an algorithm for learning the optimal policy under this setting.
In this subsection, we review an average-reward RL algorithm, Differential Q-Learning \cite{wan2021learning}, as we will develop our DR variant of it by instantiating our three timescales framework.
We can also develop a DR variant of other average-reward RL algorithms, including RVI-$Q$ learning \cite{abounadi2001learning}.
The differential $Q$-learning shares the same spirit with the vanilla $Q$-learning by incrementally updating the $Q$ function from the temporal difference.
In particular, 
\begin{align*}
 Q_{n+1}(s_n, a_n) &= Q_n(s_n, a_n) + \alpha_n \delta_n
\end{align*}
where $\alpha_n$ is learning rate and $\delta_n$ is the temporal-difference (TD),
\begin{align*}
 \delta_n = r_{t} - \overline{r}_n + \max_{a\in \gA} Q_n(s^{\prime}_{t}, a) - Q_n(s_n, a_n).
\end{align*}
The innovation of the differential Q-learning is mainly on using the $\overline{r}_n$ as the reference for the $Q$ value, which is updated by 
\begin{align*}
 \overline{r}_{n+1} = \overline{r}_n + \nu \alpha_n \delta_n,
\end{align*}
for some positive constant $\nu>0$.
Compared with the RVI-$Q$ learning, the differential Q-learning algorithm is much easier to use and robust to the choice of hyperparameter.

\subsection{Algorithmic Design}

To develop a DR variant of differential $Q$-learning, note that the DR temporal difference error implied by Equation~\ref{eq:dro_average_opt} is 
\begin{align*}
 \delta_n = r_n - \bar{r}_n + \inf_{P\in \gP}\sE_{P}[\max_{a\in \gA} Q_n(s_n^{\prime}, a)] - Q_n(s_n, a_n).
\end{align*}
Our three timescale framework comes into the role of solving the infimum over the ambiguity set.
To be specific, we use the same inner two timescales as in Equation~\ref{eq:Z1_chi}, \ref{eq:Z2_chi}, and \ref{eq:eta_chi}. 
Then we design the third loop using the template provided by the differential $Q$-learning, i.e., we update $\bar{r}_n$ to approximate $r^{\star}$,
\begin{align}
 \bar{r}_{n+1}(s_n,a_n) &= \bar{r}_n(s_n,a_n) + \nu \zeta_3(n) \bar{\delta}_n, \label{eq:bar_r_average}
\end{align}
where $\nu>0$ is some learning rate and the 
empirical TD error as 
{\small
\begin{align*}
 \bar{\delta}_n &= r_n - \bar{r}_n(s,a) - Q_n(s,a) -\eta_n + \sqrt{1+\rho}\sqrt{Z_{n,2}(s_n,a_n)}. 
\end{align*}
}
We modify the update for the $Q$ function as 
\begin{align}
 & Q_{n+1}(s_n, a_n) = Q_n(s_n, a_n) + \zeta_3(n) \bar{\delta}_n.
\label{eq:Q_update_average}
\end{align}
Using the argument established by our three timescales stochastic approximation framework, we can prove the a.s.
We also defer the discussion about the assumptions needed for the a.s. convergence as they are standard and shared by RVI-$Q$ learning and differential $Q$-learning algorithms.
\begin{theorem}[Informal]
 Under proper assumptions, $(Q_n, \bar{r}_n)$ in our Algorithms~\ref{alg:average_dro_Q_chi} and \ref{alg:average_dro_Q_KL} converge to the $(Q^{\star}, r^{\star})$ a.s..
\end{theorem}


\section{Experiments}




\subsection{Discounted Reward}
We use the Windy Beach task as the test-bed for our proposed DRQ algorithm. Suppose a robot is on the beach. It starts from the initial state of $(2,0)$ and navigates to the goal state of $(2,3)$. For each step, the robot can take action and move to an adjacent grid. However, the wind on the beach is strong and will make the robot move in an arbitrary direction with a probability $p$.
If it arrives at the goal state, it will receive a reward of $+5$, and the task will end if it is unfortunate to be hit by a wave (in the region of $\{(3,j) \mid 0 \leq j \leq 3 \}$), it will receive a penalty of $-1$.

We train the agent in a nominal environment with $p=0.5$ and evaluate its performance in the perturbed environments. The uncertainty set radiuses are set to $\rho=0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5$ to demonstrate different levels of robustness. For each run, we train the DRQ algorithm for 3 million steps. For each step, we explore the environment with a $\epsilon$-greedy strategy, where $\epsilon=0.1$ in our experiment. If the robot has reached the goal state, the environment will be reset to the initial state. Otherwise, the robot still explores until the episode length is up to 20, in which case we will also reset the environment to avoid invalid wandering. Although the Robbins-Monro step sizes (i.e., Assumption~\ref{as:stepsize}) are essential for theoretical convergence, we empirically find that the constant step sizes work well. Specially, we use $\zeta_1(t)=0.03$, $\zeta_2(t)=0.01$ and $\zeta_3(t)=0.003$ in our experiment to track three different time scales. The discount factor $\gamma=0.9$. Each experiment is repeated with 10 random seeds, where $Q$ is initialized by randomly sampling from $N(0,1)$. The training curves are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:water_curves}, where we compare the estimated value $\max_a Q(s_0, a)$ (shaded by the standard deviation) and the optimal robust value $V^*(s_0)$ (the dashed line) of the initial state. The results show estimated value converges to the optimal value quickly, which is consistent with our analysis.


To evaluate the robustness of the learned policies, we compare their returns in the perturbed environments with $p \in \{0.4,0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.9\}$. By saying ``return'', we mean $G =\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} r_t$, where $T$ is the first time step reaching the goal state. We run the learned policies over 100 episodes for each setting and compare the return distribution in Figure~\ref{fig:water_return}. Interestingly, we find that the robust policies outperform the risk-neutral policy (the same as the policy with $\rho=0.1$) even in the nominal environment. This is because the returns do not take the discount $\gamma$ into consideration. To better understand this phenomenon, we compare the episode lengths $T$ in Figure~\ref{fig:water_lengths}, showing that $T$ increases with $\rho$. Longer time to complete tasks conflicts with discount criteria, but it is preferred in conservative scenarios. As for the different $\rho$'s, we find that $\rho=1.0$ is the best within a relatively wide range ($p\in \{0.6,0.7,0.8\}$). In contrast, $\rho=1.5$ is preferred in the environment of extreme uncertainty with $p=0.9$. It suggests that DRO provides a trade-off for different preferences of robustness, overcoming the over-conservatism of the other robustness notions.


\subsection{Average Reward}
\label{subsec:exp_average}
In this part, we consider the Access-Control Queuing task~\cite{sutton1998reinforcementa} to evaluate the average-reward DRQ-learning. A queue receives 4 different classes of consumers with uniform probability. Depending on their priority, they will pay different fees of 1, 2, 4, and 8. There are 10 servers to serve the consumers. At each step, we can take action to accept the arriving customer and send her to any free server, in which case the fees will be paid according to her priority. Otherwise, we can also reject her and earn nothing. If a server is busy, it will have a probability of $p$ being free in the next time step. In the nominal environment, we set $p=0.09$. A policy needs to choose whether to accept or reject depending on the number of current free servers and the priority of the arriving customer, in order to maximize the long-run average reward. 

In this task, we use the same experimental setup as the Windy Beach task for the constant step sizes, $\epsilon$-greedy exploration, and $Q$ initialization. Due to the large state space and the lack of discount, we need more samples for the Access-Control Queuing task. Hence we train the average-reward DRQ-learning algorithm over 15 million. The constant in the Differential Q-learning is set as $\eta=1$, and we do not find the performance is sensitive to it. We use the differential value of two reference states to show the convergence of the algorithm.
Specifically, we choose the state $s_0$ that has no free server with an arriving customer whose priority is 1, and the state $s_1$ where all servers are free and the arriving customer's priority is 8. The differential value of these states, i.e. $\Delta V = \max_a Q(s_1, a) - \max_a Q(s_0, a)$, are expected to be the largest among all the state pairs. The training curves shown in Figure~\ref{fig:queue_curves} illustrate that our proposed algorithm consistently converges to the optimal differential value. The learned polices are visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:queue_policy}.

Again, we perturb the with $p \in \{0.01,0.03,0.06\}$ and evaluate the robustness of the learned polices. The smaller $p$ represents a strain on service resources. We run the algorithm for 100 time steps, calculate the average reward, and repeat the experiment for 100 times. The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:water_result}. The distributionally robust policies perform better in environments where $p \leq 0.06$, demonstrated their superiority in resource-constrained conditions.
