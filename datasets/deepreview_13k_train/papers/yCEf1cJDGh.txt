\title{Truthful Aggregation of LLMs with an Application to Online Advertising}

\begin{abstract}
The next frontier of online advertising is revenue generation from LLM-generated content. We consider a setting where advertisers aim to influence the responses of an LLM to align with their interests, while platforms seek to maximize advertiser value and ensure user satisfaction. The challenge is that advertisers' preferences generally conflict with those of the user, and advertisers may misreport their preferences. To address this, we introduce MOSAIC, an auction mechanism that ensures that truthful reporting is a dominant strategy for advertisers and that aligns the utility of each advertiser with their contribution to social welfare. Importantly, the mechanism operates without LLM fine-tuning or access to model weights and provably converges to the output of the optimally fine-tuned LLM as computational resources increase. Additionally, it can incorporate contextual information about advertisers,  which significantly improves social welfare. Through experiments with a publicly available LLM, we show that MOSAIC leads to high advertiser value and platform revenue with low computational overhead. While our motivating application is online advertising, our mechanism can be applied in any setting with monetary transfers, making it a general-purpose solution for truthfully aggregating the preferences of self-interested agents over LLM-generated replies.

\end{abstract}

\textit{Large language models (LLMs)} are becoming ubiquitous -- as coding assistants, as chat interfaces, and even as alternatives to search engines \citep{bommasani2022opportunities}.
To ensure their usefulness, it is essential to closely align LLM outputs with user preferences.
However, in general, there may be multiple interested parties who disagree on the desired behavior of \textit{the same} LLM.
This leads to the question of \textit{how to generate LLM replies given multiple conflicting preferences}.


The motivating application for our work is online advertising, which is the main source of revenue for large tech companies such as Google, Meta, and Twitter. For example, in $2023$, Meta's advertising revenue of $132$ billion USD was more than $97$\% of its total revenue \citep{meta2024}. Ad auctions are the workhorse mechanism to determine the placement and prices of commercial content~\citep{varian2007,edelman2007}.
As platforms are now working on integrating advertisements into LLM-generated content~\citep{Criddle24Perplexity}, new auction mechanisms are needed for this application.

We present a new auction mechanism for this problem, which we call \textit{MOSAIC (\textbf{M}echanism for \textbf{O}ptimally \textbf{S}ampling and \textbf{A}ggregating LLM Outputs with \textbf{I}ncentive \textbf{C}ompatibility)}. The advertisers are paying, not for some particular item or bundle, but rather to influence the output generated by an LLM in a direction closer to their own preferences.
Although our mechanism could be of interest whenever one has to aggregate the preferences of multiple self-interested agents over LLM behavior (as long as it is reasonable to charge monetary payments), we see online advertising as the most natural setting of interest.
For this reason, we refer to participants in the mechanism as \textit{advertisers}.

\subsection{Problem setting}
Consider a situation where a user queries an LLM with a specific question.
We assume the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item There is a \textit{reference LLM} that can produce useful replies to the user's query. 
    \item There are \textit{advertisers}, each with their own LLM, who want to influence the reply to the user. 
\end{itemize}
In our model, each advertiser is represented via her own LLM
or directly with a \textit{reward function}, similar to the function used to fine-tune LLMs in the \textit{Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback} pipeline \citep{azar2023}. 
For this reason, we refer to an advertiser's value for a reply as her \textit{reward}.
The auctioneer's task is, given the user's query and the advertisers' preferences, to \textit{produce a reply that is useful for the user while also generating high rewards for the  advertisers}.

\paragraph{Example.}
A user queries an LLM \emph{``What are effective ways to learn a musical instrument online?''} 
There are two advertisers:
\emph{MusicMastery}, which offers online music lessons, and
\emph{InstaTune}, which sells musical instruments. MOSAIC merges the preferred replies from the advertisers' LLMs and the reference LLM to produce one combined reply to the user:
\begin{quote}
    \textit{Effective ways to learn a musical instrument online include:\\
1. Online music lessons: Platforms like \textbf{MusicMastery} offer one-on-one virtual tutoring, group classes, and pre-recorded lessons.\\
2. Interactive learning tools: \textbf{InstaTune} offers a range of interactive learning tools, such as apps, software, and games, that make learning fun and engaging. }
\end{quote}



\subsection{Overview of Contributions}

In this paper, we present MOSAIC, an auction mechanism designed to aggregate the preferences of multiple self-interested advertisers over LLM-generated replies. The first challenge we address is that \mbox{MOSAIC} must produce replies for which the advertisers receive high rewards, but without steering the LLM's behavior too far from that of the user-centric reference LLM. We address this by drawing a strong connection to the RLHF objective (e.g., \cite{ziegler2020finetuning}), equipping MOSAIC with a hyperparameter that enables the auctioneer to balance between the usefulness of the produced reply to the user
and the advertisers in an interpretable and principled way (\Cref{subsection:the_formal_model}).


Second, leveraging an importance sampling-based technique, MOSAIC is the only mechanism in the literature that converges to the optimal distribution as computational resources increase (\Cref{Corollary:mechanism_follows_optimal_distribution}).
Third, MOSAIC can incorporate contextual information, similar to how sponsored search auctions utilize advertiser descriptions. 
This accelerates convergence and increases value for the advertisers and revenue for the auctioneer (\Cref{subsection:efficiency_results}).

Fourth, we address technical feasibility and practicality. We adopt the desiderata proposed by
\citet{duetting2023mechanism}, who argued that auctions must be compatible with existing LLM technology, only using ``information obtainable from current models'' in such a way that they are ``easy to integrate into the system'' and rely only on ``easy manipulations of LLM output.'' Furthermore, it must be computationally feasible to run the auction repeatedly and with different advertiser preferences. In particular, we cannot afford the expensive process of fine-tuning an LLM's weights for each possible query. To address this, we have designed MOSAIC to work by only post-processing multiple LLM outputs -- it requires only ``API access'', not even viewing the model weights ( \Cref{section:practical_considerations}). 
Fifth, we address the fact that advertisers might have an interest in misreporting their preferences (which include their \textit{full LLMs}), analogous to over-/underbidding in traditional auctions. To address this challenge, we employ an allocation rule that is compatible with Rochet payments \citep{rochet1987}. These payments uniquely ensure that MOSAIC is strategyproof (i.e., that truthful reporting is a dominant strategy 
for each advertiser, no matter what the others do (\Cref{theorem:truthful_payments})). 
Crucially, Rochet payments ensure that MOSAIC is strategyproof even if the allocation rule has not converged.

Sixth, we introduce an offset to the Rochet payments, to ensure that
each advertiser's utility is proportional to her contribution to the social welfare. 
This alignment is important for the long-term success of the market, incentivizing only the most relevant advertisers to participate. 

Finally, we discuss \textit{individual rationality (IR),} which guarantees that a participant is weakly better off from participating in the mechanism. 
In \Cref{subsec:differences}, we discuss the unique properties of our setting that make \textit{ex-post} IR unattainable. However, in \Cref{subsec:almost_ir}, we show that MOSAIC is ``almost ex-post IR'' in a well-defined sense. In \Cref{subsection:efficiency_results}, we experimentally show that, for the distribution of instances tested, it is \textit{ex-ante} IR (i.e., advertisers are in expectation better off by participating). 
In \Cref{section:experiments}, we provide experimental results for the online advertising domain. We demonstrate that MOSAIC quickly converges to the optimal LLM with low computational cost,
generating significant value for the advertisers
and revenue for the auctioneer while also being useful to the user.


\section{Related Work}

\citet{duetting2023mechanism} 
introduced the field of mechanism design for LLMs. 
They proposed a sequential mechanism, where the output sequence is generated on a token-by-token basis and the advertisers bid for their LLM 
to generate the next token.  
However,
their approach suffers from several limitations:
(i) Advertisers face the \textit{exposure problem}: small changes in the generated sequence, such as the addition of a word like "not," can completely alter its meaning. An advertiser might pay a significant amount for the tokens generated up to a certain point, only for the continuation to unexpectedly negate or distort her intended message.\footnote{
As an example, suppose advertiser A has successfully bid for all the tokens in the sequence "Planning your next vacation? For the cheapest flights to Hawaii, book via..." However, she fails to win the bid for the crucial next token, which would have been her brand name. This leaves her having paid for the preceding tokens without getting the benefit of associating her brand with the message.} 
(ii) The mechanism is easily manipulable if the assumption that advertisers cannot misreport their LLMs is dropped.
(iii) For a given prompt, an advertiser's spend grows with the length of the generated sequence. MOSAIC does not have any of these limitations. 

\citet{dubey2024auctions} proposed a generalization of the position auction \citep{varian2007, edelman2007} to a setting where each advertiser is interested in having some specific text ad displayed alongside the organic results. In their mechanism, an LLM module coupled with an auction module work in tandem to merge the ads into a single ad summary in an incentive-compatible way.
\citet{Hajiaghayi24Ad} considered advertisers bidding in an auction to have their ads placed into various portions of a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) response. Their auction only allows advertisers to report (and misreport) scalar values to influence the inclusion of non-misreportable advertising texts in the combined output. This is in contrast to MOSAIC, which allows the aggregation of full LLMs that advertisers may arbitrarily misreport.

\citet{Feizi24Online} presented an abstract design for an LLM advertising system and detailed a number of research challenges that would have to be overcome in the course of implementation.
\citet{Conitzer24Social} drew connections between \textit{computational social choice} and LLM alignment.
\citet{Fish23Generative} presented work in the opposite direction: how can LLMs be used to solve problems in social choice?
\citet{Harris24Algorithmic} studied Bayesian persuasion in an abstract setting where a ``simulator'' (for example, a realistic LLM) of the agent is available. \citet{Werner24Experimental} gave preliminary empirical evidence that LLM-generated content can influence consumers.



\section{Framing Sequence Generation as a Mechanism Design Problem}


\subsection{Formal Model} \label{subsection:the_formal_model}

A \textit{user} issues a query $x$.
There is a \textit{reference LLM} $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ that the auctioneer aims not to deviate from too much (e.g., because it is responsible for providing useful replies to the user).
Additionally, there is a set $N$ of $n$ advertisers who have their own preferences for the reply (i.e., a token sequence) that will be returned to the user. We use the terms \textit{sequence} and \textit{reply} interchangeably. 

An LLM can be abstracted as a mapping from (partial) token sequences to a distribution over the next token, or equivalently as an implicit distribution over token sequences. 
We use this second abstraction, i.e., $\pi_i(y | x)$ denotes the probability that advertiser $i$'s LLM $\pi_i$ assigns to output sequence (i.e., reply) $y$ for the user query $x$. 


We let $r_i(x, y)$ denote advertiser $i$'s \textit{reward} for sequence $y$, given query  $x$.\footnote{
In theory, the advertiser's reward for a generated sequence could also depend on user-specific information, but we abstract that away. 
Equivalently, we assume that the reward function $r_i$ provided by advertiser $i$ is specific to the given user that asked the query $x$.}
Informally, the auctioneer's goal is to sample the final sequence from a distribution that optimizes the  advertisers' expected rewards without substantially diverging from $\pi_{\text{\text{ref}}}$. Formally, the goal is to maximize:
\begin{gather} \label{eq:objective}
    J(\pi) =  \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi} \left [\sum_{i \in N} r_i(x, y) \right ] - 
    \tau D_{\text{KL}}(\pi(\cdot | x) || \pi_{\text{\text{ref}}}(\cdot | x))   
\end{gather}
where $\tau > 0 $ is a hyperparameter enabling the auctioneer to balance producing replies 
closer to the reference LLM or with higher reward for the  advertisers, and $D_{\text{KL}}$ is the Kullback-Leibler divergence. 

This objective is similar to the standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) approach \citep{ziegler2020finetuning}, but replaces the human feedback reward function $r_{\text{HF}}(x,y)$ with the sum of the  advertisers’ rewards.
For an overview of RLHF, we recommend \citet[\S 3]{rafailov2023dpo}.

The optimal solution to the optimization problem in (\ref{eq:objective}) was derived by \citet{peters2007}: 
\begin{gather} \label{eq:opt_distribution}
    \pi_r (y | x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{\text{ref}}}(y|x) \exp \left ( 
    \frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{i \in N} r_i(x, y)
    \right ), 
\end{gather}
where $Z(x) = \sum_{y \in T^*} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp \left ( \frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{i \in N} r_i(x, y)\right ) $ is the partition function.
Let $\Vec{R}$ be the set of all possible reports by the  advertisers. 
A \textit{mechanism} is defined as a pair $(\pi, p)$. 
The \textit{allocation rule} $\pi: \Vec{R} \rightarrow \Delta(T^*)$ maps any report profile 
$\Vec{r} = (r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n) \in \Vec{R}$ of the  advertisers' rewards to a distribution over sequences $\delta(T^*)$. 
We denote the  advertisers' \textit{aggregate reward} as $r(x, y) = \sum_{i = 1}^n r_i (x, y)$, and their reward profile as $\Vec{r}(x, y) = (r_1(x, y), r_2 (x, y), \dots , r_n (x,y))$.
The \textit{payment rule} $p: \Vec{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ maps any report profile of the  advertisers' rewards to a payment profile $\Vec{p}$, where $\Vec{p}_i$ is the payment of the $i$-th advertiser to the mechanism.  
A mechanism is \emph{strategyproof} if and only if, for any advertiser $i \in N$, truthfully reporting her rewards is always the optimal strategy, regardless of the reports of other advertisers. In our setting, this holds in expectation because the reply is sampled from the optimal LLM for the platform’s objective, based on the advertisers' reports. Formally:
\begin{definition}[Strategyproof]
A mechanism $(\pi, p)$ is \emph{strategyproof} if, for all advertisers $i \in N$, for all true reward functions $\Vec{r}_i \in \Vec{R}_i$, for all reports $\Vec{r}_{-i} \in \Vec{R}_{-i}$ by the other advertisers, and for all possible misreports $\Vec{r}_i' \in \Vec{R}_i$ by advertiser $i$:
  $  \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi (\Vec{r})} [u_i (y, \Vec{r}_i, \Vec{r}_{-i} ; {r}_i, x)] \ge 
    \mathbb{E}_{y' \sim \pi (\Vec{r}_i', {r}_{-i}, x)} 
    [u_i (y, \Vec{r}_i', \Vec{r}_{-i} ; \Vec{r}_i)],$
where $u_i (y, \Vec{r}_i, \Vec{r}_{-i}; r_i, x) = r_i(x, y) - p_i (\Vec{r})$ is the utility of advertiser $i$ for reply $y$ when her payment is $p_i(\Vec{r})$.
\end{definition}



\subsection{The shortcomings of VCG in this setting} \label{section:vcg_problems}
At first sight, it may seem that the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism would be suitable for our setting~\citep{vickrey1961,clarke1971,groves1973}. VCG selects the outcome that maximizes the sum of all agents' values.
This can be either the single optimal sequence in \Cref{eq:objective} or the optimal distribution of \Cref{eq:opt_distribution}. 
The VCG mechanism has a corresponding payment rule to incentivize truthful reporting: it charges each agent her externality, that is,
the total reduction in value (respectively expected value) for the other agents that her participation in the mechanism caused. 

However, in our setting, VCG is not a viable option:
The VCG allocation rule requires calculating the \textit{exact} optimal solution to the optimization problem, which is intractable for choosing an LLM to maximize \Cref{eq:objective} and is even difficult for choosing a single optimal sequence.
If a sub-optimal solution is chosen, VCG's strategyproofness is no longer guaranteed~\citep{Nisan07Computationally,Nisan1999Algorithmic,Lehmann2002Truth}.
Thus, VCG is not a suitable mechanism in our setting.



\section{The MOSAIC Mechanism: Allocation Rule}

\subsection{Convergence to Optimality, Advertiser Contexts and Importance Sampling}

In this section, we introduce MOSAIC's allocation rule.
The high-level idea is that first, 
a set of $M$ \textit{candidate replies} is generated based on some LLM $\pi_{\text{gen}}$ responsible for that task. 
Then, the probability of returning each candidate reply is re-weighted based on the advertisers' reports and the reference LLM $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ so that in the limit, as $M \to \infty$, the probability of returning each reply converges to its probability under the optimal distribution of \Cref{eq:opt_distribution}. This approach shares similarities with the rejection sampling approach
that has been
used at various points in the LLM training pipeline (e.g.,~\citet{bai2022constitutional}).  



\begin{algorithm}[t!] 
\caption{Allocation Rule for MOSAIC} \label{alg:static_mechanism_improved}
\DontPrintSemicolon  % Uncomment if you don't want semicolons to print
\KwIn{User prompt $x$, reference LLM $\pi_{\text{\text{ref}}}$, LLM used for candidate reply generation $\pi_{\text{\text{gen}}}$, 
advertiser reward functions $\Vec{r}$, number of candidate replies to generate $M$, reference LLM weight $\tau$}
\KwOut{Reply $y$ drawn according to the optimal distribution as defined in \Cref{eq:objective} for the aggregate reward function $r(x, y) = \sum_{i = 1}^N r_i(x,y)$}

\SetKwInput{KwInput}{User Prompt $x$}                % Set the Input
\SetKwInput{KwOutput}{Output}              % Set the Output
\SetKwInput{KwInitialization}{Initialization} % Set the Initialization
\SetKw{KwTo}{in}                           % 'in' keyword
\SetKwFor{For}{for}{do}{endfor}            % For loop
\SetKwIF{If}{ElseIf}{Else}{if}{then}{else if}{else}{endif} % If, Else If, Else

\BlankLine
Sample $y_j \sim \pi_{\text{gen}}(\cdot | x ), \; 1 \le j \le M$   \;
Calculate $r(x, y_j) = \sum_{i = 1}^N r_i(x, y_j), \; 1 \le j \le M$   \;
\Return $y \sim \text{softmax} \left 
(\frac{r(x, y_1)}{\tau} + \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1 | x)}{\pi_{\text{gen}}(y_1 | x  )},  
\dots, 
\frac{r(x, y_M)}{\tau} + \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_M | x)}{\pi_{\text{gen}}(y_M | x  )}
\right )   $  \;
\end{algorithm}

\begin{corollary} \label{Corollary:mechanism_follows_optimal_distribution}
The limiting policy $ \pi_{\theta, M}(\cdot | x)$ induced by \Cref{alg:static_mechanism_improved} is the KL regularized policy that optimizes the aggregate reward function: 
\begin{gather}
    \lim_{M \to \infty} \pi_{\theta, M}(\cdot | x) = \arg \max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi (\cdot | x)}[r(x, y )] - \tau D_{\text{KL}} (\pi || \pi_{\text{ref}})
\end{gather}
\end{corollary}
Based on \Cref{Corollary:mechanism_follows_optimal_distribution}, 
MOSAIC converges to the optimal distribution for \textit{any} LLM $\pi_{\text{gen}}$ 
such that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\pi_{\text{gen}}$.
The intuitive choice  for $\pi_{\text{gen}}$ is $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
However, 
the practical convergence rate would be very slow. 
The reason is that $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ may only generate replies with high rewards for the advertisers exceedingly rarely.
Advertisers have high rewards for responses that explicitly mention their brands, 
but $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ considers replies with mentions of specific brands extremely unlikely. 
Thus, if we used $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ to generate the candidate replies, \Cref{alg:static_mechanism_improved} would sample the final reply from a set of candidate replies with low rewards for the advertisers.
To address this challenge, we generate candidate sequences not from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot | x)\), but instead from a \textit{context-aware LLM}, \(\pi_{\text{con}}(\cdot | x; \Vec{c})\). The instance-specific context \(\Vec{c}\) is designed to bridge the gap between the parts of the output space favored by $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ and those valued by the advertisers. 
In \Cref{app:importancesampling}, we formally motivate the usage of $ \pi_{\text{con}}(\cdot | x; \Vec{c})$.
Our allocation rule can be interpreted as an importance-based sampling estimator that  simulates the probability with respect to the optimal LLM $\pi_{r}$ of a single sample drawn from $\pi_{r}$.
We show that the estimator is unbiased and that its variance is 
$\text{Var}(\widehat{\mu}_{\text{IS}} | x)  = 
    \frac{1}{M} \left ( \sum_{y \in Y} \frac{\pi_{r} (y | x)^4}{\pi_{\text{gen}}(y | x)}
    - \left ( \sum_{y \in Y} \pi_{r} (y | x)^2 \right )^2 \right )$.
The estimator's variance is inversely proportional to the number of candidate replies and  proportional to 
$ \frac{\pi_{r} (y | x)^4}{\pi_{\text{gen}}(y | x)} $. 
Thus, generating the replies via an LLM $ \pi_{\text{con}}(\cdot | x; \Vec{c}) $ that more closely matches $\pi_{r}(\cdot | x)$  reduces the estimator's variance. 

In our application of integrating advertisers' interests into LLM outputs, $\Vec{c}_i$ is a context-specific description of the $i$-th advertiser. 
These descriptions, supplied by the advertisers themselves, should be easily verifiable and factually accurate, akin to ``MusicMastery: offering online music lessons'', or ``InstaTune: selling musical instruments.''\footnote{A practical way of implementing $\pi_{\text{con}}(\cdot | x ; c)$ given $\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot | x)$ is to 
augment the input $x$ to the reference LLM with the advertiser descriptions.
In our example: ``Try to mention \textlangle advertiser x\textrangle, \textlangle advertiser x description\textrangle.'
\label{footnote:context_aware_llm}
} 
This approach is analogous to search engine optimization in sponsored search advertising, where advertisers supply and potentially optimize their own descriptions to influence how they are presented by the auction mechanism.
 
Our experiments in \Cref{subsection:efficiency_results} demonstrate that, when using reasonable computational resources, using the context-aware LLM to generate candidate replies achieves substantially higher rewards and utility for the advertisers, increased revenue for the auctioneer, and faster convergence.
In the rest of the paper,
we refer to using the reference and context-aware LLMs  as the baseline and context-aware versions of our mechanisms, respectively.








\subsection{Applicability and Practical Considerations of MOSAIC} 
\label{section:practical_considerations}

\paragraph{Generation of Candidate Sequences.}
The computationally most expensive part of our mechanism is the generation of candidate sequences, which requires an LLM inference call for the generation of each candidate sequence and potentially $n$ additional calls to calculate all advertisers' rewards.
Thus, the computational cost of our mechanism scales linearly in the number of candidate sequences, underscoring the importance of approximating the optimal distribution with few candidate sequences.

\paragraph{Input Methods and Computational Efficiency.}

The mechanism's allocation and payment rules do not depend on the  advertisers' full reward functions, but only on their rewards for the candidate sequences. Thus,
our mechanism requires only ``API access'' to the involved LLMs without fine-tuning or access to their weights. 
\citet{rafailov2023dpo} established a mapping between an agent's LLM and her implicit reward function, allowing our mechanism to use as inputs sequence probabilities (i.e., LLM inference calls) instead of rewards.
\paragraph{Reward elicitation.} Our mechanism can be integrated with computationally efficient methods for eliciting advertisers' reward functions and sampling from $\pi_{\text{con}}$, reducing computational overhead. For instance, \citet{li2024qprobe} demonstrated that a simple linear function on a model's embedding space can approximate an LLM's reward function. If  advertisers' reward functions are represented this way, an auction instance with $M$ candidate sequences and $n$  advertisers would require only $M$ LLM inference calls instead of $n \cdot M$, and $n \cdot M$ linear multiplications, reducing overhead by a factor of $n$.

\paragraph{Parallelization.}
The generation and evaluation of each candidate sequence are independent processes. This independence allows our mechanism to be fully parallelized, ensuring that the response time for a user query is comparable to that of directly querying a single LLM.
\paragraph{Inherent Competition.}
 In conventional auctions, competition among bidders is crucial for driving up prices; when the market is thin, prices tend to be low. Auctioneers often use reserve prices to mitigate this problem. However, reserve prices are challenging to calibrate and can lead to lost revenue if not optimized properly. In our design, advertisers inherently compete with the reference LLM, ensuring consistent competition even in thin markets. This built-in competition mitigates the need for reserve prices and ensures a baseline revenue even with only one advertiser.

\section{The MOSAIC Mechanism: Payment Rule}

In this section, we first show how the allocation rule from \Cref{section:allocation_rule} can be combined with an appropriate payment rule so that the resulting mechanism is strategyproof (\Cref{subsection:payments_through_convexity}). 
Then, we detail how auctions for LLM-generated content differ from standard auctions (\Cref{subsec:differences}). 
Taking those differences into account, we create a payment offset,
so the mechanism is both strategyproof and social welfare aligned (\Cref{section:bidding zero offset}).
\subsection{Strategyproof Payments through Cyclic Monotonicity} \label{subsection:payments_through_convexity}

The allocation rule we introduced in \Cref{section:allocation_rule} satisfies \textit{cyclic monotonicity}~\citep[\S~24]{Rockafellar1970}.
 \citet{rochet1987} first proposed the use of cyclic monotonicity in mechanism design as a generalization of the monotonicity property in single-parameter settings \citep{Myerson81Optimal}.
For general settings, cyclic monotonicity of the allocation rule is a sufficient and necessary condition for the existence of a payment rule so that the resulting mechanism is strategyproof. 
By applying this theory we can prove:

\begin{theorem} \label{theorem:truthful_payments}
Let $\Vec{r}_{-i}$ be the reward profile of all  advertisers other than $i$, and let $\vec{r}_{-i}$ be the aggregate reports of all other  advertisers.
The allocation rule induced by \Cref{alg:static_mechanism_improved} can be combined with a payment rule  $p: \Vec{R} \xrightarrow[]{} \mathbb{R}^n $ such that in the mechanism $(\pi, p)$ 
for any advertiser $i \in N$,
report profile $\vec{r}_{-i}$ and 
set of generated candidate replies, reporting truthfully maximizes advertiser $i$'s expected utility, with the expectation taken over
the draw of the final reply from the set of candidate replies. 
Advertiser $i$'s expected utility in $(\pi, p)$  under truthful bidding is: 
\begin{align}
    U_{C,i}(\Vec{r}_i, \Vec{r}_{-i} ; \pi_{ref}, \pi_{gen} ) 
    & = 
    \tau \log \left ( 
        \sum_{j = 1}^M \exp \left ( \frac{1}{\tau} \sum_{k \in N} r_k(x, y_j)   
        \right ) 
        + \log \frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j | x)}{\pi_{\text{gen}}(y_j | x )}
        \right ) + C, \; C \in \mathbb{R}
    \label{equation:utility_myerson_spelled_out}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
Note that, based on \Cref{theorem:truthful_payments}, in our mechanism it is \textit{always} a dominant strategy for an advertiser to report truthfully. 
Crucially, this is not the case for VCG, where truthful reporting would be optimal only if the allocation rule had converged to the optimal distribution as defined in \Cref{eq:opt_distribution}.
\subsection{Differences from Auction Mechanisms for Item Allocation} \label{subsec:differences}
Auction mechanisms designed to sell items or ad slots typically rely on simplifying assumptions that do not apply in a setting with LLM-generated content.
Key differences include:
(i) \textit{Non-Negative Values:} These mechanisms assume agents' values are non-negative due to having zero value for the empty bundle and free disposal. In contrast, in our setting, an advertiser's reward can be negative based on the discrepancy between her LLM and the reference LLM.
(ii) \textit{Advertiser-Specific Allocations:} These mechanisms allocate different item bundles to different agents. Here, a single reply is produced, and advertisers' rewards depend on that reply's probability  with respect to their LLMs.
(iii) \textit{Zero Utility for Non-Participation:} In most auction settings, not participating yields zero utility. 
Here, non-participation can result in negative utility since the produced reply may be unfavorable to non-participating advertisers.


\subsection{advertiser-Specific Utility Offset} \label{section:bidding zero offset}
We now modify MOSAIC's payment rule by adding a \textit{payment offset} (and thus a utility offset):  
    $C = - U_{i}(\Vec{0}, \Vec{r}_{-i} ; \pi_{ref}, \pi_{con} ) $.
Informally, we additionally
charge each advertiser her utility in \Cref{equation:utility_myerson_spelled_out}
for bidding zero for all candidate replies.
This offset maintains the nice properties of our mechanism (i.e., strategyproofness and convergence to the optimal distribution), while also achieving two additional properties that are critical for the long-term success of a market for LLM aggregation. The first is \textit{``Almost IR:''} An advertiser with weakly positive reward for all candidate replies has weakly positive expected utility for all reports by the other advertisers. In particular, an advertiser with zero reward for all outputs is guaranteed zero utility. 
The second is \textit{``What you give is what you get:''} an advertiser's ex-interim expected utility is monotone in how well-aligned her exponentiated reward for the replies is with the interim allocation rule if she were to not participate.


\subsubsection{Our Mechanism is ``Almost Individually Rational''} \label{subsec:almost_ir}
Individual rationality is important to incentivize agents to participate in the mechanism. 
In \Cref{app:almost_ir}, we explain why the standard notion of individual rationality (i.e., weakly positive utility from participation in the mechanism) encountered in standard auction settings is impossible to achieve in our setting while converging to the optimal distribution and maintaining 
strategyproofness.
Then, we explain how, with our payment offset, our mechanism is ``almost IR:''
In \Cref{lemma:zero_bid_offset_ex_interim_utility_zero} we prove that the ex-interim utility of an advertiser who has zero reward for all candidate replies and bids truthfully is deterministically zero, i.e.,  advertisers that do not contribute to the social welfare (but also do not detract from it) have zero utility. 
Similarly, in \Cref{lemma:zero_bid_offset_ex_interim_utility_positive} we prove that 
if an advertiser's reward for all candidate sequences is (weakly) positive, then her ex-interim utility is (weakly) positive. 


\begin{remark}
In \Cref{section:experiments}, we experimentally show that our offset payment rule, coupled with our context-aware allocation rule, results in both high expected rewards and positive expected utility for the advertisers (i.e., ex-ante individual rationality), as well as significant revenue for the auctioneer.
\end{remark}
\subsubsection{``What you give is what you get''} \label{section:what_you_give_is_what_you_get}
Our allocation rule, which is the only one over a finite set of replies that converges to the optimal LLM, is also the (sub)gradient of the utility to ensure truthfulness \citep{rochet1987}. 
Because the allocation rule is the same for all advertisers, their utilities must  also be the same, up to advertiser-specific offsets, as indicated by \Cref{equation:utility_myerson_spelled_out}. However, not all advertisers contribute equally to social welfare.
In \Cref{app:market_unraveling}, we explain why implementing the mechanism without carefully designed offsets would lead to free-riding. Specifically, if an advertiser's utility in \Cref{equation:utility_myerson_spelled_out} is positive, even when her relevance to the user query is low, it incentivizes her participation. This dilutes the utility for all participants, discouraging more relevant advertisers from participating. As a result, the expected rewards for advertisers decline, and the generated replies become less useful for users.
To avoid free-riding, it is crucial to align each advertiser’s utility with her contribution to social welfare.


\begin{lemma} \label{lemma:ex_interim_utility_alignment}
For the offset 
$C =  - \tau \log \left ( \sum_{j = 1}^M \exp \left (  \vec{\beta}_{-i,j}   \right ) 
\right )$
 advertiser $i$'s ex-interim utility is: 
\begin{gather}
   U_i(\Vec{r}_i ; \vec{\beta}_{-i})  =  \tau \log \left ( 
\sum_{j = 1}^M \exp \left ( \frac{r_i(x, y_j)}{\tau} \right ) 
\pi_{\text{int}} (y_j | x ; \Vec{r}_{-i})_j \right ) 
\label{eq:expected_utility_static_mech_zero_bid_offset_2}
\end{gather}
\end{lemma}
In words, \Cref{lemma:ex_interim_utility_alignment} proves that every agent's ex-interim utility is monotone in how well-aligned the interim allocation rule $\pi_{\text{int}}$
(i.e., the probability of returning each of the already generated candidate replies)
without her and her exponentiated rewards for the candidate replies are.

\begin{remark} \label{remark:what_you_give_is_what_you_get}
In \Cref{subsection:fairness_results}, we experimentally show that the offset described in \Cref{section:bidding zero offset} creates a strong positive correlation between an advertiser’s contribution to social welfare and her expected utility gain from participation in the mechanism. Moreover, our experiments show that for the distribution of instances tested, MOSAIC satisfies ex-ante IR. \end{remark}


\section{Experiments}

In this section, we experimentally evaluate the performance of our mechanism. 
We focus on the online advertising domain, the main motivation application for our mechanism.

\subsection{Experiment Setup}
We create synthetic instances consisting of user queries (e.g., ``How to learn a musical instrument online?'') and advertisers (e.g., ``MusicMastery, offering online music lessons''). We use Llama-2-7b-chat-hf as the reference LLM \citep{touvron2023llama}. Following \citet{duetting2023mechanism},  we create the advertisers' LLMs by adding advertising instructions to the reference LLM.
The context-aware LLM is created as described in \Cref{footnote:context_aware_llm}.

Following \citet{rafailov2023dpo}, advertisers' reward functions are defined as $r_i(x, y) = \log \frac{\pi_i (y | x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}} (y | x)}$. For the auctioneer's objective, we set $\tau = 1$ in \Cref{eq:objective},  balancing advertisers' rewards and divergence from the reference LLM.

We use 50 user queries, each with two advertisers, and test each query on 25 different random seeds, resulting in 1,250 instances. 
Following \citet{li2024qprobe,rozière2024codellama}, we sample from all LLMs using a temperature of 0.8 and top-p 0.95.

\section{Conclusion}



We have introduced MOSAIC, a novel auction mechanism for aggregating preferences over LLM outputs. MOSAIC provably converges to the theoretically optimal distribution and it also facilitates a principled method for balancing participants' expected rewards with the divergence from a reference LLM. 
Thus, our mechanism is particularly well-suited for online advertising, allowing the integration of advertiser LLMs with a reference LLM responsible for generating user-centric replies.

A well-chosen payment rule removes any incentive to misreport preferences, achieving the central mechanism design goal of strategyproofness. 
While ex-post IR is incompatible with strategyproofness in our setting, 
we experimentally show that our mechanism is ex-ante individually rational 
and ``almost individually rational'' in a certain sense.
Furthermore, it ensures that each agent’s utility gain is proportionate to her contribution to social welfare, an essential alignment property  in this setting. 

Experimentally, we have demonstrated that by incorporating contextual information, MOSAIC's outputs rapidly converge to the optimal distribution, generating significant value for the advertisers while also effectively recapturing a considerable portion of this value as revenue. These findings demonstrate the practical efficacy and potential of our approach in real-world settings.

There are several interesting directions for future work.
While our mechanism converges quickly, developing an approach to estimate the number of candidate replies required on a per-query basis would reduce the platform's costs. 
Another interesting direction is to develop a formal framework for incorporating context into the mechanism, which could further improve the speed of convergence.
Finally, beyond advertising, one could apply a similar mechanism design approach to other problems: for example, as web site owners increasingly demand to be paid for having their content ingested by LLMs, one could consider designing a strategyproof procurement (i.e. reverse) auction to decide which sources are included in an LLM summary.
