\title{Multigraph Message Passing with Bi-Directional Multi-Edge Aggregations}

\begin{abstract}
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have seen significant advances in recent years, yet their application to multigraphs, where parallel edges exist between the same pair of nodes, remains under-explored. Standard GNNs, designed for simple graphs, compute node representations by combining all connected edges at once, without distinguishing between edges from different neighbors. There are some GNN architectures proposed specifically for multigraphs, yet these architectures perform only node-level aggregation in their message passing layers, which limits their expressive power. Furthermore, these approaches either lack permutation equivariance when a strict total edge ordering is absent, or fail to preserve the topological structure of the multigraph. To address all these shortcomings, we propose MEGA-GNN, a unified framework for message passing on multigraphs that can effectively perform diverse graph learning tasks. Our approach introduces a two-stage aggregation process in the message passing layers: first, parallel edges are aggregated, followed by a node-level aggregation of messages from distinct neighbors. We show that MEGA-GNN is not only permutation equivariant but also universal given a strict total ordering on the edges. Experiments show that MEGA-GNN significantly outperforms state-of-the-art solutions by up to 13\% on Anti-Money Laundering datasets and is on par with their accuracy on real-world phishing classification datasets in terms of minority class F1 score.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) (\cite{xu2018gin, mpnn, gat, pna, graphsage}) have become Swiss Army knives for learning on graph-structured data. However, their widespread adoption has primarily focused on simple graphs, where only a single edge can connect a given pair of nodes. This simplification overlooks a crucial aspect of many real-world scenarios, where multigraphs, graphs that feature parallel edges between the same pair of nodes, are common. For instance, financial transaction networks, communication networks and transportation systems are often modeled as multigraphs, allowing multiple different interactions between the same two nodes. Existing GNN architectures are inherently limited in handling such multigraph structures since they aggregate messages only at the node level, combining information from all incoming edges at once without distinguishing between parallel edges and their sources. Although such an aggregation mechanism is sufficient to construct provably powerful GNN models on simple graphs (\cite{maron_2019, xu2018gin}), the expressivity of such models are theoretically and empirically limited on directed multigraphs as shown by \cite{egressy2024provably}. 

In the literature, only two GNN-based solutions, namely Multi-GNN (\cite{egressy2024provably}) and ADAMM (\cite{adamm}), have been proposed for multigraphs, yet both exhibit critical limitations as summarized in Table \ref{tab:comparison}. Multi-GNN, while addressing expressivity concerns, fails to preserve permutation equivariance.  ADAMM, on the other hand, fails to effectively support diverse graph learning tasks, thus limiting its practical applicability. To address these limitations, we propose \textbf{M}ulti-\textbf{E}d\textbf{G}e \textbf{A}ggregation GNNs, henceforth referred to as \textbf{MEGA-GNN}. MEGA-GNN is a unified message passing framework specifically designed for multigraphs. It employs a two-stage message aggregation process: first, parallel edges between the same two nodes are aggregated, and then, the aggregated messages from distinct neighbors are further combined at the node level. 

Our theoretical analyses show that MEGA-GNN is provably powerful, meaning it can detect any directed subgraph pattern within multigraphs if a strict total ordering of the edges is possible.

Our main contributions are three fold: \textbf{(1)} We introduce a generic bi-directional message passing framework for multigraphs using two-stage aggregation where parallel edges are first aggregated, followed by a node-level aggregation of messages from distinct neighbors in each message passing layer. \textbf{(2)} We prove that our framework supports essential properties such as permutation equivariance and universality. \textbf{(3)} We validate our framework on financial transaction datasets, where we significantly outperform the state-of-the-art for illicit transaction detection and match the state-of-the-art for phishing account detection.

A notable contribution of Multi-GNN is the multigraph port numbering on edges, which allows the model to differentiate between edges from the same neighbor and edges from different neighbors (see Figure \ref{fig:ports}). In addition to multigraph port numbering, Multi-GNN incorporates Ego-IDs (\cite{egoid2021}) and bi-directional message passing (\cite{reverse_mp}). \textbf{ADAMM} proposed aggregating all parallel edges between two nodes to a single undirected super-edge, thereby transforming the multigraph into a simple graph before message passing. An initial features for this super-edge are computed using DeepSet (\cite{deep_set}) and the subsequent message passing layers operate on these features. However, this approach results in a loss of critical structural information inherent in the multigraph. By collapsing multiple edges into single one, it cannot generate features for individual edges, limiting its ability to perform graph learning tasks that require latent edge features.

Notably, because this approach does not compute latent features for the original edges, it cannot perform multi-edge aggregations repeatedly in several message passing layers.

Another major limitation of ADAMM is its lack of support for bi-directional message passing, which has been shown to lead to significant accuracy gains for directed multigraphs(\cite{egressy2024provably}).

\textbf{In summary}, a general framework for message passing on multigraphs that supports permutation equivariance and diverse graph learning tasks has yet to be proposed. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a novel message passing architecture that is explicitly designed for multigraphs.



\section{Multigraph Message Passing}


This section introduces a novel message passing scheme for multigraphs which combines node-level aggregation with \textit{multi-edge} aggregation and supports a general set of graph learning tasks such as node, edge, and graph classification. Additionally, the resulting models are permutation invariant or equivariant depending on the chosen multi-edge aggregation function and the downstream task. 

\subsection{Motivation for Multi-Edge Aggregations in Multigraph GNNs}
\begin{figure}[t] 
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/mot.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Comparison of two-stage and single-stage aggregation strategies for multigraphs}. The left panel illustrates a sample multigraph. The center panel depicts the two-stage aggregation process, wherein the first stage aggregates parallel edges, followed by a second stage node-level aggregation. The right panel shows the single-stage node-level aggregation scheme commonly employed by standard GNNs, which aggregates all edges, including the parallel edges, at once. 
    }  
    \label{fig:motivation}
\end{figure}
We argue that a two-stage aggregation is more powerful than a single-stage aggregation in a multigraph setting. We first give a motivating example, which shows that two-stage aggregation functions are more expressive than their single-stage counterparts. 

Consider the example graph depicted in Figure \ref{fig:motivation}, with four edges $e_{ij1}, e_{ij2}, e_{kj1}$ and $e_{kj2}$. Suppose that we have an aggregation function that operates on multisets and can simultaneously compute the sum and the maximum of the elements included in the multiset. To demonstrate that two-stage aggregation is more powerful than the single-stage one, we will show that two-stage aggregation can perform any computation that the single-stage aggregation can, but not vice versa. A single-stage aggregation can compute the following:
$$
\text{SUM} \{ e_{ij1}, e_{ij2}, e_{kj1}, e_{kj2} \}, \quad 
\text{MAX} \{ e_{ij1}, e_{ij2}, e_{kj1}, e_{kj2} \}.
$$
The two-stage aggregation can also compute these by aggregating the edges in pairs first:
$$
\text{SUM} \{ \text{SUM} \{ e_{ij1}, e_{ij2} \}, \text{SUM} \{ e_{kj1}, e_{kj2} \} \}, \quad
\text{MAX} \{ \text{MAX} \{ e_{ij1}, e_{ij2} \}, \text{MAX} \{ e_{kj1}, e_{kj2} \} \}.
$$
On the other hand, the two-stage aggregation allows for more nuanced computations, such as:
$$
\text{SUM} \{ \text{MAX} \{ e_{ij1}, e_{ij2} \}, \text{MAX} \{ e_{kj1}, e_{kj2} \} \}, \quad
\text{MAX} \{ \text{SUM} \{ e_{ij1}, e_{ij2} \}, \text{SUM} \{ e_{kj1}, e_{kj2} \} \},
$$
which cannot be achieved through single-stage aggregation. The distinction is significant; the two-stage aggregation enables us to capture the statistics of edges originating from different neighbors. Consider a financial application, in which a receiver may receive the highest total payment from one sender (Sender 1), while the highest single payment may originate from another sender (Sender 2). Using the two-stage aggregation, we can detect the maximum of the sums of payments from each sender, allowing us to distinguish between their total contributions. In contrast, the single-stage aggregation can only compute the maximum among all payments without differentiating between identical and distinct senders, which can obscure critical insights.

\subsection{Preliminaries} 

\textbf{Notation}: 
We denote multisets with $\{\!\{\}\! \}$ and sets with $\{\}$.
Let $\gG = (\gV,\gE)$ be a directed multigraph with $|\gV|=v$ nodes and $|\gE|=e$ edges. The edges $\gE = \{\!\{ (i,j)\ |\ i, j \in \gV \}\! \}$ constitute a multiset of pairs of nodes, where each pair ($i,j$) represents a directed edge from node $i$ to node $j$. In a multigraph, several edges may exist between the same source and destination nodes, referred to as parallel edges.  In this work, we consider attributed multigraphs; each parallel edge between the same two nodes has its own feature vector, which requires additional considerations. 

Let $\gE^{supp} \subseteq \gE$ denote the support set of $\gE$, which consists of unique $(i,j)$ pairs that have a multiplicity of at least one in the multiset $\gE$. Furthermore, let $ME_{ij} = \{\!\{ (i,j) \in \gE \}\! \} $ denote the multiset of parallel edges between a source node $i$ and a destination node $j$. We denote the cardinality of $ME_{ij}$ as $P_{ij}$. For a node $j \in \gV$, we define the incoming and outgoing neighbors as $N_{in}(j) = \{ i \in \gV | (i,j) \in \gE^{supp} \}$ and $N_{out}(j) = \{ i \in \gV | (j,i) \in \gE^{supp} \}$, respectively.

The nodes and edges of the graph $\gG$ are associated with feature vectors. Each node and edge has an initial feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_n}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(0)} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_e}$, with $p \in [1..P_{ij}]$, respectively. Similarly, at layer $l \in [1..L]$ in a model with $L$ layers, nodes and edges are represented by latent feature vectors $\mathbf{x}_i^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{D^{h}_{n}}$ and $\mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(l)} \in \mathbb{R}^{D^{h}_{e}}$, respectively. Here $D_n, D_e, D^{h}_{n}, D^{h}_{e} \in \mathbb{N}$ denote the dimensions of the corresponding vectors. 

 

\textbf{Message-Passing GNNs}: In the standard Message Passing Neural Networks (MPNNs) proposed by \cite{mpnn}, the information on the graph is propagated using an iterative application of local message passing operations. The following shows how an MPNN can be applied to a multigraph:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:1}
\begin{split}
    \mathbf{x}_j^{(l)} = g_v^{(l-1)} \bigg( \mathbf{x}_j^{(l-1)}, \text{AGG} \{\!\{ f^{(l-1)}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(l-1)})\ |\ i \in N_{in}(j), p \in [1..P_{ij}]\ \}\! \} \bigg),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $f^{(l-1)}$ is a function that constructs the messages from the incoming neighbors of node $j$. These messages are then aggregated using the AGG function, which is typically a permutation-invariant operation such as sum, mean, or max. After the message aggregation step, the latent features of node $j$ is updated using the update function $g_v^{(l-1)}$. Additionally, latent edge features could also be updated using the following formula, where $g_e^{(l-1)}$ is the edge update function:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:2}
\begin{split}
    \mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(l)} = g_e^{(l-1)} \big( \mathbf{x}_i^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{x}_j^{(l-1)} \big).
\end{split}
\end{equation}

We now formally define some key concepts that will be used throughout the paper.

\begin{definition} \label{def:1}
    \textup{\textbf{(Strict Total Order)}}
    Strict total order is a binary relation $<$ on a set $\gA$, which satisfies the following conditions for all  $a$,$b$,$c$ $\in$ $\gA$: (1) $a\neq b$, either $a<b$ or $b<a$, (2) not $a<a$, (3) if $a<b$ and $b<c$, then $a<c$ (see \cite{munkres2000topology}, p. 22).
\end{definition}

We define a strict total order on the edges of the attributed graph $\gG$ using their initial feature vectors. Specifically, for any two edges, the ordering is established by: $\mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(0)} < \mathbf{e}_{i'j'q'}^{(0)}$.

\begin{definition} \label{def:2}
    \textup{\textbf{(Universality)}}
    An MPNN is universal if it can approximate every invariant or equivariant continuous functions defined on graphs (\cite{payre19}). According to \cite{Loukas2020What}, universality is achieved if the following conditions are satisfied: there are enough layers, the layers are sufficiently expressive and wide, and the nodes can uniquely distinguish each other.
\end{definition} 
\begin{definition} \label{def:3}
    \textup{\textbf{(Permutation Equivariance)}}
    A function $\psi$ is said to be permutation equivariant with respect to node and edge permutations if, for any permutation $\rho$ acting on the nodes and edges of a graph $\gG = (\gV, \gE)$, the following holds: $$\psi(\rho \circ \gG(\gV, \gE) )\ =\ \rho \circ \psi( \gG(\gV, \gE) )$$
\end{definition} 

\subsection{Multigraph Message Passing with Multi-Edge Aggregations} \label{sec:4.2}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/artificial_nodes4.pdf}
    \caption{\textbf{Illustration of Multi-Edge Aggregation using artificial nodes in a multigraph}. (a) A multigraph with parallel edges $e_{ij1}, e_{ij2}, e_{ij3}$ between the nodes $i$ and $j$. (b) Artificial nodes positioned between adjacent pairs of nodes to handle the aggregation of parallel edges. First, the information from the \textit{parallel} edges are aggregated into some embedding vectors in the artificial nodes, and then the destination node performs a node-level aggregation on these embeddings. (c) The reverse message passing mechanism. In directed multigraphs, reverse edges are created in the reverse direction of the original edges, and then additional artificial nodes are introduced to handle the aggregation of these reverse edges. Separate message computations are performed to handle the original and reversed edges. The result is a bi-directional multigraph message passing solution.
    }  
    \label{fig:multi}
\end{figure}

Commonly used GNN architectures \cite{gcn, gat, graphsage} are not designed for multigraphs. Performing message aggregation solely at the node level as shown in Equation \ref{eq:1}, limits the expressive power of these models on multigraph learning tasks. As a remedy, we propose a message passing framework (MEGA-GNN) for multigraphs with two-stage aggregation, wherein the first stage, termed \textit{multi-edge} aggregation, aggregates parallel edges, followed by a second stage node-level aggregation. To enable effective multi-edge aggregation, we introduce novel \textit{artificial nodes} into the multigraph.

We define the set of artificial nodes, positioned between each unique pair of nodes in the multigraph (as shown in Figure \ref{fig:multi}(b)) as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:3}
    \begin{split}
        \gV^{art} = \{ v_{ij}^{art}\ |\ (i,j) \in \gE^{supp}\}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
The incoming neighbors of the artificial node $v_{ij}^{art}$ are given by the multiset $ME_{ij}$, the cardinality of which is $P_{ij}$. Let $\mathbf{h}_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the latent features of the artificial node $v_{ij}^{art}$.

In the first aggregation stage, parallel edges are aggregated at the artificial nodes, thereby reducing the multigraph to an equivalent simple graph in the message passing layer $(l\!-\!1)$: 
\begin{equation} \label{eq:4}
\begin{split}
    \mathbf{h}_{ij}^{(l-1)} = \text{EdgeAgg} \{\!\{ \mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(l-1)} \ |\ p \in [1..P_{ij}] \}\!\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
In the second aggregation stage, node-level aggregation is performed.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:5}
\begin{split}
    \mathbf{a}_j^{(l-1)} &= \text{AGG} \{\!\{ f^{(l-1)}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(l-1)},\mathbf{h}_{ij}^{(l-1)})\ |\ i \in N_{in}(j) \} \!\} \\
    \mathbf{x}_j^{(l)} &= g_v^{(l-1)} \bigg( \mathbf{x}_j^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{a}_j^{(l-1)} \bigg).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Here, the destination node $j$ receives a single (aggregated) message from each of its distinct incoming neighbors. As in Equation \ref{eq:2}, the latent features of each individual edge can be updated:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:6}
\begin{split}
    \mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(l)} = g_e^{(l-1)} \big( \mathbf{x}_i^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{e}_{ijp}^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{h}_{ij}^{(l-1)} \big).
\end{split}
\end{equation}

This two-stage aggregation process, enabled by our innovative use of artificial nodes, preserves the topology of the original multigraph. Equations \ref{eq:4}, \ref{eq:5}, and \ref{eq:6} can be applied iteratively, allowing for effective propagation and updates of both node and edge latent features in each message passing layer. Unlike previous methods such as ADAMM \cite{adamm}, which collapses parallel edges into a single edge prior to message passing—thereby preventing updates on the original multigraph edges—our approach maintains distinct edges through individual updates, enabling richer feature propagation in multigraph learning tasks.

\subsection{Bi-directional Multigraph Message Passing} \label{sec:bi}

Bi-directional message passing (\cite{egressy2024provably}) has been shown to significantly improve model expressivity, especially for directed graphs. In standard message passing, nodes cannot receive information from their outgoing neighbors, limiting their ability to account for outgoing edges (e.g., preventing them from accessing their out-degree). Receiving messages from outgoing neighbors is distinct from using undirected edges, where incoming and outgoing edges are treated identically.

In this section, we present formulations for bi-directional message passing with two-stage aggregation in multigraphs. In MEGA-GNN, nodes receive messages from outgoing neighbors by incorporating reversed versions of their outgoing edges. Formally, the reverse edge multiset is defined as $\hat{\gE} = \{\!\{ (j,i)\ |\ (i, j) \in \gE \}\! \}$ and the features of reverse edges are initialized as $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ijp}^{(0)} = \mathbf{e}_{jip}^{(0)}$, where $p \in [1..P_{ji}]$. To enable two-stage aggregation for reverse edges, a new set of \textit{artificial nodes} $\hat{\gV}^{art}$ is introduced. These nodes are placed between each unique pair of nodes in the reverse direction, mirroring their arrangement in the original multigraph (see Figure \ref{fig:multi}(c)).
\begin{equation} \label{eq:7}
    \begin{split}
        \hat{\gV}^{art} = \{ \hat{v}_{ij}^{art}\ |\ (j,i) \in \gE^{supp}\}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
The incoming neighbors of the artificial node $\hat{v}_{ij}^{art}$ are given by the multiset $ME_{ji}$, with cardinality  $P_{ji}$. Let $\hat{\mathbf{h}}_{ij} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denote the latent features of the artificial node $\hat{v}_{ij}^{art}$. 

In the first aggregation stage, outgoing parallel edges from node $j$ to node $i$ are aggregated.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:8}
\begin{split}
    \hat{\mathbf{h}}_{ij}^{(l-1)} = \text{EdgeAgg} \{\!\{ \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ijp}^{(l-1)} \ |\ p \in [1..P_{ji}] \}\!\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
In the second aggregation stage, node-level aggregation is performed.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:9}
\begin{split}
    \hat{\mathbf{a}}_j^{(l-1)} &= \text{AGG} \{\!\{ \hat{f}^{(l-1)}(\mathbf{x}_i^{(l-1)}, \hat{\mathbf{h}}_{ij}^{(l-1)})\ |\ i \in N_{out}(j) \}\!\} \\
    \mathbf{x}_j^{(l)} &= g_v^{(l-1)} \bigg( \mathbf{x}_j^{(l-1)}, \mathbf{a}_j^{(l-1)}, \hat{\mathbf{a}}_j^{(l-1)} \bigg), \\
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{f}^{(l-1)}$ is a function that constructs the messages for the outgoing edges and $a_j^{(l-1)}$ is computed using the equations in the previous subsection. Therefore,  in Equation \ref{eq:9}, the destination node $j$ is updated using messages from both its incoming and outgoing neighbors. 
As in Equation \ref{eq:6}, the latent features of the reverse edges are updated:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:10}
\begin{split}
    \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ijp}^{(l)} = \hat{g}_e^{(l-1)} \big( \mathbf{x}_i^{(l-1)}, \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{ijp}^{(l-1)}, \hat{\mathbf{h}}_{ij}^{(l-1)} \big), \\
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{g}_e^{(l)}$ is the update function for the reverse edges.

\subsection{Properties}

In this section, we establish several key theoretical properties of MEGA-GNN and Multi-GNN, analyzing and comparing their expressiveness and permutation equivariance under different conditions. In particular, we show that MEGA-GNN is always permutation equivariant, a property not shared by Multi-GNN. Next, we prove that MEGA-GNN is universal given a strict total order on the edges. 
\begin{theorem} \label{th:1}
\textup{\textbf{(Permutation Equivariance).}} Given permutation invariant \textit{EdgeAgg} and $\text{AGG}$ functions, the framework defined by Equations \ref{eq:4}, \ref{eq:5}, and \ref{eq:6} is permutation equivariant.
\end{theorem} 

 For neural networks operating on graph-structured data, permutation equivariance is a crucial property, ensuring that the model's output remains consistent to the ordering of nodes and edges. Our model is explicitly designed to maintain permutation equivariance at both the node and edge levels. Permutation invariance for graph-level outputs can be achieved by using a permutation-invariant  $\textit{graph readout}$ function.
\begin{lemma} \label{lm:1}
    The message passing framework defined by Equations \ref{eq:4}, \ref{eq:5}, and \ref{eq:6} can compute unique node IDs in connected multigraphs given a strict total ordering of the edges.
\end{lemma}

The proof of Lemma \ref{lm:1} (provided in \ref{proof:theorem_2}) shows that, when a strict total ordering among the edges (e.g., based on timestamps or other contextual edge features) is available, the model is able to assign unique identifiers to the nodes in a connected multigraph. 
\begin{theorem} \label{corr:uni}
    \textup{\textbf{(Universality).}}
    Given sufficient layers with sufficient expressiveness and width as well as a strict total ordering of the edges, the MEGA-GNN is universal.
\end{theorem} 
The definition of universality is given in Definition \ref{def:2}. Lemma \ref{lm:1} establishes that the MEGA-GNN can compute unique node IDs in connected multigraphs when a contextually-driven strict total ordering of edges is provided, which makes nodes to uniquely distinguish each other. Assuming MEGA-GNN also has sufficient layers, depth and expressiveness, it meets all the conditions presented in \cite{Loukas2020What} and is, therefore, universal in the sense defined by \cite{Loukas2020What}.

We emphasize that the ability to assign unique node IDs does not violate the permutation equivariance of MEGA-GNN. In practice, the model does not explicitly assign IDs or rely on node/edge indices or their order. Instead, edge features such as timestamps provide a natural strict total ordering. As shown theoretically, if such an ordering exists, MEGA-GNN has the ability to assign unique node IDs, ensuring that permutation equivariance and universality can coexist without conflict.

All the above claims remain valid when the bi-directional message passing mechanism of Section \ref{sec:bi} is used.

To sum up, the theoretical analyses presented in this section lead to the following insights: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Both MEGA-GNN and Multi-GNN are permutation equivariant and universal when there is a strict total ordering of the edges.
    \item When such an ordering is not available, 1) MEGA-GNN is permutation equivariant but non-universal, and 2) Multi-GNN is universal but not permutation equivariant.
\end{itemize}


\section{Experimental Results}



This section presents our experiments designed to evaluate the performance of MEGA-GNN. We focus on financial transaction multigraphs, and address two key applications in financial crime analysis: illicit transaction detection through edge classification and phishing account detection in Ethereum transactions through node classification. We provide detailed descriptions of the datasets used in our experiments and the baselines against which we compare our method.

\textbf{Anti-Money Laundering (AML)}:  Given the concerns over privacy and legal regulations, there is a scarcity of publicly available financial transaction data. Recently, \cite{altman2023realistic} released realistic synthetic financial transaction data for money laundering detection. In this synthetic data, a multi-agent world simulates transactions between entities such as banks, individuals, and companies. We use Small LI, Small HI, Medium LI, and Medium HI datasets, where LI indicates low-illicit and HI indicates high-illicit, referring to the ratios of illicit transactions in the datasets. The task is to perform binary classification on the transactions, labeling them as illicit or non-illicit. The edges include four attributes related to transactions: timestamp, received amount, currency type, and payment format.

\textbf{Ethereum Phishing Transaction Network (ETH)}: Since access to real financial transaction data from banks is limited due to privacy concerns, cryptocurrencies provide an alternative data source. In our study, we use the Ethereum transaction dataset (\cite{eth_kaggle}), where accounts are treated as nodes and transactions as edges. Each node has a label indicating whether it is a phishing node or not. The edges include two attributes related to transactions: timestamp and received amount.

 We use the same temporal split as the Multi-GNN baseline across the AML datasets and apply a temporal split of 65-15-20 on the ETH dataset across the edges (transactions). We adopt Ego-IDs (\cite{egoid2021}) in our model in the edge classification experiments (see Table \ref{tab:edge_results}) and incorporate bi-directional message passing (see Section \ref{sec:bi} for details) in both edge and node classification experiments (see Table \ref{tab:edge_results} and \ref{tab:node_results}). To ensure the statistical significance of our results, each experiment is repeated five times with different random seeds, and the mean and the standard deviation of these runs are reported.

\textbf{Baselines} We select several baselines for comparison. In particular, we include GFP+LightGBM and GFP+XGBoost \cite{blanuša2024IBM}, as well as Multi-GNN \cite{egressy2024provably}, as the state-of-the-art approaches. We should note that the baseline method Multi-GNN also incorporates Ego-IDs (\cite{egoid2021}). Additionally, we use ADAMM \cite{adamm} as a baseline for Table \ref{tab:node_results} on the ETH dataset. Within the proposed multigraph message passing framework (see Section \ref{sec:4.2}), we explore different aggregation methods such as GIN (\cite{xu2018gin}), PNA (\cite{pna}) and GenAgg (\cite{kortvelesy2023generalised}).

We evaluate our proposed MEGA-GNN models across four different synthetic AML datasets, as shown in Table \ref{tab:edge_results}. Six distinct MEGA-GNN models are employed, using different combinations of aggregation functions across the two stages. For clarity, we adopt the following naming convention: MEGA-GIN refers to the model that applies GIN \cite{xu2018gin} aggregation at both stages, while MEGA(GenAgg)-GIN applies GenAgg (\cite{kortvelesy2023generalised}) for multi-edge aggregation and GIN for node-level aggregation. 
Table \ref{tab:edge_results} shows that our MEGA-GNN models consistently outperform both traditional GNN baselines, such as GIN and PNA, as well as state-of-the-art models, including Multi-GNN and GFP-based approaches, yielding significant performance improvements across all datasets. On average, our models achieve a $9.25\%$ increase in minority-class F1 score on the HI datasets and a $13.31\%$ improvement on the LI datasets compared to the state-of-the-art. A clear trend of enhanced performance with the incorporation of multi-edge aggregation is visible across all these datasets,  regardless of the specific combinations of aggregation functions used. For instance, on the AML Medium HI dataset, MEGA-GIN boosts performance from $58.92\%$ (Multi-GIN) to $68.83\%$, and similarly, MEGA-PNA advances the performance from $66.48\%$ (Multi-PNA) to $78.26\%$. These gains of nearly $10\%$ are consistent across all the AML datasets, underscoring the power of multi-edge aggregation.

\subsection{Node Classification}

We further evaluate our proposed models on real-world financial transactions using the ETH dataset \cite{eth_kaggle}, which focuses on the identification of phishing accounts. The results, summarized in Table \ref{tab:node_results}, highlight the effectiveness of our MEGA-GNN variants. Notably, MEGA-PNA achieves state-of-the-art performance, surpassing the Multi-PNA model \cite{egressy2024provably} in terms of the minority class F1 score. 

Our method demonstrates consistent improvements over baseline models. For instance, MEGA-GIN improves the minority class F1 score by $6.11\%$ over the Multi-GIN model, while MEGA-GenAgg surpasses Multi-GenAgg by $16.52\%$. The best-performing model, MEGA-PNA, delivers the highest F1 score among all models, achieving a slight improvement over the state-of-the-art Multi-PNA.

When compared to the ADAMM (\cite{adamm}) baseline, our MEGA-GNN variants exhibit a striking improvement, consistently delivering over $20\%$ higher performance across base architectures (GIN, PNA, GenAgg). These results highlight the power of our multi-edge aggregation mechanism as well as the bi-directional message passing capabilities we contributed in Section \ref{sec:bi}. 

\subsection{Ablations and Throughput Analysis}

In Table \ref{tab:ablation}, we report the results of our ablation study on the impact of EGO-IDs (\cite{egoid2021}) and Bi-directional Message Passing across the AML Small LI, AML Small HI, and ETH datasets. EGO-IDs assign unique identifiers to root nodes in the sampled batch, while Bi-directional MP uses the formulation explained in Section \ref{sec:bi}. The unidirectional multi-edge aggregation applies only the two-stage aggregation mechanism (see Section \ref{sec:4.2}) on top of the base GNN model. 

Notably, this analysis shows that MEGA-GNN outperforms most baselines even without using bi-directional message passing and EGO-IDs. Bi-directional MP slightly improves performance for the AML datasets and significantly improves it for the ETH dataset, highlighting the benefit of this optimization for directed multigraphs. EGO-IDs do not yield consistent improvements for AML datasets but cause a performance degradation for the ETH dataset. 

Figure \ref{fig:inference} compares the inference performance of different MEGA-GNN and Multi-GNN variants in terms of the number of transactions processed per second using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU. These results show that the runtime overhead of the multi-stage aggregations performed by MEGA-GNN is minimal. However, bi-directional MP leads to a significant overhead. Interestingly, MEGA-PNA without bi-directional MP offers some favorable speed and accuracy combinations.

\section{Related Work}



The expressive power of GNNs is crucial in evaluating their capabilities, and is mainly discussed in the context of simple graphs, which can have only a single edge between two graph nodes.  \cite{xu2018gin} demonstrated that the expressive power of standard message-passing GNNs on simple graphs is fundamentally limited by the 1-WL (Weisfeiler-Lehman) test for distinguishing isomorphic graphs. To address this limitation, they introduced the Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN), which is provably the most expressive among standard message-passing GNNs. Recent research on enhancing GNN expressivity has pursued several directions. Higher-order GNNs were proposed by \cite{maron_2019,morris2019},  \cite{Bouritsas2022, Barcel21neurips}, leverage pre-computed local structural features around target nodes, while \cite{bevilacqua2022equivariant, bevilacqua2024efficient, frasca2022understanding} focus on increasing expressivity through subgraph-based aggregation techniques.

The expressiveness of GNNs is closely linked to their universality, as more expressive models can approximate a broader class of functions. \cite{sato2021random} demonstrated that incorporating unique node identifiers can make GNNs universal, though it results in the loss of permutation invariance. However, \cite{abboud2021surprisingpowergraphneural, Loukas2020What} showed that partially randomized node features can achieve universality while preserving permutation invariance. In a related line of work, set functions, such as those proposed in \cite{deep_set}, have been shown to be universal under certain input constraints, and \cite{fuchs2023universalityofneural} made the connection between universal set and graph functions. While not theoretically more expressive than GIN, \cite{pna} demonstrated that combining arbitrary aggregation functions leads to better empirical performance. In \cite{kortvelesy2023generalised}, a learnable aggregation function is proposed that improve sample efficiency compared to both PNA and DeepSet-based aggregations on multiset neighborhoods.

There has been relatively little work on GNNs for multigraphs. Two notable prior works are \cite{adamm} and \cite{egressy2024provably}. The former (ADAMM) transforms a multigraph into a simple graph using a DeepSet-based approach \cite{deep_set}, while the latter (Multi-GNN) uses simple adaptations on top of baseline GNN methods to achieve universality. However, these methods still simplify the graph structure or lose permutation equivariance.

Multi-level message aggregation schemes have been proposed also in other settings, but these methods are not directly applicable to multigraphs. For instance, Hypergraph GNNs (\cite{HyperNN_2, unignn_hyper_1}) first aggregate node features within each hyperedge to compute latent features of the hyperedges, which are then used to aggregate node features. In contrast, our multigraph GNNs directly handle multi-edges, representing multiple connections between the same pair of nodes. Furthermore, our work differs from Relational GNNs (\cite{rgcn, compgcn}), which are designed for heterogeneous graphs with different edge types. MEGA-GNNs do not require explicit edge type definitions and operate directly on edge features. 


\section{Discussion and Conclusion}


We contribute the first message-passing framework explicitly designed for multigraphs, enabling a two-stage aggregation scheme in every message-passing layer. Unlike the method of \cite{egressy2024provably}, our framework guarantees permutation equivariance without strict total ordering of the edges. Our framework also achieves universality when we make the same assumptions made by \cite{egressy2024provably}. Furthermore, unlike the method of \cite{adamm}, we do not transform a multigraph into a simple graph before the message passing layers. Thus, our message passing framework supports a more general set of multigraph learning tasks including the classification of multigraph edges.

Our experiments on financial transaction datasets show that MEGA-GNN significantly outperforms or matches the accuracy of state-of-the-art multigraph GNN models. Notably, when detecting illicit transactions on the synthetic AML datasets of \cite{altman2023realistic}, we achieve improvements of up to $13.31\%$ in minority-class F1 scores over the state-of-the-art method (\cite{egressy2024provably}), demonstrating the effectiveness of MEGA-GNN. 
When detecting phishing accounts on the Ethereum transaction dataset (\cite{eth_kaggle}), we achieve improvements of around $20\%$ in minority-class F1 scores over the method of \cite{adamm}, while slightly improving the results of \cite{egressy2024provably}.

\textbf{Limitations and Future Work}. Our work does not consider dynamically evolving multigraphs with several different snapshots. Future work could investigate strategies to support such setups. Other interesting research directions include improving the scalability and GPU performance of our methods, and building federated multigraph learning solutions with differential privacy guarantees.
