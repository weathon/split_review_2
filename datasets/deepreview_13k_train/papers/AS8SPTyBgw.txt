\title{Turing completeness of prompting}

\begin{abstract}
Since the success of GPT, large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized machine learning and have initiated the so-called *LLM prompting* paradigm. In the era of LLMs, people train a single general-purpose LLM and provide the LLM with different *prompts* to perform different tasks. However, such empirical success largely lacks theoretical understanding. Here, we present the first theoretical study on the LLM prompting paradigm to the best of our knowledge. In this work, we show that prompting is in fact Turing-complete: there exists a finite-size Transformer such that for any computable function, there exists a corresponding prompt following which the Transformer computes the function. Furthermore, we show that even though we use only a single finite-size Transformer, it can still achieve nearly the same complexity bounds as that of the class of all unbounded-size Transformers. Overall, our result reveals that prompting can enable a single finite-size Transformer to be efficiently universal, which establishes a theoretical underpinning for prompt engineering in practice.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


The mainstream architecture of large language models (LLMs; e.g., OpenAI, 2024; Anthropic, 2024; Meta, 2024; Google, 2024) is Transformers (Vaswani et al., 2017). There has been a series of theoretical studies on Transformers under realistic abstractions (P´erez et al., 2019; Bhattamishra et al., 2020; Hahn, 2020; Pe´rez et al., 2021; Hao et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023a; Chiang et al., 2023; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2023; Roberts, 2023; Merrill & Sabharwal, 2024a;b; Hou et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). For example, Pe´rez et al. (2021) have shown that the class of all Transformers with hardmax attention is Turing-complete: for any computable function $\varphi\in\mathsf{T I M E}_{1}(t(n))$ , there exists a Transformer that computes $\varphi$ using $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ chain-of-thought (CoT; Wei et al., 2022b) steps and $\mathrm{O}(\log(n+t(n)))$ precision on length- ${\cdot n}$ inputs; Merrill & Sabharwal (2024a) have later improved the CoT complexity to $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ for ${\mathsf{T I M E}}(t(n))$ functions. These works have finely characterized the capacities and limits of Transformers under the classic one-model-one-task paradigm.  

Nevertheless, existing theoretical studies fail to align with the LLM prompting practice, i.e., the one-model-many-tasks paradigm. In the era of LLMs, people train a single general-purpose LLM and provide the LLM with different prompts to perform different tasks. Since the success of GPT (Brown et al., 2020), the LLM prompting paradigm has revolutionized machine learning (Liu et al., 2023b). For example, a bonus capability arising from prompting is zero-shot learning (Wei et al., 2022a): when provided with suitable prompts, LLMs can even perform novel tasks that are not present in their training corpora. \section{1.2 TECHNICAL OVERVIEW  }


In fact, it is not hard to deduce by combining Theorem 1 of Hennie & Stearns (1966) and Theorem 3.4 of P´erez et al. (2019) that there exists an efficient Transformer that can simulate any Turing machine encoded in the prompt, but explicitly constructing this Transformer is cumbersome. Instead, this paper provides a simple constructive proof, which makes it easy to further study the properties of the construction such as CoT complexity and precision complexity.  

A core step of our constructive proof is to construct a new model of computation (called 2-PTMs) that can be easily encoded into a prompt using a finite alphabet. Furthermore, we show that 2-PTMs are not only Turing-complete but also nearly as efficient as Turing machines.  

Theorem (informal version of Theorem 4.1). Any TIME $(t(n))$ function can be computed by a 2- PTM within $\mathrm{O}(t(n)\log t(n))$ steps.  

Given any computable function $\varphi$ , we encode its 2-PTM into a prompt $\pi_{\varphi}$ . Then, it remains to construct a Transformer $\varGamma$ that can execute 2-PTMs. Since it is known that Transformers without CoTs are not universal (Hahn, 2020), the Transformer $\varGamma$ needs to use CoT steps to execute 2-PTMs. Specifically, we use CoT steps to record the execution steps of the 2-PTM so that the Transformer can restore the state of the 2-PTM at any step. This establishes the CoT complexity of $\varGamma$ .  

Corollary (informal version of Corollary 4.5). Our constructed $\varGamma$ can compute any TIME $(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(t(n)\log t(n))$ CoT steps.  

To incorporate input $\textbf{\em x}$ into computation, we use $\mathrm{O}(|{\pmb x}|)$ CoT steps to emulate an imaginary process of writing the input $\textbf{\em x}$ onto a tape of the 2-PTM. This implies the precision complexity of $\varGamma$ .  

Corollary (informal version of Corollary 4.7). Our constructed $\varGamma$ can compute any TIME $(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(\log(n+t(n)))$ ) bits of precision.  

Finally, we construct a decoder-only Transformer that achieves the desiderata above by leveraging ReLU activation, layer normalization, and causal attention.

Turing machines. Turing machines (TMs; Turing, 1937a) are an abstract model of computation that defines the notion of computability (Church, 1936; Kleene, 1936; Turing, 1937b). An $m$ -tape TM is defined by a septuple $(Q,q_{\mathrm{start}},q_{\mathrm{halt}},\varSigma,..,m,\delta)$ where $Q$ is the finite set of states, $q_{\mathrm{start}}\in Q$ is the initial state, $q_{\mathrm{halt}}$ is the halting state, $\varSigma$ is the finite alphabet of the tapes, $\rightharpoonup$ is the blank symbol, $m\,\in\,\mathbb{N}$ is the number of tapes, and $\delta:(Q\setminus\{q_{\mathrm{halt}}\})\times\Sigma^{m}\,\rightharpoonup\,\mathbf{\dot{Q}}\times(\Sigma\times\{\Sigma,\mathbf{S},\mathtt{R}\})^{m}$ is the transition function. See, e.g., Arora & Barak (2009) for details. By the Church–Turing thesis (Church, 1936; Kleene, 1936; Turing, 1937b), a function $\varphi:\,\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi\,\to\,\{0,1\}^{*}$ is said to be computable if there exists a TM that computes $\varphi({\pmb x})$ for all inputs $\pmb{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi$ .   
Shannon (1956) has shown that any TM $M$ over any alphabet can be simulated by a TM $M^{\prime}$ over the binary alphabet $\varSigma=\{0,1\}$ with $\l_{-}=0$ (although $M^{\prime}$ uses more states). Hence, we will assume that TMs have a binary alphabet for simplicity throughout this paper.   
Time complexity. Let $n$ denote the length of the input. A function $t(n):\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}$ is said to be a complexity function iff $t(n)$ is non-decreasing and either $t(n)$ is a constant $>1$ or $t(n)\to\infty$ as $n\to\infty$ . In this work, we refer to the time complexity as the the time complexity on a random-access machine (RAM) unless otherwise stated (e.g., when working with TMs). If $\Theta({\dot{t}}(n))$ is a complexity function, let $\mathsf{T I M E}_{m}(t(n))$ denote the class of functions that can be computed by an $m$ -tape TM within $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ steps, let ${\mathsf{T I M E}}(t(n)):={\mathsf{U}}_{m\geq1}{\mathsf{T I M E}}_{m}(t(n))$ denote the class of functions that can be computed by a TM within $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ steps, and let ${\mathsf{P}}\subset{\mathsf{T I M E}}({\mathrm{poly}}(n))$ denote the class of (indicator functions of) languages that can be decided in polynomial time.

ReLU neural networks. A ReLU neural network $\psi:\mathbb{R}^{e_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{e_{L}}$ is a composition $\psi:=\psi_{L-1}\circ$ $\cdot\cdot\cdot\circ\psi_{0}$ of basic operations $\psi_{l}:\mathbb{R}^{e_{l}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{e_{l+1}}$ $(l=0,\ldots,L-1)$ where each basic operation $\psi_{l}(z)$ $(z\in\mathbb{R}^{e_{l}})$ ) is either an affine map $\psi_{l}(z):=W_{l}z+b_{l}$ $\mathbf{\Delta}^{W_{l}}\in\mathbb{R}^{e_{l+1}\times e_{l}}$ , $\pmb{b}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{e_{l+1}}.$ ), an entry-wise ReLU activation $\psi_{l}(z):=\operatorname*{max}\{z,\mathbf{0}\}$ (Fukushima, 1969) with $e_{l+1}=e_{l}$ , or a layer normalization $\begin{array}{r}{\psi_{l}(z):=\frac{z}{\|z\|_{2}}1_{[z\neq\mathbf{0}]}}\end{array}$ (Ba et al., 2016) with $e_{l+1}=e_{l}$ .

Following existing theoretical works (e.g., Pe´rez et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2022; Merrill $\&$ Sabharwal, 2024a), we use hardmax attention as a realistic abstraction of softmax. Given an $\mathbb{R}$ sequence $\pmb{s}$ , if the maximum value of $\pmb{s}$ appears $t$ times, then we define $\begin{array}{r}{\mathrm{hardmax}_{i}(\pmb{s})\ :=\ \frac{1}{t}}\end{array}$ if $s_{i}$ equals the maximum value and $\mathrm{\hard{max}}_{i}(s)\,:=\,0$ otherwise. Each attention head $k$ has a query map $\mathrm{qry}_{l,k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{l,k}}$ , a key map $\mathrm{key}_{l,k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{l,k}}$ , a value map $\mathrm{val}_{l,k}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , and a similarity map $\dim_{l,k}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ $(k=0,\ldots,H_{l}-1)$ , all of which are ReLU neural networks. Given a Transformer $\varGamma$ , let generate $\L_{r}:\Sigma^{+}\to\Sigma^{+}\cup\Sigma^{\omega}$ denote autoregressive generation using $\varGamma$ . \section{3 TURING COMPLETENESS OF PROMPTING  }


Theorem 3.1 (Turing completeness of prompting). There exist a finite alphabet $\varSigma$ , a finite-size decoder-only Transformer ${\mathbf{\bar{\alpha}}}^{*}:\Sigma^{+}\ \rightarrow{\mathbf{\bar{\alpha}}}^{*}\Sigma$ , and coding schemes tokenize : $\{0,1\}^{*}\ \to\ \Sigma^{*}$ and readout : $\Sigma^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{*}$ with which prompting is Turing-complete: for every computable function $\varphi:\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi\to\{0,\bar{1}\}^{*}$ with $\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi\subseteq\{0,1\}^{*}$ , there exists a prompt $\pi_{\varphi}\in\Sigma^{+}$ such that for every input $\pmb{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi,$ , generateΓ $\lnot({\pmb\pi}_{\varphi}\cdot\mathrm{tokenize}({\pmb x}))$ computes1a finite CoT, and  

$$
\mathrm{readout}(\mathrm{generate}_{T}(\pmb{\pi}_{\varphi}\cdot\mathrm{tokenize}(\pmb{x})))=\varphi(\pmb{x}).
$$  

Here, $\Sigma,T,$ , tokenize, readout are independent of $\varphi$ ; $\pi_{\varphi}$ is independent of $\textbf{\em x}$ ; for any input $_x\in$ $\{0,1\}^{*}$ , tokenize and readout run in $\mathrm{O}(|\pmb{x}|)$ and $\mathrm{O}(|\dot{\varphi}({\pmb x})|)$ time on a random access machine (RAM), respectively.  

Our Theorem 3.1 shows that prompting can enable a single Transformer to be universal and establishes a theoretical underpinning for prompt engineering in practice. Note that CoT is necessary for Turing completeness because it is known that Transformers without CoTs cannot even compute the parity function (Hahn, 2020). As a CoT typically contains more information than the answer $\varphi(x)$ alone, we need a map readout : $\Sigma^{*}\to\{0,1\}^{*}$ here to extract the answer, resembling the fact that humans need to read out the answer from the generated CoT.  

Furthermore, to elucidate the technical non-triviality of our Theorem 3.1, we remark that our Theorem 3.1 has ruled out trivial possibilities:  

• Memorization? It is impossible that the Transformer $\varGamma$ simply memorizes all computable functions, because there are infinitely many computable functions while $\varGamma$ has only a finite size (i.e., it has a finite number of finite-bit parameters).   
• Self-answering? It is impossible that the prompt $\pi_{\varphi}$ simply reveals the answers for all possible inputs, because there can be infinitely many inputs while the prompt is finite.   
• Tautology? It is impossible that $\varGamma$ simply restates $\pmb{\pi}_{\varphi}\cdot\mathrm{tokenize}(\pmb{x})$ and lets tokenize or readout compute $\varphi({\pmb x})$ instead, because the time hierarchy theorem (Hartmanis & Stearns, 1965) implies that a computable function $\varphi$ in general can require more than $\Omega(\operatorname*{max}\{|x|,|\varphi(x)|\})$ time while tokenize and readout run in only $\bar{\mathrm{O}}(|\pmb{x}|)$ time and $\mathrm{O}(|\varphi({\pmb x})|)$ time, respectively.  

Our proof of Theorem 3.1 is constructive. In the rest of this section, we will present the core constructions in our proof, including the prompt, the CoT steps, the input tokenizer, and the Transformer.  



\section{3.1 CONSTRUCTION OF PROMPTS  }


In this subsection, we show how to construct a prompt $\pi_{\varphi}$ for any given computable function $\varphi$ . The basic idea here is that we let the prompt encode a formal description of the function $\varphi$ and later construct a Transformer that can execute the description. The construction of the Transformer is deferred to Section 3.4.  

As computability means that there exists a TM that computes $\varphi$ , one might seek to encode the TM into the prompt. Unfortunately, a TM can have an unbounded number of states and an unbounded number of tapes, but we are only allowed to construct all prompts using a single finite alphabet $\varSigma$ and to execute all prompts using a single finite-size Transformer. Even though one can use sophisticated schemes to encode TMs (Turing, 1937a), it remains highly non-trivial to construct a Transformer to efficiently execute such encoded TMs.  

Hence, instead of working with TMs directly, here we want a model of computation that (i) can be easily encoded by a single finite alphabet, that (ii) is still Turing-complete, and that (iii) is nearly as efficient as TMs. Although a possible approach is to use an imperative model of computation such as Wang machines (Wang, 1957) and Davis–Sigal–Weyuker’s Post–Turing machines (DSWPTMs; Davis et al., 1994), it is still unknown how to efficiently simulate arbitrary TMs using Wang machines or Davis–Sigal–Weyuker’s Post–Turing machines. This suggests that these models of computation might not be the best candidate for constructing the prompt.  

To fulfill our desiderata, we propose a new imperative model of computation that extends Wang machines and DSW-PTMs. Inspired by the Hennie–Stearns theorem (Hennie & Stearns, 1966), we let our model of computation use two bi-infinite tapes A and B. Each tape has infinitely many cells over the binary alphabet $\{0,1\}$ and a head pointing to a cell. We call this model two-tape Post–Turing machines (2-PTMs). A 2-PTM is defined by a finite instruction sequence $\pmb{\iota}=\,\langle\iota_{0},\iota_{1},\ldots,\iota_{|\pmb{\iota}|-1}\rangle$ where each instruction $\iota_{j}$ $\mathbf{\Psi}^{\left(0\leq j<\left|\mathfrak{t}\right|\right)}$ is one of the following:  

• #: halt;   
• $\tau\mathtt{L}\left(\tau\in\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\}\right)$ : move the head for tape $\tau$ one cell left, and go to $\iota_{j+1}$ ;   
• $\tau\mathtt{R}\left(\tau\in\left\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\right\}\right)$ : move the head for tape $\tau$ one cell right, and go to $\iota_{j+1}$ ;   
• $\tau0\,(\tau\in\{\tt A,\tt B\})$ : write 0 to the pointed cell of tape $\tau$ , and go to $\iota_{j+1}$ ;   
• $\tau{1}$ $(\tau\in\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\})$ : write 1 to the pointed cell of tape $\tau$ , and go to $\iota_{j+1}$ ;   
$\mathrm{~\boldmath~\cdot~}\,\tau:_{k}(\tau\in\{{\sf A},{\sf B}\};k\neq j)$ : if the pointed cell of tape $\tau$ is 0, go to $\iota_{k}$ ; else, go to $\iota_{j+1}$ ;   
• $\tau_{:\,k}\,(\tau\in\{\mathtt{A},\mathtt{B}\};k\neq j)$ : if the pointed cell of tape $\tau$ is 1, go to $\iota_{k}$ ; else, go to $\iota_{j+1}$ .  

Let $\mathcal{T}$ denote the set of all possible instructions. Before execution, the input is written to tape $\mathtt{A}$ , and the head for tape A is pointing to the leftmost cell of the input. All blank tape cells are filled with 0. Then, execution starts from instruction $\iota_{0}$ and halts upon instruction $\#$ , and the output is the content left on tape A starting from cell 0. We will show in Section 4.1 that 2-PTMs are not only Turing-complete but also nearly as efficient as TMs.  

Next, we describe how to construct a prompt for a given computable function. Recall that 2-PTMs use the binary alphabet $\{0,1\}$ with blank symbol being 0. To distinguish the input symbol 0 and the blank symbol 0, we employ Shannon’s encoding $\bar{S}:\{0,1\}^{*}\,\rightarrow\,\{0,1\}^{*}$ (Shannon, 1956) to translate inputs and outputs of computable functions:  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{S(\epsilon):=\epsilon,\quad S(0):=10,\quad S(1):=11;}\\ &{S({\pmb x}):=S({\pmb x}_{0})\cdot\cdot\cdot S({\pmb x}_{|{\pmb x}|-1}),\quad{\pmb x}\in\{0,1\}^{*}.}\end{array}
$$  

Thus, we identify 00 as the blank symbol. Note that Shannon’s encoding $S$ is injective, and that both $S$ and its corresponding decoding $\zeta^{-1}$ are computable in linear time. Since the class of 2-PTMs is Turing-complete, then given any computable function $\varphi$ , there exists a 2-PTM $\pmb{\iota}\in\mathcal{T}^{+}$ that computes $S(\varphi(x))$ from $S(x)$ for all $\pmb{x}\in\mathrm{dom}\,\varphi$ . It remains to encode $\pmb{\iota}$ into a prompt $\pi_{\varphi}$ .  

We will define a map $P:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\Sigma^{*}$ to encode each instruction $\iota_{j}$ and let the prompt be the concatenation of $P(j,\iota_{j})$ , where the alphabet $\varSigma$ will be specified later. For instructions $\#,\,\tau\mathbb{L},\,\tau\mathbb{R},$ , $\tau0,\tau1$ , we can simply create a corresponding token in $\varSigma$ for each of them:  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{P(j,\iota_{j}):=\iota_{j}\qquad\mathrm{if}\,\iota_{j}\ \mathrm{is~one~of}\ \#,\tau\mathrm{L},\tau\mathrm{R},\tau0,\tau1.}\end{array}
$$  

For instructions $\tau:k$ and $\tau?k$ , since $k$ can be any natural number, we can no longer create a token for each $k$ because otherwise the alphabet $\varSigma$ would be infinite. Instead, to help the Transformer to execute the prompt, we use a unary encoding w.r.t $k-j$ :  

$$
P(j,\iota_{j}):=\left\{\!\!\begin{array}{l l}{\tau\!:\cdot-j\!-\!k\,\cdot\!\,\emptyset}&{\mathrm{if}\,\iota_{j}=\tau\!:_{k}\mathrm{~and~}k<j;}\\ {\tau\!:\cdot+\!^{k-j}\cdot\emptyset}&{\mathrm{if}\,\iota_{j}=\tau\!:_{k}\mathrm{~and~}k>j;}\\ {\tau\?\cdot-j\!-\!k\,\cdot\!\,\emptyset}&{\mathrm{if}\,\iota_{j}=\tau\?_{k}\mathrm{~and~}k<j;}\\ {\tau\?\cdot+\!^{k-j}\cdot\emptyset}&{\mathrm{if}\,\iota_{j}=\tau\?_{k}\mathrm{~and~}k>j;}\end{array}\right.
$$  

where we create seven auxiliary tokens $\mathtt{A!,B!,A?,B?,-,+,Q}\in\mathcal{D}$ to be used by the Transformer. Finally, we construct the prompt as  

$$
\pi_{\varphi}:=\,\widehat{\mathrm{\boldmath~\cdot~}}\,P(0,\iota_{0})\cdot\cdot\cdot P(|\iota|-1,\iota_{|\iota|-1})\cdot\widehat{\mathrm{\boldmath~\mathscr~}},
$$  

where we create two auxiliary tokens ˆ, $\varsigma\in\Sigma$ to be used by the Transformer.

3.2 RECORDING EXECUTION IN COT STEPS  

It is known that finite-size Transformers without CoT steps are not universal (Hahn, 2020). To achieve Turing completeness, here we leverage CoT steps to record execution steps of the 2-PTM $\pmb{\iota}$ so that the Transformer can restore the state of $\pmb{\iota}$ at any execution step. In this subsection, we focus on the case where the input is empty; we will describe how to incorporate the input in Section 3.3.  

Let $\iota_{j}$ denote the current instruction, and let $\mathcal{C}_{\sf A},\mathcal{C}_{\sf B}$ denote the pointed cell of tapes A and B, respectively. Note that each execution step can be summarized as a quadruple $(j,\iota_{j},c_{\mathtt{A}},c_{\mathtt{B}})$ , where $\iota_{j}$ is the current instruction, and $c_{\tt A}$ and $c_{\mathrm{B}}$ are the currently pointed cell of tapes A and B, respectively. We will define a map $C:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\times\{0,1\}^{2}\to\Sigma^{*}$ that maps each execution step to one or more CoT steps. If $\iota_{j}$ is one of $\#,\tau\mathtt{L},\tau\mathtt{R},\tau0,\tau1$ , we simply use a single CoT step to record $\iota_{j}$ :  

$$
C(j,\iota_{j},c_{\mathtt{A}},c_{\mathtt{B}}):=\iota_{j}\qquad\mathrm{if~}\iota_{j}\mathrm{~is~one~of~}\#,\tau\mathrm{L},\tau\mathrm{R},\tau0,\tau1.
$$  

If $\iota_{j}$ is $\tau:k$ or $\tau?k$ , we record whether the go-to condition is satisfied or not:  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{C(j,\iota_{j},c_{\mathsf{A}},c_{\mathsf{B}}):=\left\{\begin{array}{l l}{\big/}&{\mathrm{if}\;\iota_{j}=\tau\,;_{k}\mathrm{~and~}c_{\tau}\ne0;}\\ {=\cdot\,^{j-k}\cdot\mathsf{\Delta}(\mathsf{i f}\;\iota_{j}=\tau\,;_{k},\;c_{\tau}=0,\mathrm{~and~}k<j;}\\ {=\cdot\,^{k-j}\cdot\mathsf{\Delta}(\mathsf{i f}\;\iota_{j}=\tau\,;_{k},\;c_{\tau}=0,\;\mathrm{and~}k>j;}\\ {\big/}&{\mathrm{if}\;\iota_{j}=\tau\,^{2}\,k\mathrm{~and~}c_{\tau}\ne1;}\\ {=\cdot\,^{j-k}\cdot\mathsf{\Delta}(\mathsf{i f}\;\iota_{j}=\tau\,^{2}\,k,\;c_{\tau}=1,\;\mathrm{and~}k<j;}\\ {=\cdot\,^{k-j}\cdot\mathsf{\Delta}(\mathsf{i f}\;\iota_{j}=\tau\,^{2}\,k,\;c_{\tau}=1,\;\mathrm{and~}k>j;}\end{array}\right.}\end{array}
$$  

where we create two auxiliary tokens $/,\in\Sigma$ to indicate whether the go-to condition is unsatisfied or satisfied, respectively. The execution step stops once it reaches the halting instruction $\#$ .  

Finally, we append the output $\varphi({\pmb x})$ after the execution steps in the CoT. We create a new auxiliary token : $\in\varSigma$ to mark the beginning of the output and reuse the token $\S\in\Sigma$ to mark the end of the output, and we put the output $\varphi(\pmb{x})\in\{0,1\}^{*}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}$ between : and $\mathfrak{S}$ . Hence, we define readout as extracting the part of the CoT between : and $\mathfrak{S}$ (see Algorithm 2). By construction, the number of CoT steps is proportional to the number of execution steps plus the length of the output.

3.3 CONSTRUCTION OF INPUT TOKENIZER  

In this subsection, we describe our input tokenizer tokenize : $\{0,1\}^{*}\to\Sigma^{*}$ , which is independent of the function $\varphi$ and can encode any input $\mathbf{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\{0,1\}^{*}$ without changing the prompt $\pi_{\varphi}$ . Although it is possible to introduce additional auxiliary tokens to represent inputs, here we provide a simpler construction that makes use of only the existing tokens in $\varSigma$ .  

The key idea here is to use CoT steps to emulate an imaginary process of writing the input to tape A. We will define a map $E:\{0,1\}^{\bar{+}}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}$ that encodes an input $\textbf{\em x}$ into $\mathrm{CoT}$ steps. Since 2-PTMs assume that the head for tape A is initially pointing to the leftmost cell of the input, we write the input from right to left onto tape A. Hence, for each bit $x_{i}$ of the input $\textbf{\em x}$ , we write its Shannon encoding $\bar{S(x_{i})}$ from right to left onto tape A:  

$$
E(0):={\tt A L A L A1},\qquad E(1):={\tt A L A1A L A1}.
$$  

For the entire input, we concatenate the CoT steps of each bit from right to left:  

$$
E(\pmb{x}):=E(\pmb{x}_{|\pmb{x}|-1})\cdot\cdot\cdot E(\pmb{x}_{0}),\qquad\pmb{x}\in\{0,1\}^{+}.
$$  

Intuitively, $E(x)$ represents execution steps of an imaginary program that writes $S(x)$ onto tape A.  

To ensure that the tape-A head is at cell 0 after writing the input, we first move the tape-A head $|S({\pmb x})|=2|{\pmb x}|$ steps right. Hence, we define the CoT steps for writing $S(x)$ as $Z:\{0,1\}^{\bar{+}}\rightarrow\Sigma^{+}$ ,  

$$
Z(x):={\tt A R}^{2|x|}\cdot E({\pmb x}),\qquad{\pmb x}\in\{0,1\}^{+}.
$$  

Nevertheless, there is a caveat: a 2-PTM should start from $\iota_{0}$ , but these extra $\mathrm{CoT}$ steps $Z(x)$ will confuse the Transformer into starting from $\iota|Z(\pmb{x})|$ . To address this caveat, our input tokenizer tokenize additionally employs an imaginary go-to step to let the Transformer go back to $\iota_{0}$ . Following Equation (12), we re-use $=,-,\,\Theta$ to construct the imaginary go-to step:  

$$
\mathrm{tokenize}(\pmb{x}):=\left\{\!\!\begin{array}{l l}{{\epsilon}}&{{\mathrm{if~}\pmb{x}=\epsilon,}}\\ {{Z(\pmb{x})\cdot=\cdot\,-|Z(\pmb{x})|\,\cdot\,\upbeta}}&{{\mathrm{if~}\pmb{x}\neq\epsilon.}}\end{array}\right.
$$

3.4 CONSTRUCTION OF TRANSFORMER  

In this subsection, we sketch how to construct a decoder-only Transformer $\varGamma$ that executes prompts through CoT steps as described in Section 3.2. This means that we need to execute 2-PTMs using ReLU activation, layer normalization, and causal attention.  

Boolean algebra via ReLU activation. Let $\mathrm{ReLU}(z):=\operatorname*{max}\{z,0\}$ denote the ReLU function. Boolean algebra is a basic building block of our Transformer for, e.g., checking the go-to condition. A core operation in Boolean algebra is the $\wedge$ operation. Here, we implement $\wedge$ via ReLU activation:  

$$
u\wedge v=\operatorname{ReLU}(u+v-1),\qquad u,v\in\{0,1\}.
$$  

Together with negation $\lnot v\,=\,1\,-\,v$ , they can implement all other Boolean operations such as $\u\mathbin{\bar{\vee}}v=\neg((\neg u)\mathbin{\bar{\wedge}}(\neg v))$ and $u\oplus v=(u\land(\neg v))+((\neg u)\land v)$ .  

Equality check via layer normalization. Let $\begin{array}{r}{\mathrm{LN}(z):=\frac{z}{\|z\|_{2}}1_{[z\neq\mathbf{0}]}}\end{array}$ denote layer normalization (LN). When checking whether a tape cell has been written or not, we will need an equality check between two real numbers: Equal : $\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\{0,1\}$ where $\operatorname{Equal}(u,v):=1_{[u=v]}$ $(u,v\in\mathbb{R})$ . Since Equal is not a continuous map, it cannot be implemented using only affine maps or ReLU activation. Instead, we implement Equal via layer normalization as follows:  

$$
\mathrm{Equal}(u,v):=\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{LN}(u-v))+\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{LN}(v-u)),\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{R}.
$$  

Farthest retrieval via causal attention. A core operation to be used by the Transformer $\varGamma$ can be abstracted as follows: Given a sequence of values $\pmb{v}=\langle v_{0},\dots,v_{|v|-1}\rangle$ with $v_{i}\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ for all $i$ , find the smallest $i$ such that $\begin{array}{r}{\sum_{j=0}^{i}v_{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{|v|-1}v_{j}}\end{array}$ . 4 COMPLEXITY BOUNDS  

We (i) show in Section 4.1 that 2-PTMs are Turing-complete and nearly as efficient as TMs and (ii) use this result to characterize the complexities of our constructed $\varGamma$ in Sections $4.2\:\&\:4.3$ . Throughout this section, let $t(n)$ denote a complexity function. Following the convention in complexity theory, we allow different computable functions to have different constant factors in big O.  

4.1 EFFICIENT SIMULATION OF TMS BY 2-PTMS  

Since 2-PTMs are an essential component of our construction, we need the complexity bounds of 2-PTMs to analyze the complexities of $\varGamma$ . While Wang machines and DSW-PTMs suffer from a polynomial slowdown over TMs (Neary et al., 2014), we show that our 2-PTMs in fact has only at most a logarithmic slowdown over TMs.  

Theorem 4.1 (efficient simulation). T $\mathsf{I M E}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{T I M E}_{2\mathrm{-}\mathrm{PTM}}(t(n)\log t(n)).$  

Theorem 4.1 shows that our 2-PTMs are Turing-complete and nearly as efficient as TMs. To prove it, we need the following Lemma 4.2 to establish an equivalence between two-tape TMs and 2-PTMs. Lemma 4.2 (two-tape simulation). $\mathsf{T I M E}_{2}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{T I M E}_{2\mathrm{-}\mathrm{PTM}}(t(n)).$ .  

Proof sketch of Lemma 4.2. For any computable function $\varphi\in\mathsf{T I M E}_{2}(t(n))$ , there exists a two-tape TM ${\cal M}\,=\,(Q,q_{\mathrm{start}},q_{\mathrm{halt}},\{0,1\},\ldots=0,m=2,\delta)$ that computes $S(\varphi(x))$ from $S(x)$ within time $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ , according to Shannon (1956). Suppose w.l.o.g. that $Q\,=\,\{0,1,\ldots,K\}$ $\mathbb{\left[K\right.}\in\mathbb{N}_{+})$ ) and that $q_{\mathrm{start}}=0$ , $q_{\mathrm{halt}}=K$ . We will construct a 2-PTM $\pmb{\iota}$ of length $|\pmb{\iota}|=27K+1$ that simulates $M$ within time $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ .  

Recall that $\delta:(Q\setminus\{q_{\mathrm{halt}}\})\times\{0,1\}^{2}\to Q\times(\{0,1\}\times\{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{S},\mathrm{R}\})^{2},$ . We use exactly six instructions $\pmb{\eta}_{(q^{\prime},c_{\tt A},d_{\tt A},c_{\tt B},d_{\tt B})}$ to simulate each transition $(q^{\prime},c_{\tt A}^{\prime},d_{\tt A},c_{\tt B}^{\prime},d_{\tt B})$ , where $q^{\prime}\in Q,c_{\tt A}^{\prime},c_{\tt B}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\},d_{\tt A},d_{\tt B}\in$ $\{\mathtt{L},\mathtt{S},\mathtt{R}\}$ . If $d_{\tt A},d_{\tt B}\neq{\bf S}$ , we let  

$$
\eta_{(q^{\prime},c_{{\tt A}}^{\prime},d_{{\tt A}},c_{{\tt B}}^{\prime},d_{{\tt B}})}:=\langle\mathtt{A}c_{\mathtt{A}}^{\prime},\mathtt{A}d_{\mathtt{A}},\mathtt{B}c_{\mathtt{B}}^{\prime},\mathtt{B}d_{\mathtt{B}},\mathtt{A}\,!_{\mathrm{~27}q^{\prime}},\mathtt{A}\,\mathtt{2}_{\mathtt{27}q^{\prime}}\rangle.
$$  

If $d_{\tt A}=\tt S$ or $d_{\tt B}={\tt S}$ , we can replace the corresponding $\mathtt{A d_{A}}$ or $\mathtt{B d_{B}}$ with $\mathtt{A c}_{\mathtt{A}}^{\prime}$ or $\mathsf{B}c_{\mathrm{B}}^{\prime}$ , respectively, so η(q′,c′A,dA,c′B,dB) still has exactly six instructions.  

Then for each $q\in Q\setminus\{q_{\mathrm{halt}}\}$ , we use 27 instructions to simulate its transition rules:  

$$
\iota_{27q;27q+27}:=\langle\mathtt{A}\,?_{27q+14},\mathtt{B}\,?_{27q+8},\eta_{\delta(q,0,0)},\eta_{\delta(q,0,1)},\mathtt{B}\,?_{27q+21},\eta_{\delta(q,1,0)},\eta_{\delta(q,1,1)}\rangle.
$$  

For the halting state $q_{\mathrm{halt}}=K$ , we use one instruction $\iota_{27K}:=\#$ to simulate it.  

Since $\pmb{\iota}$ simulates each transition of $M$ by $\mathrm{O}(1)$ steps, then $\pmb{\iota}$ also has time complexity $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ .  

  

We are now ready to prove Theorem 4.1.  

Proof sketch of Theorem 4.1. Let $\varphi\,\in\,\mathsf{T I M E}(t(n))$ . Then, there exists a TM $M$ that computes $\varphi$ within $T(n)\stackrel{{}}{=}\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ steps for every length- ${\mathbf{\nabla}}n$ input.  

Case $^{\,l}$ : There exists $n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}$ such that $T(n_{0})<n_{0}$ . Thus, the behavior of the TM $M$ depends only on the first $T(n_{0})<n_{0}$ bits of the input. Hence, further increasing the length of the input does not change the behavior of $M$ . Therefore, a tighter time complexity $\tilde{T}(n)$ of $M$ is  

$$
{\tilde{T}}(n)\leq T(n_{0})=\mathrm{O}(1),\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.
$$  

This implies that $\varphi\in{\mathsf{T I M E}}_{2}(1)\subseteq{\mathsf{T I M E}}_{2\cdot{\mathrm{PTM}}}(1)\subseteq{\mathsf{T I M E}}_{2\cdot{\mathrm{PTM}}}(t(n)\log t(n)).$  

Case 2: $T(n)\geq n$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$ . Then by the Hennie–Stearns theorem (Hennie & Stearns, 1966), $\varphi\in{\mathsf{T I M E}}_{2}(T(n)\log T(n))$ . Therefore, by Lemma 4.2,  

$$
\varphi\in{\mathsf{T I M E}}_{2}(t(n)\log t(n))\subseteq{\mathsf{T I M E}}_{2\mathrm{-}\mathrm{PIM}}(t(n)\log t(n)).
$$  

It follows from the above two cases that $\mathsf{I I M E}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{T I M E}_{2\mathrm{-}\mathrm{PTM}}(t(n)\log t(n)).$  

Theorem 4.1 shows that 2-PTMs have only at most a logarithmic slowdown over TMs. In subsequent Sections $4.2\ \&\ 4.3$ , we will use Theorem 4.1 to characterize the CoT complexity and the precision complexity of our constructed $\varGamma$ .  

4.2 COT COMPLEXITY  

In this subsection, we analyze the CoT complexity of our construction. We will show that our constructed $\varGamma$ can compute any $\mathsf{T I M E}_{2}(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ CoT steps and any TIME $(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(t(n)\log t(n))$ CoT steps for any length- $n$ input.  

Definition 4.3 (CoT complexity class). Let ${\mathsf{C o T}}_{\cal{T}}(t(n))$ be the class of functions that our constructed Transformer $\varGamma$ can compute within $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ CoT steps.  

Lemma 4.4 (CoT complexity for 2-PTMs). $\mathsf{T I M E}_{\mathrm{2-PTM}}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{C o T}_{\varGamma}(t(n)).$  

Proof sketch. For any $\varphi\,\in\,\mathsf{T I M E}_{2\mathrm{-PTM}}(t(n))$ , since prompt $\pi_{\varphi}$ has length $|\pi_{\varphi}|\,=\,\mathrm{O}(1)$ , then by Section 3.2, each $\#$ $^{\ddagger},\,\tau{<},\,\tau{>},\,\tau0,\,\tau1$ takes $\overset{\cdot}{1}=\mathrm{O}(1)$ CoT step, and each $\tau:k,\,\tau\,?_{k}$ takes at most $\mathrm{O}(|\pmb{\pi}_{\varphi}|)=\mathrm{O}(1)$ CoT steps. This implies that T ${\mathsf{I M E}}_{2\mathrm{-PTM}}(t(n))\subseteq{\mathsf{C o T}}_{\varGamma}(t(n))$ . 口  

Corollary 4.5 (CoT complexity for TMs). For two-tape TMs, ${\mathsf{T I M E}}_{2}(t(n))\,\subseteq\,{\mathsf{C o T}}_{\cal T}(t(n))$ . For general TMs, TI $\mathsf{M E}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{C o T}_{\varGamma}(t(n)\log t(n))$ .  

Proof. For two-tape TMs, by Lemmas 4.2 & 4.4,  

$$
\mathsf{T I M E}_{2}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{T I M E}_{2\mathrm{-}\mathrm{PTM}}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{C o T}_{T}(t(n)).
$$  

For general TMs, by Theorem 4.1 & Lemma 4.4,  

$$
\mathsf{T I M E}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{T I M E}_{2\cdot\mathrm{PTM}}(t(n)\log t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{C o T}_{T}(t(n)\log t(n)).
$$  

Corollary 4.5 shows prompting a single Transformer can compute any $\mathsf{T I M E}_{2}(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ CoT steps and any $\mathsf{T i M E}(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(t(n)\log t(n))$ CoT steps. Notably, this is nearly the same as the CoT complexity of the class of all Transformers, which can compute any $\mathsf{T I M E}(\dot{t}(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(t(\bar{n}))$ CoT steps. The logarithmic slowdown here is because the class of all Transformers can simulate an unbounded number of tapes while our single Transformer simulates only a finite number of tapes. Assuming $\mathsf{T I M E}_{2}(t(n))\neq\mathsf{T I M E}(t(n))$ , it is unlikely that the CoT complexity $\mathrm{O}(t(n)\log t(n))$ of $\varGamma$ for TIME $(t(n))$ could be further improved to $\mathrm{O}(t(n))$ .  

4.3 PRECISION COMPLEXITY  

In this subsection, we analyze the precision complexity of our construction. We will show that our constructed $\varGamma$ can compute any $\mathsf{T I M E}(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(\log(n+t(n)))$ bits of precision for any length- ${\mathbf{\nabla}}n$ input; in particular, it can decide any $\mathsf{P}$ language within log-precision. Following the common practice in numerical analysis, we assume that each floating-point number has significant bits and guard bits (Goodman & Feldstein, 1977), where both significant bits and guard bits are used in arithmetic operations while guard bits are rounded off in number comparisons.  

Definition 4.6 (Precision complexity class). For a complexity function $p(n)$ , let ${\mathsf{P R E C}}_{\varGamma}(p(n))$ be the class of functions that our constructed $\varGamma$ can compute using ${\mathrm{O}}(p(n))$ significant and guard bits.  

Proof sketch. For any function $\varphi\,\in\,{\mathsf{C o T}}_{\varGamma}(t(n)\log t(n))$ , since the prompt $\pi_{\varphi}$ has length O(1), then by Section 3.3, the total length $I$ of the tokenized input, the prompt, and the CoT steps is  

$$
I=\operatorname{O}(n)+\operatorname{O}(1)+\operatorname{O}(t(n)\log t(n))=\operatorname{O}(n+t(n)\log t(n)).
$$  

Thus, according to Section 3.4, all the intermediate results during computation are at most $\mathrm{O}(1)$ , and attention similarities have mutual differences at least $\begin{array}{r}{\Omega\bigl(\frac{1}{I^{5}}\bigr)=\bar{\Omega}\Bigl(\frac{1}{(n+t(n)\log t(n))^{5}}\Bigr)}\end{array}$ . This implies  

$$
\mathsf{C o T}_{T}(t(n)\log t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{P R E C}_{T}(\log((n+t(n)\log t(n))^{5}))=\mathsf{P R E C}_{T}(\log(n+t(n))).
$$  

It follows from Corollary 4.5 and Equation (30) that  

$$
\mathsf{T I M E}(t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{C o T}_{T}(t(n)\log t(n))\subseteq\mathsf{P R E C}_{T}(\log(n+t(n))).
$$  

In particular, we have  

$$
\mathsf{P}\subset\mathsf{T I M E}(\mathrm{poly}(n))\subseteq\mathsf{P R E C}_{T}(\log(n+\mathrm{poly}(n)))=\mathsf{P R E C}_{T}(\log n).
$$  

Notably, Corollary 4.7 shows that prompting a single Transformer can achieve the same precision complexity as that of the class of all Transformers: it is known that the class of all Transformers can compute any ${\mathsf{T I M E}}(t(n))$ function within $\mathrm{O}(\log(n+t(n)))$ precision (Pe´rez et al., 2021) while we further show a single Transformer with prompting can as well. This suggests our precision complexity is presumably tight unless there are further advances in the complexity theory of Transformers.  



\section{5 CONCLUSION  }


In this work, we have shown that prompting is in fact Turing-complete: there exists a finite-size Transformer such that for any computable function, there exists a corresponding prompt following which the Transformer computes the function. Furthermore, we have shown that even though we use only a single finite-size Transformer, it can still achieve nearly the same complexity bounds as that of the class of all unbounded-size Transformers. Overall, our result reveals that prompting can enable a single finite-size Transformer to be efficiently universal, which establishes a theoretical underpinning for prompt engineering in practice.
