\title{Evaluating Deep Unlearning in Large Language Models}

\begin{abstract}
Machine unlearning is a key requirement of many data protection regulations such as GDPR. Prior work on unlearning has mostly considered superficial unlearning tasks where a single or a few related pieces of information are required to be removed. 
However, the task of unlearning a fact is much more challenging in recent large language models (LLMs), 
because the facts in LLMs can be deduced from each other. 
In this work, we investigate whether current unlearning methods for LLMs succeed beyond superficial unlearning of facts. 
Specifically, we formally propose a new setting of unlearning, \textit{\problemname}, which considers the logical deductions between facts. 
Moreover, we design the metric, \textit{recall}, to quantify the extent of \problemname.
To systematically evaluate \problemname, we construct a synthetic dataset \dataname, which consists of a synthetic knowledge base of family relationships and biographies, together with a realistic logical rule set that connects them. 
We use this dataset to test four unlearning methods in four LLMs at different sizes. 
Our findings reveal that in the task of \problemname\ only a single fact, they either fail to properly unlearn with high recall, or end up unlearning many other irrelevant facts. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



Large foundation models of today are trained on massive amounts of uncurated data obtained from the internet. 
Machine unlearning has emerged as a solution for removing specific data, concepts, or facts from these models in a more efficient way than retraining from scratch.
It is practical in situations where regulations, such as the GDPR~\citep{gdpr}, mandate the removal of a user's data. 
Moreover, unlearning is important when models inadvertently capture copyrighted\footnote{https://casetext.
For large language models (LLM) in particular, the ability to unlearn specific facts becomes especially important to ensure compliance with copyright laws and privacy requirements~\citep{jang2022knowledge, liu2024rethinking}

Prior work in unlearning in large language models have looked at this problem, but for the target fact, the focus has been on removing the target itself. %focuses on whether the target knowledge itself has been removed.
However, this can be superficial -- LLMs not only know single facts in isolation, but many connected facts -- and very often, the fact that has been unlearnt can be deduced from facts that are already known by the model. Thus, successful unlearning in this setting should also remove other facts that imply the fact to be unlearnt. As a concrete example, consider Figure~\ref{fig:intro}. Here, the target fact \textit{``Camila Flores's child is Wyatt Ross"} can be deduced from fact A \textit{``Wyatt Ross's father is Xavier Ross"} and fact B \textit{``Camila Flores's husband is Xavier Ross"}.
If the LLM only unlearns the target fact but retains A and B, this is insufficient as an adversary who extracts A and B  from the LLM can deduce the target fact.

In this work, we consider a new setting for unlearning, which we call \textit{\problemname}, and investigate to what extent current unlearning methods succeed at this setting. \problemnamecap\ is formulated by stating a set of facts and logical rules that connect the facts. 
The fact is \problemnamepassive\ if the target fact cannot be deduced from the retained facts in the LLM through the given logical rules.
We further propose two metrics, recall and accuracy, for evaluating unlearning methods at \problemname.
Recall measures to how well an unlearning method unlearns the relevant facts so that the target fact cannot be deduced; while accuracy measures what extent other irrelevant facts are retained by the unlearning process. 
In order to systematically evaluate \problemname, we construct a synthetic dataset \dataname\ as a benchmark. 
The dataset consists of two parts: a synthetic knowledge base and a realistic logical rule set. The knowledge base contains biographical information about a group of people (e.g., \textit{``The birthyear of Sloane Lee is 1908"}), as well as family relationships (e.g., \textit{``Wyatt Ross's father is Xavier Ross"}).
The logical rules are realistic rules describing family relationships (e.g. (X, \textit{husband}, Z)$\wedge$ (Y, \textit{father}, Z)  $\to$ (X, \textit{child}, Y)).
The unlearning method is tested on a subset of facts on family relationships together with the rules.
\begin{figure*}[!t]
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figs/example_intro}	
\caption{An example that unlearning only the target fact is insufficient. The successful extraction of \textit{``Wyatt Ross's father is Xavier Ross"} and \textit{``Camila Flores's husband is Xavier Ross"} can imply the target fact.}
\label{fig:intro}
\end{figure*}

We then use our dataset to evaluate four common unlearning methods (Gradient Ascent, Negative Preference Optimization, Task Vector, and Who's Harry Potter) on four popular LLMs (Phi-1.5, GPT2-XL, Llama2-7b, Llama3-8b).
We find that while these methods are good at unlearning the target fact itself without losing accuracy, they either fail to \problemnameactive\ with high recall or lose more than $20\%$ irrelevant facts while \problemnameing\ just single target fact.
Additionally, it is found that the unlearning methods have better performance on larger LLMs; this validates the hypothesis that more inherent understanding of facts in larger LLMs helps with \problemname\ naturally.

This illustrates that the machine unlearning methods of today are largely insufficient for properly unlearning facts from LLMs; 
either they do not unlearn what is needed to deduce a target fact, or they over-unlearn and unlearn many extraneous facts. 
We hypothesize that this might be because the existing unlearning methods do not sufficiently account for the nature of facts and the reasoning capabilities of LLMs. 
We posit that future methods that unlearn facts suitably from LLMs should be aware of these enhanced capabilities. 


\section{Preliminary}

Given a set of objects $\calO$ and relations $\calT$, a fact $k$ is represened by the triplet $(o_1, r, o_2)$ of the relation $r$ and two objects $o_1, o_2$.
For example, \textit{``Camila Flores's child is Wyatt Ross"} can be represented in $(\textit{Camila Flores}, \textit{child}, \textit{Wyatt Ross})$.
The knowledge base $\calK\subseteq \calO\times\calT\times\calO$ is a set of facts.

Facts can be deduced from each other through reasoning; an example has been shown in Figure~\ref{fig:intro}.
A popular way to formulate the reasoning among facts is through a logical rule $R$, which is widely used to discover new knowledge~\citep{galarraga2013amie,yang2017differentiable,xu2022ruleformer,cheng2023neural,luo2023chatrule}.
The rule $R$ has the form of $B_1 \wedge \cdots\wedge B_n\to A$,
where $B_1\cdots, B_n$ and $A$ are atoms and each atom is a tuple $(X, r, Y)$ of logical variables $X,Y$ and a relation $r$.
One example of rule is (X, \textit{husband}, Z)$\wedge$ (Y, \textit{father}, Z)  $\to$ (X, \textit{child}, Y).
By substituting the objects in $\calO$ to the logical variables in $B_1, \cdots, B_n, A$, facts on the left can together deduce the fact on the right.

With a set of rules $\calR$, a knowledge base $\calK$ is \textit{deductively closed}~\citep{cheney2009provenance,cohen2016tensorlog,huang2021scallop} with respect to $\calR$, if there is no new fact can be deduced from $\calK$ and $\calR$.
For example, suppose 
$(\textit{Camila Flores}, \textit{husband}, \textit{Xavier Ross}),(\textit{Xavier Ross}, \textit{child}, \textit{Wyatt Ross})\in \calK$ and (X, \textit{husband}, Z)$\wedge$ (Y, \textit{father}, Z)  $\to$ (X, \textit{child}, Y) is in the rule set $\calR$.
If $\calK$ is deductively closed with respect to the rule set including the rule set $\calR$,
$(\textit{Camila Flores}, \textit{child}, \textit{Wyatt Ross})$ must be in the knowledge base $\calK$ too.
The deductive closure is defined as follows.
\begin{definition}[Deductive closure]
	The deductive closure of $\calK$ with respective to the rule set $\calR$, notated as $\Omega(\calK, \calR)$, is the smallest set such that 1. $\calK\subseteq \Omega(\calK, \calR)$; 2. $\Omega(\calK, \calR)$ is \textit{deductively closed} with respect to a set of rules $\calR$.
\end{definition}


\section{\problemname}


Prior work in fact unlearning from LLMs focuses on simply unlearning the target fact in isolation, and not other facts that logically imply it. 
This might cause the LLM to forget only this one specific fact, but retain others that can be combined to deduce the fact in question. 
In this section, we introduce the new setting of unlearning, \problemname, which considers the logical deductions between facts.
To assess the effectiveness of unlearning methods in this setting, we propose two evaluation metrics: recall and accuracy.

\subsection{Fact \problemname\ }
\begin{figure}
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/illustration}
	\caption{An illustration of \textit{\problemname}. (a) an example of superficial unlearning; (b) an example of \problemname; (c) two different minimal \problemname\ sets for unlearning the same target fact; (d) the calculation of our proposed evaluation metric \textit{recall}.}
	\label{fig:illustration}
\end{figure}

Let $\calK$ represent the knowledge base of the LLM prior to unlearning and let $U_k^{\calA}\subseteq \calK$ denote the set of facts that has been removed by any unlearning method $\calA$ aimed at unlearning the target fact $k$.
If method $\calA$ \problemnamethird\ the fact $k$, it is expected that the fact $k$ should not be deduced from the retained facts $\calK\backslash U_k^{\calA}$ by the rule set $\calR$, i.e. $k$ should not be in the deductive closure $\Omega(\calK\backslash U_k^{\calA}, \calR)$.
\begin{definition}[\problemnamecap]
	The unlearning method $\calA$ \problemnamethird\ the fact $k$ with respect to the rule set $\calR$ if the fact $k$ does not belong in the deductive closure $\Omega(\calK\backslash U_k^{\calA}, \calR)$.
\end{definition}
We call the unlearning, which successfully unlearns the target fact but does not satisfy \problemname, as \textit{superficial} unlearning; we show an example in Figure~\ref{fig:illustration}(a).
Figure~\ref{fig:illustration}(b) shows an example of \problemname; from the only retained fact $(\textit{Camila Flores}, \textit{husband}, \textit{Xavier Ross})$, the target fact cannot be deduced by any rules.
We further notice that in the example of \problemname\ (Figure~\ref{fig:illustration}(b)), even if $(\textit{Xavier Ross}, \textit{wife}, \textit{Camila Flores})$ is not unlearnt, it is still an example of \problemname.
In practice, we would prefer the LLM that \problemnamethird\  the target fact but retains other facts as much as possible.
Therefore, We define what \textit{minimal} \problemname\ is.

\begin{definition}[Minimal \problemname]
	Given a fact $k$, the minimal \problemname\  set $U_{k}^{*}$ to unlearn the fact $k$ with respect to the rule set $\calR$ should meet two requirements: 1. $k\notin \Omega(\calK\backslash U_{k}^{*}, \calR)$, 2. $\forall U\subset U_{k}^*$, $k\in \Omega(\calK\backslash U, \calR)$.
	Moreover, the unlearning method minimally \problemnamethird\  $k$ with respect to the rule set $\calR$ if $U_k^{\calA}$ is any minimal \problemname\  set.
\end{definition}
Note that minimal \problemname\  set needs not be unique. 
For example, Figure~\ref{fig:illustration}(c) shows two possible minimal \problemname\  sets for unlearning the same target fact.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}
\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{DUS$(k, \calK, \calR)$ -- Random generation of the \textit{D}eep \textit{U}nlearning \textit{S}et}
\textbf{Input:} The target fact $k$, the knowledge base $\calK$, the rule set $\calR.$
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\STATE $\hat{U}_k=\{k\}$, $T=\{k\}$
		\WHILE{$T\neq \emptyset$}
		\STATE Uniformly randomly pick $k_{\rm cur}$ from $T$. $T = T\backslash \{k_{\rm cur}\}$
		\STATE Find all initializations of rules $\calI_{k_{\rm cur}}$ that implies $k_{\rm cur}$ and denote the size $|\calI_{k_{\rm cur}}|$ as $m_{k_{\rm cur}}$:
		$$\calI_{k_{\rm cur}} = \{I_j|\forall j\in[m_{k_{\rm cur}}], I_j=(k_1^j, \cdots, k_{n_j}^j, k_{\rm cur}) \in \Omega(\calK, \calR)\times \cdots \times \Omega(\calK, \calR) $$
		$$\text{ is an initiation of the rule } B_1^j\wedge \cdots\wedge B_{n_j}^j\to A_j\in \calR \}$$
		\FOR{$(k_1^j, \cdots, k_{n_j}^j, k_{\rm cur})$ in $\calI_{k_{\rm cur}}$ where $\{k_1^j, \cdots, k_{n_j}^j\}\cap \hat{U}_k=\emptyset$ in a random order}
			\STATE Uniformly randomly pick $k^j$ from $\{k_1^j, \cdots, k_{n_j}^j\}$. $\hat{U}_k=\hat{U}_k\cup \{k^j\}$, $T=T\cup \{k^j\}$.
		\ENDFOR
		\ENDWHILE
		\STATE \textbf{Output: }$U_k:=\hat{U}\cap \calK$.
	\end{algorithmic}
	\label{algo:gen_sus}
\end{algorithm}
\begin{minipage}{0.56\linewidth}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RP$(k, \calK, \calR, U_k)$ -- \textit{R}andom \textit{P}runing the \problemname\  set}
\textbf{Input:} The target fact $k$, the knowledge base $\calK$, the rule set $\calR$, the \problemname\  set $U_k$%
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\STATE $C=\{\}$, $t=0$, $U_k^*=U_k.$
		\WHILE{$C\neq\emptyset$ or $t=0$}
            \STATE $C=\{\}$, $t=t+1$
		\FOR{$k_{\rm cur}$ in randomly shuffled $U_k^*$}
		\IF{$k\notin \Omega(\calK\backslash (U_k^* \backslash  \{k_{\rm cur}\}), \calR)$}
			\STATE $C=C\cup \{k_{\rm cur}\}$, $U_k^* = U_k^*\backslash\{k_{\rm cur}\}$
		\ENDIF
		\ENDFOR
		\ENDWHILE
		\STATE \textbf{Output: }$U_k^*$.
	\end{algorithmic}
	\label{algo:pr_msus}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage} 
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.40\linewidth}\
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{MDUS$(k, \calK, \calR; N_{\rm seed})$ -- Generating multiple \textit{M}inimal \textit{D}eep \textit{U}nlearning \textit{S}ets}
\textbf{Input:} The target fact $k$, the knowledge base $\calK$, the rule set $\calR$, the number of seeds $N_{\rm seed}.$
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\STATE $\hat{\calM}_{k, \calR, \calK}=\{\}$.
		\FOR{$n_{\rm seed}=1, \cdots, N_{\rm seed}$}
		\STATE $U_k=$DUS$(k, \calK, \calR)$.\ \ $\backslash\backslash$ Algorithm~\ref{algo:gen_sus}
		\STATE $U_k^*$=RP$(k, \calK, \calR, U_k)$.  \ \ $\backslash\backslash$ Algorithm~\ref{algo:pr_msus}
		\STATE $\hat{\calM}_{k, \calR, \calK} = \hat{\calM}_{k, \calR, \calK}\cup \{U_k^*\}$.
		\ENDFOR
\STATE \textbf{Output: $\hat{\calM}_{k, \calR, \calK}$}
	\end{algorithmic}
	\label{algo:main}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}
We propose two evaluation metrics to evaluate an unlearning method $\calA$: \textit{recall} and \textit{accuracy}. 
Recall is to measure the extent of \problemname\   of an unlearning method $\calA$.
It calculates the percentage of any minimal \problemname\  set that has been unlearnt by the method $\calA$.
Because the minimal \problemname\  set is not unique, the recall is defined w.r.t. the minimal \problemname\  set that $U_{k}^{\calA}$ covers the most.
Formally, let $\calM_{k, \calR, \calK}$ denote the set of all minimal \problemname\  sets to unlearn $k$ (from the knowledge base $\calK$ with respective to the rule set $\calR$). 
The \textit{recall} for a given unlearning method $\calA$ to unlearn $k$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recall}
\text{Recall}(\calA, k; \calK, \calR)=	\max_{U_{k}^*\in \calM_{k, \calR, \calK}}\frac{|U_{k}^{\calA}\cap U_{k}^*|}{|U_{k}^*|}.
\end{equation}
We also denote $U_{k}^{\calA, *}:=\arg\max_{U_{k}^*\in \calM_{k, \calR, \calK}}\frac{|U_{k}^{\calA}\cap U_{k}^*|}{|U_{k}^*|}$ the minimal \problemname\  set that $U_{k}^{\calA}$ covers the most, which is used for calculating the recall.
Figure~\ref{fig:illustration}(d) shows an example of calculating this recall. 
There are two minimal \problemname\  sets for unlearning the target fact and by definition $U_{k}^{\calA, *}=U_{k}^{*, (1)}$ is picked for the final recall value.

Subsequently, we can define the set of facts to measure the accuracy of the LLM. 
$U_{k}^{\calA, *}$, as previously defined, is the minimal deep unlearning set to calculate the recall.
We calculate the accuracy among the knowledge base excluding this minimal \problemname\  set, $\calK\backslash U_{k}^{\calA,*}$, for measuring the model utility:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:acc}
\text{Accuracy}(\calA, k; \calK, \calR)=\frac{|(\calK\backslash U_{k}^{\calA, *})\backslash U_{k}^{\calA})|}{|\calK\backslash U_{k}^{\calA, *}|}.
\end{equation}
At the ideal situation where the unlearning method $\calA$ exactly unlearns a minimal \problemname\ set, both recall and accuracy are $1$; otherwise, either the unlearning method does not \problemnameactive\ the target fact (recall$<1$), or it unlearns extraneous facts (for unlearning the target fact $k$) (accuracy$<1$). 

\paragraph{Approximation algorithm for calculating recall and accuracy.} The calculations of both recall and accuracy rely on an optimization problem
$$
U_{k}^{\calA, *}:=\arg\max_{U_{k}^*\in \calM_{k, \calR, \calK}}\frac{|U_{k}^{\calA}\cap U_{k}^*|}{|U_{k}^*|},
$$
where $\calM_{k, \calR, \calK}$ denote the set of all minimal \problemname\  sets to unlearn $k$ (from the knowledge base $\calK$ with respective to the rule set $\calR$).
However, finding this exact $U_{k}^{\calA, *}$ in general can be NP-hard~\citep{Skiena2020-mw}. 
Alternatively, we propose Algorithm~\ref{algo:main}, which are able to find multiple minimal \problemname\  sets $\hat{\calM}_{k, \calR, \calK}$.
With $\hat{\calM}_{k, \calR, \calK}$, it is efficient to find $\hat{U}_{k}^{\calA, *}:=\arg\max_{U_{k}^*\in \hat{\calM}_{k, \calR, \calK}}\frac{|U_{k}^{\calA}\cap U_{k}^*|}{|U_{k}^*|}$ and approximately calculate the recall and accuracy afterwards.

The idea in Algorithm~\ref{algo:main} is to generate a single \problemname\ set with some randomness (line 3) and to repeat this generation process to attain multiple different \problemname\ sets (line 4); 

There are two steps to find a single minimal \problemname\  set;
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item Find the \problemname\  set (Algorithm~\ref{algo:gen_sus}). We enumerate the rules and find all combinations of facts that can imply $k$ (line 4).
For each combination, if no facts in this combination are in the returning set $U_k$, we randomly pick one fact from this combination and add it to the returning set $U_k$ (lines 5-7). 
Additionally, for the picked fact in any combination, we repeat the above process but for this fact recursively.
This algorithm guarantees that $k\notin \Omega(\calK\backslash U_k, \calR)$ and randomness from picking fact in each combination and the order for going through the combinations brings the diversity of the results.
\item Prune $U_k$ to a minimal \problemname\  set $U_k^*$ (Algorithm~\ref{algo:pr_msus}). We go through every fact $k_{\rm cur}$ in $U_k$ one by one and check if $U_k\backslash\{k_{\rm cur}\}$ from $\calK$ is still a \problemname\ set. If yes, we can safely remove $k_{\rm cur}$ from current $U_k$ and repeat this process until there is no $k_{\rm cur}\in U_k$ that can be removed. The $U_k^*$ returned by this algorithm is guaranteed to be a minimal \problemname\ set, and the randomness in the order of checking $k_{\rm cur}\in U_k$ brings the diversity of the results.
\end{enumerate}
By running Algorithm~\ref{algo:main} on the facts in the synthetic dataset introduced in the later section, we find that Algorithm~\ref{algo:main} is capable of generating a diverse set of minimal \problemname\ sets.
For more than half of the facts in our synthetic dataset, Algorithm~\ref{algo:main} can return 6-17 different minimal \problemname\ sets.
This demonstrates the effectiveness of Algorithm~\ref{algo:main} and hence leads to a good approximation for computing the recall in Equation~\ref{eq:recall}.



\section{\dataname: 	Evaluating Deep Unlearning through RELATionships}

\label{sec:synth_data}
To systematically evaluate \problemname\ in LLMs, we need a dataset that is already in the LLMs and consists of multiple instances where one or more facts imply other facts by some realistic rules.

However, we found it challenging to extract a good dataset from the real-world knowledge base because of two reasons.
First, the existing real-world knowledge bases are noisy and incomplete, e.g., we found some cases where (Country A, \textit{is neighbor of}, Country B) is in the knowledge base but (Country B, \textit{is neighbor of}, Country A) is not.
Another difficulty is at the engineering level.
It is challenging to find the correct prompt to check whether a fact is in the LLM or not~\citep{zhong2023mquake}, and this can bring many false negatives.
Because of these two reasons, there are only few and sparse examples where the fact can be deduced from other facts in the real-world knowledge base.
Alternatively, we construct a synthetic dataset named \dataname\ for systematically evaluating deep unlearning through relationships in the family.
We locate our synthetic dataset in a family network, which is a common scenario to study rule mining and knowledge discovery in the literature~\citep{galarraga2013amie,cheng2023neural,luo2023chatrule}.
This synthetic dataset includes a synthetic knowledge base consisting of $400$ family relationships and $300$ biographical facts among $100$ fictitious people, as well as a set of realistic logical rules, which are deductions among family relationships.
Family relationships include \textit{child}, \textit{father}, \textit{mother}, \textit{husband}, \textit{wife}, \textit{brother}, \textit{sister}, \textit{aunt}, \textit{uncle}, \textit{nephew}, \textit{niece}.
Biographies include \textit{birthyear}, \textit{birthplace}, and \textit{job}.
Table~\ref{tab:knowledge_examples} shows some examples of facts in family relationships and biographies, together with the question-answer pairs for checking whether this fact is in the LLM or not.
Moreover, the rule set $\calR$ has 48 rules, which are used to deduce the facts in family relationships.

We make several efforts to better mimic a knowledge base of real-world including:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
	\item \textbf{Family network generation.} We recursively expand the network. Given a node (person), with a certain probability, we generate the parents, spouse, and children of this person. We control the whole family network in 4 generations. The number of children from any couple is sampled from a truncated geometric distribution between 1 and 4. 
	\item \textbf{Name generation.} We collect two lists of first names for males and females separately and assign the first name to each person according to gender. As for the last name, each person's last name is the same as the father's if the father exists in the network. There is only one special case where the female's last name has a small probability of switching to her husband's.
	\item \textbf{Biography generation.} We have three biographical attributes, birth year, birthplace, and job:
		\begin{itemize}
		\item The birth years of people are aligned with their relationships. The birth year of any child is from a truncated Gaussian distribution given his/her mother's birth year. The difference in birth years of a couple is sampled from a reasonable distribution as well.
		\item The birthplace is the state in the United States. The child's birthplace is the same as the birthplace of the parent with a high chance, or sampled from the population distribution in the United States.
		\item The job list is collected from GPT4 for every ten years in 1900-2020. The job of a person is picked based on the birth year.
		\end{itemize}		 
\end{itemize}
We believe these realistic considerations reduce the gap in evaluations between the unlearning task in our synthetic dataset and the real-world unlearning task.
Within the synthetic dataset \dataname, we finetune pre-trained LLM on it for the next unlearning evaluation.
The unlearning method is run with the finetuned LLM and tested on a subset of facts on family relationships together with the rules.
The recall defined in Equation~\ref{eq:recall} is calculated with an approximation Algorithm~\ref{algo:main};
in addition, we measure the accuracy of the remaining facts in both family relationships and biographies to reflect the cost of unlearning.

\label{sec:exp}
In this section, we investigate to what extent current unlearning methods succeed at \problemname.
We release our dataset and code as a benchmark at 

\subsection{Set-up}
\paragraph{Unlearning methods.}
For each target LLM, we evaluate four common unlearning methods in the literature: \textit{gradient ascent}, \textit{negative preference optimization}, \textit{task vector} and \textit{who's harry potter}.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textit{Gradient ascent} (GA; \citep{jang2022knowledge}) maximizes the loss on the target data, which is a reversed process of learning that does gradient \emph{descent}. More optimization steps $T$ result in better unlearning but worse accuracy on extraneous facts. 
\item \textit{Negative preference optimization} (NPO; \citep{zhang2024negative}) minimizes the difference between the likelihood of the target data $L(x_{\rm target}; f_{\theta})$ and the likelihood $L(x_{\rm target}; f_{\rm original})$ from the original model $f_{\rm original}$, while not allowing the unlearnt model to diverge too much from the original model. The objective is defined as
$$
\mathcal{L}(x_{\rm target}, \theta) = -\frac{2}{\beta}\log \sigma \left(\beta\log\left(\frac{L(x_{\rm target}; f_{\theta})}{L(x_{\rm target}; f_{\rm original})}\right) \right).
$$
As suggested by the literature, the parameter $\beta$ that controls the degree of divergence between unlearnt and original models is set to $0.1$.
Similar to gradient ascent, the step $T$ of optimizing $\mathcal{L}(x_{\rm target}, \theta)$ is to control the trade-off between the unlearning performance and the model utility. 
\item \textit{Task vector} (TV; \citep{ilharco2023editing}) first finetunes the original model $f_{\rm original}$ on the target data $x_{\rm target}$ until the original model overfits to the target data. Let $f_{\rm overfit}$ denote the overfitting model. Then the difference overfitted and original model $f_{\rm overfit} - f_{\rm original}$ can be used as the direction towards learning $x_{\rm target}$, and intuitively its negative direction can be used for unlearning the target data. Therefore, TV defines the unlearning model as $f_{\rm original} - \alpha \cdot \left( f_{\rm overfit} - f_{\rm original}\right)$. A larger value of parameter $\alpha$ gives a higher degree of unlearning but will hurt the model utility.

\item \textit{Who's harry potter} (WHP; \citep{eldan2023s}) is based on a similar idea as TV and uses the overfitted model $f_{\rm overfit}$. 
Instead of being guided by the difference in weights it uses the probability. Let $P_{f}(x_t|x_{1:t-1})$ denote the next token $x_t$ distribution given the language model $f$ and prompt $x_{1:t-1}$. WHP samples the next token by the token distribution defined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:whp}
P_{f_{\rm original}}(x_t|x_{1:t-1}) - \alpha \max(P_{f_{\rm overfit}}(x_t|x_{1:t-1}) - P_{f_{\rm original}}(x_t|x_{1:t-1}), 0).
\end{equation} 
The role of $\alpha$ is similar to $\alpha$ in TV.
\end{itemize}
\paragraph{Target LLMs.} 
We evaluate with four popular LLMs: GPT2-XL~(\citep{radford2019language}, 1.5B) Phi-1.5~(\citep{textbooks2}, 1.3B), Llama2-7b~(\citep{touvron2023llama}, 7B), Llama3-8b~(\citep{dubey2024llama}, 8B). 

The finetuned LLMs are the target to study the unlearning.

\paragraph{Target data and evaluation metric.}
We have 11 different family relationships (e.g., \textit{child}) in the synthetic knowledge base \dataname.
For each family relationship, we pick 5 facts, which results in 55 facts in total for the unlearning evaluation.
Our task is \problemname\ \emph{single} fact and report the average performance among these 55 facts.

Specifically, for each fact, we run four unlearning methods from four LLMs and measure the recall (Equation\ref{eq:recall}) and accuracy (Equation~\ref{eq:acc}). 
Because each unlearning method has its parameter to control the trade-off between unlearning performance and model utility, to unlearn each target fact, we can collect a list of accuracy and recall by varying this parameter.
We calculate the accuracy when the recall is larger than $0.8$ (Acc$@$Recall$\geq 0.8$; higher is better) and the recall when the accuracy is larger than $0.8$ (Acc$@$Recall$\geq 0.8$; higher is better), and report their average values among the 55 facts.

\subsection{Results}
\textbf{Main observation: no unlearning method succeeds in \problemname\ even for just a single fact.}
In Figure~\ref{fig:avg_res_bar}, we show the average Acc@Recall$\geq 0.8$ and Recall@Acc$\geq 0.8$ across 55 target facts for four unlearning methods on four LLMs, as well as the standard deviation presented as the error bar.
It is observed that no unlearning method reaches the region of both Recall$\geq 0.8$ and Accuracy$\geq 0.8$; this means that all unlearning methods are not capable of attaining a high degree of \problemname\ while keeping an LLM utility after the unlearning.
Notice that accuracy of $0.8$, i.e., dropping $0.2$ from $1$, is actually a high cost, as this is the cost of unlearning only single fact; in practice, there will usually be more target facts and hence a harder setting.
For easier future comparison, we also report the numbers (Acc$@$Recall$\geq 0.8$ and Acc$@$Recall$\geq 0.8$) in Table~\ref{tab:numbers}.

\textbf{Comparison among four unlearning methods.} 
We visualize the accuracy-recall curve in Figure~\ref{fig:curve_acc_recall}, where each point is the average accuracy and recall of an unlearning method with one hyperparameter across the evaluation of 55 target facts.
We observe that GA, NPO, and TV are better WHP on all four LLMs. 
Particularly, the curve of WHP stops at a low recall; this is also reflected in Figure~\ref{fig:avg_res_bar} where the Acc@Recall$\geq0.8$ of WHP is very low.
This is because of the definition as shown in Equation~\ref{eq:whp}: for those negative dimensionalities in $P_{f_{\rm overfit}}(x_t|x_{1:t-1}) - P_{f_{\rm original}}(x_t|x_{1:t-1})$, they are invariant when varying $\alpha$ due to the operator $\max(\cdot, 0)$.
Therefore, even if we select $\alpha=10^8$ in our experiment, the recall cannot increase because no more facts would be lost due to the invariance.
\textbf{Superficial unlearning versus \problemname.} 
We measure the accuracy when the unlearning method has unlearnt the target fact but not necessarily any \problemname\ set (Acc@Superficial Unlearning).
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:avg_res_spfc_bar}, we find that GA is capable of carrying out this superficial unlearning -- it can successfully unlearn the target fact without losing significant accuracy.

\caption{Accuracy@Superficial Unlearning, where only the target fact itself is required to be unlearnt. GA successfully unlearns the target fact without losing significant accuracy.}

However, as discussed in the previous paragraph, even the best method GA loses more than $0.2$ in accuracy to attain recall$\geq 0.8$ of \problemname.
This is because \problemname\ is a more challenging setting than superficial unlearning -- \problemname\ single fact in \dataname\ can require unlearning more than 10 facts from the LLM by the definition.
\textbf{Unlearning on small models versus unlearning on larger models.}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:avg_res_bar}, while the poor-performing WHP has consistently low performance across four LLMs, three relatively more effective methods GA, NPO and TV perform better on larger LLMs (Llama2-7b and Llama3-8b) than the smaller LLMs (Phi-1.5 and GPT2-XL).
To be specific, the Acc@Recall$\geq 0.8$ of GA, NPO or TV on Llama2-7b or Llama3-8b is around $0.1$ or more higher than it on Phi-1.5 or GPT2-XL; 
It is observed similarly for Recall@Acc$\geq 0.8$.
the Recall@Acc$\geq 0.8$ of NPO on Llama2-7b or Llama3-8b is also about $0.2$ higher.
We hypothesize this is because larger LLM has a better inherent understanding of the correlations between facts, which can be important in attaining a good performance of \problemname.
 
\textbf{Family relationships accuracy versus biographies accuracy.} 
We take a closer look at the accuracy of two subgroups of the knowledge base, the family relationships and biographies.
The results are plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:avg_twoparts_res_bar}. 
We observe that the accuracy of biographical facts can be retained better than the accuracy of family relationships.
Specifically, the Acc@Recall$\geq0.8$ of GA on biographical facts is higher than $0.8$ on Llama2-7b and Llama3-8b, while it is only $0.6$ on facts in family relationships.
This is because, during unlearning, facts in family relationships are closer to the target facts (which are also family relationships) than the biographical facts and are likely to get more easily affected.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Benchmarks and evaluations in LLM unlearning.} 
TOFU~\citep{tofu2024} is a benchmark containing fictitious authors and their related biographic question-answering texts such as ``Awards" and ``Book".
They evaluate the unlearning utility by comparing the answer from LLM given the to-be-unlearned question and the ground truth answer.
WMDP~\citep{li2024the} provides knowledge in biosecurity, cybersecurity, and chemical security, which matches the realistic desire for studying unlearning.
A more recent benchmark MUSE~\citep{shi2024muse} in the domain of news articles and books enriches the evaluation by introducing the metric from both memorization and privacy leakage aspects.
\citet{yao2024machine} introduces a benchmark of evaluating the unlearning in pre-trained data and the metric of unlearning utility is to compute the perplexity of the data from the memorization aspect.
\citet{patil2024can} and \citet{lucki2024adversarial} evaluate the unlearning from an adversarial attack aspect of knowledge extraction.
This branch of work focuses on proposing more realistic domains and more robust ways to evaluate the unlearning, and the challenge at their benchmark is to unlearn a large batch of facts or texts while keeping the model utility.
However, none of them consider the interrelation between the target facts and other facts also in the LLM.
\problemnamecap\ in our paper is introduced to capture the connections among multiple facts in the unlearning, and we found that \problemname\  is challenging enough for just unlearning a single fact.

\paragraph{Unlearning methods in LLM.} In addition to the methods evaluated in Section~\ref{sec:exp}, one popular extension is assuming the existence of a ``retain" set independent of the target facts. When doing gradient ascent or other gradient-based variants, \citet{yao2023large} and \citet{chen2023unlearn} minimize the loss on the ``retain" set simultaneously to avoid quickly losing other irrelevant facts and hence help with the model utility.
Another popular category is the model-editing based~\citep{meng2022locating, meng2023massediting, wang2024large}, which hypothesizes that the knowledge is saved in certain MLPs in the transformer and proposes an explicit-form solution for the weight update to unlearn the target facts. 

\paragraph{Other settings of machine unlearning.} 
There are other settings of machine unlearning because of different purposes and model structures.
\citet{ginart2019making} and \citet{guo2020certified} define the goal of unlearning as the model trained without a single person's data to meet the data regulation for data owner's rights. 
\citet{tiwary2023adapt, kong2024data} study the concept or feature removal in the generative models, e.g. remove ``white breast" in all generated bird photos; this is to avoid generating violent and racial content.

 


\section{Conclusion and Discussion}

In this paper, we propose a new setting for machine unlearning, referred to as \textit{\problemname}, aimed at identifying reliable fact unlearning. 
As a starting point, we construct a synthetic dataset \dataname\ of family relationships and biographies as a benchmark for research in this emerging setting. 
From empirical evaluation using our metrics, we find that current unlearning methods are not capable of deeply unlearning even a single fact while keeping the model utility.
We hypothesize that this shortcoming arises from these methods not fully considering the nature of facts and the deductions between each other. 

This work opens several promising directions for future research.
Firstly, more effective methods can be developed with the \problemname\ setting, with an awareness of the connections between facts.
One can assume the complete set of rules and the entire knowledge base may or may not be fully known, referred to as the white-box or the black-box scenarios. 
Additionally, there is potential to construct a more nuanced framework that captures more intricate facts and sophisticated deductive processes beyond the current scope of relations and logical rules.
Such advancements will enhance the modeling of \problemname\ and contribute to the development of methods for \problemnameing\ a broader range of facts.
