\title{Neuron based Personality Trait Induction in Large Language Models}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models~(LLMs) have become increasingly proficient at simulating various personality traits, an important capability for supporting related applications (\eg role-playing). To further improve this capacity, in this paper, we present a neuron-based approach for personality trait induction in LLMs, with three major technical contributions. 
First, we construct \textsc{PersonalityBench}, a large-scale dataset for identifying and evaluating personality traits in LLMs. This dataset is grounded in the Big Five personality traits from psychology and is designed to assess the generative capabilities of LLMs towards specific personality traits.
Second, by leveraging \textsc{PersonalityBench}, we propose an efficient method for identifying personality-related neurons within LLMs by examining the opposite aspects of a given trait. 
Third, we develop a simple yet effective induction method that manipulates the values of these identified personality-related neurons. This method enables fine-grained control over the traits exhibited by LLMs without training and modifying model parameters.
Extensive experiments validate the efficacy of our neuron identification and trait induction methods. Notably, our approach achieves comparable performance as fine-tuned models, offering a more efficient and flexible solution for personality trait induction in LLMs.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Recently, the potential of large language models~(LLMs) has been widely explored, not only in generating human-like text but also in simulating various personality traits. Such capabilities are crucial for applications like role-playing~\citep{pan2023llms}, gaming~\citep{carlander2024controlled}, and therapeutic settings~\citep{xu2023leveraging}, where nuanced personalities  
should be stimulated and established by the supporting system. 
Understanding and regulating the mechanism of possessing personality traits in LLMs is essential for developing responsive and adaptable AI systems.

To induce the personality traits in LLMs, existing research primarily adopt either prompt-based or training-based methods. 
Prompt-based methods~\citep{tan2024phantom,huang2023chatgpt, la2024open, jiang2023personallm, kovavc2023large} are efficient and can quickly induce personality traits without extensive retraining, while their performance highly depends on the prompt design and the foundation model. As a comparison, training approaches~\citep{pan2023llms,liu2024dynamic,li2022does} provide greater stability but often require substantial time, computational resources, and high-quality datasets.
To reduce the training costs, some study~\citep{zhu2024personality}  proposes to identify relevant attention heads and guide them in a specific direction to align with a particular personality. 
However, these studies either lack theoretical guidance (\eg psychological clues) in methodology or cannot impose fine-grained, precise control on nuanced personalities.
To address these issues, in this work, we present a \underline{\textbf{N}}euron-based approach for \underline{\textbf{P}}ersonality \underline{\textbf{T}}raits \underline{\textbf{I}}nduction in LLMs, named as \textbf{NPTI}.
As the theoretical guidance, our approach is developed based on the Big Five personality traits from  psychology~\citep{tupes1992recurrent}, which categorizes personality into five traits: openness, conscientiousness, extroversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. 
Following the Big Five personality traits, we construct a personality dataset \textsc{PersonalityBench} with specially curated instances (\ie a personality description paired with a situational question). Unlike existing related datasets mainly in the multi-choice format~\citep{jiang2024evaluating},   \textsc{PersonalityBench}  conducts generative personality evaluations with real-world test cases for LLMs, which can effectively enhance the evaluation robustness. 
Subsequently, we employ \textsc{PersonalityBench} to identify personality-related neurons. Instead of simply measuring the activation degree~\citep{zhu2024personality}, we find that traits often correspond to two opposite aspects (\eg extroverted and introverted), and thus propose to calculate the activation difference of a LLM when examining the opposing aspects of a given trait. 
Furthermore, to impose precise control on personality traits, we design a simple yet effective induction method by manipulating the values of the personality-related neurons. We seek \emph{neurons} as the manipulation units as it directly conveys fine-grained semantics on personality traits, and existing studies~\citep{dai2021knowledge,meng2022locating,tang2024language} have shown that neuron manipulation can effectively elicit or alter the behaviors of LLMs.

To summarize, this work presents a systematic personality trait induction approach, with the main technical contributions in three aspects:  

$\bullet$ \emph{Personality dataset}: We design a dataset named \textsc{PersonalityBench}, comprising 180,000 open-ended questions specifically crafted for each of the Big Five personality traits, in which the answers clearly distinguish between high and low levels of these traits in the model.

$\bullet$ \emph{Neuron identification}: We propose a new identification method for locating the neurons associated with specific personality traits in LLMs by leveraging both the positive and negative aspects of the same personality trait.

$\bullet$ \emph{Neuron manipulation}: We design a simple yet effective manipulation method for personality-related neurons, with specific modification strategies for opposite aspects of a given trait. Extensive experiments using various evaluation methods on different LLMs have verified the effectiveness and generality of our method.




\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Personality in LLMs.}
As large language models advance~\citep{zhao2023survey}, their improved human-like text interactions are increasingly used for simulating diverse personalities~\citep{wen2024self}. This capability enables language models to perform diverse functions, such as conducting sociological experiments~\citep{ziems2024can,park2023generative} and impersonating specific characters~\citep{li2023chatharuhi,shao-etal-2023-character,wang-etal-2024-rolellm}. Current research on personality in LLMs primarily falls into two categories: \emph{personality assessment} and \emph{personality induction}.
Researchers commonly utilize LLMs to assess the personalities of existing LLMs. One approach involves directly utilizing LLMs to respond to questionnaires based on established personality frameworks such as the Big Five personality traits~\citep{tupes1992recurrent} or the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator~(MBTI)~\citep{boyle1995myers}.
Alternatively, LLMs can be used to infer personality traits from given texts. This can be done by analyzing the text itself to make assessments~\citep{peters2024large,ji2023chatgpt}, or by incorporating LLMs to enhance existing personality recognition models~\citep{cao2024large}.
As for personality induction, one approach to achieve it is through \emph{prompt-based induction}~\citep{tan2024phantom,huang2023chatgpt, la2024open, jiang2023personallm, kovavc2023large}, which can be further divided into explicit prompting~\citep{xu2023expertprompting} and implicit prompting~\citep{suzgun2022challenging}. Explicit prompting gives direct descriptions of personality traits for the model, while implicit prompting provides real-world examples, letting the model infer traits via in-context learning. For example, \citet{jiang2024evaluating} use explicit personality prompts to guide LLMs in generating responses tailored to specific personality traits in open-ended questions~\citep{KWANTES2016229}.
The other approach to achieving personality induction is through \emph{training}, such as pre-training~\citep{pan2023llms} and post-training~\citep{liu2024dynamic}. \citet{li2022does} employ direct preference optimization to fine-tune Llama-2-chat-7B with question-answer pairs from the Big Five Inventory~(BFI)~\citep{john1999big}, finding that this approach significantly enhanced the model's performance on the Short Dark Triad~\citep{PAULHUS2002556}. Meanwhile, \citet{zhu2024personality} train ``probes" to capture ``activations" of each attention head, helping aligning individual preference characteristics during the model's forward computation. 
However, prompt-based induction can produce varying and unstable results depending on the specific prompts used. Model training, while more consistent, requires significant time and computational resources and often suffers from a lack of labeled data. In contrast, our neuron-change method is more lightweight and stable, enabling models to exhibit specific personalities without the need for extensive training.

\paragraph{Knowledge Mechanisms in LLMs.}%不要创新,
Research has revealed that \emph{neurons} in neural networks play a role in storing the knowledge acquired during training~\citep{mu-etal-2020-compositional,bau2020understanding,geva-etal-2022-transformer}. 
As a widely adopted architecture for language models, the Transformer~\citep{vaswani2017attention} is composed of multiple layers featuring self-attention mechanisms and feed-forward neural networks~(FFNs). Early studies demonstrate that attention heads in pre-trained models, such as BERT~\citep{devlin2018bert}, can capture and convey various forms of knowledge within the model~\citep{voita2019analyzing,clark2019does,hoover2019exbert}. 
With the development of large language models, researchers increasingly focus on exploring and exploiting the knowledge mechanisms~\citep{wang2024knowledge} embedded within language models. 
\citet{olsson2022context} present evidence suggesting that a combination of several attention heads can exhibit pattern copying behavior and be responsible for the universal in-context learning capabilities. 
Meanwhile, several studies explore how to leverage the memory of neurons inside FFNs to modify the model's behavior, such as editing specific factual knowledge~\citep{dai2021knowledge,meng2022locating} and changing the output language of LLMs~\citep{tang2024language}. Besides instance-level editing, the FFNs in the last few layers show greater importance when editing conceptual knowledge according to \citet{wang2024editing}. 
Unlike previous research, our work introduces a novel approach that identifies neurons within FFNs responsible for controlling personality traits and modifies them to exhibit specific personality characteristics.


\section{Approach}

Our approach is built upon a meticulously curated dataset called \textsc{PersonalityBench}, which is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:dataset}. It comprises two main steps: identifying personality-related neurons (Section~\ref{sec:find}) and manipulating these neurons to induce the desired personality traits in LLMs (Section~\ref{sec:modify}). \subsection{PersonalityBench Construction} \label{sec:dataset}
We first describe the construction process of our generative benchmark, \textsc{PersonalityBench}, designed to evaluate the ability of LLMs to exhibit consistent personality traits. This benchmark also facilitates our identification of personality-related neurons within LLMs.
Unlike existing approaches, which rely primarily on multiple-choice formats, such as the BFI~\citep{tupes1992recurrent} and MBTI~\citep{boyle1995myers}, our benchmark conducts the test in the form of generative tasks. 
Since multiple-choice questions may lead to evaluation bias or even inaccuracies~\citep{dorner2023personality}, they can't well evaluate the ability of LLMs to generate natural responses that consistently reflect specific personality traits in real-world scenarios. 
We denote the constructed \textsc{PersonalityBench} as $\mathcal{P}$, each instance within $\mathcal{P}$ consists of a \emph{personality description} paired with a \emph{situational question}.
Next, we introduce the approach to constructing the personality description and situational question in detail. 

\paragraph{Personality Description Generation.}

We generate personality descriptions based on ``Big Five personality traits''~\citep{tupes1992recurrent}, which categorizes personality into five major traits: \emph{openness}~(O), \emph{conscientiousness}~(C), \emph{extroversion}~(E), \emph{agreeableness}~(A), and \emph{neuroticism}~(N). 
Following this, each instance in our PersonalityBench  $\mathcal{P}$ is associated with a given personality trait $t$, where $t$ belongs to the Big Five traits: $\{\textit{O, C, E, A, N}\}$.
The subset of the instances with the personality trait $t$ is denoted by $\mathcal{P}_t$.
Furthermore, as described by \citet{mccrae1992introduction}, each trait can be further broken down into more detailed facets. For example, the trait of openness corresponds to imagination, artistic interests, emotionality, adventurousness, intellect, and liberalism. 
These facets can be utilized to extend the description of personality. 
After that, we leverage these traits with the associated facets to generate a diverse range of personality descriptions following \citet{jiang2024evaluating}. Specifically, we employ ChatGPT to create concise descriptive sentences in the second person by extending the adjective forms of these traits. For traits with opposite aspect (\eg introverted), we incorporate their antonyms. Initially, we produce a description for each of the ten personality aspects, then refine these manually. These refined descriptions serve as exemplars for generating more diverse descriptions.

    \item \emph{Openness}: imagination, artistic interests, emotionality, adventurousness, intellect, liberalism;
    \item \emph{Conscientiousness}: self-efficacy, orderliness, dutifulness, achievement-striving, self-discipline, cautiousness;
    \item \emph{Extroversion}: friendliness, gregariousness, assertiveness, activity level, excitement-seeking, cheerfulness;
    \item \emph{Agreeableness}: trust, morality, altruism, cooperation, modesty, sympathy;
    \item \emph{Neuroticism}: anxiety, anger, depression, self-consciousness, immoderation, vulnerability.

\paragraph{Situational Question Construction.}
Based on the generated personality descriptions, we further construct the situational questions specifically designed to elicit distinct responses from individuals with varied personality traits. Traditional evaluation questions~\citep{hilliard2024eliciting}, such as ``\emph{What do you usually do at the weekend?}'' often fail to capture meaningful differences across personality types, as they may prompt superficially similar responses. To address this, we design a set of targeted questions grounded in real-world behaviors, which are tailored to amplify personality-related behaviors. Specifically, we utilize the \textsc{IPIP-NEO-300} questionnaire~\citep{goldberg1999broad,goldberg2006international} to generate the situational questions that reflect various real-world behaviors. 
This questionnaire provides a detailed investigation into individuals' behaviors across different personality facets. For instance, an ``\emph{adventurous}'' individual is characterized by a tendency to ``\emph{likes to visit new place}''. To further diversify the questions, we incorporate common real-world topics introduced in UltraChat~\citep{ding2023enhancing}, such as technology, environment, and arts. To generate the situational questions, we employ specially designed prompts for ChatGPT to simulate complex scenarios involving dilemmas, conflicting priorities, or challenging decisions that align with specific facets based on specific behaviors and topics. We further refine the results with ChatGPT to review for potential bias--whether moral or emotional--and make necessary improvements. This refinement ensures that the questions can better capture the relevant personality traits while maintaining objectivity. Detailed prompts can be found in Table~\ref{fig:c_pb} and Table~\ref{fig:o_pb}.

Finally, our \textsc{PersonalityBench} consists of 180,000 instances of the Big Five personality traits for neuron identification, with 36,000 instances for each trait on average. As for evaluation, we utilize a similar idea to construct situational questions based on \textsc{SocialIQA}~\citep{sap2019socialiqa}, which has approximately 90 questions for each trait.



 
\subsection{Identifying Personality-Related Neurons}
~\label{sec:find}
Based on the \textsc{PersonalityBench}, we can identify neurons that regulate specific personality traits. In what follows, we first specify the meaning of neuron in our work, then present the identification method based on activation difference. 

\paragraph{Neuron Specification.} Currently, most LLMs are built upon an auto-regressive Transformer architecture~\citep{vaswani2017attention}, where the key components are multi-head self-attention (MHA) and feed-forward networks (FFN). 
Prominent LLMs, such as LLaMA~\citep{touvron2023llama} and Gemma~\citep{team2024gemma}, commonly employ GLU~\citep{shazeer2020glu} as a variant of the activation function in the FFN module. Within a given layer, the FFN module can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq-glu}
\bm{h} = \left( \sigma \left( \hat{\bm{h}} \bm{W}_1 \right) \odot \left(\hat{\bm{h}} \bm{W}_3 \right) \right)\cdot \bm{W}_2,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\bm{h}}\in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ represents the output of the MHA module for a specific token in this layer. The function $\sigma(\cdot)$ typically denotes a non-linear activation function, such as SiLU~\citep{ramachandran2017searching}. The learned projection matrices are $\bm{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d'}$, $\bm{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d' \times d}$, and $\bm{W}_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d'}$. In this context, a \emph{neuron} is conceptualized as applying a linear transformation to a specific column of the weight matrix $\bm{W}_1$ followed by a non-linear activation function to the result.


\paragraph{Activation-based Identification.} Given the subset $\mathcal{P}_t$ corresponding to a trait $t$ in \textsc{PersonalityBench}, we prompt LLMs with these instances to generate responses to questions. During this process, we calculate the activation probability of the $i$-th neuron in each layer when tokens are generated as follows:
\begin{equation} 
\text{Pr}_i = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathbb{I}\left(\sigma(\hat{\bm{h}} \bm{W}_1)_i > 0\right),
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the total number of generated tokens and $\mathbb{I}$ represents the indicator function. Since one trait has positive ($t+$) and negative ($t-$) aspects (\eg extroverted and introverted), we can further compute the activation difference $\delta$ for the $i$-th neuron between the opposing personality traits:
\begin{equation}
\delta = \text{Pr}_i^{t+} - \text{Pr}_i^{t-}.
\end{equation}
Finally, we set a difference threshold to identify personality-related neurons. Neurons are classified as controlling the positive aspect of trait $t$, denoted as $\mathbb{P}_t^+$, if  $\delta$ exceeds 10\%. Conversely, neurons with a $\delta$ below -10\% are designated as controlling the negative aspect of trait $t$, represented by $\mathbb{P}_t^-$. This classification allows us to distinguish neurons that significantly influence specific personality traits in either a positive or negative direction.

\subsection{Manipulating personality-related Neurons}
~\label{sec:modify}

As the personality-related neurons largely influence the personality behaviors of LLMs, we can induce the LLMs' personality by adjusting the values of these neurons. 
To account for the varying importance of neurons with different $\delta$ values, we introduce a weighted function $f$ based on the Sigmoid function. This function assigns higher $f(\delta)$ values to neurons with larger $\delta$ values, reflecting their greater influence on personality traits.
Our approach to eliciting a positive shift in personality trait $t$ involves increasing the activation value of positive neurons, deactivating negative neurons, and maintaining the original values of neutral neurons. The modified values for each neuron can be formulated as follows:
\begin{equation} \label{eq-modify}
n = 
\begin{cases} 
\min(0, n_{ori}), & \text{if neuron} \in \mathbb{P}_t^- \\
n_{ori} + \gamma \cdot a_{95} \cdot f(\delta), & \text{if neuron} \in \mathbb{P}_t^+ \\
n_{ori}, & \text{others}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $n_{ori}$ represents the original neuron value, $\gamma$ is a hyperparameter controlling the magnitude of change, and $a_{95}$ denotes the 95th percentile of the neuron's original activation, which ensures the modification respects each neuron's upper bounds. 
For positive neurons, we aim to amplify their influence to steer the LLM towards a more positive trait expression. Conversely, we manually deactivate negative neurons to suppress their contribution to negative trait, inspired by~\citet{tang2024language} that deactivating neurons tied to a specific language significantly weakens the model's output in that language, with minimal impact on others. To induct a negative shift in the personality trait, we reverse the conditions in Equation~\ref{eq-modify}, deactivating positive neurons and enhancing negative ones.


\section{Experiment}

\label{others}
\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Models.}
We primarily conduct our experiments on the LLaMA-3-8B-Instruct model~\citep{dubey2024llama}, known for its impressive performance in natural language understanding and generation. It has strong capabilities and adapts well to various tasks, making it an ideal base model for our studies. To extensively assess the effectiveness of our method, we also incorporate other LLMs under various configurations: Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3~\citep{jiang2023mistral}, Gemma2-9B-it~\citep{team2024gemma}, and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct~\citep{qwen2} to verify the compatibility of our methods.


\paragraph{Baseline Methods.}
We select the following four methods as our baselines: 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \itemsep0em 
     \item \textbf{Simple prompt induction}: This method employs a single adjective to guide the model toward different personality traits (\eg \emph{you are an ``extraverted/introverted'' person}). More specific adjectives and prompt can be respectively found in Table~\ref{tab:adj} and Table~\ref{tab:sp};
    \item \textbf{\textsc{$P^2$} induction}~\citep{jiang2024evaluating}: In this approach, the model receives a detailed ChatGPT-generated description of a particular personality trait. Prompt is shown in Table~\ref{tab:p2};
    \item \textbf{\textsc{PAS}}~\citep{zhu2024personality}: This method involves using the \textsc{IPIP-NEO-300} questionnaire to train a probe that identifies the attention heads most closely related to a specific personality trait. During testing, this probe is then used to adjust the model's personality;
    \item \textbf{Supervised fine-tuning}: We employ LoRA training~\citep{hu2021lora} to embody a specific personality. During training, we set the learning rate to 1e-4 with a cosine decay. The rank of LoRA is set to 8, and the batch size is configured to 8. Notably, this approach can be considered the upper limit of our method. The prompt we used during testing in presented in Table~\ref{tab:nop}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Implementation Details.}
During the construction of our \textsc{PersonalityBench} benchmark, we employ \texttt{gpt-4o-20240806} API with greedy search.
Our \textsc{PersonalityBench} has 180,000 instances for identifying neurons and around 450 instances for evaluating LLMs' personality induction.
When identifying neurons of each LLM with \textsc{PersonalityBench}, we employ greedy search with a repetition penalty of 1.1 to answer situational questions. The number of identified neurons for each neuron is around 20,000 in LLaMA-3-8B. As for the hyperparamters in Equation~\ref{eq-modify}, we set $\gamma=1.4$ and assign $f(\delta) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-10 \cdot ( \left|\delta \right| - 0.15)}}$. Further analysis of these settings can be found in Section~\ref{sec-ab}. We leverage the \texttt{vllm} toolkit for identifying and manipulating neurons.

\paragraph{Evaluation Setting.}
We consider three evaluation settings: automatically-evaluated and manually-evaluated generation ability test, and personality multiple-choice test as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \itemsep0em 
     \item \textbf{Generation ability with automatic evaluation}: We utilize the \textsc{PersonalityBench} constructed from \textsc{SocialIQA} for evaluation. We use ChatGPT to evaluate the responses of different LLMs to situational questions. This evaluation includes assessing the degree of expression of a specific personality trait and the fluency of each response, scored on a scale from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating a more pronounced presence of that trait. Detailed prompts can be found in Table~\ref{tab:score_p} and Table~\ref{tab:score_f}
    \item \textbf{Generation ability with human evaluation}: We select 20 questions for both the positive and negative aspects of each of the five personality traits, resulting in a total of 200 questions. Five human judges are recruited to rank the responses from five methods for each question based on the corresponding personality trait expressions. We calculate the average rank for each LLM, and a lower rank stands for a higher performance;
    \item \textbf{Multiple-choice evaluation}: We evaluate the effectiveness of multiple-choice assessments using the test set from \citet{zhu2024personality}, which includes results from the \textsc{IPIP-NEO-300} questionnaires of 300 individuals. Each questionnaire item asks respondents to assess how well a Big Five personality-related statement, like ``\emph{worry about things}”, reflects their own experiences. The degree of agreement is then rated on a 1 to 5 scale. Specifically, we use scores from the \textsc{IPIP-NEO-120} to guide neuron modifications.
    We then evaluate the alignment effects with each individual's personality using the full \textsc{IPIP-NEO-300} questionnaire by calculating the mean of the absolute differences between the scores for each question. The lower the mean, the better the alignment.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Distribution of Personality-Related Neurons}
Furthermore, we analyze and examine the distribution of neuron values associated with opposing aspect of one personality trait, as well as the distribution of neurons related to the five personality traits across different layers. 
In Figure~\ref{fig:his}, we record the values of the 12,975th neuron in the 15th layer for each token generated in response to related questions. This neuron is one of those controlling the positive aspect of agreeableness that we selected.
From this figure, we observe that when the model is induced to be ``\emph{agreeable}'', most values in this neuron are greater than zero. Conversely, when it is induced to be ``\emph{disagreeable}'', most values are less than zero, and the histogram becomes thinner and taller, with values closer to zero. This result shows similarity to the findings of \citep{radford2017learning}, who identify a sentiment neuron in their trained LSTM model, where the positive and negative values directly correspond to the sentiment of the text. %As shown in Figure \ref{fig:lay}, neurons controlling the five personality dimensions are primarily concentrated in the higher layers, consistent with the findings of previous work~\citet{wang2024editing}, which indicates that the FFNs in the last few layers are more crucial for conceptual knowledge.
In Figure \ref{fig:lay}, we present the distribution of five different personality-related neurons across the layers. Neurons controlling personalities are primarily concentrated in the deeper layers, consistent with the findings of previous work~\citep{wang2024editing}, which indicates that the FFNs in the last few layers are more crucial for conceptual knowledge. This result may suggest that the model's understanding of knowledge evolves with increasing depth, with more complex knowledge emerging in the model's deeper layers.

\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we propose the novel \textsc{NPTI} method, which can effectively perform personality trait induction for LLMs. 
To better identify personality-related neurons, we constructed the \textsc{PersonalityBench} dataset, comprising 180,000 open-ended questions tailored to capture distinct personality traits based on Big Five personality theory. 
Based on \textsc{PersonalityBench}, we designed an efficient identification method for personality-related neurons by calculating the LLM's activation difference of the opposing aspects of a given trait. Furthermore, to impose precise control on personality traits, we manipulated the values of the personality-related neurons to elicit or alter the desired personality of LLMs.
The results demonstrate that \textsc{NPTI} can effectively induce stable personality traits for LLMs with diverse families, achieving comparable performance as the supervised fine-tuning baseline. In future work, we aim to explore further refinements of our method and investigate its implications for broader applications in AI-driven interactions. %, paving the way for more engaging and responsive user experiences.
