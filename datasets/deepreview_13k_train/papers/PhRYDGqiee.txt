\title{Organizing Unstructured Image Collections using Natural Language}

\begin{abstract}
\vspace{-.05in}
Organizing unstructured visual data into semantic clusters is a key challenge in computer vision. Traditional deep clustering (DC) approaches focus on a single partition of data, while multiple clustering (MC) methods address this limitation by uncovering distinct clustering solutions. The rise of large language models (LLMs) and multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) has enhanced MC by allowing users to define clustering criteria in natural language. However, manually specifying criteria for large datasets is impractical. In this work, we introduce the task \tasktitle (\taskshort) that aims to automatically discover clustering criteria from large image collections, uncovering interpretable substructures \textit{without requiring human input}. Our framework, \methodfirsttitle (\methodshort), uses text as a proxy to concurrently reason over large image collections, discover partitioning criteria, expressed in natural language, and reveal semantic substructures. To evaluate TeDeSC, we introduce the COCO-4c and Food-4c benchmarks, each containing four grouping criteria and ground-truth annotations. We apply TeDeSC to various applications, such as discovering biases and analyzing social media image popularity, demonstrating its utility as a tool for automatically organizing image collections and revealing novel insights.

\section{Introduction}


\lblsec{introduction}
Organizing large volumes of unstructured visual data into semantic clusters is a crucial problem in computer vision and has traditionally been addressed through the lens of unsupervised deep clustering (DC)~\citep{caron2018deep,van2020scan}. Despite the advancements and improved performance, DC remains limited due to its inherently ill-posed nature~\citep{estivill2002so}, as a dataset can be clustered in multiple ways (\eg a dataset of a standard deck of cards can be grouped by \textit{suit} or \textit{rank}). Furthermore, the clusters discovered by DC rely on factors such as the inductive biases of the network, augmentations, and pretext tasks; and do not necessarily adhere to the a particular partitioning criteria that a user may have in mind (\eg \textit{suit}).

The limitation of DC in exploring visual data only along a single partition is addressed by multiple clustering (MC) methods~\citep{yu2024multiple}, which uncover distinct clustering solutions. Multiple partitions reveal hidden patterns in the data and allow for analysis from different perspectives. The recent advent of \llmlong{s} (\llm{s}) and multimodal \llm{s} (\mllm{s}) has further enhanced MC by enabling \textit{users to specify} the clustering criterion (\eg group by \texttt{suit}) in natural language~\citep{kwon2023image,yao2024multi}. While incorporating a human in the loop is appealing, as it grants the user control over the clustering results, specifying a meaningful text criterion still requires \textit{prior understanding} of the image collections. This task becomes cumbersome, especially due to the proliferation and high scene complexity inherent in large volumes of visual data.

\input{tables/diff_settings}
In this work, we propose to ease the burden of specifying text criteria off the user by \textit{automatically discovering} the clustering criteria, and introduce the task of \tasktitle (\taskshort), as shown in \reffig{teaser}{(top)}. To tackle \taskshort, we propose a general two-stage framework \methodfirsttitle (\textbf{\methodshort}), powered by cutting-edge \mllm{s} and \llm{s}, that first \textit{discovers} latent criteria (\eg \texttt{Activity} and \texttt{Location}) from a given collection of images, and then \textit{groups} the images into semantic clusters (\eg ``Surfing'',``Skateboarding'' under \texttt{Activity}) for every discovered clustering criterion. To reason at a dataset level, \methodshort first translates the visual data into textual data (\eg captions), which in turn are then collectively used as a proxy for the \llm{} to discover the hidden patterns in the image collection. The uniqueness of \methodshort lies in its: (i) \textit{comprehensiveness}, as it can exhaustively discover multiple clustering criteria that may not be evident to a user; (ii) \textit{interpretability}, as it outputs cluster names in natural language as opposed to traditional MC methods; (iii) \textit{flexibility}, as it can discover clusters at multiple granularities (coarse to fine-grained), without needing to specify the number of clusters a priori; and (iv) \textit{generality}, as, unlike the existing work~\citep{yao2024multi}, it is not limited to object-centric images but can handle complex scenes having fine-grained details. We summarize the key differences among various clustering solutions in \reftab{diff_table} and elaborate in \refapp{relatedwork}.

Current benchmarks for evaluating multiple clustering either lack realism (\clevr, \fruit, \card) or offer a limited number of criteria (\action), as shown in \reffig{demo_benchmarks}. To advance research in \taskshort, we introduce two challenging new benchmarks, \ourcoco and \ourfood, which depict images in daily life contexts and allow the dataset to be clustered as per four distinct criteria. Extensive experimental analyses on both existing and novel benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed \methodshort can effectively discover meaningful clustering criteria and successfully group semantically similar images (some examples of clustering results by \methodshort are shown in \reffig{teaser} (top)).

Lastly, we apply \methodshort to a variety of applications, including discovering biases in real-world datasets and text-to-image generative models, as well as analyzing the popularity of social media images. For example, as shown in \reffig{teaser}{(bottom)}, \methodshort can uncover less commonly studied biases (\eg \texttt{Hair color}) in \dalle-generated~\citep{betker2023improving} images, beyond the well-known biases (\eg \texttt{Gender}) that may have already been corrected for. It achieves this by discovering various human-interpretable semantic clusters (\eg ``Dark Hair'', ``Grey Hair'') across different discovered criteria and identifying overpopulated clusters. Similarly, it can identify the semantic visual elements that contribute to the popularity of social images, offering valuable insights to related practitioners. These results suggest that \methodshort is an automatic, versatile, and highly practical tool, opening up numerous new application opportunities for future research and providing the potential to generate insights from unstructured visual data at scale across various domains.



\section{\tasktitle}

\lblsec{problem_formulation}
In this section, we first formally define the task of \textit{\tasktitle{} (\taskshort{})}, and then introduce the existing and newly proposed benchmarks for \taskshort{}.

\myparagraph{Task Definition:}
Given a collection of $N$ unlabeled images $\data = \{\bx_n\}_{n=1}^{N}$, the goal of \taskshort{} is to categorize $\data$ into semantic clusters based on multiple criteria $\manyrules = \{\onerule_l\}_{l=1}^{L}$, where $L$ is the total number of criteria. In detail, a criterion $R_l$ refers to the grouping \textit{theme} according to which a set of images can be organized (or partitioned), such as \texttt{Activity}, \texttt{Location}, \texttt{Mood}, or \texttt{Time of Day}. 
All semantic clusters $\mathcal{C}_l$ under a given criterion  $R_l$ should align with the theme of that criterion. 
For instance, the semantic clusters under the criterion \texttt{Activity} should reflect activities depicted in the images (\eg ``Kayaking'', ``Cooking''). The same dataset $\data$, when grouped by a different criterion such as \texttt{Location}, could be clustered into categories like ``Outdoor'', ``Indoor'', and so on.

Unlike \ictc~\citep{kwon2023image} and \mmap~\citep{yao2024multi}, where a human operator pre-defines the clustering criteria and the number of clusters $K_l$, both the criteria names and the cluster counts are \textit{unknown} in \taskshort. A \taskshort framework must automatically \textit{discover} both the criteria and the corresponding clusters from $\data$. In contrast to classic MC setting, both the criteria and cluster names in \taskshort{} are described in natural language, making the discovered substructure human-interpretable.

\myparagraph{Benchmarks:}
Evaluating \taskshort methods requires benchmarks that can be partitioned under multiple valid criteria. Currently, only a few benchmarks~\citep{yu2024multiple} support the evaluation of \taskshort methods: \fruit{}~\citep{muresan2018fruit}, \card{}~\citep{kaggle2022card}, \action{}~\citep{kwon2023image}, and \clevr{}~\citep{vaze2024no}. As shown in \reffig{demo_benchmarks}, these benchmarks are limited by their object-centric nature with simple backgrounds (\eg \fruit{}), an insufficient number of criteria (\eg up to three in \action{}), and a lack of photorealism due to synthetic generation (\eg \clevr{}).

Given that the data encountered in real-world applications is more complex, we propose two \textit{new} benchmarks for \taskshort: \ourfood{} and \ourcoco{}. \ourfood{} is sourced from Food-101~\citep{bossard2014food}, which includes 101 \texttt{Food type} (original annotations), along with new annotations for 15 \texttt{Cuisine} types, 5 \texttt{Courses} types, and 4 \texttt{Diet} preferences, totaling \textit{four} clustering criteria. Additionally, we constructed \ourcoco{} using images from COCO-val~\citep{lin2014microsoft}, where we annotated \textit{four} criteria  with varying number of clusters: 64 \texttt{Activity}, 19 \texttt{Location}, 20 \texttt{Mood}, and 6 \texttt{Time of day}.
Examples of these newly constructed benchmarks are shown in \reffig{demo_benchmarks}. Further details, including the full list of cluster names and the annotation pipeline, are provided in \refapp{app_datasets}.


\section{Methodology: \methodtitle}

\lblsec{method}
Partitioning an unstructured image collection into semantic clusters based on different (unknown) criteria is challenging, as it requires reasoning over the visual contents of all the images \textit{concurrently}. Specifically, the system needs to first find commonalities among the images for discovering the partitioning criterion (or theme) and then group the images into semantic clusters according to the discovered criterion. To tackle the challenging \taskshort task, we propose a two-stage framework, named \methodtitle (\methodshort), that significantly \textit{deviates} from the commonly used technique of deep feature-based clustering, and instead uses \textit{text descriptions as a proxy} to reason over the images and uncover hidden patterns in $\data$. We elaborate the key design differences between \methodshort and related work in \refapp{relatedwork}.

\myparagraph{System overview:} As illustrated in \reffig{system}, the proposed \methodshort consists of two modules: Criteria Proposer and Semantic Grouper. The Criteria Proposer (or Proposer in short) processes the entire image set $\data$ to discover diverse common themes among the images and proposes grouping criteria $\manyrules$ in natural language (\eg \texttt{Location}). Once the criteria are proposed, the Semantic Grouper (or Grouper in short) discovers distinct semantic clusters that adhere to each criterion $\onerule_l$ at varying levels of semantic granularity and assigns images to their respective clusters (\eg ``Climbing gym''). In this work, we explore various design choices for both the Proposer and Grouper, detailed in the following subsections. Full implementation details, including exact prompts, are provided in \refapp{app_prompt}.

\subsection{Criteria Proposer}
As shown in \reffig{system}{(left)}, the Proposer takes the target image collection as input and outputs a set of discovered grouping criteria, expressed in natural language. The core of the Proposer design lies in its ability to \textit{concurrently} analyze and reason over the visual content of a large set of images. With this in mind, we explore three systematic approaches, which we describe below.

\myparagraph{Image-based Proposer:} We start with a baseline that leverages a state-of-the-art \mllm{}~\citep{li2024llava} designed for multi-image reasoning out-of-the-box to directly infer the criteria given a set of images. To reason over a set of images, which is crucial for discovering the criterion, we stitch a batch of images into a $8 \times 8$ image grid and input it to the \mllm{} as a single image. We then prompt the \mllm{} to propose grouping criteria for the images in the grid. All the resulting criteria from all such image grids are accumulated in a criteria pool, denoted as $\rulespool$.

\myparagraph{Tag-based Proposer:} Next, we explore a \textit{tag}-based approach that uses an open-vocabulary tagger~\citep{radford2021learning} to assign 10 tags to each image in $\data$, using the vocabulary from WordNet~\citep{miller1995wordnet}. These tags serve as semantic descriptors, translating the visual content of each image into textual form. We then gather all the assigned tags from the images, input them into a \llm{}, and prompt it to discover grouping criteria based on these tags.

\myparagraph{Caption-based Proposer (Main):} 
While image tags effectively capture certain visual semantics, we found that they predominantly reflect object-related content. To encompass a broader spectrum of visual information—such as \textit{environmental settings} or \textit{interactions}—we instead use a \mllm{} to generate captions for each image in $\data$. Descriptive captions provide a richer and more holistic semantic context. 
Staying within the 128k token limit of modern \llm{s}~\citep{meta2024llama31, openai2024gpt4o}, we feed a subset of captions into a \llm{}, which we prompt to elicit partitioning criteria. The criteria generated for each subset are accumulated in $\rulespool$. Experiments in \refsec{assessment_proposer} show that the Caption-based Proposer is the {most effective}; so we use it as our main method and consider the others as baselines.

\myparagraph{Criteria refinement:}
Since the Proposers operate on subsets, the criteria accumulated in $\rulespool$ may include redundant or noisy entries. To address this, we input all initially proposed criteria from $\rulespool$ into a \llm{}~\citep{meta2024llama31}, prompting it to consolidate similar criteria and discard noisy ones. This step refines and updates the criteria pool into $\manyrules$, which is ready to be used in the subsequent stage.

\subsection{Semantic Grouper}
The automatically discovered criteria $\manyrules$ serve as thematic indicators for revealing the semantic substructures (or clusters) within the image set $\data$. To uncover these substructures, as shown in \reffig{system}{(right)}, the Grouper takes $\data$ and each criterion $\onerule_{l}$ as inputs. It then discovers cluster names $\mathcal{C}_l = \{s_{k}^{l}\}_{k=1}^{K_{l}}$ conditioned on $\onerule_{l}$ and assigns each image to its corresponding cluster. The core design of the Grouper focuses on \textit{aligning} semantic substructure discovery with a specified partitioning criterion. Similar to the Proposer, we also explore three distinct approaches for the Grouper.

Furthermore, as clusters under a given criterion can be formed at varying semantic granularities, depending on user preferences, we have designed our Grouper to cluster $\data$ at three levels of granularity: coarse, middle, and fine-grained. This design enables \methodshort to provide new insights into the data at different granularities.
For example, for the criterion food \texttt{Cuisine}, \methodshort can organize images at a coarse-grained continental level (\eg ``European'' or ``Asian''), a middle-grained regional level (\eg ``Mediterranean'' or ``Southeast Asian''), or a fine-grained national level (\eg ``Italian'' or ``Thai'').

\myparagraph{Image-based Grouper:} 
Given a target criterion $\onerule_{l}\in\manyrules$, we first prompt a \llm{} to generate a question $q_l$ specific to $\onerule_l$-\ie, for criterion \texttt{Mood} the generated question is: ``What mood is conveyed by this image? Answer with an abstract, common, and specific category name, respectively.'' We then use $q_l$ to guide a \vqalong{} (\vqa{}) model to infer semantic cluster names for each image as $\left(s_{\text{coarse}}^{l}, s_{\text{mid}}^{l}, s_{\text{fine}}^{l}\right) = \text{VQA}(\bx_{n}, q_{l})$. Consequently, by aggregating the cluster assignment results at each granularity level across $\data$, we can derive multi-granularity semantic substructures.

\myparagraph{Tag-based Grouper:} 
Given a criterion, we prompt a \llm{} to generate a list of common, middle-grained tags specific to that criterion (\eg{} ``Recreational facility''). Following \citet{liu2024shine}, we then obtain coarse- and fine-grained tags by querying the \llm{} to generate super- and sub-categories (\eg{} ``Indoor'' and ``Climbing gym'') for each middle-grained tag. Unlike lexical databases such as WordNet or ConceptNet~\citep{speer2017conceptnet}, which do not support free-form input and may be limited in accommodating certain criteria, tag synthesis using a \llm{} provides a flexible and reliable alternative. Subsequently, we use an image tagger to assign the most relevant tag from the candidate tags at each granularity to each image, resulting in multi-granularity substructures after aggregation.

\myparagraph{Caption-based Grouper (Main):} 
We prompt a \mllm{} to generate captions that specifically focus on the target criterion for each image, as $e_{n}^{l} = \text{MLLM}(\bx_{n}, \onerule_{l})$. Next, we use a \llm{} in a three-step process to assign images to clusters at multiple granularity levels: \textit{i) Initial Naming}: First, we prompt the \llm{} to assign a class name to each caption as $s_{n}^{l}=\text{LLM}(e_{n}^{l}, \onerule_{l})$, resulting in an initial set of names $\manynames_{\text{init}}^{l}$ of size $N$; \textit{ii) Multi-granularity Cluster Refinement}: Using these initial names as basis, we prompt the \llm{} to refine them into three structured granularity levels: $\left(\manynames_{\text{coarse}}^{l}, \manynames_{\text{mid}}^{l}, \manynames_{\text{fine}}^{l}\right)=\text{LLM}(\manynames_{\text{init}}^{l}, \onerule_l)$. These structured names serve as candidates for cluster assignment; \textit{iii) Final Assignment}: Each image is then assigned a class name from each granularity level based on its caption, as $\left(s_{\text{coarse}}^{l}, s_{\text{mid}}^{l}, s_{\text{fine}}^{l}\right) = \text{LLM}(e_{n}^{l}, \manynames_{\text{coarse}}, \manynames_{\text{mid}}, \manynames_{\text{fine}})$. Experiments in \refsec{assessment_grouper} show that the Caption-based Grouper performs the best; thus we use it as our main method.


\section{Experiments}

\lblsec{method_assessment}
\myparagraph{Implementation details:}
For all experiments, we run with our proposed \methodshort framework, using the following configuration: \textit{i)} CLIP ViT-L/14~\citep{radford2021learning} as the Tagger, \textit{ii)} \llava{-7B}~\citep{liu2024llava} as the \mllm{}, \textit{iii)} \llamathreeone{-8B}~\citep{meta2024llama31} as the \llm{}, and \textit{iv)} \blip{-2 Flan-T5$_{\text{XXL}}$}~\citep{li2023blip} as the \vqa{} model. For the Image-based Proposer, however, we use \multillava{-7B}~\citep{li2024llava} as the \mllm{} due to its strong multi-image reasoning capability. Additionally, we explore a variant of the Image-based Grouper using \llava{-7B} as the \vqa{} model. Further implementation details, including exact prompts, are provided in \refapp{app_prompt}.

\myparagraph{Evaluation metrics for Criteria Discovery:}
Given the discovered criteria set $\manyrules$ of the proposer, we assess its quality from two dimensions: \textit{i) Comprehensiveness:} We compute \tprtitle{} (\tpr{})~\citep{csurka2024could} for the predicted criteria relative to the annotated ones as $\text{TPR} =\frac{\mid\manyrules \cap \gtrules\mid}{|\gtrules|}$, to assess to what extent the predicted set covers the ground-truth set $\gtrules$; \textit{ii) Diversity:} We compute the pairwise semantic similarity between the predicted criteria $\manyrules$, using Sentence-BERT~\citep{reimers2019sentence}, and convert it into a diversity measure by subtracting the similarity from 1. The final score is the average of these pairwise values, reflecting how well the proposed criteria avoid redundancy and capture distinct aspects of the data—\ie, criteria such as \texttt{Location} and \texttt{Place} are nearly identical and provide little additional insight. A higher score indicates better diversity. It is important to note that the number of valid grouping criteria is subjective and potentially unlimited, making False Positives difficult to define. Thus, we use \tpr{} as the primary evaluation metric.

\myparagraph{Evaluation metrics for substructure uncovering:}
Following \citet{conti2023vocabulary} and \citet{liu2024democratizing}, for each criterion and its substructure uncovered by the grouper, we evaluate its alignment with the substructure defined by the ground-truth labels along two dimensions: \textit{i) Semantic Consistency:} For each image, we measure the semantic similarity between its assigned cluster name and the ground-truth label under the current criterion using Sentence-BERT. The average similarity across the dataset is reported as \sacctitle (\sacc), reflecting how well the predicted substructure semantically aligns with the ground-truth. \textit{ii) Structural Consistency:} We compute the \cacclong (\cacc) between the predicted and ground-truth substructures (clusterings) to evaluate how accurately the predicted structure groups images. By combining \cacc and \sacc, we evaluate the overall alignment of the predicted substructure with the ground-truth across various criteria.

\myparagraph{Evaluation strategy:}
Since the granularity of the ground-truth annotations is unknown during evaluation, we choose the substructure from the three predicted granularities that achieves the highest \cacc relative to the ground-truth for assessing our \methodshort. Unlike methods~\citep{kwon2023image, yao2024multi}, which rely on the ground-truth number of clusters to create flat clusterings that perfectly match the ground-truth granularity, we believe our approach offers a fair and practical evaluation strategy for systems capable of providing multi-granularity output.

\subsection{Study of the Criteria Proposer}
\lblsec{assessment_proposer}

\myparagraph{Expanding ground-truth criteria for comprehensive evaluation:}  
The newly introduced benchmarks provide up to \textit{four} ground-truth criteria to evaluate the predicted criteria, but for image collections with high scene complexity (\eg, \ourcoco), these are still insufficient to cover all valid grouping options. To address this, we expanded the ground-truth criteria for each of the six benchmarks in \refsec{problem_formulation} by using human annotators to identify additional criteria. This resulted in expanded sets that have: \{10, 4, 11, 7, 17, 11\} distinct criteria for \{\fruit{}, \card{}, \action{}, \clevr{}, \ourcoco{}, \ourfood{}\}.
We refer to the per-image annotated criteria set (see \reffig{demo_benchmarks}) as \textbf{Basic} ground truth and the expanded set as \textbf{Hard}.
See \refapp{app_benchmark_criteria} for the exact criteria and annotation details.

\myparagraph{Which Criteria Proposer is best?}
Our comparative results, presented in \reffig{study_proposer}(a-b), show that the \textit{Caption-based Criteria Proposer consistently outperforms
its counterparts
by a significant margin} across all six benchmarks.
As shown in \reffig{study_proposer}(a), the caption-based proposer achieves an average of 100\% \tpr on the Basic criteria set and 75.1\% \tpr on the Hard set across the benchmarks.
Its superior performance is particularly evident under the Hard criteria, where it surpasses the second-best tag-based proposer by +32.2\% \tpr.
Meanwhile, 
the criteria predicted by the caption-based proposer also show greater diversity in \reffig{study_proposer}(b), coming closest to the diversity of human-annotated criteria.

Although the tag-based proposer performs well on object-centric benchmarks like \clevr{} or \ourfood{}, it significantly lags behind the caption-based proposer on more challenging benchmarks such as \ourcoco{} and \action{}, which feature more complex, realistic images. This discrepancy arises because image tagging is a more object-centric way of representing image semantics, potentially missing the rich context depicted in images.
Conversely, while the image-based proposer outperforms the tag-based proposer on \ourcoco{} and \action{}, it is less effective overall because it is limited to reasoning over a small subset of images and loses visual details when combining images into a grid.
\myparagraph{Studying the influence of image quantity on Criteria Discovery:}
\reffig{study_proposer}(c) shows the \tpr performance of the caption-based proposer across varying image scales (100\% to only 1 image) used for criteria proposing, tested on the Hard criteria sets of six benchmarks. Interestingly, satisfactory performance is achieved with just a few images in \textit{object-centric} benchmarks like \card and \clevr. In fact, even a single image often provides sufficient information for reasonable criteria discovery in these object-centric datasets, where a brief glimpse often represents the entirety adequately. For example, seeing one playing card allows the proposer to easily suggest criteria like ``Rank'' and ``Suit'' in the \card dataset. However, this does not hold for more complex datasets like \ourcoco, \ourfood, and \action, which contain diverse and realistic scenarios. Here, a reduction in image scale leads to a clear drop in \tpr performance, as these datasets require a larger set of images to capture their intricate and varied thematic criteria. Since \methodshort operates \textit{without} prior knowledge of the dataset, we default to using the \textit{entire} dataset to ensure comprehensive criteria discovery.

\subsection{Study of the Semantic Grouper}
\lblsec{assessment_grouper}
\myparagraph{Which Semantic Grouper is best?}
To determine which grouper best uncovers the substructures indicated by the discovered criteria and aligns most closely with the ground truth, we use the \hmeantitle (\hmean) of the \cacc and \sacc for each criterion. This allows us to identify the best overall performer for each criterion.
In the comparison, to further illustrate the relative performance position of our framework, we first establish a \textit{pseudo} upper-bound reference by leveraging the zero-shot classification capabilities of CLIP ViT-L14~\citep{radford2021learning} with ground-truth labels for each criterion, where the grouping criteria, cluster names, and number of clusters are \textit{all known}. Additionally, we apply KMeans~\citep{ahmed2020k} with the ground-truth number of clusters, using robust visual features from CLIP ViT-L14, DINOv1-B/16~\citep{li2022emergent}, and DINOv2-G/14~\citep{oquab2023dinov2} to establish baseline positions for clustering performance.

Our comparative analysis, presented in \reffig{study_grouper}, shows that the \textit{Caption-based Semantic Grouper performs best}, ranking first in 12 out of 19 tested criteria based on the \hmean, across six benchmarks.
Specifically, in terms of \cacc, the caption-based grouper achieves an average of 59.9\% across all tested criteria, closely aligning with the pseudo-upper-bound average of 58.1\% and significantly outperforming feature representation-driven baselines, with the highest being DINOv2 at 40.6\% \cacc. This demonstrates the effectiveness of our text-driven approach, as the cluster names inferred by the caption-based provide robust guidance for grouping. For \sacc, while the caption-based grouper falls short of the upper-bound reference average of 74.2\%, where exact ground-truth class names are used, it still achieves a notable average of 60.5\% across all tested criteria, surpassing its counterparts. To some extent, this gap in semantic consistency is expected due to the open nature of the semantic space—\ie, names like ``Joyful'', ``Happy'', and ``Cheerful'' are often used to describe the same \texttt{Mood}, yet they do not exhibit full semantic equivalence.
The \blip{-2} image-based grouper also shows commendable performance, ranking first in 5 of the 19 tested criteria. This suggests that the criterion-customized prompts generated by \llm{} help \blip{-2} accurately label relevant visual content. However, its per-image labeling approach may lead to noisy clusters due to a lack of overall alignment between them.
The tag-based grouper lags behind in all benchmarks likely due to a mismatch between the generated tags and the concepts in the dataset.

\myparagraph{Comparison with criterion-conditioned clustering methods:} 
We compare our top-performing Caption-based Grouper with recent methods that support text-conditioned clustering: \textit{i)}~\ictc~\citep{kwon2023image}, which uses LLaVA~\citep{liu2024visual} and GPT-4~\citep{openai2023gpt4} to cluster images based on user-specified text criteria, and \textit{ii)}~\mmap~\citep{yao2024multi}, which generates cluster names with GPT-4 for user-specified criteria as pseudo prototypes, then uses these prototypes to derive proxy word embeddings for each image via prompt tuning CLIP~\citep{radford2021learning} before applying KMeans clustering. It is important to note that \textit{both \ictc and \mmap require user-provided (ground-truth) criteria and the number of clusters ($K_l$) as auxiliary prior input to work. }. In stark contrast, our grouper uses the \textit{criteria discovered} by the proposer and requires \textit{no} pre-set cluster counts to forge high-quality clusters, operating entirely automatically. As shown in \reftab{comp_sota}, our caption-based grouper not only outperforms \mmap but also achieves performance comparable to \ictc across the six tested benchmarks. This highlights that our framework can deliver equally satisfactory results on the \taskshort task \textit{without} requiring users to pre-define grouping criteria or cluster counts in their large image collections. We provide implementation details of \ictc and \mmap in \refapp{app_implementation_compared}.

\myparagraph{Necessity of multi-granularity cluster refinement:}
To validate the effectiveness of the multi-granularity cluster refinement design, we design control experiments with our Caption-based Grouper, using three different methods for constructing cluster names to organize images: \textit{i) Initial Names:} using the initially assigned names as the final output, \textit{ii) Flat Refinement:} prompting the \llm{} to refine the initial names into a flat list with a unified granularity, and our \textit{iii) Multi-granularity Refinement}. We then compare the performance of these approaches in \reffig{study_granularity}. We observe that both refinement methods significantly improve clustering accuracy compared to using noisy initial names, highlighting the importance of having granularity-consistent cluster names for accurately revealing substructures. Additionally, our proposed multi-granularity method surpasses flat refinement, as it enables the grouper to generate clustering results at different granularities, providing greater flexibility in aligning with the label granularity-the user-preferred level of detail.

\section{Applications}

\lblsec{applications}

\myparagraph{Discovering and mitigating dataset bias:} Given an image collection that contains \textit{spurious correlations}~\citep{geirhos2020shortcut}, we are curious whether we can proactively find this issue caused by data bias directly from the training images \textit{without} relying on either the annotations~\citep{sagawa2019distributionally} or \textit{post hoc} misclassified images~\citep{kim2024discovering}.
\begin{wrapfigure}{R}{0.39\textwidth}
\centering
\lesspace
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/app_celebA.pdf}
    \caption{
    \textbf{Results of dataset bias discovery and mitigation.} Worst group and average accuracies(\%) are reported.
    }
    \lblfig{app_celebA}
    \lesspace
\end{wrapfigure}
As a case study, we applied the proposed \methodshort framework to the 162k training images of the CelebA~\citep{liu2015deep} dataset—a binary hair color classification dataset where the target label ``Blond'' is spuriously correlated with the demographic attribute ``Female'' in its training split. Additional details are provided in \refapp{app_dataset_bias}.

\myparagraph{Findings:} As expected, our method identified the grouping criteria \texttt{Hair color} and \texttt{Gender}.
Next, we analyzed the predicted gender distributions within the ``Blond'' and ``Not Blond'' (all other colors) clusters. As shown in \reffig{app_celebA}(a), we observed that the gender distribution within the ``Blond'' cluster is highly skewed, with 86.5\% of the images representing females, closely matching the ground-truth distribution (94.3\%).
Such an imbalance confirms the potential issue of spurious correlations between ``Blond'' and ``Female''.
To further validate this observation, following B2T~\citep{kim2024discovering}, we used our predicted distributions to train a debiased model with GroupDRO~\citep{sagawa2019distributionally} and compared it with other unsupervised bias mitigation methods, including JTT~\citep{liu2021just}, CNC~\citep{zhang2022correct}, B2T, and GroupDRO trained with ground-truth labels.
As shown in \reffig{app_celebA}(b), our debiased model achieved robust performance, comparable to that of B2T, demonstrating the reliability of its discovered distributions.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/app_occupation_main.pdf}
    \caption{
    \textbf{Bias discovery results in T2I models.} Bias evaluation results are shown for the associated occupations.
    }
    \lblfig{app_occupation}
\end{figure}
\myparagraph{Discovering novel bias in Text-to-Image Diffusion Models:}
Stereotypical biases related to gender or race~\citep{naik2023social} in images generated by widely accessible text-to-image (T2I) models like Stable Diffusion XL (SDXL)~\citep{podell2023sdxl} and \dalle~\citep{betker2023improving} have sparked intense discussions~\citep{nicoletti2023bias} and significant research~\citep{bianchi2023easily}. However, beyond these well-known biases extensively studied in social science, we wonder: \textit{Are there other biases present in T2I-generated images?}
To explore this question, we selected \textit{nine} occupations (\eg, Nurse or CEO) from prior studies~\citep{bianchi2023easily, bolukbasi2016man} as subjects for our case study on bias.
For each occupation, we generated 100 images using the prompt ``A portrait photo of a $<$OCCUPATION$>$'' with both DALL-E3 and SDXL, resulting in a total of 1.8k images (see \reffig{gallery_dalle} and \reffig{gallery_sdxl} for examples).
We applied \methodshort to these images and automatically discovered 10 grouping criteria (possible bias dimensions) along with their predicted distributions for each occupation image set. To inspect the biases, we calculated the normalized entropy of each distribution to quantify the bias intensity, as proposed by \citet{d2024openbias}, and identified the dominant cluster—the one containing the most images—as the possible bias direction.
Furthermore, we conducted a user study with 54 anonymous participants to assess how well our bias discovery results align with human judgment. We found that our system's bias intensity scores closely matched human ratings—with an Absolute Mean Error (AME) of 0.1396 on a scale from 0 to 1—and that its predicted bias directions agreed with human evaluations 72.3\% of the time. We highlight some key findings below and provide complete results and evaluations in \refapp{app_occupation}.

\myparagraph{Findings:}
In \reffig{app_occupation}, we present some of our notable findings.
Without any predefined biases, our method successfully identifies well-known social biases associated with certain occupations.
As shown in \reffig{app_occupation}{(a–c)}, the SDXL-generated images exhibit \textit{near-total} social bias for occupations like Nurse, Firefighter, and Basketball Player, displaying gender and racial imbalances that exceed official statistics such as those from \citet{blsEmployedPersons}.
In contrast, \dalle shows markedly improved performance in mitigating social biases—likely due to its bias mitigation ``guardrails''~\citep{dalle_guards}.
More importantly, as shown in \reffig{app_occupation}{(d–f)}, our method revealed previously unnoticed novel bias dimensions where both models exhibit moderate biases. For example, we found that SDXL strongly associates CEOs with ``Grey'' hair color, while \dalle favors ``Dark'' hair. Interestingly, and contrary to trends observed in well-known social biases, \dalle shows stronger biases than SDXL in \texttt{Hair style} and \texttt{Grooming} for Nurse and Teacher. For instance, 91\% of the Teacher images generated by \dalle depict individuals who are \textit{clean-shaven} (\reffig{app_occupation}{(f)}).
The gap in \dalle's performance between addressing well-known and novel biases suggests that even industrial T2I models with guardrail systems may overlook some novel biases, underscoring the importance of thoroughly examining biases beyond the commonly recognized ones.

\myparagraph{Analyzing Social media image popularity:}
Given a set of social media photos with significantly varying popularity for each photo, it is natural to ask: \textit{What visual element makes a photo popular?}
As a case study, we applied \methodshort to 4.1k Flickr photos from the SPID dataset~\citep{ortis2019prediction}, each accompanied by a popularity score based on the number of views.
After our method grouped the photos into semantic clusters based on 10 discovered criteria, we calculated two scores for each cluster: \textit{i)} $\bar{P}$: the average popularity of photos within the cluster, and \textit{ii)} $\bar{P}_w$: the average popularity $\bar{P}$ weighted by the cluster's proportion relative to the entire dataset.
Here, we consider the cluster with the highest $\bar{P}$ as \textit{viral}, meaning its photos are highly popular but may be few in number, and the cluster with the highest $\bar{P}_w$ as \textit{major popular}, where the majority of popular photos are concentrated.
We discuss the key findings below and provide complete results in \refapp{app_popularity}.

\myparagraph{Findings:}
As shown in our analysis in \reffig{app_popularity}, by combining the interpretable groupings from our method with popularity scores, the visual elements that drive virality (clusters with the highest $\bar{P}$) and the common traits shared among widely popular images (clusters with the highest $\bar{P}_w$) across different dimensions clearly emerged.
Interestingly, we further observe that viral visual elements often exhibit sharp semantic \textit{contrast} with those of the majority of popular images, such as \textit{Musical activities} vs.\ \textit{Rest and relaxation}, and \textit{High-intensity expressions} vs.\ \textit{Neutral emotion}. This suggests that viral content is more attention-grabbing, likely due to novel, unexpected, or intense visuals that stand out, especially in an era where the average attention span is less than 9 seconds~\citep{mcspadden2015you, farid_2024}. 
During our analysis, as shown in \reffig{app_popularity}, we unexpectedly found that some of the most popular images in certain clusters contained \textit{not safe for work (NSFW)} content, which had not been previously identified in the SPID dataset. This suggests that some images gain popularity due to negative or provocative visuals. It also highlights the need for more thorough inspection of visual datasets to avoid harm, and our proposed framework can be a useful tool for this purpose.


\section{Conclusion}

\lblsec{conclusion}
In this work, we introduce the task of \tasklong and propose \methodshort, a system that automatically discovers grouping criteria in natural language from large image collections and uncovers interpretable data substructures based on these criteria. We rigorously evaluate various design choices of \methodshort on four existing and two newly proposed benchmarks, and demonstrate its ability to reveal valuable insights that might otherwise go unnoticed in various real-world applications.
