\title{Accelerating semidefinite programming beyond limit: ADMM with tune-free operator stepsize}

\begin{abstract}
In this work, we significantly alleviate the long-standing scalability issue of semidefinite programming (SDP), by equipping a novel tune-free operator stepsize to the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) optimizer. To our best knowledge, this is the first operator stepsize in the context of SDP. More importantly, it is tune-free and computationally cheap (defined on dot product). Preliminary tests show that our operator ADMM surpasses the acceleration limit of the standard scalar version (limit found via grid search), i.e., our operator stepsize can outperform an arbitrarily fine tuned scalar one.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


Semidefinite programming (SDP) is widely recognized as one of the most important breakthroughs in the last century, with wide applications across fields, including machine learning, control, robotics, and communications. However, there exists a long-standing obstacle for SDP to gain further popularity — the scalability issue. It arises in middle or large scale problems, where an exponentially growing computation cost with data dimension is generally unacceptable. How to improve the scalability has been intensively studied, with several main directions: (i) exploiting structures, e.g., sparsity, symmetry, low-rankness etc.; (ii) approximation via linear and second-order cone programs. (iii) augmented Lagrangian methods, such as Newton-CG and alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM); A comprehensive survey on scalability can be found in Majumdar et al. (2020). This manuscript will focus on the ADMM approach.  

To start, we briefly review some history. SDP was developed as a generalization of linear programming (LP). The first polynomial-time solver of LP was introduced by Karmarkar (1984), termed the interiorpoint method (IPM). Later, Nesterov & Nemirovsky (1988); Nesterov & Nemirovskii (1994) extended it to any convex program, provided that the function is self-concordant. SDP can easily satisfy this condition, and hence been considered not much harder to solve than LP Vandenberghe & Boyd (1996).  

Despite the great successes of IPMs, they are in general not well-suited for problems of middle or large scale. This is related to their second-order nature, where an inverse of the Hessian matrix is required, or at least an approximate inverse. Such an inverse operation is highly expensive for a large size variable. Even worse, the Hessian matrix is in general dense and rarely admits some structures like sparsity to reduce the computation cost. In the literature, employing first-order algorithms Beck (2017), Teboulle (2018) is considered one of the most promising directions. An outstanding candidate is ADMM Glowinski & Marroco (1975); Gabay & Mercier (1976). It has become increasingly popular, largely owes to a comprehensive survey by Boyd et al. (2011). In fact, one may already encounter ADMM, except under a different name. In recent years, many well-known algorithms have been revealed as equivalent to ADMM, such as the Douglas-Rachford Splitting (DRS) Lions & Mercier (1979); Douglas & Rachford (1956) and the Primal-Dual Hybrid Gradient (PDHG) method Pock et al. (2009); Esser et al. (2010); Chambolle & Pock (2011); O’Connor & Vandenberghe (2020).  

The procedures for the first-order algorithms to solve SDP are largely similar, mainly differ on how to guarantee the solution being positive semidefinite (PSD). There are 3 typical strategies. (i) Directly define the variable in a quadratic form ${\pmb R}^{T}{\pmb R}$ , which is always PSD, see e.g. Burer & Monteiro (2003; 2005); Wang et al. (2023). However, its efficiency highly depends on the dimension of $\boldsymbol{R}$ , i.e., only efficient if it is low-rank. (ii) Enforcing PSD by a projected variable, denoted as $\Pi_{\mathbb{S}_{+}}(X)$ The success owes to that projector $\Pi_{\mathbb{S}_{+}}$ is strongly semi-smooth Sun & Sun (2002), and an inexact semi-smooth Newton-CG method can apply, see e.g. Zhao et al. (2010). (iii) The last approach is via ADMM, which is a general framework that can apply to general convex problems, even some non-convex issues, see Boyd et al. (2011). Its application to SDP is well studied in Wen et al. (2010).  

Our work corresponds to the ADMM method, most related to Wen et al. (2010). We achieved significant advances. (i) To our best knowledge, ours is the first operator stepsize in the context of SDP, not limited to the ADMM solver. For example, a diagonal matrix stepsize is not applicable here (no closed-form iterates). (ii) Our operator is specially designed, inspired by the Schur complement lemma. It enjoys the benefits of closed-form ADMM iterates and low computational cost (defined on dot product). (iii) Our operator stepsize is tune-free. It will be automatically updated based on a certain degree-4 polynomial. Numerically, we observed significant advantages compared to the empirical choice of scalar stepsize 1 and 1.6, as suggested in Wen et al. (2010). Even more, we performed a grid search to find the best scalar stepsize choice (least iteration number complexity sense), which can be viewed as the acceleration limit (not a priori knowledge). Preliminary tests show that our operator stepsize has surpassed such a limit.  

For notations, $\|\cdot\|$ denotes the Euclidean norm, induced by the inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ . By $\circ$ we denote the operator composition. The uppercase bold, lowercase bold, and not bold letters are used for matrices, vectors, and scalars, respectively.  

1.1 ADMM FRAMEWORK  

To start, we introduce the general ADMM framework. It involves two sub-problems that typically admit closed-form solutions for a scalar stepsize, but often not when generalized to an operator one.  

Consider a general convex program:  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}{\underset{{\boldsymbol{x}},z}{\mathrm{minimize}}}&{~f({\boldsymbol{x}})+g(z),}\\ {\mathrm{subject{to}}}&{~{\boldsymbol{A}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-\boldsymbol{B}{\boldsymbol{z}}={\boldsymbol{c}},}\end{array}
$$  

with functions $f,g$ being convex, closed and proper (lower semi-continuous) and bounded linear operators $A,\,B$ being injective. A solution is assumed exists.  

The standard ADMM iterates, with a scalar stepsize $\gamma>0$ , are  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{x^{k+1}=\underset{x}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;f(x)+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|A x-B z^{k}-c+\lambda^{k}/\gamma\|^{2},}\\ &{z^{k+1}=\underset{z}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;g(z)+\frac{\gamma}{2}\|A x^{k+1}-B z-c+\lambda^{k}/\gamma\|^{2},}\\ &{\lambda^{k+1}=\lambda+\gamma\big(\mathcal{A}x^{k+1}-\mathcal{B}z^{k+1}-c\big),}\end{array}
$$  

The above can be generalized to an operator stepsize,  

$$
\begin{array}{l}{{\displaystyle x^{k+1}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{x}\,f(x)+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}x-\mathcal{B}z^{k}-c+\mathcal{M}^{-1}\lambda^{k}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2},}}\\ {{\displaystyle z^{k+1}=\operatorname*{argmin}_{z}\,g(z)+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{A}x^{k+1}-\mathcal{B}z-c+\mathcal{M}^{-1}\lambda^{k}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2},}}\\ {{\displaystyle\lambda^{k+1}=\lambda^{k}+\mathcal{M}\big(\mathcal{A}x^{k+1}-\mathcal{B}z^{k+1}-c\big),}}\end{array}
$$  

where $\mathcal{M}\succ0$ is positive definite, and where $\|\pmb{v}\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\sqrt{\langle\pmb{v},\mathcal{M}\pmb{v}\rangle}$ is known as the $\mathcal{M}$ -norm.  

Owing to $\mathcal{M}$ being positive definite, the decomposition $\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{S}\circ\mathcal{S}$ always exists. We will directly discuss the selection of $\boldsymbol{S}$ , which is instantly transferable to $\mathcal{M}$ .  

1.2 SEMIDEFINITE PROGRAMMING  

Semidefinite programming (SDP) include two standard forms, see Vandenberghe & Boyd (1996).  

• (i) The standard primal SDP, which minimizes a linear function subject to a linear matrix inequality,  

$$
{\mathrm{subject}}10\quad{\cal A}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}{\cal A}_{i}\succeq0.
$$  

with $\pmb{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n},~i=0,1,\dotsc,m$ being symmetric matrices.  

$\bullet$ (ii) The standard dual SDP, which is written in a matrix variable,  

$$
{\begin{array}{r l}{\underset{X}{\operatorname{minimize}}}&{\;\;\langle\mathbf{A}_{0},X\rangle,}\\ {\operatorname{subject{to}}}&{\;\;\langle\mathbf{A}_{i},X\rangle=c_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,m,}\\ &{\;\;X\succeq0.}\end{array}}
$$  

The ADMM steps for solving the above two formulations will be similar, detailed below.  

1.3 ADMM SOLVER  

Here, we apply the abstract ADMM framework to solve the two SDP problems, equation primal and equation dual. We can compactly write them into  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}{\underset{X,Z}{\mathrm{minimize}}}&{{}~f(X)+\delta_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}}(Z),}\\ {\mathrm{subject\to}}&{{}~~~\mathcal{A}X=Z,}\end{array}
$$  

with $\pmb{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ , $\b{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ being matrix variables (can reduce to vectors), where $\delta_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}}$ denotes the indicator function on the semidefinite cone $\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}$ . The above unified framework can be specified into  

(i) equation primal via  

$$
f({\pmb x})=\langle{\pmb x},{\pmb x}\rangle,\quad{\mathcal{A}}{\pmb x}={\pmb A}_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}{\pmb A}_{i},
$$  

with $\pmb{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , dom $f=\mathbb{R}^{n}$ .  

(ii) equation dual via  

$$
f\left(X\right)=\langle A_{0},X\rangle,\quad\mathrm{dom}\,f=\{X\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\,|\,\langle A_{i},X\rangle=c_{i},\,\forall i\},
$$  

and $A=\mathcal{T}$ vanishes (identity operator).  

1.3.1 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS (SCALAR CASE)  

Here, we present the ADMM closed-form iterates for solving equation 1.2, given a scalar stepsize. (i) For equation primal, its $\Chi$ -update is given by  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{{x}^{k+1}=\ \left(\bar{A}^{T}\bar{A}\right)^{-1}\left(\bar{A}^{T}\left(Z^{k}-{\Lambda}^{k}/\gamma-\bar{A}_{0}\right)-c/\gamma\right),}\\ {\bar{A}=[\mathrm{vec}(A_{1}),\cdot\cdot\cdot\ ,\mathrm{vec}(A_{m})]\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times m},\bar{A}_{0}=\mathrm{vec}(A_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times1}.}\end{array}
$$  

(ii) For equation dual, its $\Chi$ -update is given by solving the following KKT system:  

$$
\left[\begin{array}{c c}{\gamma I}&{\bar{A}}\\ {\bar{A}^{T}}&{0}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{vec}(X^{k+1})}\\ {\mu}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{vec}(\gamma Z^{k}-\Lambda^{k}-A_{0})}\\ {c}\end{array}\right],
$$  

which is an overdetermined system that is instantly solvable via a pseudo inverse.  

The other iterates are the same for the primal and dual SDP. The Z-update is  

$$
Z^{k+1}=\ \Pi_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}}\,\left(A{\pmb X}^{k+1}+{\pmb\Lambda}^{k}/\gamma\right),
$$  

which is a projection, setting all the negative eigenvalues to zeros. At last,  

$$
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{k+1}=\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}^{k}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}\big(\mathcal{A}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{X}}^{k+1}-\mathbf{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{k+1}\big).
$$  

1.3.2 GENERALIZATION CHALLENGE  

The main challenge for employing an operator stepsize is that — in general, the ${\cal Z}_{}$ -update no longer admits a closed-form iterate, i.e., the following:  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{\pmb{Z}^{k+1}=\underset{z}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \delta_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}}(\pmb{Z})+\frac{1}{2}\|\pmb{A}\pmb{X}^{k+1}-\pmb{Z}+\mathcal{M}^{-1}\pmb{\Lambda}^{k}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{2},}\\ {=\underset{z}{\mathrm{argmin}}\ \delta_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}}(\pmb{Z})+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{S}\pmb{A}\pmb{X}^{k+1}-\mathcal{S}\pmb{Z}+\mathcal{S}^{-1}\pmb{\Lambda}^{k}\|^{2}.}\end{array}
$$  

does not admit a closed-form solution in general.  

1.4 OUR CONTRIBUTION  

Our contribution involves two main aspects. (i) First, we propose the following specially designed operator stepsize, inspired by the Schur complement lemma, see Proposition 2.1:  

$$
\begin{array}{r l r l}&{\boldsymbol{S}=\left[\begin{array}{c c c}{\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{2}}}\,\mathbf{1}_{1}}&&{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\,\mathbf{1}_{0}}\\ {\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}\,\mathbf{1}_{0}}&&{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_{2}}\end{array}\right],}&{\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}{c c c}{\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}}}\,\mathbf{1}_{1}}&&{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}}\,\,\mathbf{1}_{0}}\\ {\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}}}\,\mathbf{1}_{0}}&&{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}}\,\mathbf{1}_{2}}\end{array}\right].}\end{array}
$$  

where $\mathbf{1}_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{m}$ , $\mathbf{1}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(n-m)}$ , $\mathbf{1}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-m}$ are ones matrices.  

$\bullet$ It is computationally cheap, due to defined on element-wise multiplication (a.k.a. dot product).   
Particularly, its inverse $S^{-1}$ does not require computation, owing to the above explicit form.  

• It addresses the closed-form iterates challenge, aforementioned in Section 1.3.2. Specifically, equation 1.9 admits the following closed-form solution:  

$$
\begin{array}{r}{\pmb{Z}^{k+1}=\pmb{S}^{-1}\Pi_{\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n}}\bigg(\pmb{S}\pmb{A}\pmb{X}^{k+1}+\pmb{S}^{-1}\pmb{\Lambda}^{k}\bigg).}\end{array}
$$  

(ii) The above operator stepsize $\boldsymbol{S}$ does not need any tuning. It will be automatically calculated via the closed-form root of a degree-4 polynomial. Moreover, such a stepsize update can be early stopped to save some runtime.  

Below, we summarize our operator ADMM algorithm, with slightly different steps for equation primal and equation dual. They may be simplified if some tailored structures exploited.  



\section{3 OPERATOR STEPSIZE SELECTION  }


Here, we show how to select our operator stepsize automatically. It involves two steps. First, minimize an upper bound, which yields a theoretical optimal choice (not a priori knowledge). Then, we approximate such a choice successively.  

3.1 THEORETICAL CHOICE  

To start, we need a characterization of the ADMM convergence rate. It is first established in He & Yuan (2015) through variational inequality. Below, we will adopt a recent fixed-point argument from Ryu & Yin (2022), which is slightly more convenient.  

Lemma 3.1. (Ryu & Yin, 2022, Theorem $^{\,l}$ ) ADMM admits the following worst-case convergence rate:  

$$
\|\zeta^{k+1}-\zeta^{k}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{k+1}\|\zeta^{\star}-\zeta^{0}\|^{2},
$$  

where initialization $\zeta^{0}$ can be arbitrary.  

Our ADMM iterates as in equation 2.6 corresponds to the above fixed-point view, via  

$$
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{k+1}=\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}}\boldsymbol{X}^{k+1}+\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{k}.
$$  

Corollary 3.1. Under zero initialization ${\pmb X}^{0}={\pmb Z}^{0}={\pmb\Lambda}^{0}={\bf0},$ , the worst-case optimal choice of our operator stepsize $\boldsymbol{S}$ can be determined via  

$$
m i n i m i z e~~~\|\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{X}^{\star}+\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\star}\|^{2},
$$  

3.1.1 SOLUTION DETAILS  

Now, we solve the above problem. For the sake of light notation, denote $\hat{X}=\mathcal{A}X$ . Also, define partitioning  

$$
\hat{\mathbf{X}}=\left[\begin{array}{l l}{\hat{X}_{1}}&{\hat{X}_{0}}\\ {\hat{X}_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}}&{\hat{X}_{2}}\end{array}\right],\quad\mathbf{\mathbf{\Lambda}}\mathbf{\Lambda}=\left[\begin{array}{l l}{\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{1}}&{\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}}\\ {\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}}&{\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{\Lambda}_{2}}\end{array}\right],
$$  

where $\hat{X}_{1},\mathbf{{A}}_{1}\in\mathbb{S}^{m}$ , $\hat{X}_{2},\mathbf{{A}}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-m}$ are symmetric matrices, and where $\hat{X}_{0},\mathbf{A}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(n-m)}$ .  

Lemma 3.2. Invoke the definition of $\boldsymbol{S}$ in equation 2.3. equation 3.3 can be rewritten into  

$$
m i n i m i z e\ \frac{\gamma_{1}}{\gamma_{1}}\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\gamma_{1}}\|\mathbf{\hat{A}}_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}+\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}\|\mathbf{\hat{A}}_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}+2\gamma_{1}\|\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{2}{\gamma_{1}}\|\mathbf{A}_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}.
$$  

The above admits closed-form solutions, related to the root of the polynomial below.  

Lemma 3.3 (polynomial root). Consider the following degree-4 polynomial:  

$$
a\rho^{4}+b\rho^{3}+d\rho+e=0.
$$  

Suppose all coefficients are real and $b,d$ not simultaneously equal $O$ . Then, it admits 4 closed-form roots as  

$$
\rho=\left\{\begin{array}{l l}{\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{b}{2a}-u_{4}-\sqrt{u_{5}-u_{6}}),}\\ {\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{b}{2a}-u_{4}+\sqrt{u_{5}-u_{6}}),}\\ {\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{b}{2a}+u_{4}-\sqrt{u_{5}+u_{6}}),}\\ {\frac{1}{2}(-\frac{b}{2a}+u_{4}+\sqrt{u_{5}+u_{6}}),}\end{array}\right.
$$  

where  

$$
u_{4}={\sqrt{{\frac{b^{2}}{4a^{2}}}+u_{3}}},\quad u_{5}={\frac{b^{2}}{2a^{2}}}-u_{3},\quad u_{6}=-{\frac{{\frac{b^{3}}{a^{3}}}+{\frac{8d}{a}}}{4u_{4}}},
$$  

and where  

$$
u_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{27}}{2}(a d^{2}+b^{2}e),\,u_{2}=u_{1}+\sqrt{(b d-4a e)^{3}+u_{1}^{2}},\,u_{3}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}a}(\sqrt{u_{2}}-\frac{b d-4a e}{\sqrt[3]{u_{2}}}).
$$  

Theorem 3.1. The worst-case optimal choice of stepsize $\boldsymbol{S}$ , or equivalently the parameter pair $(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})$ , is given by  

$$
\gamma_{1}^{\star}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{2}^{\star}\|\Lambda_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}^{\star}}\|\Lambda_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}+2\|\Lambda_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}}{\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}^{\star}}\|\hat{X}_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{\star}\|\hat{X}_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}+2\|\hat{X}_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}}}.
$$  

with $\gamma_{2}^{\star}$ being a positive root of the following degree-4 polynomial:  

$$
\begin{array}{r l r}&{\gamma_{2}^{\star4}\|\hat{X}_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}\|\Lambda_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{\star3}(\|\hat{X}_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}\|\Lambda_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}+\|\hat{X}_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}\|\Lambda_{1}^{\star}\|^{2})-\gamma_{2}^{\star}(\|\Lambda_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}\|\hat{X}_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}+\|\Lambda_{0}^{\star}\|^{2}\|\hat{X}_{1}^{\star}\|^{2})}\\ &{\hphantom{\gamma_{2}^{\star4}\|\hat{X}_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}}-\|\Lambda_{2}^{\star}\|^{2}\|\hat{X}_{1}^{\star}\|^{2}=0,}&{(3.11\,\ k_{1}^{\star})^{\star},}\end{array}
$$  

with a closed-form solution available via Lemma 3.3.  

3.2 PRACTICAL USE  

The above involves certain optimal point information, hence not instantly useful in practice. To address it, we replace the optimal solutions (unknown) by the current iterates (known).  

Similar approximation idea already appears in the machine learning field, but on a different issue, the importance sampling, see e.g. Yuan et al. (2016), (Rizk et al., 2022, Sec. IV. C).  

Corollary 3.2. The $(k+1)$ -th operator stepsize $S_{k+1}$ can be determined via  

$$
\gamma_{1}^{k+1}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma_{2}^{k+1}\|\pmb{\Lambda}_{1}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}^{k+1}}\|\pmb{\Lambda}_{2}^{k+1}\|^{2}+2\|\pmb{\Lambda}_{0}^{k+1}\|^{2}}{\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}^{k+1}}\|\hat{\pmb{X}}_{1}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{k+1}\|\hat{\pmb{X}}_{2}^{k+1}\|^{2}+2\|\hat{\pmb{X}}_{0}^{k+1}\|^{2}}}.
$$  

with $\gamma_{2}^{k+1}$ being a positive root of the following degree-4 polynomial:  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}&{\gamma_{2}^{k+14}\|\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}_{2}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|{\mathbf{\Lambda}}_{1}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{k+1^{3}}(\|\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}_{2}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|\Lambda_{0}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\|\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}_{0}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|\Lambda_{1}^{k+1}\|^{2})}\\ &{\,\,-\,\gamma_{2}^{k+1}(\|\Lambda_{2}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}_{0}^{k+1}\|^{2}+\|\Lambda_{0}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}_{1}^{k+1}\|^{2})-\|\Lambda_{2}^{k+1}\|^{2}\|\hat{{\mathbf{X}}}_{1}^{k+1}\|^{2}=0,}\end{array}
$$  

with a closed-form solution available via Lemma 3.3.

4.1 BOOLEAN QUADRATIC PROGRAM  

We start with the Boolean quadratic program. It is a fundamental problem in digital communication, particularly popular in circuit design.  

Ideally, one would like to solve the following Boolean program:  

$$
\begin{array}{r l}{\underset{\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathrm{minimize}}}&{\|\boldsymbol{A x}-\boldsymbol{b}\|^{2},}\\ {\mathrm{subject}\tan}&{x_{i}\in\{-1,1\},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,}\end{array}
$$  

with $\mathbf{\Delta}x\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times1}$ , $\pmb{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times1}$ , $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ . This problem is well-known to be NP-hard. In the literature, it is common to instead solving a semidefinite relaxed version, which we compactly written as  

$$
{\begin{array}{r l}{\underset{X}{\operatorname{minimize}}}&{\langle A_{0},X\rangle,}\\ {{\mathrm{subject}}\,\mathrm{to}}&{\operatorname{diag}(X)=\mathbf{1},}\\ &{X\succeq0,}\end{array}}

\section{5 CONCLUSION  }


For the first time, an operator stepsize is designed for semidefinite programming, with a special structure inspired by the Schur complement lemma. It enjoys several nice properties, including closed-form iterates, cheap computational cost (owing to dot product), and tune-free. Compared to the standard scalar stepsize, our operator one admits extra degrees of freedom, which mathematically allows it to surpass the acceleration limit (of the standard version). This aspect has been confirmed numerically, where preliminary tests show great advantages in iteration number complexity and runtime. Overall, we believe our operator ADMM significantly alleviated the long-standing scalability issue of semidefinite programming.
