\title{Entropic Distribution Matching for Supervised Fine-tuning of LLMs: Less Overfitting and Better Diversity}

\begin{abstract}





Large language models rely on Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to specialize in downstream tasks. Cross Entropy (CE) loss is the de facto choice in SFT, but it often leads to overfitting and limited output diversity due to its aggressive updates to the data distribution. This paper aim to address these issues by introducing the maximum entropy principle, which favors models with flatter distributions that still effectively capture the data. Specifically, we develop a new distribution matching method called GEM, which solves reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence minimization with an entropy regularizer. 

For the SFT of Llama-3-8B models, GEM outperforms CE in several aspects. First, when applied to the UltraFeedback dataset to develop general instruction-following abilities, GEM exhibits reduced overfitting, evidenced by lower perplexity and better performance on the IFEval benchmark. Furthermore, GEM enhances output diversity, leading to performance gains of up to 7 points on math reasoning and code generation tasks using best-of-n sampling, even without domain-specific data. Second, when fine-tuning with domain-specific datasets for math reasoning and code generation, GEM also shows less overfitting and improvements of up to 10 points compared with CE.














\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}





Large Language Models (LLMs) \citep{openai2023gpt4, touvron2023llama, team2024gemma} are powerful generative models excelling in specialized tasks across various fields. Typically, LLMs first go through a pre-training stage \citep{radford2019language, brown2020language}, where they learn to predict the next token from a large corpus of texts, such as books, scientific papers, and code. Despite this extensive pre-training, LLMs often struggle to follow instructions and answer users' queries effectively, because such scenarios are not commonly encountered during pre-training. To improve their performance in these tasks, instruction tuning \citep{raffel2020exploring, wei2021finetuned, chung2024scaling}, also known as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) \citep{ouyang2022training, bai2022training}, is employed. This process involves using high-quality labeled data (i.e., prompt-response pairs) and typically utilizes supervised learning with Cross Entropy (CE) loss to maximize the likelihood of the labeled data.  Later on, these models may be further aligned with human preferences to ensure their outputs align with human values \citep{ouyang2022training, bai2022training}.

SFT elicits the knowledge acquired from pre-training to answer various downstream questions and further paves the way for future developments, making it crucial part of the post-training pipeline \citep{zhou2023lima, tunstall2023zephyr, liu2023makes, young2024yi}. We expect models to generalize well by providing accurate answers and hope these answers are diverse as well. While the importance of generalization is clear, we also stress the significance of generation diversity. In creative writing, diversity sparks new ideas \citep{colton2012computational}, and in chit-chat dialogues, users often appreciate having multiple output options to suit their preferences \citep{li2015diversity}. Many modern AI interfaces, such as ChatGPT and Claude AI, recognize this need by incorporating features like regeneration buttons. Additionally, generation diversity is vital when advanced generation algorithms are applied in LLMs to tackle complex tasks \citep{snell2024scaling}. For example, the best-of-n sampling, commonly used in math reasoning \citep{snell2024scaling} and code generation  \citep{chen2021evaluating}, benefits from selecting the optimal response from a diverse set of generated options.

However, models trained using CE loss in SFT often struggle with overfitting (see, e.g., \citep{fu2024disperse, gekhman2024does}) and poor generation diversity (see, e.g., \citep{padmakumar2023does, omahony2024attributing}). In theory, optimizing CE loss corresponds to minimizing the \emph{forward} Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the data distribution and the generative distribution of the LLM.\footnote{It is called ``forward" KL because the loss is defined across the data distribution. We will later discuss \emph{reverse} KL approaches, where the loss is defined across the generative model's distribution.}  This distribution matching process aggressively increases the likelihood of observed data, which often exhibit narrow coverage of real distributions that have diverse outcomes that we want the LLM to learn. However, the CE loss is unaware of this, and the optimized model biases toward low-entropy distributions, resulting in reduced output diversity. One particular example is shown in \cref{fig:framework} (details are provided in \cref{sec:additional_results}). Prior research \citep{ pereyra2017regularizing, dubey2018maximum} links low-entropy predictive distributions with poor generalization, indicating that these issues are interconnected. While practitioners often use weight decay regularization with CE loss \citep{ouyang2022training, bai2022training}, it does not fully address these problems, necessitating more principled approaches.









\textbf{Our contributions.} We formulate fine-tuning of LLMs as a distribution matching problem and  propose two principles. The first principle advocates \emph{generative} distribution matching methods to encourage the model learns from both ground truth supervision and its own generated mistakes. This contrasts with the passive imitation of supervision used in the CE loss. The second principle is that the model should assign higher probabilities to the observed data while preventing over-memorization\footnote{By over-memorization, we mean that the probabilities of the supervised data become overwhelmingly dominant in the distributions after learning.}, especially when dealing with the limited data. To implement these principles, we study the formulation of reverse KL divergence minimization with entropy regularization. However, this formulation is technically challenging and may require adversarial training techniques akin to those used in GANs \citep{goodfellow2014generative}. Our main technical contribution is the development of a new training algorithm, referred to as GEM (\underline{G}enerative \underline{E}ntropy-regularized \underline{M}atching of distributions), that addresses the above challenge and is tractable as the CE loss. By adhering to the proposed principles, GEM favors flatter distributions that effectively capture the data; see \cref{fig:framework} for an illustration.











We demonstrate the effectiveness of our model by fine-tuning the Llama-3-8B pre-trained model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B}} with two types of datasets. First, we fine-tune the model using the \texttt{UltraFeedback} dataset \citep{cui2024ultrafeedback} to develop general instruction-following abilities. Our results show that GEM achieves lower evaluation perplexity than CE and better performance on the \texttt{IFEval} benchmark \citep{zhou2023instruction}, indicating reduced overfitting. We also assess output diversity by evaluating the model's ability to generate varied content in creative tasks, such as poem and story writing \citep{mostafazadeh2016corpus}. Our findings show that GEM significantly enhances output diversity. This improved diversity translates into performance gains in math reasoning (\texttt{GSM8K} \citep{cobbe2021training}) and code generation tasks (\texttt{HumanEval} \citep{chen2021evaluating} and \texttt{MBPP} \citep{austin2021program}) when utilizing advanced generation strategies like Majority Voting (MV) and Best-Of-N (BON). In these tasks, GEM achieves performance improvements of up to 7 points. In a second experiment, we fine-tune the model on domain-specific datasets for specialized abilities in math reasoning (\texttt{MetaMathQA} dataset \citep{yu2023metamath}) and code generation (\texttt{MagicCoder-OSS-Instruct} dataset \citep{wei2024magicoder}), seperately. In this setting, GEM can outperform CE by up to 10 points when using MV and BON, further demonstrating its effectiveness. Overall, these results suggest that GEM can mitigate overfitting and improve output diversity compared with CE.






\section{Related Work}












\textbf{Supervised Fine-tuning.} SFT is the first stage of the post-training pipeline and plays an important role in subsequent developments. As mentioned in the introduction, using CE loss during the SFT stage often leads to overfitting and reduced output diversity. To address this, there is a line of research in scaling up SFT data (see, e.g., \citep{yu2023metamath, wei2024magicoder, zhang2024scaling}), which, while effective, increases computational burden. Our work aims to develop training methods that more effectively leverage supervised data to mitigate overfitting and to enhance output diversity. While recent studies such as \citep{chen2024self, li2024getting} attempt to improve CE-trained models through techniques like self-play, we aim to address the limitations of CE loss and design methods that can improve the pre-trained models directly.


We also emphasize the importance of output diversity based on previous research. SFT-trained models are often further refined through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to better align with human values \citep{ouyang2022training, bai2022training}. \citet{xiao2024algorithmic} studied the impact of SFT models on preference learning in RLHF, showing that if an SFT model collapses (i.e., becomes biased toward certain outputs with near-certain probability), it may further lead to preference collapse in the alignment. Their findings highlight the need to address collapse during the SFT stage. Additionally, SFT-trained models are often used as synthetic data generators for self-improvement (see, e.g., \citep{adler2024nemotron, dubey2024llama}). In this context, maintaining output diversity is crucial. It helps find better solutions and alleviate the mode collapse issue \citep{guo2023curious, shumailov2023curse}. For specialized applications, \citet{wang2023self} highlighted the benefits of output diversity through majority voting in math reasoning. Recent research \citep{brown2024large, snell2024scaling} have explored enhancing search over multiple generated responses with a verifier, showing that scaling up test-time compute is effective. Unlike these studies focused on inference-time techniques, our paper improves SFT training methods to promote diversity. 



\textbf{Entropy Regularization.}  \citet{dubey2018maximum} investigated the application of maximum entropy in visual fine-grained classification tasks, where the challenge lies in distinguishing between very similar categories of objects. Although their objective differs from ours, their insights remain valuable. They proposed that achieving zero CE loss is not essential for high accuracy. Instead, they suggested that a conditional probability distribution where the argmax corresponds to the correct class is sufficient for many applications. This concept motivates our use of entropy regularization, which allows for assigning probabilities to alternative options beyond the observed data. Prior to our work, \citet{pereyra2017regularizing} also explored entropy regularization in the context of neural network training. Their method closely resembles the CE with entropy regularization that we investigate in this paper, and they found that penalizing confident outputs improves generalization. It is important to note that \citet{pereyra2017regularizing} focused on image classification tasks, while our focus is on text generation where data is sequential in nature and is more challenging. In the context of LLMs, \citet{hu2023amortizing} also explored the maximum entropy regularization by using GFlowNet \citep{bengio2021flow}, but their methods require a reward function rather than supervised data. 






 











\section{Preliminary}



\textbf{Large Language Models (LLMs).} LLMs have a large vocabulary, denoted as $[K] = \{1, 2, \ldots, K\}$ and process text by splitting it into a series of tokens $(x_1, \ldots, x_T)$, where each token $x_i \in [K]$ and $T$ represents the sequence length. Let $f$ be the generative distribution modeled by the language model. The notation $f(\cdot|x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$ specifies the categorical distribution over $[K]$, conditioned on the context $(x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$. Typically, $f$ is parameterized by a neural network, often a transformer \citep{vaswani2017attention}, with the parameter $\theta$. For the $i$-th token at time step $t$, its prediction probability is given by
\begin{align*}
    f_{\theta}(i | x_1, \ldots x_{t-1}) = \sm (z_t) =  \frac{\exp (z_t[i]) }{\sum_{i^{\prime}} \exp (z_t[i^{\prime}])}.
\end{align*}
where $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{K}$ is the logit output from the neural network given the input $(x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$, and $z_t[i]$ is $i$-th element of $z_t$. This auto-regressive process specifies the joint probability of a sequence of tokens as $f_{\theta}(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} f_{\theta}(x_t|x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$. 

LLMs are pre-trained to predict the next token in a sequence, thereby learning complex conditional probability distributions from vast amounts of data. In practical applications, LLMs are tasked with generating a response $y$ to a given prompt $x$. However, these question-answer scenarios often differ from the textbook-like pre-training data, causing pre-trained LLMs to struggle in generating responses that follow human instructions effectively.



\textbf{Supervised Fine-Tuning.} To address the above issue, Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is introduced. This process involves using a supervised dataset with high-quality prompt-response pairs $\{ (x^{i}, y^{i} )\}_{i=1}^{N}$ for demonstration. The CE loss is the de facto training objective for this purpose: $ \min_{\theta} \sum_{i=1}^{N}  -\log f_{\theta}(y^{i}|x^{i})$. In theory, this corresponds to minimizing the \emph{forward} KL divergence between the data distribution $p$ and the generative distribution $f_{\theta}$:
\begin{align*}
    \min_{\theta} \KL \lp p, f_{\theta} \rp \Longleftrightarrow \max_{\theta} 
\expect_{x \sim \rho(\cdot)} {\expect_{y \sim {p}(\cdot|x)} [\log f_{\theta}(y|x)] },
\end{align*}
where $\rho$ is the prompt distribution, which is usually not modeled during the SFT stage. Thus, the distribution $\rho$ can be treated as a constant and we omit it when the context is clear. In practice, many questions can correspond to multiple valid answers, and it is nearly impossible to collect a comprehensive high-quality dataset that encompasses all possibilities. As a result, the empirical data tends to be limited in size and often exhibits a narrower distribution than desired. In such scenarios, the CE loss function aggressively maximizes the likelihood of the available empirical data, adjusting the generative distribution $f_{\theta}$ to closely align with it. However, this approach can lead to poor generation diversity and overfitting, as previously noted.













\section{Entropic Distribution Matching}

\label{sec:method}


In this paper, we explore principled approaches to address limitations of existing SFT that uses the CE loss, especially when dealing with limited data. We present two core principles: the first focuses on the methodology of distribution matching, while the second offers guidance on learning from limited data. 
Our first principle advocates for a \emph{generative} approach to distribution matching. This approach encourages the model to learn from its own generated data and mistakes, rather than merely imitating supervised demonstrations. Unlike the traditional CE loss, which leads the model to imitate training data labels passively, a generative approach involves active learning through self-generated feedback. This principle is grounded in cognitive science \citep{schulz2007serious, gweon2014sins}, which demonstrates that children learn more effectively through exploration and experimentation, adjusting their understanding based on discrepancies between expectations and reality. Similarly, research on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) \citep{goodfellow2014generative, jolicoeur2018relativistic} supports this notion by showing how models can learn to produce realistic data through iterative refinement. To summarize, we propose:
\begin{shaded}
Principle 1: The distribution matching approach should be ``generative", meaning the model learns from both ground truth supervision and its own generated mistakes. 
\end{shaded}
Our second principle addresses the challenge of overfitting. We draw inspiration from neuroscience, specifically the concept of avoiding over-memorization and achieving balanced learning. In neuroscience, synaptic plasticity, particularly homeostatic plasticity, underscores the importance of maintaining balance in learning processes \citep{turrigiano2008self, turrigiano2012homeostatic}. Overly strengthening certain neural connections can lead to rigid, maladaptive behaviors, analogous to how assigning excessively high probabilities to observed tokens can result in over-memorization in models, thereby limiting their ability to adapt and generalize. Based on these insights, especially for limited data, we propose:
\begin{shaded}
Principle 2:  The model should assign higher probabilities to the observed data while preventing over-memorization.
\end{shaded}  










\subsection{Proposed Formulation: Reserve KL with Entropy Regularization}


To implement the two principles outlined above, we propose studying the formulation of \emph{reverse} KL divergence minimization with maximum entropy regularization. The objective is defined as follows:
\begin{align}   \label{eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy}
    \max_{f}  \expect_{x} \big\{ \underbrace{\expect_{y\sim f(\cdot|x)} \ls \log p(y|x) \rs - \expect_{y \sim f(\cdot|x)}[\log f(y|x)]}_{=-\KL(f, p)} + \gamma \cdot  \underbrace{\expect_{y \sim f(\cdot|x)}[\log f(y|x)]}_{\gH(f)}  \big\}.
\end{align}
The first term in this formulation corresponds to the \emph{reverse} KL divergence between the   target distribution $p$ and the model distribution $f$. This term supports Principle 1 by encouraging the model to learn from its generated data samples. The second term, entropy regularization, aligns with Principle 2 by preventing over-memorization, as it ensures that the probabilities for labeled data do not become excessively high. Furthermore, it brings another benefit: the output diversity can be improved. In this case, greedy sampling can reliably output the knowledge in the training data. We also note that adding entropy regularization to the CE loss supports Principle 2 but not Principle 1; its limitations are discussed in \cref{sec:discussion}, and we will show that it is inferior to the proposed approach in \cref{sec:experiments}.




While the objective defined in \cref{eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy} appears promising, it presents significant challenges in practice. The main challenge is that we only have access to empirical data from the distribution $p$, not its full probability density function, making the reverse KL term impossible to compute directly. Additionally, calculating the expectation of the reverse KL across the model's generative distribution is not easy. This paper contributes a new algorithm to address these challenges.  


\subsection{Proposed Algorithm: GEM}

In this section, we present a practical algorithm for solving the optimization problem of reverse KL with entropy regularization. As discussed earlier, the key is to obtain an estimate for the log probability density function $\log p$. We first address the case where $y$ is not sequential.  We begin by outlining a conceptually simple but technically complicated solution. Building on this proposal, we then introduce a more tractable solution by new techniques.
\paragraph{An Initial Proposal.} Drawing inspiration from GANs \citep{goodfellow2014generative, jolicoeur2020relativistic}, one may propose the following formulation for estimating the distribution $p$:
\begin{align} \label{eq:rp_gan}
    \min_{q} \max_{r} \expect_{x} \expect_{y^{\real} \sim p(\cdot|x)} \expect_{y^{\gene} \sim q(\cdot|x)} \ls h \lp  r(x, y^{\real}) - r(x, y^{\gene})   \rp \rs.
\end{align}
Here we use the subscript $\real$ to denote the supervised data and $\gene$ to denote the model-generated data for clarity. In addition, $h$ is a monotonically increasing function (e.g., a linear function) that will be discussed later, and  $r: \gX \times \gY \rar \mathbb{R}$ acts as a discriminator, designed to maximize the gap between samples drawn from the data distribution $p$ and those from the opponent distribution $q$. The goal of the opponent $q$ is to minimize the gap.  When $h$ is a linear function, it simplifies to the reward maximization problem $\max_{q} \expect_{y^{\gene} \sim q(\cdot|x)}[r(x, y^{\gene})]$, a fact that shall be used later.

On its theoretical foundation, \citet{jolicoeur2020relativistic} proved that the inner maximization problem is to find a divergence function between the distributions $p$ and $q$, and thus the outer minimization problem is to reduce this divergence. Therefore, the solved $q$ serves as a good estimate of $p$. If we can solve problem \eqref{eq:rp_gan}, we can substitute $\log p = \log q$ in \cref{eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy}. While this offers a conceptually viable solution, it also presents several challenges. In particular, the optimization problems in \cref{eq:rp_gan,eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy} involves three variables: $r$, $q$, and $f$, which are hard to solve:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The variables $q$ and $r$ are introduced to be adversarially trained in \cref{eq:rp_gan};
    \item The distribution $f$ needs to be further solved in \cref{eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy} after $q$ has been solved.
\end{itemize}
It is important to note that optimizing the reward function $r$ and the distribution $q$ can be particularly challenging when dealing with sequential data, often requiring Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms (see, e.g., \citep{ho2016gail}). Our initial attempts to implement the above proposal were not successful. We introduce a new tractable solution below.
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{GEM}
\label{algo:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require{Dataset $\gD = \{(x_i, y_i^{\real})\}$}
\For{iteration $k = 1, \ldots, $}
\State{Set $q_k = \sm (1/\beta * \log  f_{\theta_k})$} %
\State{Define the loss function}   %
\begin{align*}
    {\gL}_{q}(f_{\theta}) = \sum_{i} \sum_{y^{\gene}} q(y^{\gene}|x_i) \cdot h \lp  \ls \log f_{\theta} (y^{\real}_i|x_i) - \log f_{\theta} (y^{\gene}|x_i) \rs \rp
\end{align*}
\State{Update $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + \eta \cdot \nabla_{\theta} {\gL}_{q}(f_{\theta}) \mid_{\theta = \theta_k} $}
\EndFor
\Ensure{Generative model $f_{\theta}$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Proposed Solution.} At a high level, our approach simplifies the process by solving a single-stage optimization problem, eliminating the need to first estimate the distribution $p$ before solving for $f$.  Our approach involves two key techniques:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reparameterization}:  We reparameterize the discriminator using the generative distribution $f$. Specifically, we define $r$ as a real-valued function and parameterize it as $\log f(y|x)$, ensuring it remains real-valued.
    \item \textbf{Direct entropy regularization}: We introduce entropy regularization for the distribution $q$ directly, deriving a closed-form solution for $q$. This technique offers two advantages. First, it establishes a connection between $q$ and $f$, eliminating the need of solving problem \eqref{eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy} separately. Second, since $q$ has a closed-form solution, it does not require explicit training.
\end{itemize}
Specifically, our formulation is that:
\begin{align}
    \max_{f} \quad & \gL_{q}(f) \triangleq \expect_{x} \expect_{y^{\real} \sim p(\cdot|x)} \expect_{y^{\gene} \sim q(\cdot|x)} \ls h \lp  \log f (y^{\real}|x) - \log f(y^{\gene}|x)   \rp \rs \label{eq:main_1} \\
   \text{s.t.} \quad &  q = \argmax_{\pi} \expect_{x} \expect_{y \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}\ls \log f(y|x) \rs  + 1/\beta \cdot \gH(\pi(\cdot|x)) = \sm (1/\beta * \log f) \label{eq:main_2}.  
\end{align}
In this formulation, we optimize $f$ as if we were optimizing the reward function $r$. Simultaneously, we optimize $q$ with the reward given by $\log f(y|x)$, incorporating entropy regularization. Fortunately, this yields a closed-form solution, so we do not need to maintain or explicitly train $q$. We only need to optimize the distribution $f$ in \cref{eq:main_1}. Note that although $q$ is dependent on $f$ generally, we do not calculate the gradient through $q$ when calculating the gradient of $f$. This is similar to the target network used in RL \citep{mnih2015human, li2022note}. We have the following theoretical justification for this formulation.

\begin{prop}   \label{prop:fixed_point}
Assume that $h$ is a linear function. For any distribution $p$, $\gL_{q}(f)$ has a unique stationary point, and this stationary point (with $\beta = 1/({\gamma + 1}) > 0$) corresponds to the optimal solution of Problem \eqref{eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy}.
\end{prop}
\cref{prop:fixed_point} implies that solving the proposed problem in \cref{eq:main_1,eq:main_2} provides the optimal solution of reverse KL with entropy regularization in \cref{eq:reverse_kl_with_entropy}. In practice, we can parameterize $f$ using a transformer and optimize the parameters with gradient ascent. We outline such a training procedure in \cref{algo:main}, referring to this approach as GEM, which stands for \underline{G}enerative and \underline{E}ntropy-regularized \underline{M}atching of distributions. Note that the exact expectation $\mathbb{E}_{y^{\gene} \sim q(\cdot|x)}[\cdot]$ is calculated because we assume $y^{\gene}$ is non-sequential here (we will deal with the sequential case later). Concretely, in this case the generative distribution is categorical and has finite support, making the expectation easy to compute. This differs from the continuous optimization in GANs \citep{goodfellow2014generative}, where the generator uses inexact stochastic gradients by drawing several samples. We also note that \cref{prop:fixed_point} relies on $\beta > 0$, meaning that the proposed formulation cannot solve the pure reverse KL minimization problem.


\paragraph{Intuition and Example.} We provide an intuitive understanding of GEM by explaining its training mechanism on a simple model: for a fixed $x \in \gX$, we model $f_{\theta}(y|x) = \sm(\theta_x)$ with $\theta_x \in \mathbb{R}^{K}$. Consider $h$ as the linear function described in \cref{prop:fixed_point}. For a paired sample $(y^{\real}, y^{\gene}) = (i, j)$, we have the gradient for this sample: 
\begin{align*}
    \nabla_{\theta} \gL_q(f_{\theta})[i, j] =  \left\{ \begin{array}{cc}
       w_{ij} e_{ij}   &  \text{ if } \quad  i \ne j \\
       \mathbf{0}  &  \text{ otherwise}
    \end{array} \right.
\end{align*}
Here $w_{ij} = p(y^{\real}|x) q(y^{\gene}|x)$ lies in $[0, 1]$, and $e_{ij}$ is the vector with $i$-th element being $1$ and the $j$-th element being $-1$ and $0$ otherwise. Thus, the gradient of this paired data gives a direction for moving the logit $\theta_x$ from $j$-th position to $i$-th position, with the magnitude determined by the weight $w_{ij}$.

Consider a numerical example where $\theta_x = [2, 1]$ with $K=2$, resulting in $f = [0.73, 0.27]$. For $\beta = 0.7$, we have $q = [0.81, 0.19]$, which is more peaked compared with $f$. Given the data distribution $p = [0.9, 0.1]$, the gradient of GEM is $[0.1, -0.1]$, leading to a relative logit change of $0.2$. In comparison, the CE's gradient in this case is $[0.17, -0.11]$, resulting in a relative logit change of $0.28$, which is 1.4 times larger. Thus, the probability change of GEM is more milder than CE, and GEM would eventually converge to a flatter distribution $[0.82, 0.18]$ due to the induced entropy regularization.


In the above analysis, we see that the distribution $q$ determines the weights of probability transportation. Generally, for $0 < \beta < 1$, a \emph{narrowed} distribution $q$, shifted from $f$, prioritizes the high-probability regions in $f$ for probability transportation, while low-probabilities regions in $f$ contributes less. This contrasts with CE, which would push probabilities of non-labeled tokens towards the labeled ones, potentially causing overfitting. We also observe that $h$ also determines {how much} probability is shifted. Specifically, we have $w_{ij} = p(y^{\real}|x) q(y^{\gene}|x) h^{\prime}$ for a general function $h$. For the linear function studied, $h^{\prime}$ is  always equal to $1$. Another possible choice for $h$ is the log-sigmoid function $h(u) = \log\sigmoid(u) = u - \log (1 + \exp(u))$, which is studied in previous research \citep{jolicoeur2020relativistic, sun2020towards, rafailov2023direct, chen2024self}. This function provides a weighting effect. Since $h^{\prime} = \sigmoid (  \log f(y^{\gene}|x) -\log f(y^{\real}|x)) \in (0, 1)$, it results in a large weight when $y^{\real}$ is not yet dominant in the probability distribution, and a small weight when $y^{\real}$ has already become dominant. Later on, we will study this function in experiments.


\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{GEM for Sequential  Data}
\label{algo:gemax_sequential_data}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require{Dataset $\gD = \{(x_i, y_1, \ldots, y_{T}) \}$}
\State{Initialize $\widetilde{\gD} = \emptyset$}
\For{sample index $i$}  \Comment{``Reset" data distribution}
\For{timestep index $t = 1, \ldots, T$}
\begin{align*}
    \widetilde{x} &= x_i \oplus (y^{\real}_1, \ldots y^{\real}_{t-1}), \quad  \widetilde{y} = y^{\real}_{t} \\ 
    \widetilde{\gD} &\lar  \widetilde{\gD} \cup \{ (\widetilde{x}, \widetilde{y}) \}
\end{align*}
\EndFor
\EndFor
\State{$f_{\theta} \lar $ Call \textbf{Algorithm 1} on $\widetilde{\gD}$}
\Ensure{Generative model $f_{\theta}$}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\paragraph{Extensions to Sequential Data.} In the above part, we have derived the algorithm for the case $y$ is non-sequential. We note that optimization in the sequential case could be highly difficult. With a little abuse of notations, let $y = (y_1, \ldots, y_T) \triangleq y_{1:T}$. We can extend the formulation in \cref{eq:main_1,eq:main_2} to the following:\footnote{Generally speaking, the prompt $x$ would also be sequential, but this does not affect our discussion and formulation as it serves the input to the conditional distribution.} 
\begin{align*}
    \max_{f} \quad & \expect_{x} \expect_{y^{\real}_{1:T} \sim p(\cdot|x)} \expect_{y^{\gene}_{1:T} \sim q(\cdot|x)} \ls h \lp  \log f (y^{\real}_{1:T}|x) - \log f(y^{\gene}_{1:T}|x)   \rp \rs \\
   \text{s.t.} \quad &  q = \argmax_{\pi} \expect_{x} \expect_{y_{1:T} \sim \pi(\cdot|x)}\ls \log f(y_{1:T}|x) \rs  + 1/\beta  \cdot \gH(\pi (\cdot|x)) 
\end{align*}
Here, we encounter a challenge: the joint distribution of $y_{1:T}$,  as a cascaded categorical distribution, is quite complicated. This results in the expectation $\expect_{y^{\gene}_{1:T}}[\cdot]$ cannot be easily calculated as before. While Monte Carlo estimation—drawing samples to approximate the gradient—might seem like a viable solution, we found it does not work in experiments. We believe the main reason is that the sample space is huge,\footnote{For the Llama-3-8B model, its vocabulary size is 128k. For a typical case with a sequence length of 2048, the sample size is $128000^{2048}$.} and the pre-trained distribution $f$ is quite different from the data distribution $p$ that we aim to learn.\footnote{Specifically, pre-trained models cannot generate the \texttt{EOS} (end-of-sentence) token properly, resulting in repetitive and less informative sequences, even with infinite length. But the supervised data has an \ttt{EOS} token and finite length. To bypass this challenge, methods proposed in \citep{chen2024self, li2024getting} rely on models that has been SFT-trained with CE. As mentioned, their methods cannot operate with pre-trained models directly.} As a result, when we use stochastic sampling to estimate the gradient, it does not provide effective feedback.



To deal with the above challenge, we propose decomposing the multi-stage sequential optimization problem into multiple single-stage optimization problems and solve each efficiently. Concretely, we restrict the distribution matching to the case that the prefix samples up to time step $t$ are drawn from the data distribution $p$ and solves the  optimization problem at the $t$-th time step as before. Its mathematical formulation is given below: 
\begin{align}   
     \max_{f} \gL_q^{\seq}(f) &= \expect_{x} \lb \sum_{t=1}^{T} \expect_{y_{1:t-1}^{\real} \sim p(\cdot|x)} \expect_{y^{\real}_t \sim p(\cdot|x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real})} \expect_{y^{\gene}_t \sim q(\cdot|x, y^{\real}_{1:t-1})} \ls \Delta \rs \rb \label{eq:loss_f_sequential} \\
    \text{where} \quad \Delta &= \ls h \lp    \log f(y^{\real}_t |x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real}) - \log f(y^{\gene}_t|x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real})  \rp \rs, \nonumber 
\end{align}
The main advantage of this formulation is that for each sub-problem, we still have access to the exact conditional distribution, so the gradient estimation is accurate. The same idea applies to the training of distribution $q$:
\begin{align*}
    q = \argmax_{\pi} \expect_{x} \lb \sum_{t=1}^{T}  \expect_{y^{\real}_{1:t-1} \sim p(\cdot|x)} \expect_{y_t \sim \pi(\cdot|x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real})} [\log f(y_t|x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real})] + 1/\beta \cdot \gH (\pi(\cdot|x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real})) \rb.
\end{align*}
That is, we still have the closed-form solution that $q(\cdot|x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real}) = \sm (1/\beta \cdot \log f(\cdot|x, y_{1:t-1}^{\real}))$ when used in \cref{eq:loss_f_sequential}. We acknowledge that our technique draws inspiration from the data distribution ``reset" trick introduced by \citep{ross11dagger} in imitation learning, in which the teacher first shows few demonstration actions and then the student is asked to finish the other actions in a full trajectory. 









\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiments}

In this section, we present our experiment results for fine-tuning the Llama-3-8B model (specifically, its pre-trained version). A brief overview of our experiment setting is provided below, with further details available in \cref{sec:experiment_details}.

\subsection{General-Purpose Fine-tuning}
\textbf{Set-up.}  In this section, we develop an LLM that is capable of following instructions for various prompts. To this end, we utilize the \texttt{UltraFeedback} dataset \citep{cui2024ultrafeedback}, specifically the version filtered by the HuggingfaceH4 team\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/HuggingFaceH4/ultrafeedback_binarized}}. This dataset contains prompts from instruction datasets like Evol-Instruct and UltraChat, and responses generated by models such as GPT-4 and Llama-2-7B/13B/70B-Chat. For more information, see \citep{cui2024ultrafeedback}.

Each data point comprises two responses: one selected as the preferred option and the other as the rejected option, with the selection made by GPT-4. In our study, we use the preferred response for SFT, a practice commonly adopted in previous research \citep{ouyang2022training, bai2022training}. Following \citep{yu2023metamath,liu2023makes, cui2024ultrafeedback} we set the learning rate to $2 \times 10^{-5}$, employing a cosine learning rate decay schedule, and use a macro batch size of 128. The maximum sequence length, encompassing both the prompt and response, is set to 2,048 tokens. Models are trained for three epochs.


We implement the proposed GEM method with $\beta = 0.7$. As discussed, GEM has two variations: GEM-LS (GEM with \ttt{log-sigmoid}), and GE-Linear, each depending on the choice of the function $h$. Our primary baseline is the standard CE loss. Additionally, we explore a variant incorporating a weight decay of 0.1, which has been commonly used in previous studies \citep{ouyang2022training, bai2022training}. We refer to this approach as CE + WD. We also implement a method called CE + Entropy, which adds an entropy regularization term of 0.1 to the CE loss. This method aligns with the proposed Principle 2 but not Principle 1 (see \cref{sec:discussion} for more discussion).

Depending on whether we use strict or loose judgment and whether we measure accuracy in the prompt space or instruction space, there are four evaluation criteria: prompt-level strict accuracy, instruction-level strict accuracy, prompt-level loose accuracy, and instruction-level loose accuracy. For all metrics, a higher value indicates better performance.

\paragraph{Creative Writing.} We continue to assess models' output diversity in two creative writing tasks: poem writing and story writing. For poems, we use prompts from the \texttt{poetry}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/merve/poetry}} dataset on the Huggingface website, which includes 573 poems on themes such as love, nature, and mythology. For stories, we design 500 prompts based on the ROC story dataset \citep{mostafazadeh2016corpus}. In both cases, we prompt the models to write a poem or story titled ``[X]" with no more than 200 words, where [X] is a title from the respective dataset.  Following \citep{kirk2023understanding}, we use three criteria to evaluate diversity: 1) N-gram diversity: the proportion of distinct n-grams in a single response (intra-diversity); 2) Self-BLEU diversity: the Self-BLEU score, treating one response as a reference among multiple generated responses (inter-diversity); 3) Sentence-BERT diversity: the cosine dissimilarity between pairs of responses in the embedding space. All criteria range from 0 to 100 (with Sentence-BERT diversity scaled by multiplying by 100), where higher values indicate greater diversity.

To calculate these metrics, we ask the trained models to generate 16 samples using the decoding configuration \ttt{temperature=1}, \ttt{top\_k=50}, and \ttt{top\_p=0.9}. The evaluation results are presented in \cref{tab:diversity}. In this task, we note that weight decay does not improve generation diversity, although it has shown effectiveness in mitigating overfitting in previous examples. On the other hand, entropy regularization, implemented to support Principle 2, brings the benefit of output diversity.

\paragraph{Chatting, Math Reasoning, and Code Generation.}  In this part, we show that improved generation diversity offers benefits beyond creative writing tasks. Specifically, diverse generation, when quality is ensured, is advantageous when using advanced generation methods such as Best-Of-N (BON) or Majority-Voting (MV) \citep{wang2023self} to find better solutions. This is inline with recent advances in scaling up test-time compute \citep{brown2024large, snell2024scaling} and self-distillation \citep{sessa2024bond}. Specifically, we conduct three experiments in chatting, math reasoning and code generation below to validate the superiority of GEM through its improved generation diversity. In this part, we use the configuration \ttt{temperature=0.6}, \ttt{top\_k=50}, and \ttt{top\_p=0.9}. A lower temperature is chosen here to enhance response quality ($0.6$ is the default value in Llama models). The overall performance is displayed in \cref{fig:overall_performance} with detailed results in \cref{sec:additional_results}. Specific settings and analyses are provided below.

For chatting,  we assess the model's ability to generate human-preferred responses. We prompt the trained models to answer 805 questions from the \ttt{AlpacaEval} dataset \citep{alpaca_eval}. For each question, the model generates 32 responses and a reward model is then used to select the best responses. We employ the reward model \texttt{FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/sfairXC/FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1}}, which has demonstrated top performance on \ttt{RewardBench} \citep{lambert2024rewardbench}, making it a reliable choice for this task. Since the reward value itself does not mean anything, we choose the win rate as a metric. In particular, we estimate the win rate over GPT-4's generated response by the Bradley–Terry model. From \cref{fig:overall_performance}, we observe that GEM-LS can achieve about 3 points improvement in the win rate compared with CE.
For math reasoning, we evaluate performance on the \texttt{GSM8K} \citep{cobbe2021training} benchmark, which contains 1,319 test questions. We use chain-of-thought prompts \citep{wei2022chain} to guide LLMs to generate 32 responses for each question. We assess answer accuracy using both Majority-Voting (MV) \citep{wang2023self} and Best-Of-N (BON) methods. Compared with CE, GEM-LS shows improvements of up to 4.8 points (7.7\% relative) with MV and 2.5 points (2.8\% relative) with BON. The strong performance of BON@32 indicates that while the model might know how to solve these questions, it is uncertain about these solutions in generation. This aligns with previous research \citep{li2024common}, which found that even 7B language models demonstrate strong math reasoning abilities through sampling multiple responses.

For code generation, we consider two benchmarks: \texttt{HumanEval} \citep{chen2021evaluating} and \texttt{MBPP} \citep{austin2021program}. In these scenarios, the trained models are asked to generate Python code, and the executor judges their correctness. The common evaluation metric is the pass rate over multiple samples. We ask the trained models to generate 200 samples to estimate the pass@100. The generation configuration is the same as for the chatting task. We find that weight decay does not show significant improvement over CE, while GEM-LS can achieve up to a 7.6-point (10.7\% relative) improvement over CE on \texttt{HumanEval} and a 6.4-point (9.0\% relative) improvement on \texttt{MBPP} for pass@100.

These results suggest similar conclusions regarding overfitting and generation diversity as before.

\section{Conclusion}








In this paper, we propose an alternative method for the SFT of LLMs to tackle the challenges of overfitting and limited generation diversity, which are often caused by the aggressive updates of the CE loss and limited data. We demonstrate the effectiveness of combining generative distribution matching with entropy regularization. We note that the improved diversity also boosts performance in downstream tasks when advanced generation methods, such as the best-of-n sampling, are used. Overall, our results indicate that the proposed method is well-suited for generative models. 


We focus on the initial stage of post-training pipeline in this paper and recognize that the models trained with our proposed methods can be further refined in subsequent stages. Notably, the enhanced diversity achieved by our approach can be advantageous in several contexts: it supports scaling up test-time computation \citep{brown2024large, snell2024scaling}, improves exploration in RL methods \citep{schulman2017ppo, li2023remax}, addresses the preference collapse issue \citep{xiao2024algorithmic}, facilitates self-improvement through distillation with best-of-n techniques \citep{sessa2024bond}, and helps mitigate mode collapse in synthetic data generation \citep{shumailov2023curse, bertrand2023stability, wu2024progress}. We see significant potential for our method in these areas and plan to explore these topics in future work.
