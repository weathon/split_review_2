\title{Large Language Models have Intrinsic Self-Correction Ability}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) have attracted significant attention for their remarkable abilities in various natural language processing tasks, but they suffer from hallucinations that will cause performance degradation. One promising solution to improve the LLMs' performance is to ask LLMs to revise their answer after generation, a technique known as self-correction. Among the two types of self-correction, intrinsic self-correction is considered a promising direction because it does not utilize external knowledge. However, recent works doubt the validity of LLM's ability to conduct intrinsic self-correction. In this paper, we present a novel perspective on the intrinsic self-correction capabilities of LLMs through theoretical analyses and empirical experiments. In addition, we identify two critical factors for successful self-correction: zero temperature and fair prompts. Leveraging these factors, we demonstrate that intrinsic self-correction ability is exhibited across multiple existing LLMs. Our findings offer insights into the fundamental theories underlying the self-correction behavior of LLMs and remark on the importance of unbiased prompts and zero temperature settings in harnessing their full potential.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


Large Language Models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing, demonstrating remarkable capabilities in generating coherent and contextually relevant text across various domains \cite{openai2024gpt4,gao2024retrievalaugmented}. However, critiques have been raised on the hallucinating nature of the responses produced by LLMs \cite{huang2023survey}. It is observed that LLMs sometimes generate answers that are non-sensible and deviate from the provided context \cite{hallucination_def}.
At the same time, while LLMs are still not considered equivalent to human-level intelligence \cite{shen-etal-2023-large, mathur2024advancing}, they exhibit some interesting characteristics that are possessed by human beings, including the emergent ability \cite{wei2022emergent} \footnote{whether LLMs exhibit emergent ability is still in debate in academia, partially due to the definition of `emergence". See \cite{schaeffer2023are} and \cite{lu2023emergent} for details.} and performing complex tasks \cite{chiang-lee-2023-large, NEURIPS2023_forcast}. 
One intriguing aspect of human intelligence is the ability to self-correct. Self-correction (SC) is defined as the process of continuously evaluating and adjusting one's own responses or actions to get better outcomes. There are generally two types of SC: \textit{extrinsic} SC which utilizes the help of external knowledge and \textit{intrinsic} SC which uses only one's own knowledge. Inspired by humans, the research community proposed a promising hypothesis that we could leverage the power of SC to enhance the quality of LLMs. In particular, researchers are interested in whether \textit{intrinsic} SC ability could be applied in LLMs.
Over the last few years, numerous research studies have been conducted on the effectiveness of intrinsic SC of LLMs. For example, works like self-refine \cite{madaan2023selfrefine}, self-verification \cite{gero2023selfverification,weng2023large, manakul-etal-2023-selfcheckgpt}, post-editing \cite{raunak-etal-2023-leveraging}, all demonstrate significantly enhanced performance of SC on respective benchmarks compared to results obtained without SC.

However, one recent work has questioned the efficacy of intrinsic SC mechanisms in LLMs \cite{huang2024large}. A notable critique presented in the paper is that LLMs do not possess the ability to perform intrinsic self-correction. Their argument is based on a fundamental question: \textit{why couldn't an LLM get the correct answer in the initial attempt instead of going through intrinsic SC?}
They hypothesize that existing works on SC abilities in LLMs shall be attributed to three factors: external feedback (such as the existence of a retrieval model) that converts the problem to an extrinsic setting, ground-truth label as an early stopping criterion that hinders the practical usage, and uninformative prompts that limit LLM's ability when it generates the first response before SC (which raises the possibility of unfair comparison). Through the experiments, \cite{huang2024large} reported that LLMs showcase different levels of degradation in QA accuracy after intrinsic SC. 

In light of such disagreement, it is important to answer a set of fundamental questions: \textbf{Does intrinsic SC ability exist within LLMs? And if it does, why couldn't an LLM get the correct answer in the initial attempt instead of going through intrinsic SC?}

In this paper, we answer the above fundamental questions. With theoretical analyses and quantitative experiments under the same settings as \cite{huang2024large}, we empirically show that LLMs \textbf{do} have intrinsic SC ability. Added to that, we also show that LLMs cannot answer questions correctly in the initial attempt because of their inherent hallucination property. 

Building on top of the answers, we further investigate the conditions that maximize SC potential within LLMs. We identify two factors that can impact the ability of SC ability. The first factor is temperature. We show that using a non-zero temperature, such as done by \cite{huang2024large},  degrades SC ability. The second factor, equally important, is the neutrality and fairness of the prompts used during various stages of SC. A fair and unbiased prompt is a prompt that doesn't directly or indirectly influence the LLM to change or maintain its initial answer. Hence an unbiased prompt should avoid negative or positive phrases such as ``find the problem'', or ``correct your answer''.  In Fig. (\ref{fig:prompt_example}), we provide an example illustrating how biased (\cite{huang2024large}) and unbiased (ours) prompts affect the output of the LLM during different stages of SC. For simplicity, we consider a three-stage scenario where the LLM generates the initial answer in stage 1, reasons (self-corrects) on the generated answer in stage 2, and provides a final answer in stage 3.

Our contributions could be summarized as the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We provide a novel perspective on the functioning nature of intrinsic self-correction. We argue that SC ability is similar to chain-of-thought (CoT) and self-verification prompting techniques that provide LLMs more context for the final answer's generation.
    \item We highlight two key factors for the success of intrinsic self-correction, with the first factor being the temperature of LLMs, and the second factor being the fairness of the prompt. We argue that while increasing the temperature will benefit the creativity of response, it may be detrimental for SC, depending on the structure of LLM's response. At the same time, the prompt needs to be unbiased so that LLMs do not accidentally attend to tokens that might trigger unwanted answer updates during the SC process.
    \item We provide guidelines to compose unbiased prompts for future research in this direction.
    \item We empirically verify the effects of the two factors above, showcasing the accuracy degradation of generated SC responses when increasing temperature and utilizing biased prompts, and that intrinsic SC is \textit{universally} achieved by LLMs via fair prompts and zero temperature.
\end{itemize}
Our answers to those fundamental questions serve as a basis for the underlying mechanism of intrinsic SC, and we hope that our findings will shed further light on LLM's abilities and encourage future works and discussions to explore methods that could leverage the existing SC ability more effectively.



\section{New Perspective on Intrinsic Self Correction}

\label{section2}
\subsection{Preliminary}
The text generation process of LLMs could be modeled as an auto-regressive probabilistic distribution $P$. Given an LLM and a prompt of $t$ tokens $\tau = \{w_1,w_2,...w_t\}$, the auto-regressive generation of the response takes the form of Eq. (\ref{llm_temp}), where $y$ is the (unnormalized) logits from the LLM, $w_{t+1}$ is a potential next token, $l_{w_{t+1}}$ is the scaled logit value for $w_{t+1}$, and $T$ is the temperature. In other words, the generated content of any LLM follows the distribution of its logits, readjusted by the temperature. As temperature approaches 0, it is clear that the generation process changes from sampling in a distribution to selecting the index of the maximum value of logits, as described in Eq. (\ref{llm_temp0}).
\begin{equation}
\label{llm_temp}
    P(w_{t+1}|w_1,...w_t) \sim y \quad\text{,}\quad l_{w_{t+1}} = \frac{e^\frac{y_{w_{t+1}}}{T}}{\sum_{k=1}^N e^\frac{y_k}{T}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{llm_temp0}
    \lim_{T\to0^+} l_{w_{t+1}} = 
    \begin{cases}
1 &\text{if }y_{w_{t+1}} = \max y\\
0 &\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
While the definitions above may sound trivial, they are nonetheless important foundations of this work. In particular, they provide justifications to some of the analyses in the future sections. We draw several insights on this foundational formation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Under $T=0$, any arbitrary LLM should output the same response on some fixed prompt $\tau$. Unfortunately, models of the GPT family \cite{openai2024gpt4, brown2020language} do not follow this principle, possibly due to their query optimizations and internal sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE) design of GPT-4. In particular, GPT-4 exhibits very high variance for its answers under $T=0$. Those unknown behaviors cause some variations in the evaluation process.
    \item Intrinsic SC increases the prompt length $t$ constructively to reduce hallucination, which will help improve the generation quality of the final response. This is similar to self-verification \cite{weng2023large, gero2023selfverification} and chain-of-thought (CoT) \cite{wei2022chain}. We will elaborate on this in Section 2.2.
    \item Increasing temperature might not cause visible accuracy degradation in the evaluation process for response generation without SC, but it might be detrimental to the intrinsic SC due to the non-optimal sampling process. We will elaborate on this in Section 4.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Does intrinsic SC exist in LLMs?}
In this section, we answer the first fundamental question of whether intrinsic SC exists in LLMs by providing an alternative explanation of the underlying mechanism of self-correction. For now, we focus on one model (GPT3.5-0613) and fix the temperature at zero.

We start the analysis with the chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting technique \footnote{CoT prompt could be elicited with phrases such as "explain your reasoning" or "think step by step" in LLMs during the prompting.}, which is proven to be useful in enhancing LLM's performance in accuracy \cite{wei2022chain}. Based on the auto-regressive nature of LLM generation, \cite{chu2023survey} models the CoT process as a conditional probability shown in Eq. (\ref{cot}), where A is the answer to the prompt (and question) $\tau_1$, and $R_1$ is the rationale provided by LLM in the CoT process. The reasoning context introduced by CoT helps the LLM make a better final decision. Similarly, using the effective depth of the transformer circuit, \cite{feng2023towards} argues that CoT increases the effective depth of the circuit by letting the generated outputs repeatedly loop back to the input.

\begin{equation}
\label{cot}
    p(A|\tau) = p(A|\tau_1,R_1)p(R_1|\tau_1)
\end{equation}
Similarly, it is apparent that intrinsic SC also has the potential to achieve CoT-like behavior. Generally, we could model the generation process of a typical intrinsic SC according to Eq. (\ref{sc_general}). The first stage is a generic CoT prompting and answering schema. In the second stage, based on the first part's results, we use a prompt $\tau_2$ to let LLM provide rationales ($R_2$) and a decision ($D$) on the correctness of the previous answer. Then, in the last stage, we instruct LLM with $\tau_3$ to give a final answer $A'$ based on all previous information. We refer readers to Fig. (\ref{fig:prompt_example}) for an example of each stage.

\begin{equation}
\label{sc_general}
    p_{SC}(A'|\tau) = \underbrace{p(A'|\tau_3,D,R_2,\tau_2,A,R_1,\tau_1)}_{\textit{stage 3}}\underbrace{p(D,R_2|\tau_2,A,R_1,\tau_1)}_{\textit{stage 2}}\underbrace{p(A|R_1,\tau_1)p(R_1|\tau_1)}_{\textit{stage 1}}
\end{equation}

The second stage is the key factor of the SC process. 
Since it requires the LLM to provide a decision over the correctness of the previous answer as well as the rationales, there are two different orderings for the output depending on the position of the decision and rationale. We could model those two orderings as a conditional probability distribution, as shown in Eq.(\ref{stage2_expand_s4}) where \textbf{Order 1} refers to having the decision first followed by rationale and \textbf{Order 2} being the other way around:
\begin{equation}
    \label{stage2_expand_s4}
    \begin{split}
\textit{Order 1: }\underbrace{p(R_2|D,\tau_2,A,R_1,\tau_1)}_{\textit{rationale}}\underbrace{p(D|\tau_2,A,R_1,\tau_1)}_{\textit{decision}}\\
\textit{Order 2: }\underbrace{p(D|R_2,\tau_2,A,R_1,\tau_1)}_{\textit{decision}}\underbrace{p(R_2|\tau_2,A,R_1,\tau_1)}_{\textit{rationale}}
\end{split}
\end{equation}

Depending on the different designs of prompts, we discuss the different behaviors of intrinsic SC below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Case 1: if $\tau_1$ uses CoT and the second stage follows Order 1 } In this case, intrinsic SC is just an implicit form of self-verification \cite{gero2023selfverification,weng2023large}. Based on the rationales given in stage 1, stage 2 will provide an answer on the consistency of the provided rationales by reasoning on them. As shown in Table \ref{table:cases}, there is an improvement in accuracy after SC. We will show more experiments in Section 5 that this case provides some accuracy improvement to the final result.
    \item \textbf{Case 2: if $\tau_1$ uses CoT and the second stage follows Order 2 } In this case, SC could be seen as a CoT answer over a CoT prompt. By providing more rationales in the second stage, SC provides additional analyses over the provided rationales in the first stage and makes a better decision. If well implemented, this case could lead to better performance compared to the first one, as shown in Table \ref{table:cases}. However, such an approach might not be very feasible as we cannot fully control the decomposition orderings of LLMs' output through prompts. During our experiments, GPT-3.5 shows a strong preference for the decomposition Order 1, and the designed prompt is only able to successfully change some responses' order.
    \item \textbf{Case 3: if $\tau_1$ does not use CoT and the second stage follows Order 1 } When $\tau_1$ does not use CoT, the decomposition of the first stage in Eq. (\ref{sc_general}) is simply $p(A|\tau_1)$. In this case, intrinsic SC has no use, and it might cause an accuracy drop after correction. This is because the decision is first given without any rationale, and subsequent provided rationales are now dependent on a possibly hallucinated answer. 
    \item \textbf{Case 4: if $\tau_1$ does not use CoT and the second stage follows Order 2 } This serves the same purpose as CoT, and thus accuracy will improve. By letting LLMs provide rationales first, then decide on an answer without rationales, and finally make a more informed answer, SC will improve the accuracy. The improved accuracy is comparable (if not matching) to merely using CoT prompt with no self-correction as shown in Table \ref{table:cases}.
    
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why can't LLM answer questions correctly in the initial attempt?}
When LLMs hallucinate, one of the scenarios they exhibit is that they will output answers incorrectly even when having the correct knowledge. While that is not the full schema of LLM hallucination, in this paper, we simply refer to this type of scenario as hallucination. Assuming LLMs have a non-zero possibility of hallucination, we show that LLMs will not reach their full ability in the initial attempt with Lemma. \ref{lemma1}.    
\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma1}
    LLMs are generally under-performing compared to their true ability because hallucination will cause the overall accuracy to decrease.
\end{lemma}
This lemma serves as a justification for the second fundamental question and we provide the proof of Lemma. A. As drawn by the conclusion in the previous section, intrinsic SC can reduce hallucinations of the generated responses and achieve better quality similar to other prompt engineering techniques.

\section{Increasing Temperature Might Decrease Accuracy During SC}

As mentioned in Section 2.1 and Eq. (\ref{llm_temp}), the temperature hyperparameter controls the randomness of the generated context. The research community generally agrees that increasing temperature (within a reasonable range) will increase the creativity of LLMs, making them generate more diverse content \cite{hinton2015distilling}. Works like \cite{wang2020contextual} also try to dynamically optimize the temperature at the token level for best generation quality. Furthermore, very recent work \cite{renze2024effect} has claimed to find no correlation between increasing temperature and losing accuracy on benchmarks for temperatures between 0.0 and 1.0. However, as we will show in this section, temperature choice is a non-trivial aspect of the success of some LLMs during intrinsic SC. While increasing the temperature indeed shows no effect on the original response, it might have a negative impact on the subsequent SC process. 

During experiments, we notice that GPT-3.5 suffers from the increase in temperature, whereas other models are more robust towards the temperature. We provide the theoretical analysis below, based on the decomposition of the second stage of SC.

\subsection{Theoretical Analysis}
We first show that the randomness of the decision becomes smaller with decreasing temperature. We denote a positive decision ("yes") as $D=1$, and vice versa $D=0$ for a negative decision ("no"). Therefore, the decision procedure according to the context, which may include the prompts, rationales, and previous answers, degrades to a 0/1 binary classification that follows the binomial distribution.
\begin{equation}
    p(D = 1) = \frac{\alpha_1^{\frac{1}{T}}}{\sum_i\alpha_i^{\frac{1}{T}}} =  \frac{\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}}{\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}+(1-\alpha)^\frac{1}{T}},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in (0,1)$ is the original positive decision probability of the model. Therefore, considering the variance of the binomial distribution, we have:
\begin{equation}
    Var(D) = \alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}(1-\alpha)^\frac{1}{T}/(\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}+(1-\alpha)^\frac{1}{T})^2
\end{equation}
Next, we consider the effect of $T$ going from $0$ to $+\infty$:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial Var(D)}{\partial T}  = \frac{\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}} [\ln (\alpha)-\ln (1-\alpha)][\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}-(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}}]}{T^2\left(\alpha^{\frac{1}{T}}+(1-\alpha)^{\frac{1}{T}}\right)^3} \geq 0,
\end{equation}
The inequality holds because $(\ln (\alpha)-\ln (1-\alpha))$ and $(a^{1/T}-(1-\alpha)^{1/T})$ share the same positive and negative sign, since we have $\alpha \in (0,1)$ and $T>0$. This means when we have an output preference ($ 0 < \alpha < 1 $), the variance of the decision increases monotonically with the model's temperature, and the model with 0 temperature will be less likely to give the wrong decision due to less randomness. When the temperature goes to infinity, the model randomly selects an answer from \{"yes", "no"\} to give the decision, causing an unexpected decision error. At the same time, confidence in the decision is correlated to the variance: $\alpha = 0.5 $ gives the highest variance of 0.25, whereas $\alpha$ approaching 0 or 1 will reduce variance.

With an increasing variance, $D$ has an increasing possibility of being flipped to the other side. Thus, when $T$ is non-zero, we will have more decisions that do not represent the true knowledge of LLMs. In other words, those temperature-flipped binary decisions are in fact hallucinations, and under Lemma. \ref{lemma1}, those hallucinations will cause accuracy decrements after the SC process.

Finally, we refer to Eq. (\ref{stage2_expand_s4}) and examine the two decompositions. Through the analysis of the effect of $\alpha$ on the variance, we argue that Order 1 is more prone to temperature changes than Order 2. With the second ordering, the final decision after rationales is very close to a copying task, and LLMs are known to have very high confidence in performing this task ($\alpha$ very close to 0 or 1). On the other hand, providing the decision is a reasoning task that is much harder with less confidence ($\alpha$ closer to 0.5). Using Fig. (\ref{fig:prompt_example}) as an example, imagine a hypothetical answer such that GPT-3.5 puts the first sentence (decision) in stage 2 at the end. If so, to obtain the same information, it only needs to perform selective copying based on the rationales above, which is much easier (and thus more confident) than the current way.

\section{Designing Fair Prompt for Self-Correction}

Prompt engineering is a crucial part of LLM's generation. Numerous works have shown that a wise choice of prompt will significantly benefit the quality of the generated response \cite{wei2022chain,zhang2023automatic,weng2023large}. In this section, we show that prompt design matters for some of the models during SC, and a \textbf{fair} prompt will elicit \textit{universal} intrinsic SC ability of LLMs. In addition, we provide guidelines for fair prompting.

For the fairness of comparison, we only focus on Case 1 described in Section 2.2. Case 1 has the same setting with \cite{huang2024large}, with the only difference being the semantic bias of the prompt. We retain the prompt from the first stage of intrinsic SC, using the same prompt as in \cite{huang2024large}, and we show that inadequate prompts will cause LLMs to behave incorrectly.

\subsection{Theoretical Analysis}



Referring back to Eq. (\ref{sc_general}), we see the final output from stage 3 depends on eight factors. Among them, $A$, $R_1$, and $\tau_1$ are fixed from stage 1. Excluding those fixed constants, $D$ and $R_2$ depend solely on $\tau_2$. Thus, we could conclude that the final answer only depends on two variables $\tau_2$ and $\tau_3$, which are the two prompts in stage 2 and 3.

The analysis of the prompt's effect shares the same principle as Lemma \ref{lemma1}.
We investigate the effect of a biased prompt that encourages the model to change answer \cite{huang2024large} on the final result. We assume that LLM strictly follows the prompt and randomly changes $\gamma\%$ of the answers that it would not have done with the fair prompt, with $\gamma>0$. The randomness of the changes is expected, as those are the cases in which LLMs would not have changed in a normal setting, and thus they should not be attributed to certain characteristics, such as correctness. For ease of analysis, we assume that LLM does not change any answer with a fair prompt (thus the accuracy difference after SC is 0).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{when $\tau_2$ is biased.} Since $D$ depends on $\tau_2$, $\gamma\%$ of $D$ will be inverted. Similar to the argument in Section 4, randomly inverting $D$ will lead to an accuracy drop. Also, $A'$ largely depends on $D$, we could say that roughly $\gamma\%$ of $A'$ will also be changed, with more correct answers changing to incorrect versus the other side. The effect of randomly changing answers for $A'$ has the same proof as Lemma. \ref{lemma1}.
    \item \textbf{when $\tau_3$ is biased.} The extra $\gamma\%$ of changes could be seen as hallucinations that are forced upon LLMs. Then, the analysis follows the argument in Lemma. \ref{lemma1}. Since the answers before SC are better than random guessing, after answers are randomly re-assigned for $\gamma\%$ of the responses during the SC process, more incorrect answers will appear after the assignment.
    \item \textbf{when both $\tau_2$ and $\tau_3$ are biased.} The detrimental effects of the two $\gamma\%$ changes accumulate.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Intrinsic SC could be achieved with fair prompt under zero temperature}
We consider three sets of prompts. The first set (\textbf{Problem Set 1}) is used by \cite{huang2024large}, but we argue that they are biased towards encouraging the LLMs to change answers due to the use of the negative phrase ``find problems''. The second set (\textbf{Problem Set 2})  is devised from the first one by removing the negative phrases and asking the LLM to check if the answer is correct or incorrect and act accordingly. Although such prompts might seem fair to a human due to both aspects balancing out, the use of the words correct and incorrect in the prompt can indirectly influence the LLM. Therefore in the last set of prompts (\textbf{Problem set 3}), we strip the prompts from such phrases and words to make a completely unbiased prompt. This process serves as a guideline for unbiased prompt composition.1-6. As shown in Table \ref{accuracy_temp0}, using an unbiased prompt and zero temperature, all of the four LLMs exhibit accuracy improvement on two different benchmarks. However, biased prompts might cause a decrease in accuracy in some LLMs. It is noteworthy that this decrease seems to be related to the benchmark and model. On CommonSense QA benchmark \cite{talmor-etal-2019-commonsenseqa}, the bias of prompt has significant impacts on GPT-3.5, but not so much on the other two models. On the other hand, the bias of prompts seems to affect GPT-4 and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.2 to certain degrees, but it has no impact on GPT-3.5 for the GSM8K benchmark \cite{GSM8K}. We argue that this might be because those models are instruction fine-tuned, and GPT families are further fine-tuned with reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) \cite{ouyang2022training}. Depending on the different training datasets that are not always disclosed, an LLM's accuracy after SC might be robust to biased prompts.

Our results align with the self-verification results obtained by Weng et. al. \cite{weng2023large} where they obtained a slight improvement using a different model. The on-par results further validate our claim in Section 2.2 that intrinsic SC is similar to other existing prompt engineering techniques.

\section{Discussions and Societal Impacts}

Our theoretical analysis and experiment results provide a more fundamental analysis of the intrinsic self-correction ability of large language models. 
We believe that this work will benefit the LLM research community in general by pointing out some preferred experiment settings that shall be used in the future on SC-related works. We also believe that our work will benefit the LLM user community in general by reducing hallucinations in the LLM's response. Reducing LLM's hallucinations might effectively prompt the safety of the online environment, as well as reduce deceptions and fake content generated by LLMs. However, it should also be pointed out that the same technique could be used to generate higher-quality fake content that is harder to be identified.



\section{Limitations}


While the paper aims to investigate the fundamental functioning nature of intrinsic self-correction, we recognize several limitations of the presented results. First, due to constraints of computational resources and staff, we are only able to provide evaluations on four models and two datasets. Second, on the detailed analysis of temperature (Section 4) and prompt design (Section 5), we are only able to focus mainly on a subset of the models and the CommonSense QA dataset. Third, due to the same constraint, we are not able to provide error bars for the results. We estimate that our experiments use around 200-300 US dollars in total on GPT queries and generate hundreds of CSV files for human annotation, and providing error bars would involve extensive experiments that could not be supported. While this is the common approach for the LLM community due to costs, we acknowledge that variances in the results might lead to potentially different conclusions that we fail to address in the paper. 


\section{Related Works}



During LLM inference, self-correction (SC) refers to the ability to iteratively improve future responses by reflecting on the errors from previous responses. In the field of LLM, such characteristics could be achieved by, broadly speaking, two sources: \textit{intrinsic} SC and \textit{extrinsic} SC. Intrinsic SC ability comes from LLMs themselves, and it is achieved mostly by prompt engineering \cite{huang2024large, madaan2023selfrefine}. Some of the works in this direction include \cite{shinn2023reflexion} and \cite{kim2023language}, but \cite{huang2024large} believes that those works utilize oracle label during inference, hindering their practicality in the real world. On the other hand, \textit{extrinsic} SC utilizes knowledge possessed beyond native LLM during the refinement process. Common approaches in this area include using human feedback \cite{wang2023shepherd}, retrieval from external databases with RAG \cite{gou2024critic, chen2024teaching, peng2023check, qin2024robust}, or other models \cite{gao-etal-2023-rarr, nathani2023maf}. It is a consensus in the research community that \textit{extrinsic} SC helps improve the factuality and quality of responses, whereas \cite{huang2024large} questions the validity of \textit{intrinsic} SC.
In this paper, we focus on \textit{intrinsic} SC in LLMs. We follow the problem formulation in \cite{huang2024large} and try to adhere to their experiment settings as closely as possible.



\section{Conclusion}


In this paper, we have explored the intrinsic self-correction capabilities of large language models. Backed up with theoretical analyses and empirical experiments, we provide a novel perspective on the problem and argue that intrinsic SC ability is a form of self-verification and CoT. Also, we emphasize the importance of two critical aspects of performing intrinsic SC: zero temperature and fair prompts. Through experiments, we show that LLMs are \textit{universally} able to self-correct, contingent upon those two factors. We aspire that our work serves as a guiding beacon for future research into the SC capabilities of large language models.
