\title{OOD-Chameleon: Is Algorithm Selection for OOD Generalization Learnable?}

\begin{abstract}

    Out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization is challenging because distribution shifts come in many forms.
    A multitude of learning algorithms exist and each can improve performance in \emph{specific} OOD situations.
    We posit that much of the challenge of OOD generalization lies in \emph{choosing the right algorithm for the right dataset}. 
    However, such algorithm selection is often elusive under complex real-world shifts.
    In this work, we formalize the task of \emph{algorithm selection for OOD generalization} and investigate whether it could be approached by learning.

    We propose a solution, dubbed \alg that treats the task as a supervised classification over candidate algorithms.
    We construct a \emph{dataset of datasets} to learn from, which represents diverse types, magnitudes and combinations of shifts (covariate shift, label shift, spurious correlations).
    We train the model to predict the relative performance of algorithms given a dataset's characteristics.
    This enables \emph{a priori} selection of the best learning strategy,
    i.e.\ without training various models as needed with traditional model selection.

    Our experiments show that the adaptive selection outperforms any individual algorithm
    and simple selection heuristics,
    on unseen datasets of controllable and realistic image data.
    Inspecting the model shows that it learns non-trivial data/algorithms interactions,
    and reveals the conditions for any one algorithm to surpass another. 
    This opens new avenues for (1)~enhancing OOD generalization with existing algorithms instead of designing new ones, and (2)~gaining insights into the applicability of existing algorithms with respect to datasets' properties.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{The many faces of OOD generalization.} 
Out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization refers to a model's ability to remain accurate when the distributions of the training and test data differ.
``OOD'' is a catch-all term since it encompasses many types of distribution shifts~\citep{wiles_fine-grained_2021, ye_ood-bench_2022, nagarajan_understanding_2021}. 
In medical imaging for example~\citep{oakden-rayner_hidden_2020}, a model may have to process X-rays from various demographics (\emph{covariate shift}), pathologies (\emph{label shift}), and co-occurrences of patient attributes (\emph{spurious correlations}). 
These types of shifts have often been studied independently, and are often best addressed with different algorithms.
However, real data is often complicated by \emph{combinations} of shifts of different types and magnitudes~\citep{wiles_fine-grained_2021, yang_change_2023} that interact in complex ways with the learning algorithms~\citep{jiang_joint_2023, cabannes_ssl_2023, benoit_unraveling_2024}.

\paragraph{The trade-offs of learning algorithms.}
OOD generalization is challenging because it is fundamentally underspecified~\citep{damour_underspecification_2022,teney_predicting_2022}.
The training set alone is not enough to inform about the nature of the shifts nor to constrain the behavior of the model on OOD test data. 
This is why a multitude of learning algorithms exist, which rely on different assumptions or side information (e.g.\ domain labels), from standard ERM~\citep{vapnik_nature_2000} to simple interventions such as Resampling, GroupDRO~\citep{sagawa_distributionally_2020}, and more-complex ones~\citep{liu_towards_2023-1}.

A well-known study by \citet{gulrajani_search_2020} showed that none of these methods surpasses an ERM baseline across a collection of datasets. This is \emph{not} surprising though: each dataset exhibits different characteristics and types or magnitudes of shifts that call for different methods~\citep{benoit_unraveling_2024}. Numerous analyses indeed confirm that no single method can handle a multitude of shifts~\citep{wiles_fine-grained_2021, nguyen_avoiding_2021, ye_ood-bench_2022, liang_metashift_2022, yang_change_2023}.

\centering
\textit{``It would be helpful for practitioners to be able to select the best approaches without requiring comprehensive evaluations and comparisons.''~\citep{wiles_fine-grained_2021}}
\end{mdframed}

\paragraph{We posit that OOD generalization can be improved if we knew which algorithm to apply in each situation.}
However, under complex real-world shifts, finding the right learning algorithm without trial and error is elusive for human experts.
We therefore investigate the possibility of \emph{learning} to predict a priori, for a new unseen dataset, the algorithm that will produce the most robust model.
This \emph{a priori} prediction contrasts with traditional
model selection that first requires training multiple models and often relies on restrictive heuristics
\citep{garg_leveraging_2022, baek_agreement---line_2023, miller_accuracy_2021, teney_id_2023, liu_neuron_2024}. 
In a concurrent work, \citet{bell_reassessing_2024} proposed to select
an algorithm to deal with spurious correlations
based on past performance on benchmarks most similar to the target data.
In comparison, we target many types of shifts (spurious correlation, covariate shift, label shift) and propose a learning-based approach instead of similarity heuristics.
\paragraph{\alg.} 
We frame the algorithm selection as a classification over a set of candidate algorithms. 
We train an algorithm selector named \alg in a setup akin to \emph{meta-learning}
\citep{vanschoren_meta-learning_2018, ozturk_zero-shot_2022} in two ways.
(1)~It is trained on a \emph{dataset of datasets} that exemplify diverse types, magnitudes, and combinations of shifts. 
Concretely, we construct such a dataset of datasets by sampling from synthetic distributions or real datasets e.g.\ CelebA~\citep{liu_deep_2015}, in many controlled ways to simulate various distribution shifts. 
(2)~We seek to \emph{learn to learn}, or learn to apply a learning algorithm, by discovering interactions between a dataset's characteristics and the performance of candidate algorithms. 
We evaluate the performance of various algorithms on the dataset of datasets, and we train the algorithm selector with supervision to predict the
relative performance of the algorithms given a statistical descriptor of a dataset
(see Figure~\ref{fig:pull_fig}).
We implement and evaluate training objectives such as regression, multi-label classification~\citep{furnkranz_multilabel_2008}, and pairwise comparison~\citep{bradley_rank_1952}.

Our experiments start with a controllable setup to evaluate various design choices.
We then push the concept further with realistic image data.
Results show that the system consistently selects algorithms with significantly lower test error than any single candidate algorithm, on unseen datasets with complex types of distribution shifts.
We further verify that it achieves this by learning non-trivial, non-linear data/algorithm interactions. 
More importantly, we show transfer across datasets by training the selector on CelebA-derived datasets,
then applying it to COCO~\citep{lin_microsoft_2014}.
As a byproduct, we demonstrate that the algorithm selector can reveal which dataset characteristics are important for any candidate algorithm to outperform another.

In summary, our findings call for improving OOD generalization by learning to better apply existing algorithms, instead of designing new ones. 
Additionally, this line of research can help understand the applicability of existing algorithms, especially under complex conditions such as combined distribution shifts that are difficult to study theoretically.

\textbf{Our contributions} are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=-4pt,leftmargin=11pt]
    \item We formalize the task of \emph{algorithm selection for OOD generalization} (Section~\ref{sec:background}). It consists in predicting the best strategy to obtain a robust model given a statistical descriptor of the dataset.

    \item We describe a reusable workflow to construct a \emph{dataset of datasets} that exemplifies a variety of distribution shifts in different types, magnitudes, and combinations. (Section~\ref{sec:meta_data}).

    \item We propose a demonstrator of the viability of the task (Section~\ref{sec:alg}):
    an algorithm selector trained with supervised learning to predict the most suitable algorithm from a set of candidates.

    \item We empirically verify that the proposed model predicts algorithms
    leading to high OOD accuracy,
    by learning non-trivial data/algorithm interactions (Section~\ref{sec:exps}).
    We also show that the model can reveal properties of datasets that make algorithms more effective than others (Section~\ref{sec:pattern}). 
\end{itemize}


\section{Formalizing the Algorithm Selection for OOD Generalization}

\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Is the Selection of the Best Learning Algorithm Even Possible?} % Or the context of OOD algorithm selection
\label{sec:ood_task}

A {universal} solution to the selection of a learning algorithm cannot exist according to the no-free-lunch theorem~\citep{wolpert_no_1997}).
The key here is to restrict ourselves to a \emph{distribution of distribution shifts} that are likely in real-world data~\citep{wiles_fine-grained_2021, goldblum_no_2023}.
We consider the three major types of shifts: \textbf{covariate shifts (CS)}, \textbf{label shifts (LS)}, and \textbf{spurious correlations (SC)}.
Formally, given a joint distribution $P(X,Y)$ over inputs $X$ and labels $Y$,
the three types correspond to shifts of $P(X)$, $P(Y)$ and $P(Y|X)$. 
Each sample $x\in X$ is typically characterized by both a robust feature $x_c \in X_c$ that is reliably predictive, and an attribute $a \in A$ that is not.
A covariate shift implies a variation of the attribute $a$ such as a shift in the background typically associated with a specific object in an image. 
A shift of spurious correlations implies a variation of an attribute/label co-occurrences,
which means a shift on $P(Y|A)$ but not $P(Y|X_c)$.
Because of the co-occurrence, a model could learn to rely on parts of the input related to the attribute.
This model would become unreliable on data where the co-occurrence has shifted. 
Finally, to measure OOD performance, We use the standard \textbf{worst-group accuracy (WGA)} on test data.
A \textbf{group} $\mathcal{G}\in Y\times A$ refers to a unique attribute--label combination~\citep{sagawa_distributionally_2020}.

\subsection{OOD Algorithm Selection as a Meta-Learning Task}
\label{sec:formulation}

Our eventual goal is to obtain a robust model given a dataset, referred to below as the \textbf{OOD task}. 
An OOD task is defined by its dataset $D=D^\mathrm{tr} \cup D^\mathrm{te}=\left\{\left(x_i, y_i\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n} \cup \left\{\left(x_i, y_i\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{n_\mathrm{te}}$, where training and test data show potential distribution shifts.
Solving an OOD task means running a learning algorithm
$\mathcal{A}(\cdot): D^\mathrm{tr} \rightarrow h_\theta$
that takes the training set and produces a parametrized model.
We can then evaluate the performance of $\mathcal{A}$ by computing
$\mathrm{WGA}(\{h_\theta(x_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_\mathrm{te}}, \{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n_\mathrm{te}})$ ($D^\mathrm{te}$ is not available during training). 
To better solve OOD tasks, we propose to automate the \textbf{OOD algorithm selection}
(i.e.\ selecting the best $\mathcal{A}$ among candidates)
by learning from experiences over many OOD tasks.
The training data $\mathbb{D}$ (a \textbf{meta-dataset}) for this process is built from a \textbf{dataset of datasets} 
that represent a variety of distribution shifts. 
Each example in this meta-dataset
$\mathbb{D}=\{D_j^\mathrm{tr}, \mathcal{A}_j, P_{j}\}$ % _{j=1}^{j=N}$
is an OOD task annotated with the performance of an algorithm on this task.
In practice, we feed $D^\mathrm{tr}$ into a 
\textbf{dataset descriptor}~\citep{rivolli_meta-features_2022, jomaa_dataset2vec_2021}
that summarize its distributional characteristics with a function $f(\cdot):D^\mathrm{tr} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^l$, so that we can leverage vector-based models. 
This makes $\mathbb{D}=\{f(D_j^\mathrm{tr}), \mathcal{A}_j, P_{j}\}$. 
Then, we expect to learn from $\mathbb{D}$
an \textbf{algorithm selector} $\phi(w, \cdot): D^\mathrm{tr} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$ that takes a training set and predicts the most suitable algorithm\footnote{By abuse of notation, $\mathcal{A}$ represents in practice a one-hot identifier of an algorithm.}. 
We will describe in Section~\ref{sec:learner} several realizations of the algorithm selector.
A straightforward option is a mapping from a dataset descriptor
$f(D^\mathrm{tr})$
and algorithm identifier $\mathcal{A}$
to its predicted performance, i.e. $\phi(w, \cdot): f(D^\mathrm{tr}) \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ that is trained by solving:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:meta_obj}
    \min_w \mathbb{E}_{\{f(D_j^\mathrm{tr}), \mathcal{A}_j, P_{j}\} \sim \mathbb{D}} \; \mathcal{L}_O(\phi(w, \{f(D_j^\mathrm{tr}), \mathcal{A}_j\}), P_{j})
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_O$ is a loss function such as the MSE. 
The trained $\phi(w, \cdot)$ can then be used on any new dataset (representing a new unseen OOD task) to predict the performance of all the candidate algorithms. The best algorithm is eventually used on the new dataset to obtain a robust downstream model.


\section{\alg: Learning When to Use a Learning Algorithm}

\label{sec:alg}
We now describe our solution to tackle OOD algorithm selection including \emph{three consecutive steps}:
\begin{enumerate} [topsep=-2pt, leftmargin=15pt] % [itemsep=3pt,topsep=0pt,leftmargin=11pt]
    \item Obtaining a collection of example datasets with a variety of distribution shifts to learn from.

    \item Assembling the meta-dataset $\mathbb{D}$. That is, training candidate algorithms on each dataset to get the corresponding performance, and summarizing each dataset with some dataset descriptor $f(\cdot)$.

    \item Training an algorithm selector on the meta-dataset to learn robust data/algorithm relations.
\end{enumerate}
Each step poses special challenges which we address as follows.

\subsection{Constructing Datasets with Various Distribution Shifts}
\label{sec:meta_data}
We first generate a dataset of datasets that exhibit
three types of shifts in various combinations and degrees.
The generation procedure takes in: (1)~a triple
$(d_\mathrm{cs}, d_\mathrm{ls}, d_\mathrm{sc})\in [0,1]^3$
that specifies the degree of covariate shift (CS), label shift (LS), and spurious correlation (SC), and (2) the size of the training set $n$.
It computes the required number of samples of each group necessary to achieve these properties.
The actual dataset can then be built with these numbers using synthetic data
(Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}) or by resampling an existing real dataset such as CelebA~\citep{liu_deep_2015} (Section~\ref{sec:exps}).

\paragraph{Quantifying distribution shifts.}
We need a way to specify the degree of each type of shift.
Prior work by \citet{yang_change_2023} has used information-theoretic measures
to quantify the degrees of SC, LS, and CS respectively as
the normalized mutual information $I(a;y)$ between labels and attributes,
entropy of class $H(y)$, and
entropy of attributes $H(a)$.
However, we cannot use them to directly compute the required number of samples for each group.
Hence we propose an alternative illustrated 
in Figure~\ref{fig:quantifying_shifts} with a 2-way classification of shapes with two color attributes.
The four groups correspond to \inlineimg{figures/inline/y1a0.pdf} $\mathcal{G}_1=\{i | y_i=1, a_i=1\}$, \inlineimg{figures/inline/y0a0.pdf} $\mathcal{G}_2=\{i|y_i=-1, a_i=1\}$, \inlineimg{figures/inline/y0a1.pdf} $\mathcal{G}_3=\{i|y_i=-1, a_i=-1\}$, \inlineimg{figures/inline/y1a1.pdf} $\mathcal{G}_4=\{i|y_i=1, a_i=-1\}$.
We define the degree of SC as the ratio of samples where class labels and attribute labels agree,
i.e.\ where a correct classification of the attribute entails a correct classification of the class
We define the degrees of LS and CS as the ratio of class/attribute as follows
(numerators colored as in Figure~\ref{fig:quantifying_shifts}):  
\begin{equation}
    d_\mathrm{sc}=\frac{\colorbox[RGB]{225, 236, 216}{$|\mathcal{G}_1|+|\mathcal{G}_3|$}}{\sum_i |\mathcal{G}_i|}, \quad 
    d_\mathrm{ls}=\frac{\colorbox[RGB]{253, 243, 219}{$|\mathcal{G}_1|+|\mathcal{G}_4|$}}{\sum_i |\mathcal{G}_i|}, \quad
    d_\mathrm{cs}=\frac{\colorbox[RGB]{252, 229, 227}{$|\mathcal{G}_1|+|\mathcal{G}_2|$}}{\sum_i |\mathcal{G}_i|},
    \label{eq:degree}
\end{equation}
where $|\cdot|$ is the set cardinality. 
The three degrees are in $[0,1]$ by definition, and $\sum_i |\mathcal{G}_i|=n$ size of $D^\mathrm{tr}$. 
To construct a dataset with desired shifts, we start with the three desired degrees and the training set size,
then solve linear equations for $|\mathcal{G}_i|$ and obtain the required group sizes in the training set.
We sample a balanced test set $D^\mathrm{te}$ with $|\mathcal{G}_i| = n_\mathrm{te}/i$.
\subsection{Formulations of the Algorithm Selector}
\label{sec:learner}
Given a collection of datasets $\{D_j\}_{j=1}^N$ created as described above,
we apply a set of algorithms $\{\mathcal{A}_m\}_{m=1}^M$ on each.
Each case yields a downstream model with OOD performance
$P_{jm}$
that we store in a meta-dataset
$\mathbb{D}=\{f(D_j^\mathrm{tr}), \mathcal{A}_m, P_{jm}\}_{j,m}$ (of size $MN$)
where $f(\cdot):D^\mathrm{tr} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^l$ is the dataset descriptor (will discuss in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}).
This meta-dataset is used to train the algorithm selector $\phi$
(an MLP in most of our experiments)
with one of the three objectives described below
The different formulations are visually summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:algorithm_selector}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/formulations4.pdf} % \hspace{0.5em}
	\caption{\textbf{We propose three possible objectives to train the algorithm selector.}
        The meta-dataset~$\mathbb{D}$
         contains here the performance of three algorithms $\mathcal{A}_{\{1,2,3\}}$ on one dataset $D_\mathrm{I}$.
         \regression~(\textbf{left}) estimates the algorithms' absolute performance.
    \etoe~(\textbf{middle}) and \ensemble~(\textbf{right}) estimate the binarized (unary and pairwise) suitability of the algorithms.}
	\label{fig:algorithm_selector}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Formulation 1 (\regression): classification via regression.} 
Equation~\ref{eq:meta_obj} tackles the algorithm selection task with a classification via regression. 
The algorithm selector $\phi(w, \cdot): f(D^\mathrm{tr}) \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ takes the dataset descriptors and a one-hot algorithm vector as input, predicts the OOD performance, and then chooses the algorithm with the highest one. 
This may be suboptimal because of (1)~the mismatch between the goal of \textit{directly selecting (i.e., classifying)} suitable algorithms, and (2) a regression being often more difficult than classification with neural networks~\citep{devroye_probabilistic_2013}. 
This motivates two alternative formulations as pure classification tasks. 

\paragraph{Formulation 2 (\etoe): multi-label classification.}
For each dataset $D_j$, we have $M$ (the number of algorithms) records $\{f(D_j^\mathrm{tr}), \mathcal{A}_m, P_{jm}\}_{m=1}^{M}$ in the meta-dataset $\mathbb{D}$.
We aggregate each such set of $M$ records into a \textit{single} training sample $\{f(D_j^\mathrm{tr}), Y_\mathcal{A}\}$ where $Y_\mathcal{A} \in \{0,1\}^M$ is a one- or multi-hot vector indicating the suitability of one or several algorithms on $D_j$. 
An algorithm is considered suitable if $(P_{jm} \,-\, \min_m\!P_{jm}) \leq \epsilon$ for a small threshold $\epsilon$ (e.g.\ $0.05$).
This aggregation converts the performance numbers into discrete labels and also serves as ``{denoising}''
because we consider algorithms with close performance similarly suitable.
The process results in a smaller meta-dataset (from $MN$ to $N$) but allows for learning more robust data/algorithm interactions because of denoising. 
We train the algorithm selection as a
binary multi-label classifier $\phi(w, \cdot): f(D^\mathrm{tr}) \rightarrow \{0,1\}^M$
with a cross-entropy objective $\min_w \mathcal{L}(\phi(w, f(D_j^\mathrm{tr})), Y_\mathcal{A})$.

\paragraph{Formulation 3 (\ensemble): pairwise preference learning.} 
Going one step further, an alternative formulation decomposes the M-way multi-label classification into $\binom{M}{2}$classifications~\citep{hullermeier_label_2008} across pairs of algorithms.
The intuition is that pairwise comparisons are easier than comparing all the candidates~\citep{bradley_rank_1952}
as recently shown i.a.\ in the alignment of language models~\citep{ouyang_training_2022}.
We train $\binom{M}{2}$ pairwise classifiers $\phi_{km}(w, \cdot): f(D^\mathrm{tr}) \rightarrow \{\texttt{win}, \texttt{lose}, \texttt{tie}\}$ to predict the relative ranking of $\mathcal{A}_k$ and $\mathcal{A}_m$.
They are considered on par when $|P_{jk} - P_{jm}| \leq \epsilon$. 
Formally, these 3-way classifiers are trained on $\{f(D_j^\mathrm{tr}), Y_{\mathcal{A}}\}, j\in [1,...,N]$, where $Y_{\mathcal{A}} \in \{\texttt{win}, \texttt{lose}, \texttt{tie}\}$. 
At test time,
for a target dataset, we aggregate the $\binom{M}{2}$ predictions using Copeland's classical voting method~\citep{saari_copeland_1996}.
It starts from 0 for all algorithms and adds/subtracts 1 for the winner/loser of each pairwise prediction, and does nothing for ties.
The final scores give a ranking across all algorithms from which we select the top value.
\subsection{Dataset Descriptors: A Case Study with Controllable Experiments}
\label{sec:synthetic}

Now that we have built the algorithm selector, we turn our attention to its input -- the vectorized datasets (i.e., dataset descriptors). 
The dataset descriptors should summarize properties relevant to the performance of the various algorithms.
Recent work~\citep{nagarajan_understanding_2021, hermann_foundations_2023, yang_identifying_2024, chen_when_2022, ye_ood-bench_2022, wang_towards_2024} discovered various properties related to OOD performance that could serve as dataset descriptors. However, many are difficult or impossible to measure before training, or work only under restricted conditions. 
Other works on learning model selection~\citep{arango_quick-tune_2024, ozturk_zero-shot_2022}
use simple descriptors (e.g., number of training samples, image channels, number of classes, etc.) that clearly cannot predict OOD performance.
We propose two categories of properties to include in dataset descriptors: (1)~\textbf{distribution shift characteristics} and (2)~\textbf{data complexity characteristics}. 
We hypothesize that the former includes the degrees of the distribution shifts ($d_\mathrm{sc}$, $d_\mathrm{ls}$, $d_\mathrm{cs}$ in Equation~\ref{eq:degree}), and the availability\footnote{
Similar concepts exist in prior works: signal/noise ratio~\citep{yang_identifying_2024}, magnitude~\citep{wang_towards_2024, joshi_towards_2023}, simplicity~\citep{qiu_complexity_2024}, and spurious/core information ratio~\citep{sagawa_investigation_2020}. %are discussed in prior works.
} $r$ of the spurious correlation (i.e., how easily the model relies on the spurious feature to make predictions). 
And the latter includes the size of the training set $n$ and the input dimensionality $d$ (e.g.\ image resolution in vision data). 
\paragraph{Controllable experiments.}
We investigate the relevance of the proposed descriptors with a synthetic test case.
We consider the binary classification from Figure~\ref{fig:quantifying_shifts}. 
The input $X$'s distribution follows the modified synthetic example from~\citet{sagawa_investigation_2020}.
The distribution of each group is defined by their input $x=[x_c, x_a] \in \mathbb{R}^{2d}$,
with $x_c$ and $x_a$ of dimension $d$ generated from Gaussian distribution: 
\begin{equation*}
    x_c \mid y \sim \mathcal{N}\left(y \mathbf{1}, \sigma_{\text {c }}^2 I_d\right) ,\quad x_a\mid y \sim \mathcal{N}\left(a \mathbf{1}, \sigma_{\text {a }}^2 I_d\right)
    \label{eq:synthetic}
\end{equation*}
The availability of the spurious features is defined as $r=\sigma_{\text {c }}^2/\sigma_{\text {a }}^2$
(more available when higher).

\paragraph{Experimental setup.} 
We construct the meta-dataset $\mathbb{D}$ with the created collection of datasets, train the algorithm selector with different objectives on it, and then evaluate how well the algorithm selector predicts suitable algorithms for unseen datasets.

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=-4pt,leftmargin=11pt]
    \item \textbf{Candidate algorithms.}
    We select 5 algorithms, namely \textit{ERM}~\citep{vapnik_nature_2000}, \textit{GroupDRO}~\citep{sagawa_distributionally_2020}, \textit{oversampling} the minority groups, \textit{undersampling} the majority groups, and \textit{logits correction}~\citep{nagarajan_understanding_2021} (i.e.\ adjust the prediction logits with a temperature), because: (1)~they are shown to perform comparably with others~\citep{nguyen_avoiding_2021, gulrajani_search_2020, yang_change_2023}, (2)~\textit{do not} require extensive hyperparameter tuning, (3)~can handle different distribution shifts~\citep{nguyen_avoiding_2021} and (4)~span different types of approaches, namely regularization-based, reweighting-based, margin-based and standard ERM. Including more algorithms is straightforward. 
    \item \textbf{Obtaining OOD performance on the collection of datasets.}
    We obtain each algorithm's worst-group error $P_{jm}$ on each dataset's test set $D_j^\mathrm{te}$.
    We use a linear classifier for training on each dataset because (1)~it is sufficient to solve the synthetic example and was also used by~\cite{sagawa_investigation_2020}, (2)~it is relevant to the common setting of linear probing over frozen features~\citep{setlur_prompting_2024, you_calibrating_2024}.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation.}
    We create around 2,000 datasets with unseen properties (i.e.\ different and unseen dataset descriptors)
    to test the generalizability of the algorithm selector.
    \item \textbf{Algorithm selection baselines.}
    (1)~\textit{Random selection}: randomly selecting an algorithm per dataset. 
    (2)~\textit{Global best (GB)}: choosing the single top algorithm based on its performance over the whole meta-dataset. 
    (3)~\textit{\etoe~ (Naive descriptors)}: \etoe~with the naive dataset descriptors from~\citet{ozturk_zero-shot_2022}. This is useful to indicate whether our dataset descriptors provide useful additional information.
    (4)~\textit{Oracle selection}: using the ground truth best algorithm per dataset.

\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Results.} In Table~\ref{tab:results_toy}, we show that the learned algorithm selectors generalize to unseen datasets. 
We examine the 0--1 accuracy, which considers an algorithm prediction on an unseen dataset as correct if it belongs to the ``ground truth'' suitable algorithms as defined earlier in Section~\ref{sec:learner}.
We also look at the worst-group error of the selected algorithms, averaged across unseen datasets.
We see that (1)~our formulations accurately predict suitable OOD algorithms, with significantly higher 0--1 accuracy and lower worst-group error than the baselines.
(2)~The algorithm selectors generalize significantly better with our dataset descriptors in lieu of naive descriptors, c.f.\ columns 4 and 6. 
(3)~Comparing our three formulations (columns 4, 5, 6) shows that a classification objective is significantly better than a regression. 

In Figure~\ref{fig:analysis} (left), we verify that the different OOD algorithms perform differently across datasets (each count in the histogram is a dataset).
This confirms that using the right algorithm for the right dataset improves OOD generalization. 
In Figure~\ref{fig:analysis} (middle), we show that the algorithm selector (\etoe) generalizes better with a larger meta-dataset, yet it already significantly outperforms the best non-parametrized baseline (Global best) even with a small meta-dataset.

The accuracy drops (c.f. the leftmost bar) reveal the importance of each piece of information for an accurate prediction. 
We see that the degree of shift and data complexity matter most,
specifically the input dimensionality $d$, the availability of the spurious feature $r$, and the degree of spurious correlation $d_\mathrm{sc}$.

We take an even closer look at the learned data-algorithm interactions with leave-one-descriptor-out training of \textbf{pairwise} algorithm selectors (i.e., selecting from only two algorithms).
In Figure~\ref{fig:pattern}, for each pair of algorithms, the performance drop of each bar compared to the leftmost bar (``N/A'') indicates the significance of the corresponding information for distinguishing the two algorithms. 
For example, when comparing over- and undersampling, the data size $n$ and degree of spurious correlation $d_\mathrm{sc}$ matter most. 
We find this to be consistent with the analysis in \cite{nguyen_avoiding_2021} that implies that, while undersampling can cope with more distribution shifts than oversampling, 
it is inferior when the number of samples for the minority group is too small (i.e.\ when $n$ or $d_\mathrm{sc}$ is too small). 
This approach can thus help discover data characteristics important for one algorithm to outperform another, and better understand the applicability of existing algorithms. 
We observe for example that the importance of some descriptors varies for other pairs of algorithms
(Figure~\ref{fig:pattern} middle/right), which could deserve further investigations.



\section{Towards Realistic OOD Algorithm Selection}

\label{sec:exps}

We now push the concept further with realistic data to answer the following research questions.
\textbf{(RQ1)}~Is the learned algorithm selection also effective with datasets of real images?
\textbf{(RQ2)}~Does our algorithm selector, trained on the semi-synthetic datasets built from CelebA for example,
transfer to unseen datasets from another domain (e.g.\ COCO~\citep{lin_microsoft_2014})?
\textbf{(RQ3)}~How is the system affected by
different dataset descriptors and different training variations of OOD tasks (linear probing vs.\ fine-tuning)?
\textbf{(RQ4)}~How complex are the learned data-algorithm interactions?

\paragraph{Experimental setup.}
The setup resembles that of Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} 

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=3pt,topsep=-4pt,leftmargin=11pt]
    \item \textbf{Dataset of datasets.}
    We generate 1,056 example datasets from CelebA~\citep{liu_deep_2015}
    with various sizes, types of shifts, etc.
    We simulate various ``availabilities'' of the spurious feature by
    using various annotations from CelebA, %as class and spurious attribute.
    e.g.\ \texttt{mouth slightly open} as class label and
    \texttt{wearing lipstick} as spurious attribute (see Table~\ref{tab:r}). 
    \item \textbf{Dataset descriptors.}
    We now \emph{estimate} the availability of spurious feature
    (rather than using ground truth values as in Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}) with
    $r= \sum_{y} d_y/\sum_{a} d_a$, where $d_y$ and $d_a$ are average distances of the samples' embeddings to the cluster center of labels or attributes, respectively. 
    Using estimated availabilities allows for generalizing to unseen availabilities, 

    \item \textbf{Obtaining OOD performance on the collection of datasets.} 
    We use a pre-trained ResNet18~\citep{he_deep_2015} or CLIP model (ViT-B/32)~\citep{radford_learning_2021} to solve each OOD task and obtain the ``ground truth'' performance of each algorithm.
    We do a linear probing or a fine-tuning (on ResNet18 for the experiment of Table~\ref{tab:ft}). 
    \item \textbf{Evaluation}. We create 264 and 47 unseen datasets with CelebA and COCO, respectively, and use the \{\texttt{cat}, \texttt{dog}\} and \{\texttt{indoor}, \texttt{outdoor}\} images for COCO. These datasets are not new, but to our knowledge, recent datasets such as MetaShift~\citep{liang_metashift_2022} do not have a sufficient number of samples to create satisfying datasets.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{RQ1-RQ2: Algorithm selection is learnable on real-world data.} 
In Table~\ref{tab:celeba_coco}, we see that all three formulations of \alg can still select algorithms for unseen OOD datasets from both CelebA and COCO with significantly lower worst-group error and higher 0--1 Accuracy than any baselines.
The results on COCO also verify the robustness of our dataset descriptors in capturing general properties relevant to the performance of the algorithms in a way that transfers across datasets.
Additionally, we see that \ensemble~achieves the best performance among the proposed three realizations, using classification instead of a regression objective, as well as the learning of pairwise comparison
In Table~\ref{tab:comp_single_alg}, we compare \ensemble~with single algorithm baselines,
where a single algorithm is used for all unseen datasets. 
We see that our method performs an \emph{adaptive} selection of algorithms for each unseen dataset, thereby achieving lower worst-group error than any single algorithm.
Furthermore, the ratios of the selected algorithms across unseen datasets are close to the ground truth oracle selection. 
This also shows that choosing algorithms according to the nature of the dataset is both learnable and helpful.

\paragraph{RQ4: The algorithm selector learns non-trivial data-algorithm interactions.}
We evaluate the complexity of the learned data-algorithm interactions by comparing
various architectures for the algorithm selector $\phi(\cdot, w)$
(see Table~\ref{tab:comp_para}; ``Linear (ResNet)'' means for example
a linear model for the selector and a ResNet18 for the OOD task). 
First, we see that a linear model is significantly worse than an MLP. This shows that
the model \textbf{makes accurate predictions on the basis of non-linear interactions between datasets' characteristics and algorithms' performance}.
Second, we see that a k-NN is also significantly worse than an MLP.
This shows that the model \textbf{works not only by memorizing a large number of example shifts}, which a k-NN could also do. On the contrary, accurate predictions on unseen datasets require non-trivial generalization.



\section{Related Work}

\label{sec:related_work}

\textbf{OOD generalization} has been widely studied but
several benchmark studies concluded that there is no one-fits-all solution to distribution shifts
\citep{gulrajani_search_2020, wiles_fine-grained_2021, nguyen_avoiding_2021, ye_ood-bench_2022, liang_metashift_2022, yang_change_2023, bell_reassessing_2024}.
These meta studies show that OOD algorithms perform differently in different situations.
An algorithm only shines when its underlying assumptions are met. 
This is the motivation for our goal of \emph{adaptively} using existing algorithms,
and investigating whether this process can be learned and automated.

\textbf{Algorithm selection.}~
Model selection~\citep{forster_key_2000, raschka_model_2020} and algorithm selection~\citep{rice_algorithm_1976, kerschke_automated_2019}
are integral parts of any machine learning workflow. 
Regarding OOD generalization, a series of works~\citep{liu_neuron_2024, baek_agreement---line_2023, garg_leveraging_2022, miller_accuracy_2021, lu_characterizing_2023} use heuristics to estimate a models' OOD performance on a target dataset when no labeled OOD data is available.
These heuristics have one or several of these downsides: (1)~they can only be estimated after training, (2)~they require (unlabeled) test data, and (3)~they work only under restricted conditions. 
In contrast, our work aims for \emph{a priori} selection of the best algorithm, i.e.\ before training on the target dataset. 

Concurrently to our work,~\citet{bell_reassessing_2024} identified a very similar motivation.
They proposed to select among algorithms that deal with spurious correlations
based on their performance on benchmarks most similar to the target dataset. 
The differences with our work are: (1)~we use a \emph{learning} approach rather than a similarity heuristic, (2)~our learning process relies on \emph{semi-synthetic} data rather than existing benchmarks, which helps cover a broader set of distribution shifts, and (3)~we also consider \emph{label and covariate shifts} and their combinations, not only spurious correlations. 

\textbf{Meta-learning.}~~
Our ``learning-to-learn'' process resembles meta-learning~\citep{vilalta_perspective_2002, hospedales_meta-learning_2022} in that it carries over prior experience from historical tasks to future similar ones. 
\citet{achille_task2vec_2019, ozturk_zero-shot_2022, arango_quick-tune_2024, zhang_model_2023} learn to select among pretrained models for downstream tasks
or for  outlier detection \citet{zhao_automatic_2021}. 
They either first require training a model on the target dataset
or they are not suited to OOD generalization.

\textbf{AutoML.}~~
Our work is distantly related to AutoML, which aims to identify complete ML workflows, often by informed trial-and-error
\citep{hutter_automated_2019, he_automl_2021}. Our work differs in its aim for \emph{a priori} algorithm selection, and its side benefit of unveiling datasets' properties that are predictive of the applicability of existing algorithms.


\section{Conclusions}

\label{sec:conclusion}
This work explored a new avenue for improving OOD generalization by better using existing algorithms instead of creating new ones.
We formalized the task of \emph{OOD algorithm selection}
and took the first step to learn it.
We treated it as a classification over candidate algorithms,
learned with supervision from a \emph{dataset of datasets}
representing a variety of distribution shifts.
The resulting system not only predicts low-test-error algorithms on unseen datasets,
but also reveals key properties of datasets that allow algorithms to outperform one another.
More could be learned in the future about existing algorithms e.g.\ by training the selector as an interpretable decision tree.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
The proposed solution served to verify the viability of the new task.
Several limitations and promising directions remain to be addressed in future work.
(1)~\textbf{Candidate algorithms}.
We only included a few algorithms representative of broad categories, and known to be simple but effective in different settings.
It is straightforward to include more algorithms and variations thereof (e.g.\ different hyperparameters).
It will be interesting whether the approach remains accurate when differences across algorithms are fine-grained.
(2)~\textbf{Real-world settings}. The approach needs to be applied to real-world data (e.g.\ from WILDS, \citet{koh_wilds_2021}) and larger-scale models. This will evaluate the limits of transferability of the learned selector, as started in Section~\ref{sec:exps}.
(3)~\textbf{Dataset descriptors}. A promising direction to improve transferability is to replace our dataset descriptors with learned representations of datasets e.g.\ with Set Transformers~\cite{lee_set_2018}
or Dataset2Vec~\cite{jomaa_dataset2vec_2021}.
