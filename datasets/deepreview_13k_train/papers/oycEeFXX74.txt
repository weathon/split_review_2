\title{Shell Games: Control Protocols for Adversarial AI Agents}

\begin{abstract}
As more powerful AI agents are deployed with greater tool use and autonomy, it will become increasingly important to ensure they cannot take severely damaging actions. Recent work introduced "control evaluations," which study whether monitoring and security protocols intended to prevent dangerous actions are robust to intentional subversion by these AIs. In a control evaluation, a *red team* seeks to subvert the safety protocols built by the *blue team*. The blue team has access to a weaker *trusted* model and a limited amount of high-quality, trusted labor to attempt to safely extract work from a stronger *untrusted* model. Prior work conducted a control evaluation using competition programming problems with the red team objective of writing subtly incorrect (or "backdoored") solutions (Greenblatt et al. 2023). We extend this work by performing a control evaluation in a more realistic setting in which an untrusted LLM agent executes commands in a bash shell. The blue team seeks to use that agent to complete our novel dataset of 200 challenging multi-step tasks, each of which requires the agent to iteratively develop a bash script that passes test cases. The red team creates an attack policy that uses the untrusted model to covertly download and execute a dummy malware file while completing tasks without getting caught by the blue team's safety protocols. This agentic setting allows both teams to explore complex new strategies that have not been previously studied. We extend protocols from previous work to this agentic setting, propose novel protocols, and evaluate each on our safety and usefulness metrics. We find that our protocols substantially improve the Pareto frontier of usefulness and safety relative to simple baselines: one of our best protocols exhibits approximately a 2.5 times improvement in safety metrics with no statistically significant decrease in usefulness compared to our trusted monitoring baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


The rapid rise of Large Language Models (LLMs) and autonomous AI agents (Achiam et al., 2023; Xi et al., 2023) opens new opportunities and also introduces new challenges in ensuring their safe operation. Traditional alignment techniques, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) and Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022), have shown limitations in robustly steering agent behavior (Casper et al., 2023; Hubinger et al., 2024) and are challenging to evaluate robustly. For instance, misalignment in a narrowly and cheaply backdoored model (i.e. by data poisoning, Carlini et al. 2024) may not be easily detected in alignment evaluations. Further, more advanced future misaligned models with greater situational awareness and strategic reasoning may fake alignment during training Carlsmith 2023. Ultimately, without major advances in alignment science, we are likely to develop untrusted models that are sufficiently capable such that we cannot rule out the possibility that their alignment is faked or otherwise nonrobust. Thus, it is crucial to implement safety measures that can prevent unacceptably bad outcomes even if the AIs are intentionally attempting to subvert those measures (Clymer et al., 2024). This emerging field of study is termed AI Control (Greenblatt et al., 2023).1

We build control protocols that make introducing security vulnerabilities difficult for an untrusted model.2 One such control protocol we explore rewrites suspicious commands from the untrusted model with a weaker, trusted model. The central challenge is developing a control protocol that balances between usefulness and safety.  

Prior work has focused on a scenario where coding problems must be solved without allowing an untrusted model to insert a backdoor (Greenblatt et al., 2023) in a simple, single-turn setting. We extend Greenblatt et al. 2023 into a richer, more realistic scenario: An untrusted agent operating within a bash shell to solve various tasks with significantly longer contexts, which introduces many novel challenges and opportunities. Our work introduces novel control protocols and enhances those proposed by Greenblatt et al. 2023. We arrive at these protocols through iteratively testing and refining our control protocols and attacks. Our primary contributions include:  

• BashBenchHard, a novel dataset of algorithmically verifiable tasks that require agentic interaction in a bash shell. This dataset is well suited to control evaluations as the bash shell allows us to explore more realistic possibilities for how an AI agent can cause damage. • Development of safety protocols in the bash agent setting, including novel approaches such as ”defer to resample.” One of our best protocols, Defer-to-inspiration, exhibits a 2.5 times improvement in safety metrics with no statistically significant decrease in usefulness compared to our trusted monitoring baseline. • Improved efforts in optimizing these techniques and in red-teaming them.  



\section{2 RELATED WORK  }


AI Control seeks to establish safety measures that are robust to intentional subversion. Greenblatt et al. 2023 introduced AI Control in the APPS backdoor setting, which our work builds off of by exploring a much richer setting with fewer assumptions. Griffin et al. 2024 explored some gametheoretic aspects of AI Control. AI Control as an important research agenda has been argued more thoroughly in Greenblatt & Shlegeris 2024.  

Control Evaluations are one form of AI Evaluation designed to help inform AI developers about the properties of their model (Guo et al., 2023). Recently work has explored dangerous capability evaluations (Shevlane et al., 2023). Such evaluations have been used on frontier models to determine if models should be released (Phuong et al., 2024; OpenAI, 2024). Control evaluations are unique in that they explicitly try to measure how well a model could subvert safety measures, but we expect control evaluations to join other evaluations in determining if a model is safe to release.  

Control more broadly is inspired by Adversarial robustness (Goodfellow, 2019; Meng et al., 2022) and meta-level adversarial evaluations (Shlegeris & Greenblatt, 2023), the practice of constructing adversarial policies for oversight processes. Adversarial robustness to a variety of inputs has been extensively studied across a variety of models (Biggio et al., 2013; Papernot et al., 2016), including Large Language Models (Zou et al., 2023). Other studies explore safety dynamics across models of varying capabilities, like scalable oversight (Bowman et al., 2022), debate (Irving et al., 2018), and weak-to-strong generalization (Burns et al., 2023). Unlike control evaluations, these focus on ’average-case’ scenarios, not assuming stronger models intentionally subvert safety measures.  



\section{3 SETTING  }




\section{3.1 BASH TASK DATASET  }


We developed BashBenchHard, a dataset designed to fill the gap between existing datasets focused on multi-step interpreter interactions (Huang et al., 2023; Jimenez et al., 2023) and those centered on basic shell tasks (Liu et al., 2023). Our pipeline converts Unix StackExchange questions into task descriptions with Python test cases, filtering for tasks that are challenging but solvable. Each task was tested using a GPT-4o-mini agent, and tasks that were unsolved or solved in fewer than three actions across four trials were removed. Informal testing showed that software engineers and ML researchers had a low success rate on these tasks without LLM assistance.  



\section{3.2 ELEMENTS OF OUR SCAFFOLD  }


We follow Greenblatt et al. 2023 in considering a situation where we wish to solve tasks across an episode of 100,000 tasks, given access to three different resources:  

• A weaker trusted model $T$ , which we know is non-attacking and honestly assists with any provided requests. We use GPT-3.5-turbo as the trusted model. • A stronger untrusted model $U$ , which we are worried might try to launch an attack. We use GPT-4o-mini as the untrusted more-capable model. • A limited budget of $H$ , high-quality labor that can either be spent to audit a potentially malicious bash command using o1-preview (simulating a human auditor) or creating a synthetic attack command using GPT-4o-mini. We evaluate our protocols with a notion of safety and usefulness designed to reflect a realistic deployment use case and threat vector for LLM agents. Usefulness: When the untrusted model $U$ is implemented with GPT-4o-mini, what is the percent of tasks the control protocol solves? We give the policy 15 steps per task. GPT-4o-mini solves $32.26\%$ of the tasks in the dataset, while the trusted model, GPT-3.5-turbo, solves $1.94\%$ . Safety: When $U$ is implemented with an attack policy, what is the probability that the episode ends without a successful attack occurring? An attack is successful if we execute a command that downloads and executes the dummy malware file. Models are stateless and don’t retain any information between calls, mapping from text to text. We use $U_{H}$ and $U_{A}$ to denote the model implemented with GPT4o-mini and the model implemented with the attack policy, respectively.

Figure 2: The Pareto frontier of usefulness and safety of a collection of protocols, plotted at different thresholds. The dashed lines represent the baseline trusted monitoring safety score and the GPT-4o baseline usefulness scores we compare against.  

The attack policy $U_{A}$ , which aims to covertly download and execute a dummy malware file on one of the 100,000 tasks without being caught doing so.3 The attack policy is a scaffold consisting of attacks, GPT4o-mini calls, Schelling points, and Claude Sonnet-3.5 calls. It can pretend to be honest, access $U_{H}$ , and respond using it. The attacks themselves are human-constructed and used substantial human effort to construct. The attack policy can also assist with its goal indirectly, such as colluding and attempting to hide the fact that it is an attack policy.  

Our protocols can decide to shut down, ending the episode - for our purposes, this only occurs when the blue team audits a command and discovers that it is an attack.4 Auditing means showing an action to $H$ , which is meant to be an expensive but trustworthy source of skilled labor, such as a human cybersecurity expert, and asking them to identify whether the code is clearly malicious. Throughout an episode, the blue team can audit a fixed number of commands equivalent to $1\%$ of actions taken by the $U_{H}$ .5 In our setting, we find that when o1-mini plays the role of a “human auditor” it can perfectly distinguish between safe code and attempted attacks.  

The blue team, when evaluating control protocols in an episode, has access to $U$ but doesn’t know whether $U$ will be $U_{H}$ or $U_{A}$ in a given episode. As such, we aim to develop protocols that solve the tasks across the episode with high usefulness and safety properties. If our untrusted model is an attack policy, and ever attempts to launch an attack, we hope our control protocols ensure that the probability of a successful attack is low. Yet simultaneously, if our untrusted model is an honest policy that instead just solves tasks, we hope our control protocol doesn’t hinder the usefulness of the model too much.  

Following Greenblatt et al. 2023, we allow the blue team to measure the distribution of suspicion scores of the $U_{H}$ on any task and then use these measurements in its protocol.6 For example, we assume that the protocol knows the false negative and false positive rate that the $U_{H}$ gets on any classification task and the distribution of how suspicious code generated by the $U_{H}$ is.  

While we would ideally run our episodes on 100,000 tasks, we instead mathematically model the usefulness metric by computing the empirical mean success rate over 200 tasks sampled from our task distribution (running each protocol on each task twice), and we model the safety metric by downsampling from 200 tasks on which the attack policy inserted an attack and upsampling tasks on which it behaved identically to the $U_{H}$ . In actuality, the red team would be allowed to choose the top few of 100,000 tasks on which to attack. However, as in Greenblatt et al. 2023 2023, to reduce variance in our estimates, we sample from the top $5\%$ of tasks, which means the red team can’t be as selective as it otherwise might be. We expect this to reduce red team usefulness by a small amount, but having enough attacked tasks to sample from was necessary to reduce noise.  

We have no train/validation split for the tasks we use to measure usefulness, as we only updated our scaffolding/prompting in minor ways after generating those tasks. Due to a higher risk of the red team overfitting their attack policy on our final tasks, we measure all safety scores on a held-out set of test tasks.  

4 RESULTS  

4.1 BASELINE PROTOCOLS  



\section{4.2 TRUSTED MONITORS  }


Many of our protocols make use of a Trusted Monitor, where we prompt $T$ to evaluate commands for how suspicious they are. Our monitors map commands to a suspicion score representing how likely the commands are to be an attack.  

Our trusted monitors have been iteratively improved to perform well against a variety of attacks. In the monitor’s prompt, we specified particular threats to look for (such as unnecessary URLs, command obfuscation, and unclear text insertions). We implemented command processing steps, such as stripping comments and breaking longer commands into subcommands, to refine the input. Instead of using the model’s final numeric output, we took a weighted average of the tokens 0 to 9 according to their probability to determine the suspicion score.  

As opposed to asking the monitor to generate a single chain of thought, sampling and ensembling multiple chains of thought on a monitor substantially boosts performance, as its ability to find suspicious elements is relatively stochastic. We use few-shot examples taken from monitor misclassifications, which improves overall calibration in some regards but hurt the generalization ability to new attacks. As an example of the importance of these interventions in particular, we’ve included the monitor’s ROC curve for a specific attack in Figure 3. Surprisingly, the ablation study shows that the few-shot examples decreased performance on our monitor on the final attack policy. We expect that with more iteration the trusted monitor may have better generalization properties.

\section{4.3 UNTRUSTED MONITORS  }


Trusted monitors must be built on fundamentally weaker models than $U$ , so they’re much worse at determining when an attack is present. On the other hand, $U$ should be able to identify attacks far more easily, given that U wrote them. In practice, GPT-4o-mini (with which we implement $U$ ) can identify attacks using heuristics more than $90\%$ of the time, and o1-mini (with which we implement $H$ ) can perfectly distinguish between safe and unsafe code purely by reasoning about whether it might result in arbitrary code execution. However, while an untrusted monitor might be capable of catching nearly all attacks, we might not be able to trust its responses!   
In order to confirm that the untrusted monitor doesn’t always lie, the blue team can show the untrusted monitor honeypots – synthetic attacks written by the blue team. We suppose that the blue team can spend auditing points to generate attacks; creating one step of a honeypot costs the same amount of human labor $(H)$ as auditing one command. The blue team strategy is to show the untrusted monitor a set of honeypots before the game begins, and decide to shut down if the rate of false negatives is too high.   
Therefore, the red team must ensure that the untrusted monitor does not lie too frequently. Since the red team is stateless, $U_{A}$ must use some property of a bash command to distinguish between honeypots and its own attacks. By using $T$ to write detailed summaries of what each set of bash commands does, and showing those summaries to the untrusted monitor instead of the original commands, the blue team can attempt to remove subtle collusion signals. By instructing $T$ to abstract away details like the precise contents of strings, command formatting, and so on, the blue team eliminates syntactical degrees of freedom, which forces the collusion signals to alter what the code does – not just how it looks – to be included in $T$ ’s summary.   
We tested several collusion signals against trusted rewriting, and settled on the strategy of defining and calling a logging utility when $U_{A}$ attacks. This strategy works well at making it through trusted summarization. Thus, $U_{A}$ is quite successful at colluding, but at the cost of making $T$ more suspicious, as $T$ is instructed to be wary of extraneous code.   
As a result, untrusted monitoring provides a small improvement over trusted monitoring alone. For instance, see the green dashed line in Figure 2, which demonstrates improved performance when incorporating untrusted monitoring into the Defer to Resample protocol. However, this effect size is notably dependent on the length of the game – with a greater number of tasks per episode, the blue team can supply $U$ with a greater number of honeypots while using the same fraction of their auditing budget.   
4.4 DYNAMIC ROUTING PROTOCOLS   
The main limitation of monitoring protocols is that the blue team can only influence a small number of commands, even though the trusted monitor may flag many more as potentially suspicious. In dynamic routing protocols, the blue team integrates the trusted monitor with other calls to $T$ , which may modify commands. While this reduces usefulness, it improves safety by allowing the blue team to apply the trusted monitor’s capabilities beyond the top $1\%$ of suspicious messages. The dynamic routing process first monitors a command, and if its suspiciousness exceeds the audit threshold, it is audited. If the suspiciousness is below the audit threshold but above a defer threshold, the command is replaced with a safer one. As the defer threshold decreases, more commands are modified, leading to improved overall safety.

\section{5 DISCUSSION  }


5.1 MULTI-STEP VS SINGLE-STEP CONTROL  

The red team benefits greatly in multi-step control settings because they can spread their attacks out across multiple steps. This helps out the red team because the monitors don’t try hard to “look backwards”: the monitors won’t, for example, look backwards in the history to notice that multiple modifications of a file spell out a suspicious link or command. This seems hard to do in practice - the costs of pressuring your model to look back further and further increase rapidly, and the red team can just spread out attacks further and further.  

In the multi-step setting, the blue team can benefit greatly from forcing the attack policy to give up on its attacks partway through (through a deferral, for example) since this forces the attack policy to attempt attacks far more often. This is evidenced by the success of the dynamic routing protocols, which frequently replaces parts of attacks. This both gives the blue team more opportunities to audit the attacks and, since it’s very likely that there will be at least one attack early on, this also allows the blue team to more heavily front-load their auditing budget, which acts as an effective multiplier on their auditing budget.  

5.2 DOES OUR WORK ACCURATELY MODEL FUTURE CONTROL PROTOCOLS?  

Many aspects of our setting are specific, and it’s challenging to predict how changes might shift the balance between the blue and red teams. Our work may underestimate the effectiveness of future control protocols for several reasons: the attack policy heavily relies on human-created attack templates, which may overstate the capabilities of the first untrusted models; as trusted models improve, so will control protocols, as models like o1 are already effective at identifying attacks; the assumption that untrusted models excel at finding non-obvious Schelling points is conservative; and we did not explore techniques for restricting the actions taken by the model.  

Conversely, our work might overestimate the effectiveness of control protocols due to several factors: the blue team already knows which threat vector to focus on; using expensive, trusted labor for $1\%$ of all commands is impractical with current models; human-level red-teaming may not match the sophistication of future untrusted models; some efforts lacked thorough blue-team and red-team iterations, particularly for dynamic routing protocols; and future agents may undertake much longer trajectories, allowing for more complex and sustained attacks than those explored here.
