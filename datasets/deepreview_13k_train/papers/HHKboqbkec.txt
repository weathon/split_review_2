\title{Scaling Multimodal Theory-of-Mind with Weak-to-Strong Bayesian Reasoning}

\begin{abstract}
Theory of Mind (ToM) enables individuals to understand and predict thoughts, emotions, and intentions of the others. To replicate this cognitive ability in machines, especially under complex multimodal environments, recent advances combine Bayesian-based state inference with deep learning models to estimate mental states, where the Bayesian model handles state transitions and a language model (LM) estimates the likelihood of intermediate states. However, while post-training an LM to specialise in ToM tasks improves performance, the computational cost increases as the LM scales, limiting the model size to 7 billion parameters. Despite this post-training process, smaller LMs still struggle with the physical and mental modelling demands of ToM due to their limited world knowledge and reasoning capacity. To address this, we propose a scalable solution that leverages the strengths of larger LMs (up to 70 and 405 billion parameters, respectively), including their vast world knowledge and atomic-level reasoning capabilities, without increasing post-training resource requirements. Our method transfers ToM-specific behaviours from a post-trained small LM to guide the latent reasoning of a larger LM during test time. This weak-to-strong control mechanism enables the larger LM to improve Bayesian likelihood estimation at each inference step, harnessing its reasoning power in ToM scenarios while reducing the need for additional training resources. Extensive experiments demonstrate the significant effectiveness of our scaled approach. It is better at inferring human mental states in complex and interactive environments, outperforming the state-of-the-art solution by $\sim4.6$% across multiple tasks on the multimodal ToM benchmark and unseen scenarios. Our code and datasets are available: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/scale-bayesian-tom-248B
\end{abstract}

\section{109 2.2 INFERRING HUMAN MENTAL STATES  }


110 To infer human mental states, we generate and evaluate hypotheses about human intentions by   
111 employing Bayesian inverse planning. These hypotheses are assessed through action likelihoods,   
112 which directly inform the posterior probabilities of different mental state explanations. To compute   
113 these crucial likelihoods, an LM is controlled by our weak-to-strong mechanism to serve as the policy   
114 model. This approach harnesses the world knowledge and reasoning capabilities of large LLMs while   
115 aligning their behaviour patterns to ToM tasks without extensive computational requirements.   
116 Behaviour modelling: a Markov decision process formulation The behaviour of an agent can be   
117 formulated as a forward generative model based on a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process   
118 (POMDP), defined by the tuple $\langle S,A,{\mathcal{T}},G,R,\Omega,O,\gamma\rangle$ (Kaelbling et al., 1998; Jin et al., 2024).   
119 Here, $s^{t}\in S$ and $a^{t}\,\,\bar{\in}\,\,A$ represent the state and action at time $t$ , respectively. $\mathcal{T}(s^{t}|s,a)$ denotes   
120 the state transition probabilities. The goal $g\,\in\,G$ determines the reward $r_{.}^{t}=R(s^{t},a^{t},g)$ . The   
121 agent’s observation $\bar{o}^{t}\in\Omega$ is obtained via the observation function $o^{t}=O(s^{t})$ . The discount factor   
122 is $\gamma\in\,(0,1]$ . Crucially, the agent’s belief, $b(s)$ , is a probability distribution over the state. This   
123 belief is dynamically updated during belief evolution $\bar{P(b^{\tau}\mid b^{\tau-\bar{1}},s^{\tau})}$ , where $b(s)$ is factorized into   
124 probabilities over the possible locations of individual objects.   
125 Inverse inference: from observed behaviours to the mental states While the POMDP formulates   
126 a forward model of agent behaviour, the heart of this Bayesian approach is to invert this process –   
127 inferring the agent’s goals and beliefs from observed behaviours, i.e., actions (Baker et al., 2017).   
128 Assuming deterministic state transitions for simplicity, we jointly infer the agent’s goal and belief   
129 based on observed states and actions. The posterior probability of an agent’s goal $g$ and belief $b^{t}$   
130 given a sequence of observed states $s^{1:t}$ and actions $a^{1:t-1}$ is expressed as:  

$$
P(g,b^{t}|s^{1:t},a^{1:t-1})\propto\prod_{\tau=1}^{t}\pi(a^{\tau}|g,b^{\tau})P(b^{\tau}|b^{\tau-1},s^{\tau})P(b^{0})P(g),
$$  

131 where $\pi(a^{\tau}|\boldsymbol{g},\boldsymbol{b}^{\tau})$ represents the agent’s policy, which captures the probability of taking action   
132 $a^{\tau}$ given a goal $g$ and belief $b^{\tau}$ . This reflects the agent’s decision-making process, influenced   
133 by its goals and the current state of its beliefs. Belief evolution is modeled as $P(b^{\tau}|b^{\tau-1},s^{\tau})=$   
134 $\frac{P(s^{\tau}|b^{\tau-1})P(b^{\tau-1})}{P(s^{\tau})}$ , where $P(s^{\tau}|b^{\tau-1})$ is the likelihood of observing $s^{\tau}$ given the prior belief $b^{\tau-1}$ ,   
135 and $P(b^{\tau-1})$ represents the prior belief at the previous step. This belief evolution describes how   
136 the belief $b^{\tau}$ evolves from the prior belief $b^{\tau-1}$ after observing a new state $s^{\prime}$ , and it follows   
137 Bayesian principles, ensuring that new evidence incrementally refines the agent’s understanding of   
138 its environment. For example, if an object is observed inside a container at time $^7$ , the corresponding   
139 belief about the object’s location is updated accordingly. In practice, $\pi(a^{\tau}|g,b_{}^{\tau})$ and $P(b^{\tau}|b^{\tau-1},s^{\tau})$   
140 can be approximated using likelihood generated by a language model. $P(b^{0})$ is set as a uniform   
141 distribution to reflect equal uncertainty about all possible object locations at the start, while $P(g)$   
142 encodes prior knowledge about the likelihood of different goals.   
143 To compare different hypotheses about the agent’s goals and beliefs, we evaluate their relative log  
144 likelihoods from a given set of hypotheses, separating the contributions from the current time step and   
145 the accumulated effect of prior steps. Consider two hypotheses, $H_{1}=\langle g_{1},b_{1}^{t}\rangle$ and $H_{2}=\langle g_{2},b_{2}^{t}\rangle$ ,   
146 representing different goal-belief pairs. Their relative log-likelihoods are compared as:  

$$
\log\frac{P(g_{1},b_{1}^{t}|s^{1:t},a^{1:t})}{P(g_{2},b_{2}^{t}|s^{1:t},a^{1:t})}=\log\frac{\pi(a^{t}|g_{1},b_{1}^{t})}{\pi(a^{t}|g_{2},b_{2}^{t})}+\log\frac{P(b_{1}^{t}|\hat{b}^{t-1},s^{t})}{P(b_{2}^{t}|\hat{b}^{t-1},s^{t})}+\sum_{\tau=1}^{t-1}\log\frac{\pi(a^{\tau}|g_{1},\hat{b}^{\tau})}{\pi(a^{\tau}|g_{2},\hat{b}^{\tau})}\,.
$$  

147 The first term compares log-likelihoods of actions and belief updates at the current step, reflecting how   
148 each hypothesis explains the agent’s latest behavior. The second term sums log-likelihoods from prior   
149 steps, ensuring the entire action history informs the hypothesis evaluation. In practice, the belief   
150 $\hat{b}^{\tau}$ is not explicitly updated as a posterior distribution $P(b^{\tau}\mid b^{\tau-1},s^{\tau})$ . Instead, it is symbolically   
151 approximated as a structured hypothesis (e.g., possible object locations) that represents the agent’s   
152 understanding of the environment at each step. By comparing action likelihoods $\pi(a^{\tau}\mid g,s^{\tau},\hat{b}^{\tau})$   
153 across hypotheses, beliefs are implicitly evaluated and updated.   
154 Weak-to-strong controlled large policy model When augmenting likelihood estimation with the   
155 guided large LM’s broad generalization capabilities, we scale up the LM used only for test-time   
156 computing and avoid the direct post-training on large LM. The true policy $\pi(a^{\tau}\mid g,\dot{b^{\tau}})$ is estimated   
157 through a language model $(\pi)$ -estimated probability ${\tilde{\pi}}(a^{t}\mid s^{t},g,{\hat{b}}^{t})$ :  

$$
\pi(a^{\tau}\mid g,b^{\tau})=\tilde{\pi}(a^{t}\mid s^{t},g,\hat{b}^{t})+\varepsilon,
$$  

158 where $\varepsilon$ represents the inherent approximation error. When applied to the Bayesian inverse planning

$$
P(g,b^{t}\mid s^{1:t},a^{1:t-1})\propto\prod_{\tau=1}^{t}[\tilde{\pi}(a^{\tau}\mid s^{\tau},g,\hat{b}^{\tau})+\varepsilon]\cdot P(b^{\tau}\mid b^{\tau-1},s^{\tau})\cdot P(b^{0})P(g).
$$  

160 It integrates the approximation into Bayesian reasoning, considering $s_{i},b_{i},g_{i},a_{i}$ updates over time.

\section{183 3 EXPERIMENTS  }


184 We examine the scaling benefits of this Bayesian weak-to-strong reasoning in multimodal ToM tasks.   
185 For the strong component, we scale up the large LMs to 70B and 405B parameters. In contrast, for   
186 the weak component, we reduce the size of the small LMs from 8B to 4B parameters. First, the   
187 results reveal a positive correlation between model size and ToM capabilities, especially when the   
188 larger models are guided by the post-trained behaviours of the smaller models. Interestingly, these   
189 post-trained behaviours are also effectively captured by smaller LMs. We also illustrate how the large   
190 LMs are progressively redirected to the answer space during the Bayesian process.

192 Datasets (i) For post-training, we use MMToM sampled from an apartment environment simulator,

198 large base LM guided by a smaller, post-trained LM—acquires robust exposure to diverse scenarios.   
199 (ii) For evaluation, we use the MMToM-QA (Jin et al., 2024), an evaluation benchmark aimed at   
200 evaluating ToM reasoning over multimodal situations. The dataset consists of 134 videos, each   
201 showing a person searching for household objects, with an average of 1,462 frames per video   
202 representing approximately 36 human actions. These videos are accompanied by 600 questions   
1$ ), evenly divided between the categories of belief inference (with 1.1,   
204 1.2, and 1.3 subtasks) and goal inference (with 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, and 2.4 subtasks). Each question is

225 Tab.1 uses human performance as the gold standard. Humans clearly outperform all models (with

249 In the Bayesian framework, we explore the role of LMs in likelihood estimation and examine how   
250 their scale and post-training affect performance across various ToM tasks. According to Tab.2, (i)

255 from 70B to 405B suggests that the benefits of scaling have not yet reached saturation, indicating

257 performance on ToM tasks, even when Larger LMs already perform well in zero-shot scenarios.   
258 This effect is consistent across model sizes, from smaller models such as 7B/8B to larger models up to   
259 70B, regardless of the specific version (Llama-2, Llama-3, or Llama-3.1). For belief inference tasks,   
260 which are closely tied to world knowledge, post-training helps align the large models’ knowledge   
261 more precisely with the input questions. For goal inference tasks, which are linked to environmental   
262 dynamics, post-training refines the models’ atomic-level reasoning (i.e., predicting $a$ based on $s,b,g)$ ,   
263 resulting in greater improvements compared to belief inference. This suggests that post-training   
264 provides a more substantial benefti for tasks that require dynamic reasoning. (iii) Our weak-to-strong   
265 control approach approximates the benefits of direct post-training in Bayesian inference. When   
266 comparing models such as Llama-2, Llama-3, and Llama-3.1, we find that direct post-training on the   
267 70B model, even with adjusted hyperparameters from the 8B model (e.g., reducing the alpha value   
268 from 32 to 16), does not produce results as robust as our method. We attribute this to the difficulty of   
269 finding optimal hyperparameters for larger models, which require more extensive tuning. In contrast,   
270 our weak-to-strong control, which uses a well-trained smaller LM to guide the larger LMs, allows for   
271 more consistent improvements without the need for extensive hyperparameter trials.

273 In the Bayesian framework, prior experiments show that post-trained behaviours from small LMs can   
274 effectively guide the pretrained capabilities of larger LMs during test time. To further study the role   
275 of post-training to weak-to-strong control, Tab.3 investigates whether post-trained behaviours can be   
276 learned effectively with reduced computational resources, while the pretrained capabilities of larger   
277 LMs are still available. Specifically, we examine whether downsized smaller LMs can effectively   
278 capture these post-trained behaviours and guide the pre-trained capabilities of larger LMs without   
279 compromising performance. We use 8B LMs as baselines in normal size, and we also downsize   
280 them to two 4B variants: Llama-3.1-Minitron-4B-Width, which reduces the hidden size of each   
281 layer; and Llama-3.1-Minitron-4B-Depth, which cuts the number of layer (Sreenivas et al., 2024).   
282 Despite their smaller size, they maintained comparable accuracy in weak-to-strong control. While   
283 the 4B-Width LM underperformed the 4B-Depth LM in zero-shot scenarios, its post-training results   
284 surpass the 4B-Depth, especially when controlling the 70B large LM, demonstrating its superior   
285 transferability. These results highlight two key points: (i) downsizing the weak component can   
286 still effectively guide larger LMs without a significant loss in accuracy, and (ii) reducing model   
287 width, rather than depth, tends to be more generalizable, as deeper models demonstrate better   
288 transferability—aligning with learning principles of the width-depth trade-offs in small-scale studies   
289 (Telgarsky, 2016; Lu et al., 2017; Raghu et al., 2017).

\section{378 4 RELATED WORK  }


379 Modelling human mental states There are many studies on understanding human behaviour by   
380 classifying and predicting physical motion patterns (Aggarwal & Ryoo, 2011; Caba Heilbron et al.,   
381 2015; Choi & Savarese, 2013; Shu et al., 2015). Beyond physical behaviour, some studies focus   
382 specifically on modeling human mental states, i.e. ToM. ToM models have followed two broad   
383 approaches: Bayesian methods and end-to-end deep learning. Bayesian ToM models (Baker et al.,   
384 2017; Jara-Ettinger, 2019; Shu et al., 2021) rely on structured probabilistic frameworks to infer   
385 mental states from sparse observations of human behaviour. On the other hand, end-to-end models   
386 such as ToMnet (Rabinowitz et al., 2018; Shu et al., 2021; Sclar et al., 2022) have been trained   
387 directly on ToM tasks, learning relationships between data patterns without explicit causal models of   
388 mental states (Sap et al., 2022; Zhi-Xuan et al., 2022; Ullman, 2023). More recently, neurosymbolic   
389 reasoning systems use the neural models for feature extraction, while also incorporating probabilistic   
390 models for structured reasoning (Wong et al., 2023; Ying et al., 2024; 2023). They face challenges in   
391 dynamic and multimodal environments, where both physical and mental state reasoning are required.   
392 Different from prior studies, our work operates in more complex and dynamic multimodal ToM   
393 environments, where physical actions and mental state reasoning are intertwined.   
394 Post-training LLMs for downstream tasks Post-training can project the pre-trained capabilities   
395 of LMs into downstream tasks such as dialogue generation (Ouyang et al., 2022), human value   
396 alignment (Bai et al., 2022), and multimodal tasks (OpenAI, 2023b; Liu et al., 2023). Previous   
397 approaches have also used reweighting techniques to adjust the output predictions of fine-tuned   
398 LLMs, interpolating the effects of fine-tuning with pre-trained knowledge to achieve human-centered   
399 and trustworthy text generation (Liu et al., 2021; Mitchell et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024). More   
400 recently, LLMs are post-trained as large action/policy models for decision-making in embodied   
401 agents, allowing them to interact with and explore environments (Kim et al., 2024; Szot et al., 2024;   
402 Li et al., 2024). Our study differs by framing LMs as policy models in the context of Bayesian inverse   
403 inference, specifically to model human mental states. We address the limitations of existing ToM   
404 methods by scaling large policy models at test time using a likelihood redirection strategy, reasoning   
405 more accurately in complex ToM scenarios. See App.A.1 for additional discussions.  



\section{406 5 DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION  }


407 This study investigates scalable Bayesian inference in complex and dynamic ToM environments.   
408 Existing methods based on normal-sized LMs often fail to provide sufficient reasoning capabilities   
409 and world knowledge, particularly when used as likelihood estimators in diverse challenging ToM   
410 scenarios. To overcome these limitations, we propose a method that abstracts and transfers the   
411 post-trained behavioral patterns of smaller LMs. This approach allows the extensive world knowledge   
412 of large LMs to be progressively redirected towards ToM reasoning tasks. The weak-to-strong control   
413 mechanism enables scalable reasoning by using small, post-trained LMs to guide large LMs at test   
414 time. This approach avoids additional post-training resources for large models, yet allows effective   
415 test-time scaling of Bayesian ToM reasoning even in dynamic and complex scenarios.

\section{600 A.1 OUR THEORETICAL RATIONALES IN SCALED TOM INFERENCE AND RELATED WORK  }


601 Our approach is based on a high-level principle derived from Theorem 1 and its proof, which implies   
602 that smaller models can approximate the scaled gradient of the loss function for larger models. This   
603 mechanism bypasses direct parameter updates in the larger model, capturing the primary adjustments   
604 needed for fine-tuning while exploiting the innate generalisation capacity of the larger model. By   
605 relying on the approximate knowledge provided by the smaller model, our framework reduces   
606 computational overhead and improves scalability.   
607 This principle is related with previous studies that have explored reweighting mechanisms for various   
608 applications (where not necessarily the same as our perspective of scaling or embodied policy model),   
609 including avoiding toxicity in text generation (Liu et al., 2021), mitigating harmful outputs in aligned   
610 models (Zhou et al., 2024), adjusting code generation (Mitchell et al., 2024), controlling sentiment   
611 in text (Han et al., 2024), and reducing hallucination or degeneration in neural text (Chuang et al.,   
612 2024; Su et al., 2022). These works demonstrate how reweighting can approximate the behaviour   
613 of large language models, mimicking direct fine-tuning in specific contexts. In contrast, our scaled   
614 ToM inference extends this principle beyond text generation tasks into the domain of social cognitive   
615 reasoning. Our framework uses language models to approximate policy behaviours for probability   
616 estimation in embodied simulators, based on the cognitive science-inspired Bayesian ToM framework.   
617 Unlike previous work focusing on text-based tasks such as sentiment or factuality control, our method   
618 addresses the unique challenges of ToM tasks, which require complex reasoning and the integration   
619 of world knowledge. These tasks involve multimodal scenarios that require understanding of agents’   
620 beliefs, goals and actions - a domain distinct from the text generation problems addressed in previous   
621 studies.

\section{629 B.2 DATA PREPROCESSING: UNIFIED SYMBOLIC REPRESENTATIONS  }


630 To enable Bayesian ToM inference at scale, following established methods mentioned in MMToM (Jin   
631 et al., 2024; Blukis et al., 2022), multimodal data (video and textual descriptions) are transformed into   
632 structured symbolic representations. This process involves three key components: visual perception,   
633 text parsing, and information fusion. Together, these components provide a unified representation   
634 of states, actions, and hypotheses required for ToM tasks.   
635 Visual Perception. The visual perception module is designed to process video frames and extract   
636 symbolic representations of the environment. For each frame, a scene graph is generated to capture   
637 the spatial and relational properties of objects and agents with the scene graph generator(Blukis et al.,   
638 2022). Following established methods in MMToM (Jin et al., 2024), voxel maps and 3D bounding   
639 boxes are utilized to infer object positions, containment relationships, and human poses. For instance,   
640 objects such as pear and basket are represented by predicates like In(pear, basket). These   
641 predicates effectively summarize the physical state of the environment, serving as critical inputs for   
642 subsequent reasoning steps.   
643 Text Parsing. To extract symbolic representations from textual descriptions, one LLM (e.g., GPT-4)   
644 processes the text into three distinct components: (i) the initial state of the environment, (ii) human   
645 actions, and (iii) the question. Each component is translated into symbolic predicates. For example:   
646   
647   
648   
649  

• The state is represented as predicates like In(pear, basket). • The action is represented as commands such as walk towards kitchen. • The question is decomposed into two hypotheses, each comprising a goal (e.g., pear) and a belief (e.g., In(pear, basket) or its negation, $\neg\mathtt{I n}$ (pear, basket)).  

650 This symbolic parsing ensures compatibility with the structured reasoning framework.  

651 Fusion. The fusion module integrates symbolic information from video and text into a unified   
652 representation. First, predicates extracted from video inputs (e.g., spatial relationships) are aligned   
653 with those parsed from text to form the initial state. Next, human actions detected from the video   
654 are matched with text-based actions, and the video sequence is segmented into discrete time steps   
655 corresponding to these actions. Starting from the initial state, the symbolic representation of the   
656 environment is updated at each time step based on newly detected predicates. This process results in a   
657 sequence of symbolic states and actions, which serve as the input for Bayesian inference. Additionally,   
658 the parsed question provides two hypotheses—goal and belief—that guide the reasoning task.  



\section{659 C THEORETICAL RATIONALE ON SCALING BAYESIAN TOM INFERENCE  }


660 Theorem 1. Let $\pi^{\mathcal{L}}$ be a pretrained base model, $\pi^{\xi}$ and $\pi^{\mathcal N}$ be smaller tunable models where   
661 $\pi^{\xi}$ is fine-tuned on the target task, and $\pi^{*}$ be the directly tuned base model. Suppose the logit   
662 adjustment $\Delta s(X_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{<t})=s_{\pi}\varepsilon(X_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{<t})-s_{\pi}\mathcal{N}(X_{t}|\boldsymbol{x}_{<t})$ approximates the scaled negative gradient   
663 of the cross-entropy loss for logits, i.e.,  

$$
\Delta s\approx-\eta\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{C E}(s_{\pi}c\,,y),
$$  

664 where $\eta$ is the learning rate. Then, the proxy-tuned model $\dot{\pi}$ , defined by  

$$
s_{\tilde{\pi}}(X_{t}|\boldsymbol x_{<t})=s_{\pi}c\left(X_{t}|\boldsymbol x_{<t}\right)+\Delta s(X_{t}|\boldsymbol x_{<t}),
$$  

665 approximates the directly tuned base model $\pi^{*}$ . The $K L$ divergence between their output distributions   
666 has this relation:  

$$
D_{K L}(P_{\pi^{*}}\|P_{\widetilde{\pi}})\le\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}\|\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{C E}(s_{\pi^{\angle}},y)\|_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}),
$$  

667 where $\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}$ is the maximum eigenvalue of the Hessian of the cross-entropy loss for the logits.  

668 Proof. When the learning rate $\eta$ is small, and the cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ is smooth and twice   
669 differentiable with respect to the logits $\boldsymbol{s}$ , then the logit adjustment $\Delta s$ approximates the scaled   
670 negative gradient of the loss as:  

$$
\Delta s\approx-\eta\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi}c\,,y).
$$  

671 The logits of the directly tuned base model $\pi^{*}$ after fine-tuning are updated using gradient descent:  

$$
s_{\pi^{*}}=s_{\pi}c-\eta\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi^{\angle}},y)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}H_{s}(\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi^{\angle}},y))+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}),
$$  

672 where $H_{s}$ is the Hessian of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ with respect to the logits. The logits of the proxy-tuned model $\tilde{\pi}$ are:  

$$
s_{\tilde{\pi}}=s_{\pi}c+\Delta s.
$$  

673 When $\Delta s\approx-\eta\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi}\varepsilon\,,y)$ , we have:  

$$
s_{\tilde{\pi}}\approx s_{\pi}c\,-\,\eta\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi}\mathcal{L}\,,y).
$$  

674 The difference in logits between the directly tuned model and the proxy-tuned model is:  

$$
\epsilon_{s}=s_{\pi^{*}}-s_{\tilde{\pi}}.
$$  

675 Then we consider their expressions:  

$$
\epsilon_{s}\approx\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}H_{s}(\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi}\boldsymbol{z}\,,y))+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}).
$$  

676 The KL divergence between the output distributions of $\pi^{*}$ and $\dot{\pi}$ is constrained using the properties   
677 of the softmax function and the Lipschitz continuity of the KL divergence:  

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\pi^{*}}\|P_{\tilde{\pi}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\epsilon_{s}\|_{2}^{2}.
$$  

678 Using the norm of $\epsilon_{s}$ :  

$$
\|\epsilon_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\approx\frac{\eta^{4}}{4}\|H_{s}(\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi}\boldsymbol{c}\,,y))\|_{2}^{2}.
$$  

679 The Hessian’s norm is constrained by its maximum eigenvalue:  

$$
\|H_{s}(\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}})\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{\operatorname*{max}}\|\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi}\mathcal{L},y)\|_{2},
$$  

680 which gives:  

$$
\|\epsilon_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\eta^{4}}{4}\lambda_{\operatorname*{max}}^{2}\|\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi}c\,,y)\|_{2}^{2}.
$$  

681 Finally, the KL divergence is:  

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{\pi^{*}}\|P_{\widetilde{\pi}})\leq\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\lambda_{\operatorname*{max}}\|\nabla_{s}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}(s_{\pi^{\angle}},y)\|_{2}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\eta^{3}).
$$  

682  

683 For theoretical implications for practical applicability, this analysis demonstrates that the weak  
684 to-strong control mechanism relies on the learned $\Delta s$ to approximate the scaled gradient   
685 $-\eta\nabla_{-}s\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{-}\mathrm{CE}(s_{-}\pi^{\mathcal{L}},y)$ with higher-order terms contributing to the residual error. Importantly, our   
686 method does not require the small LM $(\pi^{\mathcal{E}})$ to strictly approximate the exact gradient of the cross  
687 entropy loss for the large model. Instead, the large model $(\pi^{\mathcal{L}})$ leverages its intrinsic capacity for   
688 generalization and adaptation, based only on the approximate adjustment $\Delta s$ learned by the small   
689 LM.   
690 This inherent flexibility allows the large model to harness its pre-trained potential, activated by   
691 the weak-to-strong control mechanism, to effectively adapt to the current ToM task. Consequently,   
692 our method achieves stable advanced performance even in novel scenarios where the small LM   
693 provides only a coarse approximation of the gradient. This significantly reduces the reliance on   
694 strict fine-tuning and maximizes computational efficiency, ensuring the approach is both scalable and   
695 practical for the physical VirtualHome environment.

809 D.6 ON THE ROLE OF THE WEAK-TO-STRONG FRAMEWORK  

810 The weak-to-strong framework presented in this paper focuses on aligning the larger model’s distribu  
811 tion with ToM-specific beliefs and task structures while preserving its general reasoning capabilities,   
812 rather than primarily relying on the smaller model’s reasoning abilities. This design enables efficient   
813 transfer of ToM-specific task structures without compromising the broader capabilities of the larger   
814 model.   
815 The smaller model (e.g., 4B or 8B parameters) undergoes ToM-specific post-training to encode   
816 task-relevant priors, such as belief states and potential goals, without requiring advanced independent   
817 reasoning capabilities. During inference, the smaller model functions as an assistive scaffold,   
818 conditioning the larger model’s likelihood estimation in a Bayesian framework. This role is formalized   
819 through the adjustment ratio: $\frac{\pi^{\varepsilon}}{\pi^{N}}$ , where $\pi^{\mathcal{E}}$ is the post-trained smaller model’s task-specific policy,   
820 and $\pi^{\mathcal N}$ is the naive pre-trained smaller model’s policy.   
821 The larger model (e.g., 70B parameters) integrates this adjustment ratio to refine its likelihood   
822 estimation dynamically. The overall policy distribution is computed as L ππN , where πL is the policy   
823 from the larger model. This mechanism allows the larger model to retain its broad reasoning and   
824 world knowledge, ensuring its capacity for generalization while aligning with ToM-specific task   
825 structures.   
826 To validate this framework, we compared the performance of the $8\mathrm{B}\ll70\mathrm{B}$ model to the 70B-post  
827 trained model across five unseen themes, including Andersen Fairy Tales, Ancient Egyptian, and Outer   
828 Space. As shown in Table 9, the weak-to-strong mechanism achieved consistent improvements across   
829 all ToM tasks, demonstrating its ability to preserve and transfer the larger model’s general reasoning   
capabilities while aligning with ToM-specific requirements. These results, combined with theoretical   
830   
831 insights from Section C, demonstrate that the weak-to-strong framework effectively utilizes the   
832 smaller model as a task-specific lens to guide the larger model’s predictions. This collaborative   
833 dynamic ensures alignment with ToM-specific task requirements while preserving general reasoning   
834 capabilities.
