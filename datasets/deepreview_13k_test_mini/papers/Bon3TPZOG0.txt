\title{Diffusion Models Learn Low-Dimensional Distributions via Subspace Clustering}

\begin{abstract}

Recent empirical studies have demonstrated that diffusion models can effectively learn the image distribution and generate new samples. Remarkably, these models can achieve this even with a small number of training samples despite a large image dimension, circumventing the curse of dimensionality. In this work, we provide theoretical insights into this phenomenon by leveraging key empirical observations: (i) the low intrinsic dimensionality of image data, (ii) a union of manifold structure of image data, and (iii) the low-rank property of the denoising autoencoder in trained diffusion models. These observations motivate us to assume the underlying data distribution of image data as a mixture of low-rank Gaussians and to parameterize the denoising autoencoder as a low-rank model according to the score function of the assumed distribution. With these setups, we rigorously show that optimizing the training loss of diffusion models is equivalent to solving the canonical subspace clustering problem over the training samples. Based on this equivalence, we further show that the minimal number of samples required to learn the underlying distribution scales linearly with the intrinsic dimensions under the above data and model assumptions. This insight sheds light on why diffusion models can break the curse of dimensionality and exhibit the phase transition in learning distributions. Moreover, we empirically establish a correspondence between the subspaces and the semantic representations of image data, facilitating image editing. We validate these results with corroborated experimental results on both simulated distributions and image datasets.


\end{abstract}

Generative modeling is a fundamental task in deep learning, which aims to learn a data distribution from training data to generate new samples. Recently, diffusion models have emerged as a new family of generative models, demonstrating remarkable performance across diverse domains, including image generation \cite{alkhouri2024diffusion,ho2020denoising,rombach2022high,zhang2024improving}, video content generation \cite{bar2024lumiere,ho2022imagen}, speech and audio synthesis \cite{kong2020hifi,kongdiffwave2021}, fine-tuning \cite{yaras2024compressible,roich2022pivotal,ruiz2023dreambooth}
and solving inverse problem \cite{chung2022improving,song2024solving, li2024decoupled,alkhouri2023diffusion}.  In general, diffusion models learn a data distribution from training samples through a process that imitates the non-equilibrium thermodynamic diffusion process \cite{ho2020denoising,sohl2015deep,song2020score}. Specifically, the training and sampling of diffusion models involve two stages: (i) a forward diffusion process where Gaussian noise is incrementally added to training samples at each time step, and (ii) a backward sampling process where the noise is progressively removed through a neural network that is trained to approximate the score function at all time steps. As described in prior works \cite{hyvarinen2005estimation,song2020score}, the generative capability of diffusion models lies in their ability to learn the {\em score function} of the data distribution, i.e., the gradient of the logarithm of the probability density function (\emph{pdf}). We refer the reader to \cite{chen2024overview,croitoru2023diffusion,yang2023diffusion} for a more comprehensive introduction and survey on diffusion models. 

Despite the significant empirical success and recent advances in understanding sampling convergence \cite{chen2023sampling,lee2022convergence,li2023towards}, distribution approximation \cite{chen2023score,oko2023diffusion}, memorization \cite{gu2023memorization,somepalli2023diffusion,wen2023detecting,zhang2024wasserstein}, and generalization \cite{kadkhodaie2023generalization,yoon2023diffusion,zhang2023emergence} of diffusion models, the fundamental working mechanisms remain poorly understood. One of the key questions is
\begin{center}
    \emph{When and why can diffusion models learn the underlying data distribution without suffering from the curse of dimensionality?}
\end{center}
At first glance, the answer might seem quite straightforward. If the diffusion model can learn the empirical distribution of the training data that closely approximates the underlying data distribution, then the puzzle is solved! However, it has been shown in \cite{li2024good} that the number of samples for an empirical distribution to approximate the underlying data distribution across all time steps could grow exponentially with respect to (\emph{w.r.t.}) the data dimension, leading to the curse of dimensionality.  In contrast, recent empirical results in \cite{kadkhodaie2023generalization,zhang2023emergence} showed that the number of training samples for a diffusion model to learn the underlying distribution is much {\em smaller} than the worst-case scenario, breaking the curse of dimensionality. Therefore, there is a significant gap between theory and practice. 

In this work, we aim to address the above question of learning the underlying distribution via diffusion models by leveraging low-dimensional models. Our key observations are as follows: (i) The intrinsic dimensionality of real image data is significantly lower than the ambient dimension, a fact well-supported by extensive empirical evidence \cite{gong2019intrinsic,pope2020intrinsic,stanczukdiffusion}; (ii) Image data lies on a disjoint union of manifolds of varying intrinsic dimensions, as empirically verified in \cite{brownverifying,kamkari2024geometric}; (iii) We empirically observe that the denoising autoencoder \cite{pretorius2018learning,vincent2011connection,xiang2023denoising} of diffusion models trained on real-world image datasets exhibit low-rank structures (see \Cref{fig:low-rank-real-data}). Based on these observations, we conduct a theoretical investigation of distribution learning through diffusion models by assuming that  (i) the underlying data distribution is a {\em mixture of low-rank Gaussians} (\MoLRG, see \Cref{def:MoG}), and (ii) the denoising autoencoder is parameterized according to the score function of the \MoLRG. These assumptions have been carefully examined and validated by our experiments on real image datasets.

\subsection{Our Contributions}

In this work, our contributions can be summarized as follows: % \qq{adding figures}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item {\bf Equivalence between training diffusion models and subspace clustering.} Under the above setup, we show that the training loss of diffusion models based on the denoising autoencoder is equivalent to the \emph{unsupervised} subspace clustering problem \cite{agarwal2004k,vidal2011subspace,wang2022convergence} (see \Cref{thm:3}). This equivalence implies that training diffusion models is essentially learning low-dimensional manifolds of the data distribution.  

   \item {\bf Understanding phase transition from failure to success in learning distributions.} By leveraging the above equivalence and the data model, we further show that if the number of samples exceed the intrinsic dimension of the subspaces, the optimal solutions of the training loss can recover the underlying distribution. This explains why diffusion models can break the curse of dimensionality. Conversely, if the number of samples is insufficient, it may learn an incorrect distribution. This highlights a phase transition from failure to success in learning the underlying distributions as the sample size increases. 
   
    \item \textbf{Correspondence between semantic representations and the subspaces.} % Moreover, we show that our findings have the potential to enable locally disentangled image editing methods. This can be achieved by manipulating the low-dimensional embedding in key time-steps in a learning free manner.
    Interestingly, we find that the discovered low-dimensional subspaces in pre-trained diffusion models possess {\em semantic} meanings for natural images (see \Cref{fig}). This motivates us to propose a training-free method to edit images on a frozen-trained diffusion model. 
\end{itemize}

We also conduct extensive numerical experiments on both synthetic and real-world data sets to verify our assumptions and validate our theory. More broadly, the theoretical insights we gained in this work provide practical guidance as follows. First, we have shown that the number of samples for learning the underlying distribution via diffusion models scales proportionally with its intrinsic dimension. This insight allows us to improve training efficiency by quantifying the number of required training samples. Second, the identified correspondence between semantic representations and subspaces provides valuable guidance on controlling data generation. By manipulating the semantic representations within these subspaces, we can achieve more precise and targeted data generation.

\paragraph{Learning a mixture of Gaussians via diffusion models.}  Recent works have extensively studied distribution learning and generalizability of diffusion models for learning a mixture of full-rank Gaussian (\MoG) model \cite{chen2024learning,cole2024score,gatmiry2024learning,shah2023learning,wu2024theoretical}. Specifically, they assumed that there exist centers $\bm \mu_1,\dots,\bm \mu_K \in \R^n$ such that image data approximately follows from the following distribution: 
\begin{align}\label{eq:Gau}
 \bm x \sim \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\bm \mu_k, \bm I_n),   
\end{align}
where $\pi_k \ge 0$ is the mixing proportion of the $k$-th mixture component satisfying $\sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k = 1$. \citet{shah2023learning} showed that gradient descent for optimizing denoising diffusion probabilistic objective can efficiently locate the ground-truth centers of \MoG. Later, \citet{chen2024learning} extended the above analysis without assuming the separation condition on the underlying mixture components.  \citet{gatmiry2024learning} employed diffusion models to learn the \MoG~model and provided a time and sample complexity analysis to achieve a targeted total variation error. Recently, \citet{wu2024theoretical} provided a theoretical analysis of diffusion guidance in the \MoG~model. Moreover, \citet{cole2024score} analyzed the approximation and generalization of score-based diffusion models for learning sub-Gaussian distributions, which includes a mixture of Guassians as an example. 

Notably, the \MoLRG~model studied in our work is distinct from the above \MoG~model that is widely studied in the literature. Specifically, the \MoG~model consists of multiple Gaussians with varying means and covariance spanning the full-dimensional space (see \Cref{eq:Gau}), while a \MoLRG~comprises multiple Gaussians with zero mean and low-rank covariance (see \Cref{eq:MoG}), lying in a union of low-dimensional subspaces. As such, the \MoLRG~model, inspired by the inherent low-dimensionality of image datasets \cite{gong2019intrinsic,pope2020intrinsic,stanczukdiffusion}, offers a deeper insight into how diffusion models can learn underlying distributions in practice without suffering from the curse of dimensionality. Along the direction of studying diffusion models for low-dimensional data, \citet{chen2023score} studied a general low-dimensional data distribution and showed that the underlying distribution can be recovered via diffusion models. In contrast, we specifically studied a mixture of low-dimensional Gaussian distributions, enabling us to obtain a sharp bound for learning the underlying distribution. 

\paragraph{Memorization and generalization in diffusion models.} Recently, extensive studies \cite{kadkhodaie2023generalization,yoon2023diffusion,zhang2023emergence} empirically revealed that diffusion models learn the score function across two distinct regimes — memorization (i.e., learning the empirical distribution)  and generalization (i.e., learning the underlying distribution) — depending on the training dataset size vs. the model capacity. Notably, for a model with a fixed number of parameters, there is a phase transition from memorization to generalization as the number of training samples increases \cite{kadkhodaie2023generalization,zhang2023emergence}. In the memorization regime, recent works \cite{gu2023memorization,li2024good} showed that diffusion models learn the empirical optimal denoiser when the network is highly over-parameterized. \citet{yoon2023diffusion} argued that diffusion models tend to generalize when they fail to memorize training data. In the generalization regime, extensive works \cite{cole2024score, li2024generalization, yi2023generalization} studied the generalization performance of diffusion models in different settings. Interestingly, \citet{kadkhodaie2023generalization} observed that two distinct diffusion models can learn nearly identical score functions even if they are trained on sufficiently large separate, non-overlapping portions of the same dataset so that they can generate almost the same output from the same pure noise. Notably, most existing studies on the memorization and generalization of diffusion models are empirical. In contrast, our work provides rigorous theoretical explanations for these intriguing experimental observations based on the \MoLRG~model. We demonstrate that diffusion models learn the underlying data distribution with the number of training samples scaling linearly with the intrinsic dimension of the data distribution. Our theory reveals a phase transition from failure to success in learning the underlying distribution as the number of training samples increases, which sheds light on the phase transition from memorization to generalization.

\paragraph{Subspace clustering.} Subspace clustering is a fundamental problem in unsupervised learning, which aims to identify and group data points that lie in a union of low-dimensional subspaces in a high-dimensional space \cite{agarwal2004k,vidal2011subspace,lerman2018overview}.  Over the past years, a substantial body of literature has explored various approaches to the algorithmic development and theoretical analysis of subspace clustering. These include techniques such as sparse representation \cite{elhamifar2013sparse,wang2013noisy,soltanolkotabi2014robust}, low-rank representation \cite{wang2022convergence,liu2013efficient,liu2017subspace}, and spectral clustering  \cite{pmlr-v139-li21f,vidal2005generalized}. In this work, we present a new interpretation of diffusion models from the perspective of subspace clustering. This is the first time that diffusion models have been analyzed through this lens, offering new insights into how these models can effectively learn complex data distributions by leveraging the intrinsic low-dimensional subspaces within the data.

Given a matrix, we use $\|\bm A\|$ to denote its largest singular value (i.e., spectral norm), $\sigma_{i}(\bm A)$ its $i$-th largest singular value, and $a_{ij}$ its $(i,j)$-th entry, $\mathrm{rank}(\bm A)$ its rank, $||\bm A||_F$ its Frobenius norm. Given a vector $\bm a$, we use $\|\bm a\|$ to denote its Euclidean norm and $a_i$ its $i$-th entry. Let $\mathcal{O}^{n\times d}$ denote the set of all $n\times d$ orthonromal matrices.  We simply write the score function $\nabla_{\bm x} \log p(\bm x)$ of a distribution with probability density function (pdf) $p(\bm x)$ as $\nabla \log p(\bm x)$.

\section{Problem Setup}


In this work, we consider an image dataset consisting of samples $\{\bm x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, where each data point is \emph{i.i.d.} sampled from an underlying data distribution $p_{\rm data}(\bm x)$. Instead of learning this pdf directly, score-based diffusion models aim to learn the score of this distribution from the training samples.  

\subsection{Preliminaries on Score-Based Diffusion Models}\label{subsec:prelim}

\paragraph{Forward and reverse SDEs of diffusion models.} In general, diffusion models consist of forward and reverse processes indexed by a continuous time variable $t \in [0,1]$. Specifically, the forward process progressively injects noise into the data. This process can be described by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):  
\begin{align}\label{eq:forw}
    \mathrm{d}\bm x_t =  f(t) \bm x_t \mathrm{d}t + g(t) \mathrm{d} \bm{w}_t,
\end{align}
where $\bm x_0 \sim p_{\rm data}$, the scalar functions $f(t), g(t): \R \to \R$ respectively denote the drift and diffusion coefficients,\footnote{In general, the functions $f(t)$ and $g(t)$ are chosen such that (i) $\bm x_t$ for all $t$ close to $0$ approximately follows the data distribution $p_{\rm data}$ and (ii) $\bm x_t$ for all $t$ close to $1$ is nearly a standard Gaussian distribution; see, e.g., the settings in \cite{ho2020denoising,karras2022elucidating,song2020score}.} and $\{\bm{w}_t\}_{t \in [0,1]} $ is the standard Wiener process. For ease of exposition, let $p_t(\bm x)$ denote the pdf of $\bm x_t$ and $p_{t}(\bm x_t\vert \bm x_0)$ the transition kernel from $\bm x_0$ to $\bm x_t$. According to \eqref{eq:forw}, we have
\begin{align}\label{eq:trans}
   p_{t}(\bm x_t\vert \bm x_0) = \mathcal{N}(\bm x_t; s_t\bm x_0, s^2_t\sigma^2_t\bm I_n),\ \text{where}\  s_t = \exp\left(\int_0^t f(\xi)\mathrm{d}\xi\right), \sigma_t = \sqrt{\int_{0}^{t}\frac{g^2(\xi)}{s^2({\xi})}\mathrm{d}\xi},
\end{align}
where $s_t:=s(t)$ and $\sigma_t:=\sigma(t)$ for simplicity. The reverse process gradually removes the noise from $\bm x_{1}$ via the following reverse-time SDE:
\begin{align}\label{eq:reve}
    \mathrm{d}\bm{x}_t = \left[  f(t)\bm{x}_t - g^2(t) \nabla \log p_t(\bm{x}_t)\right]\mathrm{d}t +g(t)\mathrm{d}\bar{\bm{w}}_t,
\end{align}
where $\{\bar{\bm{w}_t}\}_{t \in [0,1]}$ is another standard Wiener process, independent of $\{\bm w_t\}$, running backward in time from $t=1$ to $t=0$. It is worth noting that if $\bm x_{1}$ and $\nabla \log p_t$ are provided, the reverse process has exactly the same distribution as the forward process at each time $t \ge 0$ \cite{anderson1982reverse}. 

\paragraph{Training loss of diffusion models.} Unfortunately, the score function $\nabla \log p_t$ is usually unknown, as it depends on the underlying data distribution $p_{\rm data}$. To enable data generation via the reverse SDE \eqref{eq:reve}, a common approach is to estimate the score function $\nabla \log p_t$ using the training samples $\{\bm x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ based on the scoring matching \cite{ho2020denoising,song2020score}. Because of
the equivalence between the score function $\nabla \log p_t(\bm x_t)$ and the posterior mean $\E\left[ \bm x_0 \vert \bm x_t \right]$, i.e., 
\begin{align} \label{eq:Tweedie}
    s_t\E\left[ \bm x_0 \vert \bm x_t \right] = \bm x_t + s_t^2\sigma_t^2 \nabla \log p_t(\bm x_t),
\end{align}
according to Tweedie's formula and \eqref{eq:trans}, an alternative approach to estimate the score function $\nabla \log p_t$ is to estimate the posterior mean $\E\left[ \bm x_0 \vert \bm x_t \right]$. Consequently, extensive works \cite{chen2024deconstructing,kadkhodaie2023generalization,karras2022elucidating,vincent2011connection,xiang2023denoising} have considered training a time-dependent function $\bm x_{\bm \theta}(\cdot, t):\R^{n} \times [0,1] \to \R^n$, known as {\em denoising autoencoder} (DAE), parameterized by a neural network with parameters $\bm \theta$ to estimate the posterior mean $\E\left[ \bm x_0 \vert \bm x_t \right]$. To determine the parameters $\bm \theta$, we can minimize the following empirical loss: 
\begin{align}\label{eq:em loss}
\min_{\bm \theta}\ \ell(\bm \theta) := \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \int_0^1 \lambda_t \E_{\bm \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\bm 0, \bm I_n)} \left[\left\| \bm x_{\bm \theta}(s_t \bm x^{(i)} + \gamma_t\bm \epsilon, t) -  \bm x^{(i)} \right\|^2\right] \mathrm{d}t, 
\end{align}
where $\lambda_t:[0,1] \to \R^+$ is a weighting function and $\gamma_t := s_t\sigma_t$. As shown in \cite{vincent2011connection}, training the DAE is equivalent to performing explicit or implicit score matching under mild conditions. We refer the reader to \Cref{app sub:denosier} for the relationship between this loss and the score-matching loss in \cite{song2020score, vincent2011connection}.     

\subsection{Low-Dimensional Data and Models}\label{subsec:MoG} 

\paragraph{Mixture of low-rank Gaussian data distribution.} Although real-world image datasets are high dimensional in terms of pixel count and overall data volume, extensive empirical works \cite{gong2019intrinsic,kamkari2024geometric,pope2020intrinsic,stanczukdiffusion} suggest that their intrinsic dimensions are much lower. For instance, \citet{pope2020intrinsic} employed a kernelized nearest neighbor method to estimate the intrinsic dimensionality of various datasets, including MNIST \cite{lecun1998gradient}, CIFAR-10 \cite{cifar10}, and ImageNet \cite{russakovsky2015imagenet}. Their findings indicate that even for complex datasets like ImageNet, the intrinsic dimensionality is approximately $40$, which is significantly lower than its ambient dimension.

\end{wrapfigure} 
Recently, \citet{brownverifying,kamkari2024geometric} empirically validated the {\em union of manifolds} hypothesis, demonstrating that high-dimensional image data often lies on a disjoint union of manifolds instead of a single manifold. Notably, the union of subspaces, a special case of this hypothesis, has been widely used in the subspace clustering literature \cite{elhamifar2013sparse,vidal2011subspace,wang2022convergence}. These observations motivate us to model the underlying data distribution as a {\em mixture of low-rank Gaussians}, where the data points are generated from a mixture of several Gaussian distributions with zero mean and low-rank covariance matrices. We formally define the \MoLRG~distribution as follows: 
\begin{definition}[Mixtures of Low-Rank Gaussians] \label{def:MoG}
We say that a random vector $\bm x \in \R^n$ follows a mixture of $K$ low-rank Gaussian distribution with parameters $\{\pi_k\}_{k=1}^K$ and $\{\bm U_k^\star\}_{k=1}^K$ if 
\begin{align}\label{eq:MoG}
    \bm x \sim \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\bm 0, \bm U_k^\star\bm U_k^{\star T}), 
\end{align}
where $\bm U_k^\star \in \mathcal{O}^{n\times d_k}$ denotes the orthonormal basis of the $k$-th component and $\pi_k \ge 0$ is the mixing proportion of the $k$-th mixture component satisfying $\sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k = 1$. 
\end{definition} 
Before we proceed, we make some remarks on this data model. First, to study how diffusion models learn the underlying data distribution, many recent works have studied a mixture of full-rank Gaussian distributions (see Eq. \eqref{eq:Gau}); see, e.g., \cite{chen2024learning,gatmiry2024learning,shah2023learning,wang2023hidden,wu2024theoretical}. However, compared to this model, a \MoLRG~is a more suitable model for capturing the low-dimensionality in image data distribution. Second, \citet{brownverifying,kamkari2024geometric} conducted extensive numerical experiments to validate that image datasets such as MNIST and ImageNet approximately lie on a union of low-dimensional manifolds. Because a nonlinear manifold can be well approximated by its tangent space (i.e., a linear subspace) in a local neighborhood, the \MoLRG~model, which represents data as a union of linear subspace, serves a good local approximation of a union of manifolds. Finally, assuming Gaussian distributions in each subspace in the \MoLRG~model is to enesure theoretical tractability while approximating the real-world image distributions, making it a practical starting point for theoretical studies on real-world image datasets. Now, we compute the ground-truth posterior mean $\E\left[ \bm x_0\vert \bm x_t\right]$ when $\bm x_0$ satisfies the \MoLRG~model as follows. 
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:E[x_0]}
    Suppose that $\bm x_0$ satisfies the \MoLRG~model. For each time $t > 0$, it holds that 
    \begin{align}\label{eq:E MoG}
        \E\left[ \bm x_0\vert \bm x_t\right] = \dfrac{s_t}{s_t^2 + \gamma_t^2}  \frac{\sum_{k=1}^K \exp\left(\phi_t \|\bm U_k^{\star T}\bm x_t\|^2\right) \bm U_k^\star\bm U_k^{\star T} \bm x_t}{\sum_{k=1}^K \exp\left(\phi_t \|\bm U_k^{\star T}\bm x_t\|^2\right)},\ \text{where}\ \phi_t := \frac{s_t^2}{2\gamma_t^2(s_t^2 + \gamma_t^2)}.
    \end{align}
\end{lemma}
We defer the proof of this lemma to \Cref{app sub:score}. Notably, this lemma shows that the ground-truth posterior mean is a convex combination of projections of $\bm x_t$ onto each subspace, where the weights are soft-max functions. Moreover, this lemma provides guidance on the network parameterization of the DAE $\bm x_{\bm \theta}(\cdot,t)$ as discussed below.

\paragraph{Low-rank network parameterization.} In this work, we empirically observed that the DAE $\bm x_{\bm \theta}(\cdot,t)$ trained on real-world image datasets exhibits a low-dimensional structure. Specifically, when we train diffusion models with the U-Net architecture \cite{ronneberger2015u} on various image datasets, it is observed that the numerical rank of the Jacobian of the DAE, i.e., $\nabla_{\bm x_t} \bm x_{\bm \theta}(\bm x_t, t)$, is substantially lower than the ambient dimension in most time steps; see \Cref{fig:low-rank-real-data}(a). Additionally, this pattern of low dimensionality appears to be consistent across different datasets with different noise levels $t$.  

When training diffusion models with U-Net on the samples generated according to the \MoLRG~model, the Jacobian of the DAE also exhibits a similar low-rank pattern, as illustrated in \Cref{fig:low-rank-real-data}(b). For the theoretical study based upon \MoLRG, the above observations motivate us to consider a low-rank parameterization of the network. According to the ground-truth posterior mean of the \MoLRG~ model in \Cref{lem:E[x_0]}, a natural parameterization for the DAE is 
\begin{align}\label{eq:MoG1}
    \bm x_{\bm \theta}(\bm x_t, t) = \dfrac{s_t}{s_t^2 + \gamma_t^2} \sum_{k=1}^K w_k(\bm \theta; \bm x_t) \bm U_k\bm U_k^T\bm x_t,\ \text{where}\  w_k(\bm \theta; \bm x_t) = \frac{ \exp\left(\phi_t \|\bm U_k^T \bm x_t\|^2\right) }{\sum_{l=1}^K \exp\left(\phi_t \|\bm U_l^{T}\bm x_t\|^2\right)}
\end{align}
and the network parameters $\bm \theta = \{\bm U_k\}_{k=1}^K$ satisfy $\bm U_k \in \mathcal{O}^{n\times d_k}$.   Although this approach may seem idealized, it offers several practical insights:
\begin{itemize} 
\item \textbf{Resemblance to the U-Net architecture:} If we consider a single low-rank Gaussian, the network parameterization for
approximating the score function takes the form $\bm x - s_t/(s_t^2+\gamma_t^2) \bm U \bm U^T \bm x$, which resembles the structure of a practical U-Net with a linear encoder, decoder, and skip connections. This provides theoretical insights into why U-Net is preferred for training diffusion models.
\item \textbf{Capturing low-rank property of the DAE:} As shown in \Cref{fig:low-rank-real-data}, the Jacobian of the DAE of diffusion models trained on real image data is low-rank for most time steps, and our parameterization captures this low-rank property.
\item \textbf{Providing practical guidance:} The theoretical insights gained from this work offer practical guidance. First, to learn the underlying distribution, the number of samples should be proportional to its intrinsic dimension. In practice, this informs us on how to use a minimal number of samples to train diffusion models to achieve generalization. Second, in Section 4.2, we empirically showed that
the subspace basis has semantic meanings. Based upon this, our results shed light on how to control the data generation by manipulating the semantic representation within the subspaces.
\end{itemize}

Similar simplifications have been widely used for theoretical analysis in various ideal data distributions; see, e.g., \cite{chen2023score,chen2024learning,gatmiry2024learning,shah2023learning}. Notably, under this specific network parameterization in \eqref{eq:MoG1}, learning the score function $\nabla \log p_t(\bm x_t)$ reduces to learning the network parameters $\bm \theta$ in \eqref{eq:MoG1} according to \Cref{lem:E[x_0]} and Eq. \eqref{eq:Tweedie}. 




\section{Main Results}


Based on the setups in \Cref{subsec:MoG}, we are ready to conduct a theoretical analysis of distribution learning using diffusion models. Specifically, when the underlying data distribution $p_{\rm data}(\bm x)$ is modeled as \MoLRG~and the DAE is approximately parameterized as Eq. \eqref{eq:MoG1}, we show that 
 \begin{tcolorbox}
 \begin{itemize} 
     \item The training loss \eqref{eq:em loss} of diffusion models is \emph{equivalent} to the \textbf{subspace clustering} problem, which can be reduced to the PCA problem in a single low-rank Gaussian case. 
  \item The \MoLRG~distribution can be learned by optimizing the training loss when the minimum number of samples scales linearly with the \textbf{intrinsic dimensionality} of the data.
 \end{itemize}
 \end{tcolorbox}
Note that $\min_{\bm \theta \in \bm \Theta} \ell(\bm \theta)$ is equivalent to $\min_{\bm \theta \in \bm \Theta} f(\bm \theta)$ when we have $f(\bm \theta) = a \ell (\bm \theta) + b$, where $a > 0$ and $b$ are absolute constants that do not depend on $\bm \theta$. This equivalence applies to our results in both \Cref{thm:1} and \Cref{thm:3}

\subsection{A Warm-Up Study: A Single Low-rank Gaussian Case}\label{subsec:low Gau}

To begin, we start from a simple case that the underlying distribution $p_{\rm data}$ is a \emph{single} low-rank Gaussian. Specifically, the training samples $\{\bm x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N \subseteq \R^n$ are generated according to 
\begin{align}\label{eq:Ua+e}
    \bm x^{(i)} = \bm U^\star \bm a_i + \bm e_i, 
\end{align}
where $\bm U^\star \in \mathcal{O}^{n\times d}$ denotes an orthonormal basis, $\bm a_i \overset{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\bm 0, \bm I_d)$ is coefficients for each $i \in [N]$, and $\bm e_i \in \R^n$ is noise for all $i \in [N]$.\footnote{Since real-world images inherently contain noise due to various factors, such as sensor limitation, environment conditions, and transition error, it is reasonable to add a noise term to this model.} According to \eqref{eq:MoG1}, we parameterize the DAE into
\begin{align}\label{eq:para Gau}
    \bm x_{\bm \theta}(\bm x_t, t) = \frac{s_t}{s_t^2 + \gamma_t^2} \bm U\bm U^{T}\bm x_t,
\end{align}
where $\bm \theta = \bm U \in \mathcal{O}^{n\times d}$. Equipped with the above setup, we can show the following result.  

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:1}
Suppose that the DAE $\bm x_{\bm \theta}(\cdot,t)$ in Problem \eqref{eq:em loss} is parameterized into \eqref{eq:para Gau} for each $t \in [0,1]$. Then, Problem \eqref{eq:em loss} is equivalent to the following principal component analysis (PCA) problem:
    \begin{align}\label{eq:PCA}
        \max_{\bm U \in \R^{n\times d}} \sum_{i=1}^N \|\bm U^T \bm x^{(i)}\|^2\qquad \mathrm{s.t.}\quad \bm U^T\bm U = \bm I_d. 
    \end{align}
\end{theorem}

We defer the proof to \Cref{app:pf thm1}. In the single low-rank Gaussian model, \Cref{thm:1} demonstrates that training diffusion models with a DAE of the form \eqref{eq:para Gau} to learn this distribution is equivalent to performing PCA on the training samples. Leveraging this equivalence, we can further characterize the number of samples required for learning underlying distribution under the data model \eqref{eq:Ua+e}.  

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:2}
    Consider the setting of \Cref{thm:1}. Suppose that the training samples $\{\bm x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ are generated according to the noisy single low-rank Gaussian model defined in \eqref{eq:Ua+e}. Let $\hat{\bm U}$ denote an optimal solution of Problem \eqref{eq:em loss}. The following statements hold: 
    \begin{itemize} 
        \item[i)] If $N \ge d$, it holds with probability at least $1 - 1/2^{N-d+1} - \exp\left(-c_2N\right) $ that any optimal solution $\hat{\bm U}$ satisfies
   \begin{align}\label{rst1:thm 2}
       \left\|\hat{\bm U}\hat{\bm U}^T - \bm U^\star\bm U^{\star T}\right\|_F \le \frac{c_1\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N \|\bm e_i\|^2}}{\sqrt{N} - \sqrt{d-1}},
   \end{align}
   where $c_1, c_2 > 0$ are constants that depend polynomially only on the Gaussian moment.
   \item[ii)] If $N < d$, there exists an optimal solution $\hat{\bm U} \in \mathcal{O}^{n\times d}$ such that with probability at least $1 - 1/2^{d-N+1} - \exp\left(-c_2^\prime d\right)$, 
   \begin{align}\label{rst2:thm 2}
       \left\|\hat{\bm U}\hat{\bm U}^T - \bm U^\star\bm U^{\star T}\right\|_F \ge \sqrt{2\min\{d-N, n-d\}} - \frac{c_1^\prime\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N \|\bm e_i\|^2}}{\sqrt{d} - \sqrt{N-1}},
   \end{align}
   where $c_1^\prime, c_2^\prime > 0$ are constants that depend polynomially only on the Gaussian moment. 
    \end{itemize}
\end{theorem} 

\paragraph{Remark 1.} We defer the proof to \Cref{app:pf thm2}. Building on the equivalence in \Cref{thm:1} and the DAE parameterization \eqref{eq:para Gau}, \Cref{thm:2} clearly shows a phase transition from failure to success in learning the underlying distribution as the number of training samples increases. This phase transition is further corroborated by our experiments in Figures \ref{fig:phase-transition-MoG}(a) and \ref{fig:phase-transition-MoG}(b).
 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item When the number of training samples is larger than the dimension of the subspace, i.e., $N \geq d$, any optimal solution $\hat{\bm U}$ recovers the basis of underlying subspace with an approximation error depending on the noise level. This, together with the fact that learning the underlying distributions corresponds to learning the parameters $\bm U$ under the parameterization \eqref{eq:para Gau}, implies that optimizing the training loss of diffusion models can learn the underlying distribution. Conversely, when $N < d$, optimizing the training loss fails to learn the underlying distribution.% \zk{and instead prioritize the subspace spanned by the training samples, which can be interpreted as "memorizing" the data.}
    \item Note that our theory cannot explain why diffusion models memorize training data (i.e., learning the empirical distribution). This is because the parameterization \eqref{eq:para Gau} is not as sufficiently over-parameterized as architectures like U-Net. %\zk{This is because we enforced the model to mimic the generalizable solution}. 
    We plan to explore this over-parameterized setting in future work to better understand how diffusion models achieve memorization and to extend our theoretical insights accordingly. 
\end{itemize}

\subsection{From Single Low-Rank Gaussian to Mixtures of Low-Rank Gaussians}\label{subsec:thm MoG} 

In this subsection, we extend the above study to the \MoLRG~distribution. In particular, we consider a noisy version of the \MoLRG~model as defined \Cref{def:MoG}. Specifically, the training samples are generated by 
\begin{align}\label{eq:MoG noise}
    \bm x^{(i)} = \bm U_k^\star \bm a_i + \bm e_i\;\text{with probability}\;\pi_k, \; \ \forall i \in [N], 
\end{align}
where $\bm U_k^\star \in \mathcal{O}^{n\times d_k}$ denotes an orthonormal basis for each $k \in [K]$, $\bm a_i \overset{i.i.d.}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(\bm 0, \bm I_{d_k})$ is coefficients, and $\bm e_i \in \R^n$ is noise for each $i \in [N]$. As argued by \cite{brownverifying}, image data lies on a {\em disjoint} union of manifolds. This motivates us to assume that the basis matrices of subspaces satisfy $\bm U_k^{\star T} \bm U_l^\star = \bm 0$ for each $k \neq l$. To simplify our analysis, we assume that $d_1=\dots=d_K=d$ and the mixing weights satisfy $\pi_1=\dots=\pi_K=1/K$. Moreover, we consider a hard-max counterpart of Eq. \eqref{eq:MoG1} for the DAE parameterization as follows: 
    \begin{align}\label{eq:para MoG}
     \bm x_{\bm \theta}(\bm x_t, t) = \dfrac{s_t}{s_t^2 + \gamma_t^2} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{w}_k(\bm \theta, \bm x_0) \bm U_k\bm U_k^T\bm x_t,
    \end{align}
where $\bm \theta = \{\bm U_k\}_{k=1}^K$ and the weights $\{ \hat{w}_k(\bm \theta; \bm x_0) \}_{k=1}^K$ are set as
\begin{align}\label{eq:wk1}
\hat{w}_k(\bm \theta; \bm x_0) = \begin{cases}
1,\ & \text{if}\ k=k_0, \\
0,\ & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\end{align}
where $k_0 \in [K]$ is an index satisfying $\|\bm U_{k_0}^T\bm x_0\| \ge  \|\bm U_l^T\bm x_0\|$ for all $l \neq k_0 \in [K]$. We should point out that we use two key approximations here. First, the soft-max weights $\{w_k(\bm \theta,\bm x_t)\}$ in Eq. \eqref{eq:MoG1} are approximated by the hard-max weights $\{ \hat{w}_k(\bm \theta; \bm x_0) \}_{k=1}^K$. Second, $\|\bm U_k^T\bm x_t\|$ is approximated by its expectation, i.e., $\E_{\bm \epsilon}[ \| \bm U_k^T \bm x_t\|^2 ] = \E_{\bm \epsilon}\left[ \|\bm U_k^T(s_t\bm x_0 + \gamma_t \bm \epsilon)\|^2 \right] = s_t^2 \|\bm U_k^T\bm x_0\|^2 + \gamma_t^2 d$. We refer the reader to \Cref{app sec:para MoG} for more details on these approximation. Now, we are ready to show the following theorem.  

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:3}
    Suppose that the DAE $\bm x_{\bm \theta}(\cdot,t)$ in Problem \eqref{eq:em loss} is parameterized into \eqref{eq:para MoG} for each $t \in [0,1]$, where $\hat{w}_k(\bm \theta, \bm x_0)$ is defined in \eqref{eq:wk1} for each $k \in [K]$. Then, Problem \eqref{eq:em loss} is equivalent to the following subspace clustering problem: 
    \begin{align}\label{eq:SC}
        \max_{\bm \theta} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{ i \in C_k(\bm \theta)} \|\bm U_k^T\bm x^{(i)}\|^2\qquad \mathrm{s.t.} \quad  \left[
            \bm U_1, \dots, \bm U_K \right] \in \mathcal{O}^{n\times dK},
    \end{align}
    where $C_k(\bm \theta) := \left\{ i\in [N]: \|\bm U_k^T\bm x^{(i)}\| \ge \|\bm U_l^T\bm x^{(i)}\|,\ \forall l \neq k \right\}$ for each $k \in [K]$.
\end{theorem} 

We defer the proof to \Cref{app:pf thm3}. When the DAE is parameterized into \eqref{eq:para MoG}, \Cref{thm:3} demonstrates that optimizing the training loss of diffusion models is equivalent to solving the subspace clustering problem \cite{agarwal2004k,vidal2011subspace,wang2022convergence}. Problem \eqref{eq:SC} seeks to maximize the sum of the squared norms of each point's projection onto its assigned subspace, subject to the constraint $C_k(\bm \theta)$. Moreover, the equivalence enables us to characterize the required minimum number of samples for learning the underlying \MoLRG~distribution.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:4}
    Consider the setting of \Cref{thm:3}. Suppose that the training samples $\{\bm x^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^N$ are generated by the \MoLRG~distribution in \Cref{def:MoG}. Suppose  $d \gtrsim \log N$ and $\|\bm e_i\| \lesssim \sqrt{d/N}$ for all $i \in [N]$. Let $\{\hat{\bm U}_k\}_{k=1}^K$ denote an optimal solution of Problem \eqref{eq:em loss} and $N_k$ denote the number of samples from the $k$-th Gaussian component. Then, the following statements hold: 
    \begin{itemize} 
    \item[(i)]  If $N_k \ge d$ for each $k \in [K]$, there exists a permutation $\Pi:[K]\to [K]$ such that with probability at least $1 - 2K^2N^{-1} - \sum_{k=1}^K \left(1/2^{N_k-d+1} + \exp\left(-c_2N_k\right)\right)$ for each $k \in [K]$,
    \begin{align}\label{rst1:thm 4}
        \left\|\hat{\bm U}_{\Pi(k)}\hat{\bm U}_{\Pi(k)}^T - \bm U_k^\star\bm U_k^{\star T} \right\|_F \le \frac{c_1 \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N \|\bm e_i\|^2}}{\sqrt{N}_k - \sqrt{d-1}},
    \end{align}
   where $c_1, c_2 > 0$ are constants that depend polynomially only on the Gaussian moment. 
   \item[(ii)] If $N_k < d$ for some $k \in [K]$,  there exists a permutation $\Pi:[K]\to [K]$ and $k \in [K]$ such that with probability at least $1 - 2K^2N^{-1} - \sum_{k=1}^K \left( 1/2^{d-N_k+1} + \exp\left(-c_2^\prime N_k\right) \right)$,
        \begin{align}\label{rst2:thm 4}
        \left\|\hat{\bm U}_{\Pi(k)}\hat{\bm U}_{\Pi(k)}^T - \bm U_k^{\star} \bm U_k^{\star T}\right\|_F \ge \sqrt{2\min\{d-N_k, n-d\}} - \frac{c_1^\prime\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^N \|\bm e_i\|^2}}{\sqrt{d} - \sqrt{N_k-1}},
    \end{align}
   where $c_1^\prime, c_2^\prime > 0$ are constants that depend polynomially only on the Gaussian  
    \end{itemize}
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{Remark 2.} We defer the proof to \Cref{app:pf thm4}. We discuss the implications of our results below.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \emph{Phase transition in learning the underlying distribution.} This theorem demonstrates that when the number of samples in each subspace exceeds the dimension of the subspace and the noise is bounded, the optimal solution of the training loss \eqref{eq:em loss} under the parameterization \eqref{eq:para MoG} can recover the underlying subspaces up to the noise level. Conversely,  when the number of samples is insufficient, there exists an optimal solution that may recover wrong subspaces; see Figures \ref{fig:phase-transition-MoG}(c) and \ref{fig:phase-transition-MoG}(d). % This theorem provides insight into the empirically observed transition from memorization to generalization \emph{w.r.t.} the number of training samples \cite{zhang2023emergence,kadkhodaie2023generalization}. Similar to the single low-rank Gaussian model in \Cref{thm:2}, 
    \item \emph{Connections to the phase transition from memorization to generalization.} We should clarify the difference between the phase transition described in Theorems \ref{thm:2} \& \ref{thm:4} and the phase transition from memorization to generalization. Our phase transition refers to the shift from failure to success of learning the underlying distribution as the number of training samples increase, whereas the latter concerns the shift from memorizing data to generalizing from it as the number of training samples increases. Nevertheless, our theory still sheds light on the minimal number of samples required for diffusion models to enter the generalized regime.  
    \item \emph{Semantic meanings of the subspaces.} More interestingly, we empirically discovered a correspondence between the subspace bases and the semantics of images, as demonstrated by the experiments in \Cref{sec:appli_2}. Specifically, when we move $\bm x_t$ along the direction of a singular vector of the Jacobian of the DAE trained on real image data with step size, the corresponding semantics of the generated images changes proportionally to the step size; see Figures \ref{fig:meta_1} and \ref{fig:meta_more}(a, c). This enables controlled image generation without the need for additional training. 
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Learning the \MoLRG~distribution with U-Net.} 
Next, we optimize the training loss \eqref{eq:em loss} with parameterizing the DAE $\bm x_{\bm \theta}(\cdot,t)$ using U-Net, detailed experiment settings are in \Cref{app:exp_setting_MoLRG_unet}. Since U-Net is highly over-parameterized with about 54 million parameters and is different from the posterior mean in \Cref{lem:E[x_0]}, 
we measure the generalization ability of U-Net via {\em generalization (GL) score}  \cite{zhang2023emergence} defined as follows:
\begin{align}\label{eq:GL score}
    \text{GL score} = \frac{\mathcal D(\bm x^{(i)}_{\text{gen}})}{\mathcal D(\bm x_{\MoLRG}^{(i)})},\quad \mathcal D(\bm x^{(i)}) \coloneqq \sum_{j=1}^{N} \min_{j \neq i} ||\bm x^{(i)} - \bm x^{(j)}||,
\end{align}
where $\{\bm x_{\MoLRG}^{(i)}\}_{i = 1}^{N}$ are samples generated from the \MoLRG~distribution and $\{\bm x^{(i)}_{\text{gen}}\}_{i = 1}^{N}$ are new samples generated by the trained U-Net. Intuitively, $\mathcal D(\bm x_{\text{gen}}^{(i)})$ reflects the uniformity of samples in the space: its value is small when the generated samples cluster around the training data, while the value is large when generated samples disperse in the entire space. Therefore, the trained diffsion model is in memorization regime when $D(\bm x^{(i)}_{\text{gen}}) \ll \mathcal D(\bm x^{(i)}_{\MoLRG})$ and the GL score is close to 0, while it is in generalization regime when $D(\bm x^{(i)}_{\text{gen}}) \approx \mathcal D(\bm x^{(i)}_{\MoLRG})$ and the GL score is close to 1.

In the experiments, we generate the data samples using the \MoLRG~distribution with $K=2$, $n=48$, and $d_k \in \{3,4,5,6\}$. Then, we plot the GL score against the $N_k/d_k$ for each $d_k$ in \Cref{fig:phase-transition-UNet}(a). It is observed that for a fixed $d_k$, the generalization performance of diffusion models improves as the number of training samples increases. Notably, for different values of $d_k$, the plot of the GL score against the $N_k/d_k$ remains approximately consistent. This observation indicates that the phase transition curve for U-Net learning the \MoLRG\ distribution depends on the ratio $N_k/d_k$ rather than on $N_k$ and $d_k$ individually. When $N_k/d_k \approx 60$, GL score $\approx 1.0$ suggesting that U-Net generalizes when $N_k \geq 60 d_k$. This linear relationship for the phase transition differs from $N_k \geq d_k$ in \Cref{thm:4} due to training with U-Net instead of the optimal network parameterization in Eq. \eqref{eq:MoG1}. Nevertheless, \Cref{thm:2} and \Cref{thm:4} still provide valuable insights into learning distributions via diffusion models by demonstrating a similar phase transition phenomenon and confirming a linear relationship between $N_k$ and $d_k$.

\paragraph{Learning real image data distributions with U-Net.} Finally, we train diffusion models using U-Net on real image datasets AFHQ, CelebA, FFHQ, and CIFAR-10. The detailed experiment settings are deferred to \Cref{app:exp_setting_real_unet}. According to \cite{zhang2023emergence}, we define the generalization (GL) score on real-world image dataset as follows:
\begin{align*}
    \text{GL score} :=1 - \mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i\in[N]} \left[\mathcal{M}_{\text{SSCD}}(\bm x, \bm y_i)\right]>0.6\right).
\end{align*}
Here, the SSCD similarity is first introduced in \cite{pizzi2022self} to measure the replication between image pair $(\bm x_1, \bm x_2)$, which is defined as follows: 
\begin{align*}
    \mathcal{M}_{\text{SSCD}}(\bm x_1, \bm x_2) = \dfrac{\text{SSCD}(\bm x_1) \cdot \text{SSCD}(\bm x_2)}{||\text{SSCD}(\bm x_1)||_2 \cdot ||\text{SSCD}(\bm x_2)||_2}
\end{align*}
where $\text{SSCD}(\cdot)$ represents a neural descriptor for copy detection of images. We empirically sample 10K initial noises to estimate the probability. Intuitively, GL score measures the dissimilarity between the generated sample $\bm x$ and all $N$ samples $\bm y_i$ from the training dataset $\{\bm y_i \}_{i=1}^N $. Higher GL score indicates stronger generalizability. For each data set, we train U-Net and plot the GL score against the number of training samples in \Cref{fig:phase-transition-UNet}(b).

The phase transition in the real dataset is illustrated in \Cref{fig:phase-transition-UNet}(b). As observed, the order in which the samples need to generalize follows the relationship: AFHQ $>$ CelebA $>$ FFHQ $\approx$ CIFAR-10. Additionally, from our previous observations in \Cref{fig:low-rank-real-data}, the relationship of the intrinsic dimensions for these datasets is: AFHQ $>$ FFHQ $>$ CelebA $\approx$ CIFAR-10. Both AFHQ and CelebA align well with our theoretical analysis, which indicates that more samples are required for the model to generalize as the intrinsic dimension increases. % For the FFHQ dataset, a potential reason for the misalignment is the number of subspaces $K$, which is smaller for FFHQ than for the other datasets. Under the same total number of samples $N$, the FFHQ dataset has more samples per subspace $N_k$ to generalize. % Notably, the number of subspaces $K$ for the real data distribution is not equivalent to the classes as defined by humans.

In this subsection, we conduct experiments to verify the correspondence between the low-dimensional subspaces of the data distribution and the semantics of images on real datasets. We denote the Jacobian of the DAE $\bm x_{\bm \theta}(\bm x_t, t)$ by $ \bm J_t :=  \nabla_{\bm x_t}  \bm x_{\bm \theta}(\bm x_t, t) \in \R^{n\times n}$ and let $\bm J_t = \bm U \bm \Sigma\bm V^T$ be an singular value decomposition (SVD) of $\bm J_t$, where 
$r=\mathrm{rank}(\bm J_t)$, $\bm U = [\bm u_{1}, \cdots, \bm u_{r} ] \in \mathcal{O}^{n \times r}$, $\bm V = [\bm v_{1}, \cdots, \bm v_{r}] \in \mathcal{O}^{n \times r}$, and $\bm \Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r})$ with $\sigma_{1}\ge\dots\ge \sigma_{r}$ being the singular values. To validate the semantic meaning of the basis vectors $\bm v_{i}$, we vary the value of $\alpha$ from negative to positive and visualize the resulting changes in the generated images. 

In the experiments, we use a pre-trained diffusion denoising probabilistic model (DDPM) \cite{ho2020denoising} on the MetFaces dataset \cite{metafaces}. We randomly select an image $\bm x_0$ from this dataset and use the reverse process of the diffusion denoising implicit model (DDIM) \cite{song2020denoising} to generate $\bm x_t$ at $t=0.7T$, where $T$ denote the total number of time steps. We respectively choose the changed direction as the leading right singular vectors $\bm v_{1}, \bm v_{3}, \bm v_4, \bm v_5, \bm v_6$ and use $\tilde{\bm x}_t = \bm x_t + \alpha \bm v_i$ to generate new images with $\alpha \in [-4, 4]$ shown in \Cref{fig:meta_1}. It is observed that these singular vectors enable different semantic edits in terms of gender, hairstyle, and color of the image. For comparison, we generate a random unit vector $\bm s$ and move $\bm x_t$ along the direction of $\bm s$, where the editing strength $\alpha$ is the same as the semantic edits column-wise. The results are shown in the last column of \Cref{fig:meta_1}. Moving along random directions provides minimal semantic changes in the generated images, indicating that the low-dimensional subspace spanned by $\bm V$ is non-trivial and corresponds to semantic meaningful image attributes. More experimental results can be found in \Cref{fig:meta_more} in \Cref{app:exp_setting_real_unet}.



\section{Conclusion \& Discussion}


In this work, we studied the training loss of diffusion models to investigate when and why diffusion models can learn the underlying distribution without suffering from the curse of dimensionality. Motivated by extensive empirical observations, we assumed that the underlying data distribution is a mixture of low-rank Gaussians.  Specifically, we showed that minimizing the training loss is equivalent to solving the subspace clustering problem under proper network parameterization. Based on this equivalence, we further showed that the optimal solutions to the training loss can recover the underlying subspaces when the number of samples scales linearly with the intrinsic dimensionality of the data distribution. Moreover, we established the correspondence between the subspaces and semantic representations of image data. Since our studied network parameterization is not sufficiently over-parameterized, a future direction is to extend our analysis to an over-parameterized case to fully explain the transition from memorization to generalization.
