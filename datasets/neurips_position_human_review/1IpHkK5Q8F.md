# Real-Time Hyper-Personalized Generative AI Should Be Regulated to Prevent the Rise of "Digital Heroin"

- Decision: Accept (Oral)
- Scores: 5, 8, 7

## Abstract
This position paper argues that real-time generative AI has the potential to become the next wave of addictive digital media, creating a new class of digital content akin to ``digital heroin'' with severe implications for mental health and youth development. By shortening the content-generation feedback loop to mere seconds, these advanced models will soon be able to hyper-personalize outputs on the fly. When paired with misaligned incentives (e.g., maximizing user engagement), this will fuel unprecedented compulsive consumption patterns with far-reaching consequences for mental health, cognitive development, and social stability. Drawing on interdisciplinary research, from clinical observations of social media addiction to neuroscientific studies of dopamine-driven feedback, we illustrate how real-time tailored content generation may erode user autonomy, foment emotional distress, and disproportionately endanger vulnerable groups, such as adolescents. Due to the rapid advancement of generative AI and its potential to induce severe addiction-like effects, we call for strong government oversight akin to existing controls on addictive substances, particularly for minors. We further urge the machine learning community to act proactively by establishing robust design guidelines, collaborating with public health experts, and supporting targeted policy measures to ensure responsible and ethical deployment, rather than paving the way for another wave of unregulated digital dependence.

### Summary
The paper presents a position stating that the there is a need to regulate real-time hyper-personalization of digital content across social media and tech platforms (e.g., Tiktok FYP, Instagram Reels) to avoid what might potentially become a “digital heroin”. The authors claim that the unregulated hyper-personalization enabled by extremely fast feedback loop of todays generative AI models may be harmful and cause abused consumption patterns affecting mental health and cognitive development of its users. The authors cite several past lessons in the public health crises including the abuse of tobacco and opioids which led to regulation of these “rewards” and support that this should be the same for hyper-personalized digital content before it’s too late. The authors also cite societal and ethical implications of “infinite-scroll” platforms including cause of content addiction being the most obvious effect. The authors end with recommendations on curbing hyperpersonalization of digital content anchored on existing AI regulations such as the EU AI Act as well as some guidelines and call-to-action for researchers/product developers/engineers working on digital content.

### Strengths
The paper is readable and informative for the general audience. While the target for actionable next steps and policies might be government agencies, regulators, and policy makers, any reader from any field or domain can easily understand and relate to the message and urgency of the position paper.

I like the concept of digital heroin and how it its framed in the paper being a consequence of unregulated hyperpersonalization. It gives an edge or a memorable aspect to readers. However, other readers might see this as a bold comparison by directly comparing digital content addiction to the actual drug additiction but I think the paper sufficiently justifies the parallelism between the two. 

At the high-level, the proposed Designated Addictive System (DAS) actually makes sense to label models that can potentially cause screen or use addiction as it complements what policies are already existing such as the EU AI Act.

### Weaknesses
The position of the paper is much more targeted to people behind regulating government agencies for action rather than the technical audience of NeurIPS. After all, it is government agencies who have the power to regulate and impose rules on platforms. Researchers working on feedback loop efficiency for these well-known platforms might generally agree with the position and the overall wellbeing of their users but internal company goals drive them to maximize user engagement unless government agencies step in. Hence, I believe a more appropriate venue for this position would be those that are directly in dialogue with the government. 

It might be worth discussing the recent initiatives of these real-time content platforms aimed towards curbing excessive screen times. For example, TikTok periodically reminds users age 13-17 via notification about their screen time, allows family control over the app, and has a dedicated STEM category to encourage users to watch informative short-form education content. Now, whether these initiatives are sufficient or not with respect to recent health research is unknown and the authors’ discussion on these in line with the position might actually strengthen the overall paper.

### Questions
As mentioned, these platforms such as TikTok and Instagram already have functionalities to limit screen time, allow parental controls, and periodically remind users between ages 13-17 about their screen time (see https://support.tiktok.com/en/account-and-privacy/account-information/screen-time). In the authors' standpoint, are these not enough in the long run? What I'm seeing is that we just need to reinforce better educational initiatives for minors and parents when installing these platforms and activating accounts.

### Summary
This paper presents the risks associated with generative AI-driven real-time content, arguing that such technology could represent the next evolution of digital addiction. They draw a parallel to “digital heroin” and warn of severe mental health consequences particularly for vulnerable groups, like adolescents. They examine how generative AI’s immersive and highly personalized nature can make it even harder for people to disengage. The paper also discusses broader ethical and social implications and calls for public health safeguards. Finally, the authors advocate for proactive public health interventions, offering recommendations to mitigate these risks and protect especially at-risk groups.

### Strengths
The paper effectively raises awareness about the potential dangers of generative AI-driven real-time content, clearly outlining its main argument and supporting it with convincing reasoning. The writing is consistently clear, engaging, and well-supported by credible references. The paper doesn’t just present claims; it also follows through with concrete recommendations for public health safeguards and policy action. The topic is also highly relevant for the NeurIPS community, as it sits at the intersection of AI, ethics, and public health.

### Weaknesses
An alternative perspective worth considering is the possibility of long-term societal transformation and the dynamic nature of technology’s impact over time. Societies have a demonstrated capacity to adapt to disruptive technologies over time in ways that are not always predictable in advance. As public opinion shifts, our social norms evolve, and regulations catch up, the very "addictive" potential of gen AI that the paper warns about might actually diminish.

### Questions
- Among your proposed interventions, which do you consider most practical to deploy immediately, taking into account industry cooperation, and potential for widespread adoption?
- What concrete indicators would demonstrate that your recommended interventions are working effectively? How would you track both the reduction of problematic usage patterns and the promotion of healthier engagement with Gen AI real-time content?
