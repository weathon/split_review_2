# Position: If Innovation in AI systematically Violates Fundamental Rights, Is It Innovation at All?

- Decision: Accept (Oral)
- Scores: 8, 6, 7

## Abstract
Artificial intelligence (AI) now permeates critical infrastructures and decisionmaking systems where failures produce social, economic, and democratic harm. This position paper challenges the entrenched belief that regulation and innovation are opposites. As evidenced by analogies from aviation, pharmaceuticals, and welfare systems and recent cases of synthetic misinformation, bias and unaccountable decision-making, the absence of well-designed regulation has already created immeasurable damage. Regulation, when thoughtful and adaptive, is not a brake on innovation—it is its foundation. The present position paper examines the EU AI Act as a model of risk-based, responsibility-driven regulation that addresses the Collingridge Dilemma: acting early enough to prevent harm, yet flexibly enough to sustain innovation. Its adaptive mechanisms—regulatory sandboxes, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) support, real-world testing, fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA)—demonstrate how regulation can accelerate responsibly, rather than delay, technological progress. The position paper summarises how governance tools transform perceived burdens into tangible advantages: legal certainty, consumer trust, and ethical competitiveness. Ultimately, the paper reframes progress: innovation and regulation advance together. By embedding transparency, impact assessments, accountability, and AI literacy into design and deployment, the EU framework defines what responsible innovation truly means—technological ambition disciplined by democratic values and fundamental rights.

### Summary
This paper argues that the oft-repeated sentiment that regulation stifles innovation in AI is not true. It supports this central thesis with many lines of evidence, both theoretical and practical, including the Collingridge Dilemma, and case studies of the aviation industry, drug approvals, and welfare system design. The paper does an interesting and convincing deep dive into the EU AI Act to explain the strengths of it, and offers general visions for how well-crafted and strong regulations can foster more robust and trustworthy AI systems that enhance innovation. The authors also attempt to argue that innovation needs to be defined differently and consider social impacts, rather than just novelty and capability advancement.

### Strengths
(1) The paper is written very clearly and with diverse and strong support from logic and citation.

(2) The authors convincingly dissect a regulation vs innovation claim that is frequently stated in AI communities, with rebuttal using historical examples and case studies, theoretical logic, and detailed walkthroughs of how EU AI Act sandboxes and policies would drive more responsible and long-term innovation rather than risky short-term innovation that could collapse or lose public trust. The paper is rich and will foster productive discussion.

### Weaknesses
(1) The discussion around a new concept of Responsible Innovation versus traditional definitions or vernacular uses of innovation generally can get a little strange. Could it be clearer to say AI companies and regulators need to purse Responsible Innovation rather than try to change the definition of innovation generally?

(2) There was a piece arguing that the EU AI Act restricts academic research and that the average university lab may not have the capacity to handle all the regulatory hurdles: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.03218 . This article only discusses the corporate AI side. The piece could benefit from some discussions of regulation and academic research, especially given the venue.

(3) The alternative views section is too dismissive and doesn't cite enough existing criticisms of the EU AI Act. For example: Gikay, A. A. (2024). Risks, innovation, and adaptability in the UK’s incrementalism versus the European Union’s comprehensive artificial intelligence regulation. International Journal of Law and Information Technology. This work argues that risk categorization is quite messy for example, AI that doesn't fall into the current risk list is unregulated, and AI could be improperly categorized without easy appeal systems.

### Questions
(1) Some say that a major flaw of the EU AI Act is that it allows researchers and corporations to declare that their AI work is relevant to national security, and then it immediately falls out of the scope of regulation. A relevant section is "This Regulation does not apply to areas outside the scope of Union law, and shall not, in any event, affect the competences of the Member States concerning national security, regardless of the type of entity entrusted by the Member States with carrying out tasks in relation to those competences. "

In the US, the perception is that most major AI companies are increasingly integrating into defense pipelines. Doesn't this sort of major loophole around national security either drive companies to attempt to claim that their products are relevant to national security and thus escape regulation, and/or drive more companies to military applications where they can pursue products and research with less safeguards and public oversight?

(2) Is there any existing example of AI causing a lot of damage due to being poorly regulated? Most of the arguments are about hypotheticals.

### Summary
This paper argues for the importance of regulation for enabling innovation in AI, in particular using the mechanisms and intended role of the EU AI act as an example of such regulation.

### Strengths
This paper takes a clear and salient position on AI regulation and makes several arguments for that position, using the EU AI act as an example of what the paper calls for. The topic is relevant to the NeurIPS community and the position is not vacuous nor reflects existing consensus in the community.

### Weaknesses
My chief concern on this paper is that the stated position, that regulation can help rather than hinder AI progress, seems to be somewhat secondary to arguing in support of the EU AI act specifically. While this proposed regulation is a good example to frame the paper, the arguments seem to mostly focus on the specific measures of the act (and why these are helpful rather than harmful to AI progress), rather than the more general point. Broader arguments are presented (for example, in section 4.4 the benefits of transparency requirements for building public trust and AI system adoption are argued in a general sense), but most of the paper seems to be arguing in favor of specific aspects of the EU AI act, which is a valid thing to argue for but somewhat misaligned with the stated position of the paper. For example, are there broader principles to AI regulation that non-EU policymakers should be encouraged towards, or should the goal simply be to implement equivalent regulation to the EU AI act worldwide? A more general argument (framed by EU AI act examples) could have a greater impact and spur a more informative discussion among non-EU audiences in particular.

### Questions
Are there detailed examples of early regulation successes in other fields could be helpful here? Some are mentioned briefly in section 2, but having specific arguments for the positive benefits of regulation (and specific negative impacts from a lack of regulation) and tying those to detailed anecdotes from other fields would strengthen the argument.

For some points, such as the lack of burden on SME's from the EU AI act due to sandboxing, templates, direct communication, etc, it would be helpful to dig into specific details (if possible) on how the regulatory compliance process would actually look to support the argument. As a counterargument, what if these general mechanisms are poorly implemented? For example, a sandbox could be defined in a very narrow way, or the requirements for regulator oversight within the sandbox could be too onerous for many SME's. Some specifics (acknowledging that as proposed legislation there are details that cannot be fully known until enacted) would help to reassure that properly calibrated regulation is specifically plausible and not just possible in abstract.

### Summary
This position paper argues that the oft-repeated notion of regulation and innovation being in opposition is misguided. Rather, regulation provides legal certainty and accountability that can help guide responsible innovation in AI. It draws on analogies in other sectors in support of this argument.

### Strengths
Overall this is a good, very well-written, and timely paper on a topic that will be of interest to many.
The authors structure their arguments well, backing up claims with concrete examples from the AI Act.
The authors cleanly bring together multiple topics and concepts (Collingridge dilemma, responsible innovation, transparency, ...), clearly showing how they inter-relate in support of the presented argument.

### Weaknesses
My biggest criticism is that the piece reads more like 'The AIA has implemented X to try to increase innovation' which doesn't directly address the main argument of 'The AIA is an example of regulation supporting innovation'. I understand that the AIA is still new, and so empirical data on how it may support innovation is lacking.

Relatedly, Section 2.1 also doesn’t argue that regulation fosters innovation; rather that regulation increases safety. I would like to explicitly see cases where innovation was enabled by regulation. E.g. new aviation safety technologies developed due to regulation.

The paper could be strengthened by providing citations or quotations of people arguing that regulation stifles innovation – at the moment we kind of just have to take your word that this is the dominant narrative.

Minor:
-Right apostrophes are commonly mis-typset
-The title doesn't speak to the position advocated for. I expected something very different from this paper before reading the abstract. Overall, this framing of ‘only responsible innovation is innovation’ feels only tangential to the main argument of the paper.
-Alternate positions are only very briefly mentioned, not expanded upon, and don’t add a massive amount to the paper.

### Questions
To what extent does the fact that parts of the AIA being delegated to member states (e.g. mandatory sandboxes) work against the aim of providing regulatory clarity and certainty to developers?
