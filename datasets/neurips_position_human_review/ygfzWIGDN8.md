# Position: Model Collapse Does Not Mean What You Think

- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 5, 5, 10

## Abstract
The proliferation of AI-generated content online has fueled concerns over _model collapse_, a degradation in future generative models' performance when trained on synthetic data generated by earlier models. Industry leaders, premier research journals and popular science publications alike have prophesied catastrophic societal consequences stemming from model collapse. In this position piece, we contend this widespread narrative fundamentally misunderstands the scientific evidence. We highlight that research on model collapse actually encompasses eight distinct and at times conflicting definitions of model collapse, and argue that inconsistent terminology within and between papers has hindered building a comprehensive understanding of model collapse. To assess how significantly different interpretations of model collapse threaten future generative models, we posit what we believe are realistic conditions for studying model collapse and then conduct a rigorous assessment of the literature's methodologies through this lens. While we leave room for reasonable disagreement, our analysis of research studies, weighted by how faithfully each study matches real-world conditions, leads us to conclude that certain predicted claims of model collapse rely on assumptions and conditions that poorly match real-world conditions, and in fact several prominent collapse scenarios are readily avoidable. Altogether, this position paper argues that model collapse has been warped from a nuanced multifaceted consideration into an oversimplified threat, and that the evidence suggests specific harms more likely under society's current trajectory have received disproportionately less attention.

### Summary
This position paper challenges the common narrative around "model collapse" in AI, arguing that the phenomenon is often misunderstood and exaggerated. The authors identify eight conflicting definitions of model collapse in the literature, ranging from catastrophic performance degradation to loss of tail data diversity, and demonstrate how inconsistent terminology has led to misleading conclusions. They critique unrealistic experimental setups and instead propose more realistic conditions under which catastrophic collapse is unlikely.

### Strengths
- Effectively discuss exaggerated claims about model collapse.
-  Identifies eight distinct collapse definitions, helping future research avoid confusion.
- Asking for explicit definitions, realistic experimental conditions, and focus on diversity collapse provides concrete guidance for future research.

### Weaknesses
The paper does not directly examine whether increasing neural network size helps mitigate model collapse. This is an important gap in the analysis, as model scale could theoretically influence collapse dynamics in several ways. Larger models with greater capacity might better preserve rare patterns (reducing tail collapse risks) or maintain more stable scaling behavior when trained on synthetic data. Since the AI industry's standard practice involves progressively scaling up models, understanding this relationship would strengthen the paper's arguments about real-world collapse prevention. Without addressing model size explicitly, the analysis remains incomplete regarding one of modern AI's most fundamental trends.

### Summary
The paper argues that the public and some research narratives about “model collapse” — the idea that training on AI-generated data will cause future generative models to fail — are oversimplified and often wrong. The authors show that “model collapse” has at least eight different definitions in the literature, which are sometimes mixed up or applied inconsistently. 

The main takeaway are

1. The label “model collapse” is being used for many different phenomena — some harmless, some serious, some unrealistic — and this confusion leads to misleading claims in both research and public discussion.
2. Most catastrophic “collapse” scenarios in the literature rely on unrealistic assumptions (e.g., only synthetic data is used, real data is discarded each generation), so they are unlikely to happen in practice for large AI labs.
3. Real risks do exist, but they are different from the “doom” narrative — the most credible one being diversity collapse (loss of rare or tail data over generations), which could affect model quality in subtle ways.

### Strengths
By clarifying what “model collapse” means and showing how it’s used inconsistently, the paper reduces confusion in both research and public debate. A clearer shared understanding helps:

+ Researchers avoid talking past each other and design experiments that are comparable and relevant.
+ Practitioners and policymakers focus on realistic threats instead of reacting to unrealistic “doom” scenarios.
+ The community direct effort and resources toward genuine risks like diversity loss, where solutions are possible and meaningful.

In short, the paper improves communication, grounds the discussion in real-world practices, and helps the field prioritize work on problems that actually matter.

### Weaknesses
1. The paper’s main contribution is clarifying the inconsistent use of “model collapse” and proposing more precise definitions. While this may improve communication, it is less clear how this directly advances technical understanding, leads to new methods, or produces actionable solutions.
2. Much of the discussion on terminology feels abstract and self-contained; the practical impact on improving model training, evaluation, or mitigation strategies is not fully developed.
3. The proposed “realistic conditions” for assessing collapse are sensible but limited to current large-lab practices, which may change over time or may not apply to smaller labs and niche domains.
4. The paper could do more to translate its definitional work into concrete guidance for researchers and policymakers beyond “use clear definitions.”

### Questions
I fully agree that communication clarity is nice, but the paper risks being purely semantic and having little practical effect.

1. Can you provide a specific example where using your proposed definitions would lead to a different experimental design or a different interpretation of results compared to the current literature?
2. Do you see any immediate technical implications of your analysis — for example, changes to data curation, evaluation benchmarks, or monitoring procedures in large-scale model training?

### Summary
This position paper argues that model collapse may be less problematic than current narratives suggest. The authors support this position by creating a taxonomy of existing definitions of model collapse in the literature, highlighting inconsistencies (within and across papers), and questioning the realism of assumptions made about the training workflow of next-generation models, including data paradigms (e.g., replacement, accumulation) and model initialization. Their analysis reveals that most catastrophic outcomes presented in current narratives could be alleviated through existing data curation and model training best practices.

### Strengths
The paper is well written and addresses a topic of high relevance to ongoing discussions about the risks associated with synthetic content. Each argument is carefully supported by evidence, and the authors enrich their analysis with clearly presented examples that illustrate the limitations identified in the existing literature.

### Weaknesses
This is a minor comment: although the literature is recent and therefore limited, it would be useful to address potential doubts about missing references. Clarifying the query used to collect the 28 papers, including the inclusion and exclusion criteria, would strengthen the presentation.
