# AI needs Biospheric Ethics

- Decision: Reject
- Scores: 6, 5, 4

## Abstract
The dominant paradigm in AI ethics and value alignment is highly anthropocentric. The focus of these disciplines is strictly on human values which limits the depth and breadth of their insights. Recently, attempts to expand to a sentientist perspective have been initiated. We argue that neither of these outlooks is sufficient to capture the actual complexity of the biosphere and ensure that AI does not damage it. Thus, we propose a new paradigm --- Biospheric AI that assumes an ecocentric perspective. We discuss hypothetical ways in which such an AI might be designed. Moreover, we give directions for research and application of the modern AI models that would be consistent with the biospheric interests. All in all, this work attempts to take first steps towards a comprehensive program of research that focuses on the interactions between AI and the biosphere.

### Summary
The paper addresses the topic of AI ethics applied in the biosphere. The authors explain the different ethical perspectives on the value of different beings, such as anthroponcentrism and sentientism. It is evident from the text that most of the ethical concerns of AI focus only on the human side, leaving aside others, such as animals and plants. The authors succeed in showing how important it is to consider others. The paper emphasises that current technologies can be used to communicate with those who do not have a voice to create AI-based solutions that do not harm humans but also animals, plants and the environment.

### Strengths
The paper is well-structured, organized and easy to follow. The authors take care of the details by explaining the main terms to understand the importance of the topic.

The authors include proper references to support their claim as well as examples to show the work done by the scientific community which shows a new research direction to dive into.

The article also presents an overview of the current situation regarding the use of AI-based solutions and how this impacts the biosphere. 

Finally, the paper invites the reader to reflect on how this aspect has not been considered and the possible implications of leaving it aside.

### Weaknesses
I think the article could have included the next steps in this regard. It is clear that AI ethics should not be focused on the human side but consider other actors in the biosphere but I wonder what will be the approach to follow.

In the section about Green AI, the authors could have explained more about the costs involved. For instance, the use of water resources when training models. It is also mentioned that somehow this issue is addressed in the field of Green AI, but more information would be beneficial to see what has been done.

### Summary
The paper argues for a Biospheric AI paradigm, advocating that AI ethics and value alignment should expand beyond anthropocentric (human-centered) and sentientist (sentient being-centered) views to include the entire biosphere. The authors define the biosphere as all life on Earth, fundamentally intertwined and co-dependent. They argue that harming the biosphere ultimately harms humans, and therefore, from a consequentialist perspective, AI should not harm the biosphere to protect humanity. From a deontological perspective, they argue that if the biosphere has moral rights, it's inherently wrong for AI to harm it.
The paper proposes that a Biospheric AI should be designed to follow biospheric ethics. The authors discuss a minimal biospheric ethics, which states that AI should not harm the biosphere, but also propose a more comprehensive, positive goal of long-term biospheric flourishing. They suggest research directions that align with this paradigm, such as using AI to decode animal communication and understand ecosystems, and using AI as a spokesperson for nature in legal contexts. The paper also touches on the importance of "Green AI" to minimize the environmental impact of AI itself.

### Strengths
The paper discusses the case for a Biospheric AI from many angles, which is a positive approach.

### Weaknesses
I think the paper starts by arguing against an anthropocentric view only to justify that harming the biosphere would ultimately harm humans, which is a very anthropocentric argument.

The paper seems to try to achieve too much. It brings up the point about the difference between applying the proposed paradigm to the actions of the AI or to the actions of human users of AI. However, it seems impossible for an independent AI at this stage to understand the biosphere well enough to take actions to preserve it. Indeed, humans have not found a way to reconcile their own interests without significantly altering or harming the biosphere.

### Questions
Perhaps the authors could consider how would a Biospheric AI, operating with the goal of not harming the biosphere, differentiate between natural, cyclical "harm" (e.g., predation) and destructive, large-scale harm (e.g., deforestation)? 
What criteria would be used to draw this distinction in a way that doesn't simply re-import human-centric values about what is "good" or "bad" for nature?

### Summary
This manuscript invites AI ethics to step outside the anthropocentric bubble and breathe the rarer air of “Biospheric Ethics,” arguing that the welfare of the entire biosphere—not just humans or sentient animals—should guide AI’s design. The authors walk us through consequentialist and deontological justifications, sketch speculative applications (AI as an interpreter for whales, a legal advocate for forests, a mushroom conversationalist), and urge a research agenda that fuses AI with environmental stewardship. The piece is unapologetically visionary, and it wears its philosophical heart on its sleeve.

### Strengths
- Original framing: The biosphere-first ethical lens is refreshing in a conference ecosystem saturated with human-centered alignment debates.

- Interdisciplinary sweep: The work weaves together AI ethics, environmental philosophy, indigenous worldviews, and animal communication studies, opening new conceptual doorways.

- Accessible philosophical arc: The reasoning is laid out in an orderly progression, guiding the reader from moral premises to proposed research threads.

### Weaknesses
- All forest, no trees: The argument is big-picture and atmospheric, but lacks concrete, testable instantiations. Readers looking for even a small-scale technical example or prototype will come away empty-handed.

- Optimism untethered from feasibility: While the idea of LLMs mediating plant–fungus dialogues is poetic, there is no engagement with the crushing realities of data scarcity, sensor limitations, or ecological complexity.

- Conflict avoidance: The paper acknowledges tensions between biospheric and human priorities but sidesteps the thorny problem of resolving them—precisely the kind of challenge an ethics framework must confront.

- Relevance gap for NeurIPS: Without a bridge from these philosophical positions to concrete AI methodologies, the audience may find it hard to integrate this vision into their own research.

- Risk of green idealism: Without implementation details, the proposal risks being co-opted for symbolic “greenwashing” rather than substantive change.

### Questions
- Plant at least one detailed case study showing how biospheric ethics could be encoded in an existing AI workflow, including concrete data, algorithms, and decision constraints.

- Map a staged research plan, from near-term “saplings” (e.g., datasets or benchmarks for biosphere health) to long-term “old growth” (full biosphere-aligned agents).

- Engage with failure modes: how might biospheric AI be misused, misaligned, or outcompeted by purely anthropocentric systems?
