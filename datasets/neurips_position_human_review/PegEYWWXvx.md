# Foundation Models for Scientific Discovery: From Paradigm Enhancement to Paradigm Transition

- Decision: Accept
- Scores: 4, 7, 7

## Abstract
Foundation models (FMs), such as GPT-4 and AlphaFold, are reshaping the landscape of scientific research. Beyond accelerating tasks such as hypothesis generation, experimental design, and result interpretation, they prompt a more fundamental question: Are FMs merely enhancing existing scientific methodologies, or are they redefining the way science is conducted? In this paper, we argue that FMs are catalyzing a transition toward a new scientific paradigm. We introduce a three-stage framework to describe this evolution: (1) Meta-Scientific Integration, where FMs enhance workflows within traditional paradigms; (2) Hybrid Human-AI Co-Creation, where FMs become active collaborators in problem formulation, reasoning, and discovery; and (3) Autonomous Scientific Discovery, where FMs operate as independent agents capable of generating new scientific knowledge with minimal human intervention. Through this lens, we review current applications and emerging capabilities of FMs across existing scientific paradigms. We further identify risks and future directions for FM-enabled scientific discovery. This position paper aims to support the scientific community in understanding the transformative role of FMs and to foster reflection on the future of scientific discovery.

### Summary
This paper introduces a three-stage framework to describe this evolution: (1) meta-science integration, where FMs enhance workflows in traditional paradigms; (2) human-machine hybrid co-creation, where FMs become active collaborators in problem formulation, reasoning, and discovery; and (3) autonomous scientific discovery, where FMs operate as independent agents capable of generating new scientific knowledge with minimal human intervention. However, the authors do not offer new perspectives compared to the existing literature.

### Strengths
The authors describe in detail, both in the text and in the figures, the evolving scientific paradigms empowered by FMs. FMs are progressively transitioning from tool-like infrastructure (meta-scientific integration), to interactive co-creators (hybrid human–AI collaboration), and ultimately to autonomous agents capable of end-to-end scientific discovery.

### Weaknesses
The author's position paper offers no new perspectives compared to several existing perspective papers: "Transforming science labs into automated factories of discovery. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/scirobotics.adm6991", "Empowering biomedical discovery with AI agents. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2024.09.022", "Scaling Laws in Scientific Discovery with AI and Robot Scientists. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scib.2024.09.029", "A Comprehensive Survey of Scientific Large Language Models and Their Applications in Scientific Discovery. DOI: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10833".
The author needs to present the position from a more novel and distinct perspective or viewpoint to make it significantly different from the above articles.

### Questions

### Summary
This paper argues that foundation models (FMs) are beginning to change not only the pace but also the way scientific discovery occurs. While general-purpose FMs have achieved impressive results, scientific fields such as biology, chemistry, physics, and medicine have unique requirements for model design, adaptation, and monitoring. The authors describe a three-tiered framework for this shift: (i) _meta-scientific integration_, where FMs are used to improve established research workflows; (ii) _hybrid human-AI co-creation_, where they work alongside scientists to formulate questions, reason and design experiments; and (iii) _autonomous scientific discovery_, where they develop and test ideas with minimal human guidance. From this perspective, the paper examines the current uses of FMs in science, identifies where general-purpose models fail, and outlines principles for building domain-specific FMs. The position is that maintaining confidence and effectiveness in FM-driven science requires a shift towards specialized, carefully managed models that reflect the needs and constraints of each scientific domain.

### Strengths
The article takes the bold and timely position that FMs reshape the scientific process, not just accelerate it. The three-stage framework structures the argument and leads from the theoretical possibilities to the practical limitations. TThe discussion covers methodological, technical, and epistemic dimensions, making it relevant for both machine learning (ML) and the broader scientific community. The position is well grounded in the literature on ML, computational science, natural language processing, and responsible AI, and it provides a forward-looking perspective that is highly relevant to NeurIPS’s focus on impactful and interdisciplinary research.

### Weaknesses
The paper offers little empirical evidence or detailed case studies showing where general FMs fail in science and domain-specific ones succeed. The discussion of counterarguments is brief. Some repetition in the motivation section limits the scope for a deeper exploration of implementation challenges, management trade-offs and sustainable funding models for developing domain-specific FMs.

### Questions
I recommend citing specific scientific cases where general FMs failed/underperformed and domain-specific approaches proved essential? I would also include a short paragraph mentioning the leading research groups in this emerging and promising area of research.

### Summary
This paper presents a clear position that the scientific paradigm is currently undergoing a shift, fuelled by the increasing sophistication and widespread adoption of foundation models. The authors present a clear stepwise trajectory for how they propose that this evolution may unfold (from FMs to enhance existing paradigms, to co-creators, to independent research agents), along with a nuanced discussion of the promise and risk of such an evolution.

### Strengths
(S1) This paper does an excellent job presenting a history of the evolution of scientific paradigms, and a very well supported position that a new paradigm is rapidly emerging. It's a timely submission, and I genuinely enjoyed reading it. 

(S2) The paper is clearly written, easy to follow, and figures support the narrative flow. 

(S3) To the best of my knowledge of the literature, the arguments are well supported by the provided references. The proposal for a three-stage framework is novel relative to what has been proposed before.

### Weaknesses
(W1) Overall I think the paper presents a compelling summary of the transition that is currently underway, but misses the opportunity to engage with the question of whether or not that transition is one that ought to be embraced, or constrained, and if so why and how. While this paper does a good job presenting the position that the scientific framework is changing, there is limited discussion of what the significance of this shift is, and what it means for the ML community. 

(W2) The last sentence of the paper presents a very interesting, discussion worthy point ("Embracing this shift may redefine not just how we do science, but who or what can do science."), and I think presenting this point sooner, and critically engaging with the significance, would make for a much stronger position. 

(W3) The limitations of FMs in domains that are far from what current agents can do is under-explored. Fields that depend heavily on experimental methods and data collection, for example, will experience a very different shift than those that are already fully data-driven.

### Questions
(Q1) What can (or should) the ML community do about this shift? 
(Q2) What does the shift towards FMs mean for evaluating science? Paired with the question who or what can do science is the question of who or what can judge/evaluate/review science. If FMs review FM-generated science, how do we prevent recursive validation loops where errors compound?
(Q3) The paper presents three discrete stages, but it seems like some researchers are already doing 'Hybrid Human-AI Co-Creation' while some still use FMs merely as search tools? How can these stages coexist, and what empirical markers would indicate a progression through them?
