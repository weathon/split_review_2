## Summary

This paper introduces "prompt diffusion," a method that applies a diffusion model within the prompt space to generate per-sample customized prompts for vision-language models. The approach first overfits prompts to each training sample via gradient descent (using labels), then trains a diffusion transformer to generate these overfitted prompts from noise conditioned on image features. At test time, customized prompts are generated without labels using fast ODE-based sampling (5 steps, +0.045s). The method is modality-agnostic and plugs into existing prompt learning methods (textual, visual, multi-modal). Experiments span 15 datasets, 5 baselines, and 3 generalization settings (base-to-new, cross-dataset, domain generalization), showing consistent improvements.

## Strengths

- **Consistent improvement across diverse settings and baselines**: Prompt diffusion yields higher accuracy than each of the five baselines (VPT, CoCoOp, MaPLe, PromptSRC, CoPrompt) across all three settings. On base-to-new generalization, the harmonic mean improves by 2.19% for CoCoOp (from 75.83 to 78.02) and similarly for others. On cross-dataset and domain generalization, improvements are consistent (e.g., +2.47% for CoPrompt cross-dataset, +0.65-1.34% domain generalization average). Section 5.2 reports specific gains with precise percentages anchored to Tables 1, 2, and the domain generalization table.

- **Modality-agnostic plug-and-play design**: The method integrates with textual (CoCoOp), visual (VPT), and multi-modal (MaPLe, PromptSRC, CoPrompt) prompt learning without architectural changes to those baselines (Section 4.2, Figure 2, stated explicitly in Section 4 and demonstrated in experiments). This generality distinguishes it from prior prompt learning methods tied to a single modality.

- **Fast ODE-based sampling with practical efficiency**: The diffusion model leverages the AMED-Solver to generate prompts in only 5 sampling steps, adding only 0.045 seconds to inference time while providing substantial accuracy gains (Figure 3, Section 5.3). This is concretely quantified and demonstrated.

- **Thorough ablation isolating the diffusion mechanism**: Table 3 compares diffusion against MLP, Transformer, GAN, VAE, and normalizing flows — all using the same overfitted prompt targets (V*) and image features. Diffusion substantially outperforms all alternatives (78.02 H vs. 76.32 for normalizing flows, 76.17 for VAE), demonstrating that the gains are specific to the diffusion process rather than merely coming from the overfitted targets. MLP/Transformer with V* targets do not even match the CoCoOp baseline (75.41, 75.88 vs. 75.83 H).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Information asymmetry in the main comparisons, only partially addressed by ablation**: The main experiments (Tables 1, 2, domain generalization) compare "prompt diffusion + baseline" vs. the original baseline. The diffusion model's training relies on per-sample overfitted prompts V* as targets — obtained by gradient descent using ground-truth labels — while the baselines never receive this label-derived supervision. Table 3 partially addresses this by showing that non-diffusion predictors (MLP, Transformer, GAN, VAE, flows) trained on the same V* targets do not achieve comparable gains, which is evidence that the diffusion process is the driver. However, this controlled experiment is only run for CoCoOp and reported as a single average across 11 datasets, not for all 5 baselines and not broken down per-dataset. A stronger case would demonstrate the same controlled comparison (V* targets + non-diffusion predictor) across the full experimental suite. As it stands, the overall comparisons in the main tables conflate the effects of (a) the V* target generation and (b) the diffusion mechanism, and the ablation is not exhaustive enough to fully disentangle them.

- **Omission of Bayesian prompt learning from experimental comparison**: The related work (Section 2) discusses Bayesian Prompt Learning (Derakhshani et al., 2023) as a prior probabilistic approach to prompt learning "demonstrat[ing] its ability to generalize unseen classes." This is highly relevant — both methods aim to model a distribution over prompts for improved generalization — yet no experimental comparison is provided. While the paper already includes 5 diverse baselines, the explicit discussion of this method in related work creates an expectation of comparison that is left unfulfilled.

### Minor

- **Training cost not reported**: The paper reports inference time (0.045s added) but does not report the total training time or wall-clock cost of the full pipeline, including the per-sample overfitting step. For a dataset like ImageNet (1.2M images), even 5 gradient steps per sample adds significant computation. Practitioners cannot gauge the practical trade-off without this information.

- **Standard deviations / error bars missing**: Results are reported as averages over 3 seeds, but no variance is reported. Given the modest gains (~1–3%), some readers may wonder whether the improvements are statistically significant, especially on individual datasets where gains may be small.

- **Notation in Section 4.2 is confusing**: The paper uses $\bm{V}_t^*$, $\tilde{\bm{V}}_t$, and $\tilde{\bm{V}}_t^*$ in close proximity without clearly distinguishing them. The relationship "derive the overfitted prompts $\bm{V}_t^*$" followed by "the noised prompts, denoted as $\tilde{\bm{V}}_t$" and then "yields the interim diffused prompts $\tilde{\bm{V}}_t$" is hard to follow. A reader cannot cleanly reconstruct the training loop from the main paper alone. Although the computational graph is deferred to the (extracted) appendix, the main paper should be self-contained.

- **Per-sample overfitting iteration count not explicitly stated**: The caption says "a minimal number of iterations I" and Figure 3 (right) shows accuracy peaking at 5 iterations, but the paper never explicitly states that I=5 is used in the main experiments. This should be stated plainly.

## Novel Insights

The interplay of the two reviewer perspectives reveals something interesting about evaluation methodology in generative prompting research. The harsh critic's central concern — that the diffusion model's training targets encode label information unavailable to the baselines — is essentially a concern about knowledge distillation leakage. The strength finder correctly identifies that the paper's own ablation (Table 3) anticipates and partially addresses this concern by showing that other predictors (MLP, Transformer, GAN, VAE, flows) using the same targets do not achieve comparable gains. This suggests that the field may need a standard protocol for evaluating "generative" components in prompt learning pipelines that separate the benefit of the training signal (the targets) from the benefit of the generation mechanism. The paper is closer to meeting that standard than the harsh critic suggests, but falls short of fully satisfying it. The deeper insight is that as prompt learning moves toward generative/ probabilistic formulations, evaluation methodology needs to catch up — and this paper usefully highlights that gap even as it partially addresses it.
