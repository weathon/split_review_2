## Summary

This paper proposes MCNC (Manifold-Constrained Neural Compression), a reparameterization method that constrains model weights to a low-dimensional nonlinear manifold wrapped around a hypersphere. The key idea is to replace the random *linear* subspace used in prior work (PRANC) with a random *nonlinear* manifold generated by a frozen feedforward network with sinusoidal activations. The authors evaluate MCNC on image classification (training ViT and ResNet from scratch at extreme compression rates) and LLM fine-tuning (showing throughput advantages over NOLA), demonstrating strong accuracy at high compression and practical deployment speedups.

---

## Strengths

1. **Significant accuracy gains over reparameterization baselines at extreme compression.** On ImageNet-100 with ResNet-18 at 1% model size, MCNC+LoRA achieves roughly 6–10 percentage points higher accuracy than PRANC and NOLA (Table 2). On CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-20/56, MCNC consistently outperforms both pruning methods and prior reparameterization methods at comparable compression rates (Table 3). These results directly support the claim that constraining to a nonlinear manifold finds better solutions than random linear subspaces.

2. **Substantial throughput advantage in LLM fine-tuning.** For LLaMA-2 7B and 13B fine-tuned on Alpaca, MCNC achieves 2.0× (7B) and 2.2× (13B) higher throughput than NOLA while maintaining comparable MMLU accuracy, requiring 46% fewer GFLOPs for on-the-fly parameter generation (Table 4). This is a concrete operational benefit over the closest prior work.

3. **CPU-to-GPU transfer time halved.** For a ViT-S model compressed 100×, MCNC reduces average transfer + expansion time from 80ms to 40ms (Table 9). This demonstrates a practical deployment advantage beyond the compression ratio alone.

4. **Ablation cleanly confirms the nonlinear manifold is the driver of improvement.** On MNIST at 0.2% compression, MCNC with sine activation reaches 87.7% accuracy versus 83.3% with no activation (which reduces to a PRANC-like linear mapping) — a 4.4-point gap under controlled conditions (Table 5). This provides direct evidence that the nonlinearity, not just the reparameterization framework, is responsible for the gains.

5. **Random generator matches trained generator in practice.** On CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-20 and ResNet-56, using a random sine-activated generator yields accuracy within 0.2–1.5% of a trained generator (Table 10). This justifies the practical choice of a seed-only generator, keeping the method simple to deploy.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Missing LoRA baseline in LLM fine-tuning experiments (Table 4).** The LLM fine-tuning comparison is limited to NOLA alone. LoRA — the most widely adopted PEFT method and a direct point of comparison — is not included. Since both MCNC and NOLA are applied on top of QLoRA's 4-bit quantized base model, a standard LoRA baseline (without further reparameterization compression) would answer the natural question: does the additional compression from MCNC/NOLA incur an accuracy cost relative to the standard approach, or is it cost-free? Without this baseline, the comparable accuracy between MCNC and NOLA is hard to interpret — both may degrade accuracy relative to LoRA, or neither may. This gap undermines the claim that MCNC is an effective compression method for PEFT.

2. **No variance or statistical significance reporting for main experimental results.** The core accuracy claims across Tables 1–4 are presented as single point estimates with no error bars, standard deviations, or significance tests. For example, the headline claim of +7% accuracy at 1% compression on ImageNet-100 (Table 2) — and the sometimes-small gaps between methods in Table 4 — could fall within run-to-run noise. The MNIST ablations do report results averaged across three trials, but the main vision and LLM results do not. This makes it impossible to assess the reliability of the reported gaps.

### Minor

1. **PRANC comparison in Tables 2–3 uses numbers from the original paper rather than re-running with matched hyperparameters.** For the CIFAR experiments (Table 3), the paper states it "directly compare[s] with the results reported in (Nooralinejad et al., 2023)." For the ImageNet-100 experiment (Table 2), the source of PRANC numbers is unspecified. Since PRANC results were obtained under different training setups, hyperparameter budgets, and data augmentation configurations, the relative advantage of MCNC over PRANC cannot be cleanly attributed to the method improvement rather than confounded by experimental differences. The MNIST ablation (Table 5) partially addresses this by comparing sine vs. no activation in a controlled setting, but a full controlled comparison on a non-trivial benchmark would substantially strengthen the core thesis.

2. **Generator architecture and hyperparameters (k, d) for main vision experiments are not specified in the main text.** For the LLM experiments (line 130) and MNIST ablations (line 147), the generator architecture, k, and d are given. But for the ImageNet-100 vision experiments (Tables 1–2) and CIFAR experiments (Table 3), these values are not stated in the main text — the paper defers to "section A.3" for hyperparameters. While the appendix likely contains these details (and appendix content is stripped by the parser), the main text should state k and d for each main experiment to make the paper self-contained for a reader.

3. **Wasserstein-distance coverage formalism is used only for a toy illustration and plays no role in the actual method.** The paper formalizes the generator objective via Wasserstein distance and maximum coverage of the hypersphere (Section 3.1), but then uses a random (untrained) sine network for all main experiments. An ablation (Table 10) confirms that training the generator only marginally improves accuracy. This undercuts the formal motivation: the coverage optimization is never actually needed. The paper would be stronger and more honest if it streamlined the presentation to acknowledge that a random sine MLP happens to work well, rather than framing it as solving a formal coverage-maximization problem.

4. **The pruning comparison methodology uses a non-standard adjustment for index storage.** The paper accounts for pruning indices by "assum[ing] half-precision for the indices and prun[ing] to sparsity rates 50% higher than the desired compression" (line 113). While the intent (accounting for index storage overhead) is reasonable, the specific 50% adjustment and half-precision assumption are ad hoc and not standard practice. Using a well-established storage metric (e.g., total bits including indices in a standard sparse format like CSR) would place the comparison on firmer ground. That said, the pruning comparisons are not the paper's central contribution, and MCNC's primary competition is with PRANC/NOLA rather than pruning.

## Novel Insights

The combination of two distinct findings from the reviews — (a) the nonlinear manifold consistently outperforms linear subspaces at extreme compression (up to 1% of original size on ImageNet-100), and (b) the generator itself needs no training beyond random initialization — together suggest an interesting phenomenon: the random nonlinear embedding creates a sufficiently rich coverage of the hypersphere that it consistently intersects good solution manifolds, and the added expressiveness over a linear subspace is the driver of improvement. This is not a trivial finding and has practical implications for simplifying deployment (seed-only storage). The main experimental gap that neither reviewer fully resolves is whether this advantage persists when compared fairly against a well-tuned PRANC at larger scales (e.g., ImageNet-1K from scratch or full LLM pre-training), which the paper explicitly identifies as a limitation.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a standard LoRA baseline to the LLM fine-tuning experiments (Table 4).** Without it, the accuracy comparison between MCNC and NOLA is unanchored. If both are within 1% of standard LoRA, the throughput advantage carries weight; if both are significantly worse, the value proposition changes.

2. **Report variance (at least 3 seeds) for the main accuracy results** in Tables 1–3. The MNIST ablations already do this — extending the practice to the main experiments would greatly improve the credibility of the reported advantages.

3. **For the PRANC comparison in Tables 2–3, either re-run PRANC under matched conditions or explicitly acknowledge the confound** and explain why the comparison is still meaningful.

4. **State k and d for each main experiment in the main text** (not just in the appendix). Concretely: for the ViT-Ti/ViT-S experiments (Table 1) and ResNet-18 ImageNet-100 experiments (Table 2), what are the generator input dimension (k), chunk size (d), and generator hidden dimensions?

5. **Consider streamlining the Wasserstein coverage formalism** to match what the method actually uses — a paragraph explaining that random sine networks empirically provide good coverage (with Figure 2 as supporting evidence) and that training the generator is unnecessary, rather than framing it as an explicit optimization problem that is ultimately unused.
