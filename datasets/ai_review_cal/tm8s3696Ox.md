## Summary

This paper proposes Co-Boosting, a one-shot federated learning framework that iteratively improves both the quality of synthetic distillation data and the weighted ensemble of client models in a mutually reinforcing adversarial manner. The key idea is a three-step loop per epoch: (1) generate "hard" synthetic samples via a generator trained with gradient harmonization and adversarial diversity perturbations, (2) reweight the client logit ensemble by optimizing weights on these hard samples via sign-gradient updates, and (3) distill the refined ensemble into the server model. Experiments on five datasets with varying non-IID levels, heterogeneous architectures, and unbalanced data show consistent improvements over baselines like DENSE, often by large margins.

## Strengths

1. **Large and consistent accuracy gains across diverse settings.** Table 1 shows Co-Boosting exceeds DENSE (the best data-free baseline) on all 15 dataset-α combinations, with margins up to 12.87% (MNIST, α=0.05). The improvements hold across MNIST, FMNIST, SVHN, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 at α ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.3}, and are replicated in heterogeneous-model, unbalanced-data, and varying-client-count settings (Tables 3, 5, 6, and the unbalanced data experiment). This breadth directly supports the core claim that co-boosting data and ensemble improves the server model.

2. **Ablation confirms the synergy of all three components.** Table 4 shows that no single component (GHS, DHS, or EE) alone achieves the best result; the full combination of all three reaches 65.40% (SVHN) and 47.20% (CIFAR-10), while the best individual component reaches only 62.67% and 43.75% respectively. This validates the paper's core premise that simultaneously improving data and ensemble matters more than improving either alone.

3. **Practical compatibility with model-market constraints is demonstrated.** The method requires no client-side training modifications, no extra transmissions beyond the single round, and supports heterogeneous client architectures. Table 3 shows Co-Boosting outperforms all baselines under five different client architectures (ResNet, CNN, MobileNet, ShuffleNet, etc.), directly supporting the claimed practical advantage for real-world deployment scenarios where only pre-trained models are available.

4. **Direct evidence that the ensemble weights improve on synthetic data.** Table 2 (ensemble accuracy) shows Co-Boosting's ensemble outperforms FedENS (simple average) by 2–10 points across SVHN and CIFAR-10 at various α levels. Figure 1(b) further shows that weights learned on hard synthetic data approach the quality of weights learned on real data, providing direct evidence that the ensemble improvement transfers to real test data rather than overfitting to generator artifacts.

### Fatal

### Major

### Minor

1. **Overclaimed "consistently outperforms" statement.** The paper states "Co-Boosting consistently outperforms all other baselines in all settings" (Section 4.2). However, in Table 1 at α=0.3 for MNIST, FedDF (97.66%) exceeds Co-Boosting (97.25%), and for FMNIST α=0.3, Co-Boosting (83.11%) is essentially tied with FedDF (83.14%). While the paper notes that FedDF uses a real validation set (an impractical advantage), the absolute claim is inaccurate as written. The authors should qualify it, e.g., "consistently outperforms all other data-free baselines" or acknowledge the FedDF exception explicitly.

2. **Weight update rule (Eq. 13) has unclear theoretical motivation and incomplete specification.** The weights are updated using sign-gradient steps with normalization that bounds each wₖ to [0,1] but does not constrain them to sum to 1. Since the ensemble is defined as Σ wₖ fₖ(x) (a weighted sum of logits), allowing arbitrary scale means the ensemble's logit magnitudes can drift across epochs, potentially interfering with the KL-divergence distillation loss. The paper provides no sensitivity analysis for the step size μ, no comparison with standard gradient descent, and no discussion of whether sum-to-1 normalization would be preferable. While the empirical results suggest the method works, this design choice needs justification and better validation.

3. **No stability or convergence analysis of the three-player adversarial loop.** The system involves a generator, a server model, and a weight optimizer that all change simultaneously across epochs. The paper does not show server accuracy vs. epoch, generator loss trajectories, or any analysis of whether the process converges, oscillates, or degrades. A plot of server test accuracy across epochs for at least one setting (e.g., CIFAR-10 α=0.1) would substantially strengthen the claim that the co-boosting is progressive and stable, not reliant on careful early stopping.

4. **Missing ablation on the perturbation strategy for sample diversity.** The diversity perturbation (Eq. 8–9) uses a one-step update with a random vector u rather than a standard adversarial direction. There is no ablation comparing this random-vector approach to a standard PGD-style direction (e.g., maximizing KL divergence). Such an ablation would clarify whether the diversity gain stems from the randomness or simply from the perturbation magnitude, and could potentially simplify the method.

5. **The paper does not specify whether the generator is re-initialized each epoch or continues training across epochs.** In Algorithm 1, the generator is updated for T_G iterations each epoch but its state carries forward. Because the generator's objective is non-stationary (the ensemble and server change each epoch), clarifying whether and how the generator adapts to the shifting target is important for reproducibility.

6. **No sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters μ (weight update step size) and ε (perturbation strength).** These control the ensemble weight optimization and data diversity respectively, yet no experiments vary them. Given the ad-hoc nature of the sign-based weight update (point 2), a sensitivity study for μ is particularly needed to demonstrate robustness.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.

- **"The reweighting on synthetic data risks overfitting to generator artifacts rather than improving true teacher quality"** — partially addressed by the paper's own evidence: Table 2 directly shows that the Co-Boosting ensemble (learned on synthetic hard samples) achieves substantially higher accuracy on real test data than the simple average ensemble. Figure 1(b) further shows that weights learned on hard synthetic data approach the quality of weights learned on real data. The concern is reasonable as a caution but the paper already provides counter-evidence, and the critic's own overall assessment acknowledges this.
- **"Comparison excludes relevant recent baselines using diffusion models"** — scope creep. The paper explicitly targets settings where no auxiliary data or models are available (data-free). Methods using pre-trained diffusion models operate under a different, more permissive paradigm.
- **"Missing related works" / "pure formatting/style nitpicks"** — excluded per hard rules.

## Suggestions

1. **Qualify the "consistently outperforms" claim.** Replace it with "outperforms all data-free baselines" or explicitly note that the comparison with FedDF (which uses real validation data) is asymmetrical.

2. **Add a convergence plot.** Show server test accuracy and ensemble accuracy vs. epoch for at least one primary setting (e.g., CIFAR-10 α=0.1) to demonstrate that the co-boosting process is stable and progressively improving.

3. **Address the weight normalization concern.** Clarify whether the ensemble weights are intended to sum to 1 (convex combination) or not, and discuss the implications for logit scale in the KL-divergence distillation loss. Compare sign-based updates with standard gradient descent in an ablation.

4. **Add sensitivity analysis for μ and ε.** At minimum, show results for a few values of μ (weight step size) and ε (perturbation strength) on one dataset to demonstrate that performance is not overly sensitive to these hyperparameters.

5. **Add an ablation comparing the random-vector perturbation (Eq. 8–9) with a standard adversarial direction** (e.g., maximizing KL divergence) to clarify whether randomness or perturbation magnitude drives the diversity gain.
