## Summary

This paper addresses the challenge of density modelling for protein backbone structures in internal coordinates (dihedrals and bond angles). The core idea is to *induce* a full covariance structure over internal coordinate fluctuations by imposing constraints on atom displacements in 3D Euclidean space, rather than estimating the covariance directly from data. Using a maximum-entropy derivation with Lagrange multipliers (one per backbone atom), combined with a first-order Taylor expansion relating internal and Cartesian displacements, the authors obtain a Gaussian posterior whose precision matrix has a structured form: a diagonal prior plus a weighted sum of Gram matrices \( \boldsymbol{G}_m \). The Lagrange multipliers \( \lambda_m \) are predicted by a U-Net conditioned on a latent representation from a VAE. The method is demonstrated on both unimodal (small-fluctuation, low-data) protein ensembles, where it outperforms diagonal-covariance baselines, and multimodal (large-conformational-change, high-data) ensembles, where a flow-based baseline achieves better results but the VAE still captures meaningful structure.

## Strengths

1. **Principled reduction from \(O(M^2)\) to \(O(M)\) predicted parameters via induced covariance structure.**  
   Equations 9 and the surrounding derivation show that the precision matrix \( \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}_{\kappa, \text{constr}} = 2\sum_m \lambda_m \boldsymbol{G}_m \) requires only \(M\) Lagrange multipliers (one per atom) rather than the \((2M-5)^2\) entries of a full precision matrix. This makes full-backbone internal-coordinate density modelling computationally feasible.

2. **Demonstrated advantage in the low-data, unimodal regime.**  
   The paper evaluates on three proteins (1unc: 25 conformers; 1fsd: 41 conformers; 1pga: 400 conformers) and shows via atom-position variance plots and Ramachandran distributions that the induced-covariance VAE substantially outperforms baselines that use diagonal or fixed covariances, especially in loop regions. This directly supports the paper's claim that the method is valuable where data is scarce and covariance estimation from data is unreliable.

3. **Maximum-entropy derivation with a clear mathematical path from constraints to Gaussian form.**  
   The derivation in Section 3.2–3.4 (Eqs. 4–9) is clean and well-explained: starting from a diagonal prior, imposing expected squared-displacement constraints via Lagrange multipliers, applying a first-order expansion of \( \Delta x_m^2 \) as a quadratic form \( d\kappa^\top \boldsymbol{G}_m d\kappa \), and arriving at a Gaussian posterior with structured precision. This connects the work to classical protein MCMC techniques (Favrin 2001, Bottaro 2012) while extending them to a neural-network-modulated, learnable setting.

4. **Honest assessment of limitations and clear scope.**  
   The Discussion openly states that fluctuations are "generally too small to fully cover the individual modes," that the prior variance is set as a hyperparameter, and that the VAE is a proof-of-concept rather than a SOTA model in the multimodal setting. The paper does not overclaim.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Empirical validation of the first-order approximation is absent.**  
   The entire method rests on the small-angle (first-order Taylor) approximation of atom displacements in Eq. 7–8: \( \Delta\boldsymbol{x}_m \approx \sum_i (\partial\boldsymbol{x}_m/\partial\kappa_i)\Delta\kappa_i \). This linearisation is accurate only for small fluctuations. The paper acknowledges this indirectly via the auxiliary loss that keeps \( \lambda \) in a bounded range, but it provides **no direct empirical check** of how well this approximation holds for the systems studied. For a method whose core mathematical claim depends on this step, the reader needs to see, for at least one protein, a comparison between the true squared displacement \( \Delta x_m^2 \) (computed from sampled structures and the mean) and the quadratic approximation \( \sum_{ij} \Delta\kappa_i G^{ij}_m \Delta\kappa_j \). Without this, it is unclear whether the induced covariance actually enforces the intended constraints or whether errors from the approximation are absorbed by the learned \( \lambda \) parameters. This does not invalidate the paper, but it is a significant gap in empirical rigor.

### Minor

2. **The "constraint enforcement" framing is slightly misleading.**  
   The paper repeatedly uses language like "imposing constraints on atom fluctuations" and "satisfying the constraints" (e.g., abstract, Section 3 title, Section 3.2, Section 3.4). However, the Lagrange multipliers \( \lambda_m \) are not chosen to satisfy externally specified constraint values \( C_m \); they are predicted by a neural network and trained via the ELBO. The resulting distribution does not guarantee any particular \( C_m \)—it is a flexible parameterisation that *induces* covariance, not a constraint-satisfaction mechanism. The derivation via maximum entropy is still valid as a **motivation** for the parametric form, but the narrative would be more accurate if it consistently used "induce" or "modulate" rather than "impose constraints." This is a framing issue rather than a technical flaw, but it affects how readers understand the contribution.

3. **Largest demonstrated system is modest (168 atoms = 56 residues).**  
   The paper claims the method "scales to full protein backbones," but the largest system is 1pga with 168 backbone atoms (56 residues). The multimodal datasets are even smaller (30 and 105 atoms). For a method whose selling point is scaling internal-coordinate models, the paper would benefit from a demonstration on a protein of at least several hundred residues, or a clear discussion of the computational bottlenecks (e.g., the \( O(N_\kappa^3) \) cost of inverting the precision matrix) and how they might be addressed.

4. **No information about how the partial derivatives \( \partial\boldsymbol{x}_m/\partial\kappa_i \) are computed.**  
   The paper states that the derivatives are evaluated from the mean structure (Section 3.5), but does not explain whether they are computed analytically (e.g., via the pNeRF algorithm or automatic differentiation) or approximated. This is important for reproducibility.

5. **Hyperparameter sensitivity and computational cost are not reported.**  
   The model has at least two critical hyperparameters: \( a \) (κ-prior strength) and \( w_\text{aux} \) (auxiliary loss weight). No sensitivity analysis is provided. Additionally, no training time, inference time, or memory cost is reported for any system, making it difficult to assess practical scalability.

6. **Design choice for U-Net on distance matrices is not justified.**  
   The U-Net takes the pairwise distance matrix of the mean structure as input. The paper does not explain why distance matrices are chosen (e.g., rotational invariance, encoding of global geometry) or why a U-Net architecture is appropriate for this input modality.

### Trivial
- The derivation from Eq. 9 to Eq. 10 (\( C_m = \operatorname{tr}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}} \boldsymbol{G}_m) \)) is referenced to a matrix cookbook but could be shown in one line for completeness.
- The footnote on the functional derivative could be more explicit about the \( \ln(y/q) + 1 \) term.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **First-order validation** (from Major weakness 1 above): Provide the empirical check suggested. This is the most impactful addition.
- **Expressivity characterisation**: A synthetic experiment fitting a known covariance with the induced form would clarify what structures can/cannot be represented.
- **Scaling to larger systems**: Demonstrate or at least discuss how the method could handle 200+ residue proteins, e.g., using Cholesky updates or conjugate gradients to avoid full matrix inversion.
- **Comparison to a VAE with a full covariance on the smallest system** (e.g., cln025 with 30 atoms) to quantify expressivity loss relative to the induced parameterisation.
- **Rotational invariance proof**: A brief formal note showing that \( \boldsymbol{G}_m \) is invariant under global rotations of the mean structure (since it uses the dot product of derivative vectors).

## Removed Points

These points from the original reviews are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution:

- **"Parameterization expressivity is too restrictive" (Harsh Critic Issue 2):** The paper claims to induce *a* covariance structure (non-diagonal), not to represent *any arbitrary* covariance matrix. The reduction from \((2M-5)^2\) to \(M\) parameters is correctly presented as a practical advantage of the structured form, not as a claim about universal expressivity. This criticism misreads the paper's contribution.
- **"Rotational invariance claim is not adequately justified" (Harsh Critic Issue 4):** The paper's claim is correct. Since \( \boldsymbol{G}_m^{ij} = (\partial\boldsymbol{x}_m/\partial\kappa_i) \cdot (\partial\boldsymbol{x}_m/\partial\kappa_j) \), a global rotation \(R\) transforms each derivative vector by \(R\), and the dot product \( (R\boldsymbol{v}_i)\cdot(R\boldsymbol{v}_j) = \boldsymbol{v}_i\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_j \) is invariant. The pNeRF reference frame is deterministic given the internal coordinates, so the model's outputs are independent of any arbitrary global orientation.

## Suggestions

1. **Add an empirical validation of the first-order approximation** on at least one system (e.g., 1pga). Compute the true \( \Delta x_m^2 \) for samples from the VAE and compare against the quadratic approximation \( d\kappa^\top \boldsymbol{G}_m d\kappa \), reporting the relative error per atom. This directly addresses the most significant gap in the current evaluation.

2. **Reframe the narrative** from "imposing constraints" to "inducing covariance via constraints as a modelling principle." The derivation is sound either way, but the latter more accurately describes what the model actually does (learns \(\lambda\) parameters, not satisfies pre-specified \(C_m\) values).

3. **Report computational cost** (training time, inference time, memory usage) for the largest system, and discuss how the \( O(N_\kappa^3) \) inversion cost scales with protein size.

4. **Explain the derivative computation** (how \( \partial\boldsymbol{x}_m/\partial\kappa_i \) is obtained) for reproducibility—whether via pNeRF's analytical Jacobian, automatic differentiation, or finite differences.

5. **Include a brief justification** for the U-Net + distance matrix design: distance matrices are rotationally invariant and encode global geometry, making them a natural input for predicting per-atom fluctuation magnitudes.
