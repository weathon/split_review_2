## Summary

This paper tackles long video understanding in LMMs from a context-window perspective. The authors identify that visual and language embeddings form distinct clusters in the decoder latent space (Figure 1b), motivating them to redefine "context window" into separate visual and language windows. Their core technical contribution is adapting YaRN positional embedding interpolation using a scaling factor based on the *visual* training length (rather than the language training length), plus a progressive pooling strategy that selectively downsamples non-first frames within event groups. Empirically, the tuning-free variant improves LLaVA-OneVision-7B on VideoMME from 56.2→61.0 (256 frames) and achieves 69.1 on MLVU, outperforming GPT-4o. The progressive pooling reduces GPU memory by ~45% with no performance loss.

## Strengths

- **Substantial empirical gains without training on long video-text data.** The tuning-free variant of the visual-context-window extension improves VideoMME overall from 56.2 (baseline direct extrapolation at 256 frames) to 61.0, a gain of 4.8 points. The comparison against standard YaRN (58.5 → 61.0) in Table 4 specifically isolates the benefit of using the visual training length in the scaling factor. This is the paper's strongest result — a simple, low-effort modification that yields clear improvements.

- **Progressive pooling reduces memory ~45% while maintaining or improving accuracy.** With parameters (s_h=2, s_l=8, K=4), memory drops from 73 GB to 40 GB, and VideoMME overall actually rises from 61.0 to 61.3 (Table 5). This is a practical contribution for deploying LMMs on long videos.

- **Competitive or state-of-the-art results on MLVU with a 7B model.** The method achieves 69.1 M-Avg at 512 frames, surpassing GPT-4o (64.6) and outperforming all baselines across multiple subtasks (Table 2). This provides strong external validation that the approach is competitive with much larger models.

- **Clear motivation from an identifiable empirical phenomenon.** Figure 1a (divergent language vs. visual performance trends with length) and Figure 1b (t-SNE cluster separation) provide a visually compelling, intuitive rationale for why a modality-specific treatment of positional embeddings might be needed.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The central "consistent improvement with more frames" claim is contradicted by the paper's own data.** The abstract (line 12) and introduction (line 72) state: "*Across multiple long video understanding benchmarks, our method consistently improves the performance as the number of video frames increases.*" However, on VideoMME (Table 1), going from 256 to 512 frames causes a *drop* in overall accuracy (61.3 → 60.6), Short subset (72.7 → 71.9), and Long subset (52.9 → 51.3). The paper does acknowledge this later (Section 4.3, lines 369–371) with the "attention distraction" explanation, but the unqualified blanket claim in the abstract and introduction is factually incorrect for the most-established benchmark. The authors should either qualify the claim (e.g., "on most benchmarks" or "up to a point") or provide a clear caveat in the abstract.

- **The "visual context window" as a distinct entity is not causally validated; the core conceptual claim is over-interpreted.** The paper's headline insight is that visual and language tokens form separate context windows. The evidence for this is: (a) Figure 1a shows visual performance declines while language performance improves with length — but this compares different tasks and modalities under different protocols; (b) Figure 1b shows t-SNE cluster separation, but clustering does not demonstrate that the separation *limits positional reasoning range*. The method itself (Section 3.1) applies a single global scaling factor s = L_test^v / L_train^v to all RoPE dimensions — it does **not** differentiate between visual and language token positions during RoPE modulation. Thus the "visual context window" reduces to: choose a different scalar for the YaRN scaling factor. While the comparison in Table 4 (YaRN: 58.5 vs. Ours: 61.0) does empirically validate that using the visual training length helps more than the language training length, the paper never tests whether the improvement stems from the modality-specific justification or simply from picking a different (more aggressive) interpolation factor. A direct test — e.g., applying *different* RoPE modifications to visual vs. language positions — would be needed to substantiate the claimed conceptual advance. This does not invalidate the empirical contribution (the method works), but it means the conceptual framing goes beyond what the experiments support.

- **Missing specification of L_train^v, the all-important hyperparameter.** The method defines the scaling factor as s = L_test^v / L_train^v (Eq. 3), but the paper never states what L_train^v is numerically. For LLaVA-OneVision, the pre-training uses 32-frame videos (Table 1 shows the baseline at 32 frames), so L_train^v = 32 × 196 = 6,272 visual tokens — but this is never made explicit. Readers cannot verify the computed scaling factor or reproduce the method without inferring this value. This should be stated in Section 3.1.

### Minor

- **Ambiguity between tuning-free and fine-tuned results in the main presentation.** The paper states that "unless otherwise stated, we use the tuning-free model" (line 283), and Tables 1–3 report tuning-free results while Table 4 (ablation) includes both. This is technically clear. However, the abstract uses "without retraining" (line 8) and contributions say "without the need for training on long video-text paired data" (line 79), while the fine-tuning variant (LoRA on 10K image-text pairs) is included in the ablation and sometimes yields better numbers. The distinction is correct but could mislead casual readers. A footnote in Tables 1–3 or a clearer upfront statement ("all main results are tuning-free; fine-tuning on image data further improves them") would help.

- **The "attention distraction" explanation for 512-frame degradation is plausible but untested.** The paper attributes the VideoMME drop at 512 frames and the LongVideoBench short-interval drops to "attention distraction" from excessively long sequences (lines 369–371). This is a reasonable hypothesis, but no evidence is provided (e.g., attention entropy analysis, effective attention span measurement). Adding such analysis would strengthen the paper.

- **No statistical significance reported.** Performance differences are sometimes small (e.g., 61.0 vs. 61.3). No confidence intervals, variance, or multi-run results are reported. This is common in this benchmark-driven subfield, but the paper would be stronger with at least some indication of stability.

- **The "first-frame-is-best" heuristic in progressive pooling is not validated.** The paper assumes the first frame of each event group preserves the most information (Section 3.2). No comparison against alternatives (e.g., random frame, median frame, most diverse frame) is provided. A simple ablation comparing first-frame selection to random selection would reveal how robust this heuristic is.

### Trivial

- The paper uses "pretrained" and "pre-trained" inconsistently.
- Figure 1a's caption refers to "accuracy" but the y-axis label (from the figure description) is about perplexity — the relationship between the blue and yellow curves would be clearer with a more precise description.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Generalization to other LMM backbones.** The method is tested only on LLaVA-OneVision-7B. Since the RoPE modification and pooling are backbone-agnostic, applying to InternVL2 or LLaVA-NeXT would significantly strengthen claims of generality.
- **Direct comparison at the same frame count.** Tables 1–3 compare the 256/512-frame results to the 32-frame baseline, but not to the baseline at 256 frames (direct extrapolation) — that comparison is deferred to the ablation (Table 4). Including the 256-frame baseline row in Tables 1–3 would give readers immediate context.
- **Memory measurement details.** Table 5 reports "Memory (GB)" but does not specify GPU type, batch size, or whether flash attention was used. These should be documented.

## Suggestions

1. **Correct the overclaim in the abstract and introduction.** Replace "consistently improves performance as the number of video frames increases" with a qualified statement (e.g., "generally improves performance up to 256 frames, with mixed results at 512 frames").

2. **State L_train^v explicitly.** In Section 3.1, add: "For LLaVA-OneVision, the maximum visual tokens during training is L_train^v = 32 × 196 = 6,272 (32 frames at 196 tokens/frame after bilinear pooling)."

3. **Add a causal test of the visual-context-window claim.** The simplest experiment: apply different RoPE scaling factors to visual vs. language token positions within the same sequence. If visual-only scaling drives the improvement, the claim is strongly supported. If not, the story should be softened to "we find that using the visual training length in YaRN's scaling factor works better than the default."

4. **Add a footnote to Tables 1–3** indicating that these results use the tuning-free model, with fine-tuned results shown in the ablation.

5. **Validate the first-frame heuristic** in progressive pooling by comparing to random-frame selection within each group.
