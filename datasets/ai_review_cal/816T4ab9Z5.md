## Summary

This paper investigates how augmentation affects Graph Contrastive Learning (GCL). It provides empirical evidence that stronger augmentation primarily helps downstream performance by separating different classes (increasing inter-class separation) rather than gathering same-class nodes (intra-class alignment). The paper derives a theoretical lower bound connecting downstream cross-entropy loss to the InfoNCE loss and augmentation distance (δ_aug), arguing that larger δ_aug (weaker alignment) relaxes the bound and can enable better generalization. Two simple augmentation strategies are proposed — an information-guided method that preserves important edges/features while strongly augmenting unimportant ones, and a spectrum-based method that adjusts eigenvalues during training. These methods are evaluated on six datasets with three base GCL algorithms (GRACE, GCA, AD-GCL).

## Strengths

- **Empirical analysis of inter-class vs. intra-class effects (Figure 1, Section 2.2):** The paper measures positive center distance (intra-class) and negative center distance (inter-class) as a function of dropout rate. The data shows that as dropout increases, negative center distance rises sharply while positive center distance does not decrease, and accuracy improves. This provides a clean, concrete demonstration that performance gains from stronger augmentation correlate with inter-class separation rather than intra-class gathering — a useful empirical finding that extends prior discussions about augmentation overlap being rare.

- **Theoretical bound connecting augmentation distance to generalization (Theorem 2, Section 2.3):** The paper derives a lower bound on the mean cross-entropy loss in terms of the InfoNCE loss, augmentation distance δ_aug, variance terms, and number of negative samples. While a lower bound does not prove causation, the result formalizes the intuition that larger δ_aug can loosen the constraint on downstream performance. The derived gap between contrastive loss and downstream loss is a concrete theoretical contribution to understanding why minimizing InfoNCE alone may not optimize downstream accuracy.

- **Consistent improvements across multiple GCL baselines (Table 1, Section 4):** The proposed information and spectrum augmentation methods improve or match baselines across 6 datasets and 3 base algorithms (GRACE, GCA, AD-GCL). Multiple improvements are statistically significant (e.g., GRACE+S p=0.003, AD-GCL+I p=0.003). The methods are simple to integrate and the results demonstrate broad applicability rather than being tailored to a single algorithm.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The spectrum augmentation method is underspecified and not reproducible as described (Section 3.2).** The paper proposes adjusting eigenvalues λ_i based on the training dynamics of parameters θ_i (lines 234–247), but never specifies how eigenvalue changes translate to concrete graph operations (edge addition/deletion, feature perturbation, or any other modification). The update rule "λ_i = λ_i + direction_i × λ_i × α" operates on eigenvalues abstractly, with no mapping to the adjacency matrix or feature matrix that would actually produce an augmented graph. Without specifying whether eigenvalues are modified via spectral graph editing, parameterized graph convolution filters, or some other mechanism, the method cannot be implemented or evaluated by other researchers. This is not a minor implementation detail — it is a core part of the method.

- **No controlled ablation isolating the effect of the proposed selection mechanism from simply using stronger augmentation.** The paper's own theory predicts that larger δ_aug with preserved mutual information is beneficial. However, there is no experiment comparing the proposed methods against simply increasing the dropout rates (or other augmentation strength parameters) of the base GCL algorithms. Without this control, it is unclear whether the improvements in Table 1 come from the *specific* importance-based selection mechanism or simply from the fact that the proposed methods produce stronger overall augmentation. The paper acknowledges the methods are "simple" and for "theoretical verification," but this gap directly undermines the claim that the methods validate the theory.

- **The central theoretical claim relies on a lower bound that does not establish a causal relationship (Theorem 2).** The paper argues that perfect alignment is "poisonous" to generalization because larger δ_aug makes the lower bound on CE loss smaller (less restrictive). The paper explicitly acknowledges this (line 118: "does not enforce a better performance"), but then draws strong conclusions from it. A smaller lower bound means the worst-case constraint on CE loss relaxes, but it does not imply that actual CE loss decreases — the bound could be slack, or the actual loss may be dominated by other terms (variances) whose dependence on δ_aug is not analyzed. This limits the theoretical support for the paper's headline claim to an existence argument rather than a demonstrated causal relationship.

### Minor

- **Information method implementation partially contradicts stated design goal (Section 3.1).** The paper states important elements should be "kept steady and do no modification" (line 158), but the implementation sets the retention mask to 1 with only 50% probability (line 165). This means important edges/features are still dropped half the time, and unimportant ones are deleted only half the time. The paper acknowledges this is a simple verification design (line 167), but the mismatch between the stated ideal and the stochastic implementation weakens the connection between theory and method.

- **p-values are reported only for averaged performance across datasets, without explanation of the statistical test used (Table 1).** The p-values (e.g., 0.003, 0.155) appear to be computed by pooling datasets together, which is non-standard since different datasets have different scales, variances, and sample sizes. No individual-dataset significance tests are reported. The p-value column also does not specify the null hypothesis or the test procedure, making it impossible to evaluate the statistical claims.

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity is not analyzed.** The methods introduce several hyperparameters (ξ for number of important elements, α and ε for the spectrum method, the 50% probabilities) with no analysis of how performance varies with these choices. The reader cannot assess robustness.

- **One of the proposed variants shows a slight performance decrease (GCA+I on Computers: 86.60 vs. baseline 86.77),** which is not discussed.

## Novel Insights

The two reviews largely converge on the same issues (underspecified spectrum method, modest empirical support, lower bound reasoning). The most useful cross-perspective insight is that the paper's biggest gap is not any single flaw but rather the distance between the strength of its claims ("perfect alignment is poisonous") and the evidence provided: the theory is a suggestive lower bound, the experiments lack the controlled ablation needed to attribute improvements to the proposed mechanism, and one of the two methods is unimplementable as described. Fixing any one of these would help, but the paper would need to address all three to make a compelling case.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the spectrum augmentation concretely.** The method needs a clear mapping from eigenvalue adjustments to graph edits. Possibilities include (a) perturbing edges based on eigenvector centrality to control specific eigenvalues, or (b) showing that the eigenvalue update operates on the parameters of a spectral GNN filter rather than on the adjacency matrix. Without this, the method is not reproducible.
2. **Add a controlled ablation.** Compare against baselines with stronger standard augmentation (e.g., higher dropout rates in GRACE) to show that the proposed methods add value beyond simply increasing δ_aug. This is essential to validate the claimed mechanism.
3. **Clarify the statistical methodology.** Explain how p-values are computed (which test, what null hypothesis) and report per-dataset significance or effect sizes.
4. **Tone down the causal language.** The paper's contribution is a theoretical bound and empirical correlation, not a proof that perfect alignment causes harm. The title and framing should reflect this.
