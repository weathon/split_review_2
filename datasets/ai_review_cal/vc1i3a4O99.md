## Summary

This paper identifies a "frequency bias" problem in explaining sparse autoencoder (SAE) features learned from LLMs — existing explanation methods (TopAct, N2G) over-prioritize frequent linguistic patterns over rarer but more semantically meaningful discourse topics. The authors propose explaining SAE features using a fixed vocabulary set with a mutual information-inspired objective that normalizes word activations across all learned features. They further demonstrate that these discourse-level explanations enable effective steering of LLM representations for jailbreak defense (reducing ASR from 81.6→72.8 on Salad-Bench), while baseline explanation methods fail to produce steering improvements even with tuned hyperparameters.

## Strengths

**1. Novel theoretical identification of the frequency bias problem.** Section 3.1 derives Equation 2 from a topic-model formulation, showing that in text generation each instance contains *N* times more linguistic noise patterns than discourse topics. This provides a principled explanation for why existing SAE explanation methods (TopAct, N2G) produce lexical-level rather than semantic explanations. This is a genuine insight that advances understanding of SAE interpretability.

**2. MI-inspired explanation method with clear empirical advantage.** The method in Section 3.2 (Eqs. 3–5) selects words by jointly considering how much a word activates a target feature *and* how specific that word is to the target feature versus all other features. The resulting explanations substantially outperform baselines: per Table 2, the method achieves higher explanation rates across both all features and activated-only features. The qualitative examples in Table 1 concretely illustrate the shift from repetitive lexical patterns (e.g., "used to; used to;...") to discourse-level keywords (e.g., "link, code, click, url, below, button...").

**3. Downstream validation showing discourse-level explanations are practically useful.** The controlled experiment in Figure 3 is the strongest piece of evidence: when the same "Aware Security" steering strategy is applied using explanations from TopAct, N2G, and the proposed method, only the proposed method produces a clear reduction in jailbreak ASR. This directly links explanation quality (discourse-level vs. lexical) to downstream utility, ruling out the concern that the higher explanation rates stem only from format differences.

**4. Non-obvious finding about steering strategy.** Section 4.3.2 reports that erasing harmful features had negligible effect while amplifying safety awareness succeeded — a finding that aligns with the Self-Reminder philosophy and contradicts the intuitive "erase bad knowledge" approach. This insight is enabled specifically by having discourse-level explanations that capture safety-related concepts at the right level of abstraction.

### Minor

**1. The MI objective is partially heuristic; the "theory-grounded" framing is overclaimed.** The derivation from Eq. 3 to Eq. 4 is logically sound under the standard SAE monosemanticity assumption (p(c=W_c)≈1). However, the empirical estimates in Eq. 5 — using softmax-normalized dot products between word embeddings and feature vectors as probability estimates — are a heuristic modeling choice, not a proper statistical estimator derived from data. The paper would benefit from explicitly stating that Eq. 4–5 constitute an MI-inspired scoring function rather than a formal MI estimator. This does not invalidate the method but tempers the "theoretically grounded" rhetoric.

**2. GPT-4o-based explanation evaluation is not validated against human judgments.** The paper uses GPT-4o as an annotator to determine whether explanations are "successfully explained" and claims this "demonstrat[es] the effectiveness of using machine annotators to replace human annotators" (line 151). However, no human annotation study is presented to calibrate or validate these judgments. Since the proposed method outputs keyword lists while baselines output raw text spans, the GPT-4o judge may systematically favor one format — a known confound for LLM-as-judge evaluations. A small-scale human validation (e.g., 100 features, 3 annotators) would substantially strengthen this evaluation.

**3. Jailbreak defense results lack statistical rigor.** The main downstream result (ASR 81.6→72.8) is reported without confidence intervals, variance across runs, or significance tests. The evaluation uses a random 10% subset of Salad-Bench, further reducing reliability. The claim that "erasing harmful knowledge has no significant contribution" is stated qualitatively (Section 4.3.2) without presenting the supporting numbers (e.g., ASR for the EH-only strategy). These gaps weaken what is otherwise a compelling downstream validation.

**4. Several experimental design details are under-reported.**
- The vocabulary size is fixed at 5000 with no sensitivity analysis or justification for this cutoff.
- The hyperparameter β=2.5 is selected via "grid search on some numbers" with no details on the search range or selection criterion.
- The manual feature labeling process (141 safety features, 48 harmful features) describes the quantity but not inter-labeler consistency or the specific taxonomy.
- The computational cost of computing MI-based explanations across all 65,536 features is not reported.

**5. The theoretical analysis (Section 3.1) functions as intuition-building rather than rigorous theory.** The derivation relies on a specific topic-model assumption (random walk of discourse vectors) that is not empirically validated — for example, by checking whether learned feature activations exhibit the predicted frequency bias. The analysis motivates the approach but should not be presented as a formal proof of the frequency bias claim.

### Trivial

- The paper claims that GPT-4o explanations marked "yes" are "highly interpretable" and uses this to bootstrap the claim that the machine annotator works (line 151), but this is circular: the examples were selected for being interpretable, not sampled representatively.
