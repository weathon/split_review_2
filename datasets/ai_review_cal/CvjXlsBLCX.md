## Summary

This paper proposes a strength system for games, comprising a strength estimator (SE) trained on human game records using a Bradley-Terry ranking loss, and SE-MCTS that modifies AlphaZero-style MCTS search by penalizing actions whose estimated strength deviates from a target rank. The strength estimator achieves >80% rank-prediction accuracy with 15 games in Go (vs. ~49% for supervised learning baselines with 100 games) and generalizes to unseen ranks. SE∞-MCTS achieves 51.33% human move prediction accuracy in Go (vs. 42.56% for prior SOTA SA-MCTS), with consistent results in chess.

## Strengths

1. **Large improvement in rank-prediction accuracy with fewer games.** The strength estimator (SE/SE∞) achieves >80% accuracy after only 15 games in Go, while the best supervised-learning baseline (SL_sum) reaches only 49% even after 100 games (Figure 2a). This is a substantial and well-supported improvement.

2. **Better human move alignment than prior strength-adjustment methods.** SE∞-MCTS achieves 51.33% average move-prediction accuracy in Go, significantly above SA-MCTS (42.56%) and vanilla MCTS (50.38% but non-adjustable) (Table 1). This demonstrates that the strength-guided search modification produces more human-like behavior than the previous state-of-the-art.

3. **Consistent results across a second game (chess).** SE∞ achieves >80% rank-prediction accuracy with 26 games in chess (vs. ~40% for SL baselines), and SE∞-MCTS achieves 47.25% move-prediction accuracy vs. SA-MCTS's 40.21% (Section 4.5, Table 2). This supports generality.

4. **Generalization to unseen ranks from limited training data.** When trained on only 2 or 3 ranks, the strength estimator still predicts all 11 ranks with >80% accuracy (Section 4.4, Figure 5). This is a novel capability not demonstrated by prior supervised approaches.

5. **Pragmatic handling of out-of-distribution actions via synthetic rank r∞.** Introducing a weakest rank generated by random action perturbations stabilizes strength predictions during MCTS exploration (Section 3.2). The ablation (Figure 4) shows SE-MCTS without r∞ fails to adjust strength, confirming the design's necessity.

6. **Modification of MCTS search tree rather than only the final decision.** SE-MCTS alters the selection phase by penalizing actions whose strength score deviates from the target, whereas prior methods (Wu et al., 2019) only change the post-search action choice (Section 3.3). This architectural difference is directly tied to the improved human-like behavior.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Strength adjustment validation is indirect; the central claim is somewhat overstated relative to the evidence.** The paper claims SE∞-MCTS "successfully adjusts to designated ranks," but the primary evidence (Figure 4) only demonstrates that SE∞-MCTS produces *monotonically ordered* win rates against a fixed baseline. This shows the method can produce consistently ordered playing strengths, but does not independently verify that the *absolute* playing strength of SE∞-MCTS_i corresponds to human rank r_i. The target strength scores β̄_i are derived from human game data (candidate dataset), and the rank prediction experiments (Section 4.2) confirm the strength estimator can distinguish ranks, which provides an indirect chain of evidence. However, a direct validation — e.g., comparing SE∞-MCTS_i against AIs with known, fixed Elo ratings or checking whether the win rate against human players of rank r_i is approximately 50% — would substantially strengthen the core claim. The gap between "produces ordered strengths" and "plays at designated ranks" is not fatal (the ordering evidence plus rank prediction accuracy plus human move alignment together provide meaningful support), but it does mean the headline claim is asserted more confidently than the experimental design warrants.

### Minor

2. **Method description is underspecified in one critical place.** In §3.3, the paper states: "We can calculate the composite strength score β̄(s,a) by averaging all β from the nodes within the subtree of state s." It notes this is "similar to the method used to calculate estimated Q-values," but does not specify how this is computed efficiently during search — e.g., whether β values are backed up as running averages (like Q-values), whether the entire subtree is traversed each time, or whether the averaging is over state-action pairs or over node-level aggregates. The normalization of δ(s,a) using min-max across the current search tree is also mentioned but not detailed. This ambiguity hinders reproducibility, though the high-level idea remains clear.

3. **Human move prediction results (Tables 1, 2) lack confidence intervals or variance estimates.** The evaluation uses 50 games per rank in Go (and presumably a similar setup in chess), but only point estimates are reported. Given the moderate sample size, error bars would help assess the reliability of the observed differences between methods.

4. **The architecture of the supervised learning baselines is not specified.** The paper states that SL_sum and SL_vote are "trained to classify 11 ranks for a given state-action pair" (§4.2) but does not state their network architecture, capacity, or whether they use the same 20-block ResNet as the strength estimator. If the SL baselines use a smaller network, the comparison in Figure 2 may not be fully controlled.

5. **The SA-MCTS calibration procedure is described ambiguously.** The paper says SA-MCTS is calibrated so that "each SA-MCTS_i and SE∞-MCTS_i achieve a comparable win rate, i.e., approximately 50%" (§4.3). It is not entirely clear against which opponent this 50% win rate is achieved or how the calibration was performed across 11 ranks. Clarifying this would improve interpretability.

## Removed Points
These points were flagged by reviewers but are removed after verification against the paper:

- **"MCTS baseline uses the same multi-task network, conflating strength module with base policy"** — Removed. MCTS uses only the policy (p) and value (v) heads, ignoring the β head. The multi-task training does not bias the comparison; MCTS benefits from any representation improvements that the strength loss provides. This is a conservative setup, not a biased one.
- **"SA-MCTS comparison is unfair because it is stochastic while SE-MCTS is deterministic"** — Removed. SA-MCTS's stochasticity (proportional selection) is inherent to its design as the prior state-of-the-art. Comparing against the established baseline is standard practice.
- **"Paper omits recent methods like Liu et al. (2020) or Fujita (2022)"** — Removed. Both are cited and discussed in §2.3–2.4. Liu et al. requires human interaction for strength estimation, which is a fundamentally different problem setting.
- **"Strength adjustment evaluation is fundamentally flawed / fatal"** — Demoted from "fatal" to the major weakness above. The evaluation provides meaningful evidence (monotonic ordering, human move alignment) but is not fully validating the absolute strength claim. The framing as "fundamentally flawed" is an overstatement given the evidence present.
- **"Geometric mean justification is thin"** — Removed. The paper provides explicit justification (stability, consistency across sample sizes, outlier mitigation).
- **Training details about batch construction and m sampling** — Removed. These are standard implementation details typical at this level of description.
- **"r∞ generation may not cover MCTS-specific actions"** — Removed. The random perturbation approach is a reasonable and standard method for generating out-of-distribution training data.

## Novel Insights
The two reviewers' inputs together surface an interesting tension: the strength estimator (§3.1–3.2) is thoroughly validated and clearly superior to prior work, while the SE-MCTS strength adjustment (§3.3, §4.3) introduces a genuinely novel approach (search-tree modification via strength-guided PUCT penalties instead of post-hoc action selection) but is evaluated with an experimental design that relies on internal consistency (monotonicity + calibration) rather than external anchoring to human skill levels. This creates a methodological asymmetry: the estimation side is externally validated against ground-truth human ranks, while the adjustment side is only internally validated against itself. Closing this gap — by directly linking the adjustment output to externally measured playing strength — would make the paper's two halves equally compelling.

## Suggestions
1. Add a direct validation of strength adjustment, such as comparing SE∞-MCTS_i against AIs with known, fixed Elo ratings or reporting the win rate of SE∞-MCTS_i against human players of rank r_i.
2. Clarify the subtree composite strength computation in §3.3 with pseudocode or a precise description of how β values are backed up through the tree during search.
3. Add confidence intervals or standard deviations to Tables 1 and 2, and ideally to the win-rate curves in Figures 4 and 7.
4. Specify the network architecture used for SL baseline networks to ensure fair comparison.
5. Clarify the SA-MCTS calibration procedure: against what opponent, at what granularity, and how the "approximately 50%" target was operationalized.
