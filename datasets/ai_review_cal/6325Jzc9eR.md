## Summary

VEditBench is a benchmark for text-guided video editing (TGVE) that provides 420 real-world videos (300 short, 120 long), six editing tasks (object addition/removal/swap, scene replacement, motion change, style translation), and a nine-dimensional evaluation framework spanning semantic fidelity and visual quality. The authors evaluate ten state-of-the-art TGVE models and analyze their performance across metrics and tasks, demonstrating that no single model dominates.

## Strengths

- **Larger and more diverse video dataset than prior TGVE benchmarks**: VEditBench includes 420 videos across six categories, while prior open benchmarks (LOVEU-TGVE-2023, BalanceCC) contain only 76 and 100 videos respectively, with fewer categories (Section 1, Table 1). The 3–5× scale increase is a clear improvement.

- **Expanded coverage of editing tasks**: The benchmark systematically covers six distinct editing operations — object addition, removal, swap, scene replacement, motion change, and style translation — compared to the four-task setups in prior benchmarks (Section 3.2, Figure 4). Each task has specifically generated prompts.

- **Multi-dimensional evaluation framework**: The nine-metric framework separates semantic fidelity (text alignment via CLIP/ViCLIP, video alignment via SSIM and trajectory-based Motion Similarity) from visual quality (spatial quality via Q-Align, temporal quality via Content-Debiased FVD and motion smoothness, spatio-temporal quality via Q-Align video scoring). This structure enables fine-grained diagnosis of model strengths and weaknesses (Section 4, Tables 2–3).

- **Inclusion of a long-video subset**: VEditBench-Long provides 120 videos (10–40 seconds), addressing an underexplored and practically important challenge. The analysis reveals that most models struggle with temporal consistency over longer durations (Sections 3.1, 6).

- **Human-in-the-loop data curation**: Captions and edit prompts are generated by GPT-4o and manually reviewed/corrected, improving data quality over fully automatic pipelines (Section 3.1, Figure 2).

- **Evaluation of ten diverse TGVE models**: The paper provides a broad comparison covering instruction-based and target-prompt-based models, T2I-based and T2V-based approaches, with both quantitative tables and qualitative examples (Section 5, Tables 2–3, Figure 6).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Motion Similarity metric penalizes correct behavior on the Motion Change task.** The metric (Section 4.1, Equation 1) computes a similarity score between source and target motion trajectories, where higher values indicate greater motion *preservation*. It is placed under "Video Alignment" and designed to measure coherence with the source video. For the Motion Change task — where the instruction is to alter the object's or camera's motion — a model that correctly changes the motion will receive a *lower* Motion Similarity score, which the framework interprets as worse. The paper does not acknowledge this conflict, and results are reported in aggregated tables (Tables 2–3) without task-aware separation. This means the Motion Similarity metric is measuring the wrong thing for one of six tasks. The fix is straightforward: compute motion *similarity* only on motion-preserving tasks (object swap, scene replacement, style translation, object addition) and use a motion *dissimilarity* or task-appropriate metric for Motion Change, or at minimum report this dimension separately per task. While this affects only 1 of 9 metrics for 1 of 6 tasks, it is a structural design flaw in a benchmark that aims to be a standard.

### Minor

- **No per-task numeric results table.** The paper's central analytical claim — that "model performance varies significantly across tasks" and "some models excel at object swap but struggle with motion change" (Section 6) — is supported only by radar charts (Figure 5) that show visual patterns but do not allow readers to extract precise per-task scores. While the radar charts do provide per-task information visually, the absence of a numeric table (e.g., SSIM per task, text alignment per task) makes the claimed task-specific variation impossible to verify quantitatively. For a benchmark paper whose purpose is standardization, task-level numbers should be a standard reporting practice.

- **No human evaluation or metric validation.** The benchmark introduces a trajectory-based Motion Similarity metric and applies established metrics (Q-Align, Content-Debiased FVD, SSIM) to the video editing domain, but provides no human correlation analysis. Without evidence that the nine metrics correlate with human perceptual judgments of edit quality, readers cannot assess whether the reported rankings reflect genuine quality differences or metric-specific biases. Even a small-scale human preference study would substantially strengthen the conclusions.

- **Limited documentation of model evaluation settings.** The Settings section (Section 5) describes only which models were evaluated on which subsets. The paper does not specify how edit prompts were adapted for instruction-based models (Text2Video-Zero, InsV2V) vs. target-prompt-based models (all others), which hyperparameters or guidance scales were used, whether per-video fine-tuning was performed (as required by Tune-A-Video), or what inference-step budgets were allocated. While some details may reside in the supplementary material, the main paper lacks sufficient information for reproducible evaluation.

### Trivial

- **Inconsistency in reported long-video duration.** The abstract states "10-20 seconds" (line 4) while the main text states "10-40 seconds" (lines 22, 42, 72) for long videos. This discrepancy should be resolved.

- **Missing per-category video counts.** The paper states videos are "balanced across six categories" (line 74) but does not report the exact distribution of videos among categories or per task, making it difficult to assess whether aggregated scores are dominated by certain categories.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the Motion Similarity metric**: Either compute it only on motion-preserving tasks (object swap, scene replacement, style translation, object addition), or introduce a motion-change metric (e.g., dissimilarity) for the Motion Change task, and clearly document this task-awareness.
2. **Add a per-task numeric table**: Include a table showing the key metrics (at minimum SSIM and text alignment) broken down per editing task. This would transform the claim about task-specific variation from a visual observation to a verifiable result.
3. **Add a small human evaluation study**: 100–200 pairwise comparisons between model outputs across diverse tasks would provide evidence that the nine-metric framework correlates with human perception, which is the gold standard for a benchmark paper.
4. **Expand the Settings section**: Specify prompt formatting for each model type, guidance scales, inference steps, and whether per-video fine-tuning was used.
5. **Fix the duration inconsistency**: Align the abstract (10-20s) with the main text (10-40s) for long videos.
