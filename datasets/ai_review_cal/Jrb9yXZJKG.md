## Summary

This paper introduces CAD-Editor, the first system for text-based CAD editing. The approach formulates the task as a sequence-to-sequence problem, using a pre-trained LLM (Llama-3-8B-Instruct) as backbone. The key contributions are a data pipeline that synthesizes training triplets (instruction, original CAD, edited CAD) by combining design-variation models (SkexGen, Hnc-CAD) with MLLM-based captioning (GPT-4o), and a two-stage fine-tuning strategy using a large synthetic dataset followed by a small selective dataset filtered via D-CLIP or human feedback. Results show high output validity (91.4% valid ratio) and improved instruction alignment (D-CLIP 0.21 vs. GPT-4o 3-shot -0.01) on a synthetic benchmark.

## Strengths

1. **First formalization of text-based CAD editing with a complete pipeline.** The paper identifies an underexplored task and provides a practical end-to-end solution: converting CAD models to LLM-friendly sequences, synthesizing paired training data via existing variation models, and using multi-level captioning (GPT-4o) to generate textual instructions. This pipeline is well-motivated and addresses a genuine gap in the literature.

2. **Strong output validity.** CAD-Editor achieves a Valid Ratio of 91.4% (Table 1), dramatically surpassing GPT-4o (~40–50%) and the design-variation baselines (~60–70%). This indicates that fine-tuning the LLM on the synthetic CAD sequence data substantially improves the likelihood of generating renderable, physically realizable 3D shapes — a nontrivial achievement given the strict syntax of CAD command sequences.

3. **Multi-level captioning improves data quality.** The ablation on a 10k subset (Table 2/3) shows that multi-level captioning (describe → identify differences → compress) consistently outperforms direct basic captioning across all metrics (COV, MMD, JSD, D-CLIP, Valid Ratio). This validates the design choice of decomposing the captioning task into simpler sub-tasks.

4. **Selective data fine-tuning provides additive benefit.** Both automatic (DCF) and human (HF) selection of high-quality model outputs for further fine-tuning improve D-CLIP scores beyond the synthetic-only model (Table 2/3). This demonstrates a practical bootstrapping mechanism for refining LLM performance without requiring large-scale human annotation.

5. **Qualitative breadth and iterative editing capability.** Figures 5–8 demonstrate diverse edits (size, shape, position, addition, deletion, replacement) and show that the model can be applied iteratively, which is important for practical CAD workflows.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Test set is in-distribution with the training data, limiting evidence for generalization.** The test set (2,000 examples from the DeepCAD test segment) is constructed using the **same pipeline** (Section 3.2) as the 92,070 training examples: CAD pairs via SkexGen/Hnc-CAD, textual instructions via GPT-4o with multi-level captioning. While the CAD models are held out, the instruction distribution and editing-operation types are shared. The paper does not evaluate on (a) real human-authored editing instructions, (b) human-performed ground-truth CAD edits, or (c) CAD pairs generated by a different method. This means the evaluation measures the model's ability to reconstruct edits within the synthetic distribution, not its ability to interpret diverse, open-ended text instructions from real users. The paper's central claim — "text-based CAD editing" — substantially overclaims relative to the evidence provided. **Why this matters**: The problem the paper claims to solve is real-world CAD editing driven by natural language; the evaluation does not establish that the method works for that use case.

2. **Missing critical baseline: a fine-tuned smaller LLM on the same synthetic data.** The main baselines (GPT-4o zero-shot and 3-shot) are not fine-tuned, while CAD-Editor is fine-tuned on 92,070 task-specific examples with LoRA. The performance gap is therefore unsurprising. The most informative comparison would be a model of similar scale (e.g., CodeLlama-7B or T5-Large) fine-tuned on the identical synthetic data, with and without selective data. Without this, it is unclear how much of the improvement comes from the proposed pipeline versus simply from any fine-tuning on domain data. **Why this matters**: The paper's claimed advantage over GPT-4o conflates the effect of the data pipeline with the effect of fine-tuning itself.

3. **D-CLIP is used both as a selection criterion (DCF) and as the primary instruction-alignment metric, with no reported correlation to human judgment.** The selective data via DCF picks outputs maximizing D-CLIP; the main evaluation then reports D-CLIP to measure text-CAD alignment. This creates a closed loop where the metric can be optimized without improving real editing quality. The paper does not report the correlation between D-CLIP and human evaluation scores, nor does it analyze whether improvements in D-CLIP transfer to human-perceived quality. The HF (human feedback) variant of selective data avoids this circularity but is conflated with DCF in the paper's framing. **Why this matters**: The main metric supporting the method's advantage over baselines is potentially confounded.

4. **Human evaluation success rate of 31.1% is low and lacks failure analysis.** Crowd workers judged only 31.1% of CAD-Editor's outputs as successful (aligning with instruction and of sufficient quality). Nearly 70% of outputs are failures. The paper frames this positively ("underscores the enhanced user satisfaction"), but in absolute terms the model is unreliable even on the synthetic test set. No breakdown of failure modes is provided (e.g., invalid geometry vs. irrelevant edits vs. incorrect shape attributes), making it difficult to assess whether the method is on the right track or to guide future improvements. **Why this matters**: A 31.1% success rate means a user would have to regenerate ~3-4 times on average for one acceptable edit, which limits practical utility.

### Minor

1. **Ablation for multi-level captioning is performed on 10k subset, not the full 92k dataset.** The paper explicitly acknowledges this is due to computational constraints (line 119), but the reported gains may not fully transfer to the full-scale model. The full CAD-Editor uses multi-level captioning throughout, so the ablation on a subset does not definitively confirm the benefit at scale.

2. **Selective data construction lacks detail on sampling parameters.** The paper states "sampling multiple times often yields at least one exceptional outcome" (line 71) but does not specify how many samples were generated per input for either DCF or HF, nor the size of the resulting selective dataset. This missing detail affects reproducibility and makes it hard to assess the cost-effectiveness of the selective data approach.

3. **No sensitivity analysis on filtering thresholds.** The thresholds for CAD pair filtering (0.95 for SkexGen, 0.9 for Hnc-CAD) are stated without justification or sensitivity analysis. It is unclear how performance would change with stricter or looser thresholds.

4. **Standard deviations / confidence intervals not reported.** Table 1 reports "average scores across 3 runs" but does not include variance. Given the modest test set size (2,000 examples with 5 outputs each), some metrics may have non-negligible variance.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A small-scale evaluation on real human-authored instructions (e.g., 50–100 instructions) with human-performed ground-truth edits would substantially strengthen the claim of "text-based CAD editing" rather than "synthetic-instruction-following."
- Adding a random-selection baseline for the selective data ablation would clarify whether DCF/HF provide benefit beyond simply using more data.
- Providing prompt templates used for GPT-4o (instruction generation and baseline editing) would improve reproducibility.
- Reporting inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Fleiss' kappa) for the human evaluation would assess reliability.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's framing — that the paper's genuine contribution is the data generation pipeline rather than the editing model itself — is insightful but not fully developed. The paper's pipeline (design-variation models + MLLM captioning → synthetic triples → initial fine-tuning → selective data bootstrapping) is a reusable recipe for any domain where (a) a generative model that produces variations exists and (b) an MLLM can describe visual differences between pairs. The critic correctly notes that the paper's evaluation would be stronger if framed around pipeline quality (what fraction of synthetic instruction/edit pairs are correct? does D-CLIP correlate with human preference at the sample level?) rather than as a deployed editing system. The Strength Finder correctly identifies that the 91.4% Valid Ratio and multi-level captioning ablation are genuine empirical contributions that survive even the evaluation concerns.

## Suggestions

1. **Acknowledge the in-distribution evaluation openly and qualify claims.** Reframe the paper's contribution as a data-generation pipeline and proof-of-concept for synthetic-instruction-based CAD editing. Add this as a clear limitation in the abstract and conclusion.
2. **Add a fine-tuned smaller LLM baseline** (e.g., CodeLlama-7B or T5-Large trained on the same 92k synthetic data, with LoRA). This is the most important missing experiment.
3. **Report D-CLIP vs. human evaluation correlation** at the individual sample level to address the circularity concern. If the correlation is reasonable, include it explicitly.
4. **Add a failure mode analysis** for the human evaluation (categorize the 68.9% failures into types: invalid geometry, instruction not followed, wrong shape, etc.).
5. **Specify all missing implementation details**: number of samples per input for selective data, size of the selective dataset, prompt templates, rendering resolution/angle for CLIP.
