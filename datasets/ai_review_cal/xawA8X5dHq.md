## Summary

This paper creates a fictional medical domain (the Glianorex gland) with a textbook and 264 multiple-choice questions, then evaluates diverse LLMs (7B to 110B, proprietary and open-source) in a zero-shot setting to test whether MCQ benchmarks measure genuine clinical knowledge or pattern recognition. The key finding is that all models converge around 67% accuracy with small pairwise Cohen's d values, suggesting uniform pattern-matching behavior rather than knowledge-driven performance. The methodology of using fictional content to isolate memorization from reasoning is clever and novel.

## Strengths

- **Fictional domain cleanly isolates memorization from reasoning**: By creating an entirely fictional gland with no prior data exposure, the paper ensures that models cannot rely on memorized medical knowledge. This direct experimental control (Section "Methods", first paragraph) is a genuinely clever approach that goes beyond what existing benchmarks can offer.

- **Uniform near-67% accuracy across diverse architectures and scales**: All evaluated models—from 7B to 110B, across GPT, Llama, Qwen, Yi, Mistral families, and proprietary/open-source boundaries—achieve similar accuracy with most Cohen's d values near zero (e.g., d=0.012 between Yi-1.5-34B and Qwen1.5-110B, reported in the Results text). This tight clustering despite vast differences in training data and scale is the paper's strongest evidence for pattern-recognition-driven performance.

- **Language-dependent performance reveals pattern-recognition reliance**: Models score systematically higher in English (69.5%) than French (63.8%), and the distribution of correct answers (Figure 2) is more skewed toward correct in English while more balanced in French. This language gap (Section "Evaluations") shows that models depend on language-processing fluency to infer answers, consistent with a pattern-recognition strategy rather than conceptual understanding.

- **Fine-tuned medical models improve only in English**: Domain-specific models (Internist.ai, Meerkat-7b) outperform base Mistral-7B in English but not in French (Section "Evaluations", final paragraph). This differential effect demonstrates that additional MCQ training does not produce robust cross-lingual clinical reasoning, strengthening the argument about the limitations of current evaluations.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No human baseline for interpreting the absolute accuracy level**: The paper characterizes 67% as "high performance" and builds an argument that this reflects pattern recognition. However, without knowing how human medical students (or even non-experts given the textbook) would perform on the same 264 questions, the absolute value cannot be interpreted. If humans who study the textbook score 90%+, the models' 67% would actually indicate limited understanding. If humans score ~67%, it would be consistent with reasonable inference from the text. The uniformity finding (all models cluster together) remains informative regardless, but the paper's framing of "high performance" as evidence for pattern recognition depends on an ungrounded absolute judgment.

- **Synthetic-bias confound is acknowledged but not experimentally addressed**: Both the textbook and all questions were generated by GPT-4 with the same generation pipeline (single paragraph + TOC), and models are evaluated on that output. The paper acknowledges this in Limitations but dismisses it by arguing "any bias introduced by GPT-4 was likely present in the real-world data"—a defense that conflates distributional regularities inherent to medical knowledge with idiosyncratic patterns of a specific model's generation style. A non-GPT question-generation source (e.g., Llama-3-70B or human-authored questions from the same textbook) would be needed to distinguish content-based pattern matching from generator-specific artifact exploitation.

- **Conclusions extrapolate beyond what the single fictional scenario supports**: The paper's central claim—"traditional MCQ-based benchmarks may not be sufficient for assessing the true understanding and clinical reasoning abilities of LLMs" (Discussion, "Benchmarking")—is based on a single fictional gland with 264 questions generated by one process. This extrapolates from one specific scenario to the entire suite of medical MCQ benchmarks used in the field. The jump in "Medical implications" to recommending that models "should undergo clinical trials to ensure safety" is a particularly large leap from this narrow evidence base. The experimental design tests whether models can answer questions about novel content without exposure, but does not directly test whether real-world MCQ performance is driven by the same mechanisms.

### Minor

- **Question quality and difficulty are not validated**: The paper states questions were generated by GPT-4 with a prompt asking for "a very complicated multiple-choice question requiring multiple steps of reasoning." However, there is no human review for factual consistency with the textbook, correctness of the single correct answer, or question difficulty calibration. The heavily skewed distribution in English (most questions answered correctly by all models, per Figure 2) could indicate that many questions are simple paraphrasing tasks rather than tests of multi-step reasoning. Without item-level analysis, it is unclear whether the ~67% average reflects genuine question difficulty or is driven by a mix of trivial and overly ambiguous questions.

- **Prompt design may produce a mismatch between stated question difficulty and available context**: The generation prompt requests "a very complicated multiple-choice question requiring multiple steps of reasoning" but provides only a single textbook paragraph (plus the table of contents) as context. This may lead GPT-4 to generate questions that either are trivially answerable from the single paragraph or require cross-chapter information not actually provided. The paper does not analyze how question difficulty relates to the distance between the question content and the provided paragraph.

- **Multiple significance tests are reported without correction**: Table 2 reports pairwise statistical significance among many model pairs without any multiple-testing correction (e.g., Bonferroni, FDR). Given the number of comparisons being tested, some significant results would be expected by chance. The paper should apply appropriate corrections or clarify the analysis.

- **The recommendation for clinical trials is unsupported by the evidence**: The "Medical implications" section's call for clinical trials "similarly to medical devices and drugs" is a policy-level recommendation that goes well beyond what the experimental evidence can support. The paper shows that models use pattern recognition on fictional MCQs; this does not imply that current clinical use of LLMs is dangerous or that clinical trials are the necessary next step.

- **French translation quality is not assessed**: The French questions were obtained through GPT-4 translation with a one-shot prompt, but no back-translation check, human fluency rating, or equivalence analysis is reported. The 5.7% accuracy gap between English and French could partially reflect translation artifacts rather than genuine cross-lingual reasoning differences.

### Trivial

- The p-value significance table (Table 2) uses rotated column headers that are difficult to read and the layout is not self-explanatory.
- The Limitations paragraph on "Knowledge coherence" contains an ambiguous clause ("does affect the internal validity of the study") that appears to contradict the preceding claim about coherence not undermining conclusions, making the intended meaning unclear.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Including a human baseline (medical students studying the textbook and taking the same test) would ground the interpretation of the 67% accuracy level.
- Generating a second set of questions using a non-GPT model (e.g., Llama-3-70B) would control for generator-specific pattern exploitation.
- Item-level analysis (difficulty per question, correlation with question length/number of reasoning steps, position of correct answer) would strengthen the claim about pattern recognition.
- A small set of open-ended questions from the same textbook evaluated with LLM-as-judge could provide a direct contrast to the MCQ format.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a human baseline study**: Have medical students (or even proficient English/French speakers) study the Glianorex textbook and take the same 264 questions. This single addition would transform the interpretability of the 67% figure and directly ground or refute the "high performance" claim.

2. **Tighten the conclusions to match the evidence**: Replace broad claims about "traditional MCQ-based benchmarks" with more circumscribed findings about this specific fictional benchmark. The leap to clinical trial recommendations should be removed or substantially qualified.

3. **Validate the question set**: A domain-expert review of 50–100 questions for factual consistency, answer uniqueness, and difficulty level would address the instrument validity concern. Report the distribution of question difficulty and explore what question features correlate with model accuracy.

4. **Acknowledge and bound the synthetic-bias confound more carefully**: Replace the current defense (that real-world data has similar patterns) with an explicit acknowledgment that this remains an open question, and ideally include a control experiment using human-authored or non-GPT-generated questions.
