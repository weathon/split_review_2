## Summary

This paper proposes DGAP (Discriminator-Guided Action Optimization), a framework that combines a learned step-level scorer (discriminator) with in-context prompting to improve LLM-based embodied planning. From a small set of expert demonstrations, DGAP trains a RoBERTa-based discriminator that scores LLM-generated actions at each step. Actions scoring below a threshold trigger replanning, while high-scoring action-score pairs are fed back into the prompt to guide future decisions. Experiments on ScienceWorld and VirtualHome with GPT-4 and Llama3-70B show consistent improvements over baselines, particularly on long-horizon tasks.

## Strengths

- **Consistent and substantial performance gains across two diverse benchmarks and two LLM backbones.** Table 1 (ScienceWorld) shows DGAP outperforming SwiftSage (the SOTA baseline) on the majority of 30 task types, with particularly large margins on long-horizon tasks. Table 2 (VirtualHome) reports DGAP exceeding all baselines (ProgPrompt, Inner Monologue, Tree Planner) on both EXEC and SR metrics in NovelTasks and NovelScenes settings, including under VLM-based perception. The improvement holds for both GPT-4 and Llama3-70B, demonstrating robustness to the base LLM.

- **Data-efficient discriminator training with a thoughtfully designed three-part dataset.** Section 3.1 describes a data blending strategy (expert actions scored 10, semantically least-related random actions scored 0, and augmented offline data scored by cosine similarity) that expands generalization from only 10–30 expert trajectories per task. The approach uses fewer expert demonstrations than baselines (which receive ~20–60 per task) while still achieving superior results.

- **Closed-loop refinement mechanism that reduces environment interaction.** Section 3.2's threshold-based replanning (score < τ triggers regeneration before execution) avoids costly trial-and-error in the environment. The method works with API-based LLMs (GPT-4) without parameter tuning, making it practically applicable beyond open-source models.

- **Clear empirical demonstration of efficiency gains.** Figure 5 visualizes that DGAP achieves higher cumulative expert scores in fewer environment steps than SwiftSage and the oracle on multiple tasks, directly supporting the efficiency claim with trajectory-level data.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The discriminator's training labels for the augmented offline data use an unvalidated heuristic.** Section 3.1 states that for unseen scenarios without ground-truth, "we mildly treat the first candidate generated by the LM as 10" and subsequent candidates receive `cosine_similarity(candidate, first_candidate) × 10`. This heuristic is used for a "significant proportion" of the discriminator's training set, yet the paper provides no empirical validation that these proxy scores correlate with actual task success or expert alignment. Without validation (e.g., measuring correlation with held-out expert trajectories, or comparing against simpler alternatives like raw cosine similarity), the reliability of the central guidance signal is unknown. Since the entire planning pipeline depends on the discriminator's scores, this is a structural gap.

- **The theoretical claims overstate what the implemented algorithm provably achieves.** The abstract claims DGAP "is provable to achieve a stronger policy than the LLM planner," and Section 3.3 presents Lemma 3.1 / Corollary 3.2 as a formal optimization result. However, the actual DGAP algorithm does not perform KL-constrained optimization — it uses in-context prompting with threshold-based replanning, with no parameter update, KL penalty, or mechanism for the partition function Z(s). The paper's own Section 3.3 heading calls this "qualitative analysis," but the formal lemma/corollary structure and the language "provable to obtain a higher expected return" (line 127) imply a guarantee that the implemented method does not satisfy. The theory should be reframed as motivational analogy, not as a formal proof of optimality.

### Minor

- **No error bars, standard deviations, or variance measures reported for ScienceWorld results (Table 1).** While Table 2 (VirtualHome) includes standard deviations from 3 replicates on 60 tasks, the ScienceWorld results in Table 1 report only single values per task type. Without variance estimates, it is impossible to assess the significance of observed differences (e.g., SwiftSage matching or exceeding DGAP on several tasks like 1-1, 1-4, 2-1), and the claim of "superior performance" is not uniformly supported by the visible data.

- **No quantitative efficiency metrics reported.** The paper claims "better efficiency" but reports no concrete measures — number of LLM calls per task, average replanning iterations, wall-clock time, or environment steps saved. Figure 5 provides qualitative evidence (score vs. steps trajectories), but this does not substitute for a quantitative comparison. The threshold-based replanning loop generates multiple candidate actions internally (incurring LLM queries) before executing one; the paper reports neither side of this cost trade-off.

- **Threshold τ selection is not justified.** Section 3.2 states τ = 5 for ScienceWorld and τ = 6 for VirtualHome, chosen "based on their respective training data distributions." No details, analysis, or sensitivity study are provided. The paper does not report how replanning frequency varies with τ or whether results are robust to the specific value.

- **Discriminator training details are underspecified.** No information is provided about the RoBERTa model size (base/large), training hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs, batch size, optimizer), or held-out accuracy/error of the discriminator. These details affect reproducibility and would help readers assess whether the discriminator indeed learns a meaningful scoring function.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation to isolate the contribution of the augmented offline data.** Comparing DGAP trained with only expert+random data (scores 10/0) vs. with the full augmented dataset would clarify how much the heuristic labeling contributes to performance.
- **Threshold sensitivity study** (e.g., varying τ across {3,4,5,6,7} for one environment).
- **Comparison with a simpler inference-time baseline** that scores action candidates by cosine similarity to expert demonstrations (without a trained discriminator), to test whether the regression model adds value over a non-parametric similarity metric.

## Suggestions

1. **Validate the discriminator's scoring** by measuring its correlation with actual task success on a held-out set of expert and suboptimal trajectories, and compare against simpler alternatives (e.g., raw cosine similarity with expert action embedding). This would directly address the most critical concern about the method's foundation.

2. **Tone down the theoretical framing.** Reframe Section 3.3 explicitly as a motivational analogy rather than a provable guarantee. Remove the Lemma/Corollary structure unless a formal connection between the implemented algorithm and the KL-constrained objective can be established.

3. **Add error bars or confidence intervals** to Table 1 (ScienceWorld) by reporting statistics across multiple runs or seeds.

4. **Report concrete efficiency metrics:** average number of LLM calls per task, average replanning iterations per step, and/or wall-clock time, for both DGAP and baselines.

5. **Report discriminator training details** (model size, hyperparameters, validation accuracy/error) and a sensitivity analysis for the threshold τ.
