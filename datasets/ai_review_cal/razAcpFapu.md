## Summary

This paper proposes a face embedding mapping (FEM) framework that reconstructs realistic face images from embeddings of both standard face recognition (FR) and privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems. It introduces two mapping variants (FEM-MLP and FEM-KAN) that learn to transform target-model embeddings into the input space of a pre-trained IPA-FaceID diffusion model. Experiments across multiple PPFR models, partial/protected embeddings, out-of-distribution generalization, and demographic bias are conducted.

## Strengths

- **First systematic face-reconstruction attack targeting PPFR systems**: The paper evaluates against four PPFR models (DCTDP, HFCF, HFCF-SkinColor, PartialFace) in Table 1 and two protected-embedding schemes (PolyProtect, MLP-Hash) in Table 5. Prior work focused on normal FR models or post-hoc embedding protection; this paper is the first to target PPFR systems that modify the entire face-processing pipeline. This fills a genuine gap and has practical security implications.

- **Consistent ASR advantage of KAN over MLP on PPFR models, with both quantitative and qualitative evidence**: Table 1 shows FEM-KAN outperforms FEM-MLP on all four PPFR models (e.g., DCTDP: 62.9% vs. 58.3% average ASR; HFCF: 60.0% vs. 50.7%). Table 3 further shows FEM-KAN achieves better FID, PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS on out-of-distribution identities (Synth-500). These results support the claim that KAN's learnable activation functions offer a meaningful advantage for non-linear embedding mapping in the PPFR setting.

- **Demonstrated robustness to partial and protected embeddings**: Table 4 shows FEM-KAN maintains high ASR (e.g., 59.3% on IRSE50→ArcFace) even with only 50% embedding leakage. Table 5 demonstrates successful reconstruction against MLP-Hash protection (e.g., 70.9% ASR on GhostFaceNet). These experiments go well beyond the standard complete-embedding setting.

- **Bias analysis and ablation study**: Table 7 reports ASR separately across demographic groups (Caucasian, Asian, Indian, African), identifying that African identities have the lowest reconstruction rates — transparency about failure modes that is uncommon in this literature. Table 6 provides a clean ablation isolating the contribution of each loss term.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **No quantitative comparison with any prior face reconstruction method**: The paper surveys prior work (Shahreza et al. 2022a; Otroshi Shahreza & Marcel 2024; Shahreza & Marcel 2024a,b) in Section 2.2 but never reports their performance under the same evaluation protocol. For the FR models tested (IRSE50, IR152), prior methods exist and could be compared directly. For PPFR models, the mapping approach itself could be compared against a simple linear baseline or the GAN-based mapping of Otroshi Shahreza & Marcel (2024) retrained on PPFR embeddings. Without any such baseline, the reader cannot assess whether the proposed FEMs advance the state of the art. The paper's only baseline ("without any embedding mapping" — line 166) is trivial and merely shows that embedding spaces differ. This omission undermines the central claim that the method improves on prior work.

- **Uncontrolled KAN vs. MLP comparison**: The two variants use different optimizers (SGD for FEM-KAN, AdamW for FEM-MLP) and different learning rates (10⁻² vs. 10⁻³), as stated in line 143. This confound makes it impossible to attribute any performance difference to the network architecture rather than training hyperparameters. Moreover, FEM-MLP *outperforms* FEM-KAN on two of the six target models (IRSE50: 66.5 vs. 65.1; IR152: 63.2 vs. 62.2), which is acknowledged only indirectly. The paper frames KAN superiority as a key contribution (line 31), but the evidence is weakened by the uncontrolled setup and inconsistent results.

- **Potential identity leakage in the main experiment**: The training set consists of 5000 synthetic images generated by Arc2Face from a CelebA-HQ subset (line 133). The main evaluation (Table 1) is performed on CelebA-HQ itself. The paper does not state whether training and testing identities are disjoint. If the same identities appear in both, the mapping network may learn identity-specific mappings rather than a generalizable transformation. The generalization experiment (FFHQ→Synth-500) partially addresses this concern, but the core results in Table 1 remain questionable without clarification.

### Minor

- **Trivial baseline**: The only baseline is "without any embedding mapping" — i.e., feeding the raw target embedding directly into IPA-FaceID. A linear mapping or a simple one-layer projection would be a far more informative comparison and would help separate the value of any learned mapping from the value of the specific KAN/MLP architecture.
  
- **No error bars or statistical significance**: All results are reported as single numbers without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multiple-seed averages. Given that ASR is a proportion, this makes it impossible to assess the reliability of the observed differences (e.g., whether the 1.4% gap on IRSE50 is meaningful).

- **Limited hyperparameter reporting for KAN**: The paper states "follow efficient kan implementation" (line 133) but does not specify grid size, spline order, or number of KAN layers. These are standard parameters for KAN and their absence hurts reproducibility.

- **Loss weight sensitivity**: The joint loss uses empirically chosen weights (λ₁=1, λ₂=0.5, λ₃=10) with no ablation or sensitivity analysis showing how these choices affect results.

- **High FID values on out-of-distribution data**: FEM-KAN achieves FID of 221.1 on Synth-500 (Table 3), indicating poor distributional match. The paper notes this and cautions that quality metrics may not align with visual similarity, which is reasonable — but the high FID still tempers the generalization claims.

## Removed Points

- **"First to invert face embedding from PPFR models is contradicted by Shahreza & Marcel (2024b)"** — Removed. Shahreza & Marcel (2024b) reconstructs from *protected embeddings* (PolyProtect, MLP-Hash), which are post-hoc protection mechanisms applied to embeddings. The paper's PPFR models (DCTDP, HFCF, PartialFace) modify the entire face-processing pipeline, which is a distinct setting. The paper explicitly scopes this distinction and the claim is defensible.

- **"The paper does not discuss cases where MLP outperforms KAN"** — Removed. The paper states "FEM-KAN has overall better performance than MLP-based FEM in general, **especially for target PPFR models**" (line 166), which implicitly acknowledges that MLP can win on FR models. The core issue (different training recipes) is retained as a Major weakness.

- **"Attack scenario realism — white-box vs. black-box"** — Removed as a weakness. The paper's threat model (adversary can extract embeddings and train a mapping) is standard in this literature and explicitly described. This is not a flaw; it is a clearly stated assumption.

## Suggestions

1. **Add at least one quantitative baseline from prior work.** The GAN-based mapping of Otroshi Shahreza & Marcel (2024) retrained on the same PPFR embeddings would be the most natural comparison. Even a simple linear mapping (a single fully-connected layer) would be far more informative than the current "no mapping" baseline.

2. **Match training recipes for FEM-KAN and FEM-MLP**: use the same optimizer, learning rate schedule, and number of parameters/computation. Report results across multiple seeds with standard deviations. This is essential to support any architectural claim.

3. **Clarify identity separation in the CelebA-HQ experiment**: state explicitly whether training and testing identities are disjoint in Table 1. If they are not, retrain with separated identities or use a different training set (e.g., FFHQ or VGGFace2) to validate the main results.

4. **Report KAN-specific hyperparameters** (grid size, spline order, layer widths) explicitly rather than deferring to an external implementation.

5. **Add a sensitivity analysis for the loss weights** (λ values) and include more ablation configurations beyond three.
