## Summary

This paper contributes DeepFakeVox-HQ, a large-scale deepfake audio dataset (1.3M total samples, 270k high-quality deepfakes from 14 sources), and F-SAT, a frequency-selective adversarial training method that restricts perturbations to specified frequency ranges (optimized at 4–8 kHz). The dataset is genuinely larger and more diverse than prior benchmarks, and the experimental results show that F-SAT + RandAugment improves both clean accuracy and robustness over baselines trained on the same data.

## Strengths

- **Large-scale, diverse dataset.** DeepFakeVox-HQ is the largest public deepfake audio dataset reported to date, with 1.3M samples, 40 generation models (TTS + VC), and coverage of both clean and noisy conditions. This directly addresses an acknowledged limitation of existing small/homogeneous benchmarks (Table 1, Section 3).

- **F-SAT shows clear robustness gains over baselines.** Under identical training conditions (all models trained on DeepFakeVox-HQ), F-SAT + RandAug raises attacked fake accuracy from 6.5% (RawNet3) to 87.0% for time-domain attacks and from 17.9% to 92.8% for frequency-domain attacks, while maintaining or improving clean accuracy (Table 2/ablation). The clean/robust trade-off is better than full-frequency or time-domain adversarial training.

- **Comprehensive robustness evaluation across 24 corruptions.** Figure 3(a) compares the proposed method against baselines on a broad set of audio corruptions, showing consistent improvements (average +15.3 pp). This level of coverage is uncommon in deepfake audio detection papers.

- **Systematic ablation of frequency range selection.** Table 3 compares four frequency ranges (0–8, 2–8, 4–8, 6–8 kHz) for F-SAT and identifies 4–8 kHz as optimal, supported by quantitative comparison. This provides practical guidance for applying the method.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Central motivation contradicts the paper's own evidence.** The paper repeatedly claims that "state-of-the-art AI-voice detection models often depend on high-frequency features" and that "low frequency signals ... are not relied on by the model to make predictions" (lines 35–37, echoed in the Conclusion). The sole evidence for this is Figure 2, which applies a **high-pass filter** (removes low frequencies, keeps high frequencies). Performance drops when the cutoff exceeds 4 kHz (real) and 6 kHz (fake), meaning the model **needs frequencies below those cutoffs** — i.e., it relies on low-to-mid frequencies, not high frequencies. The paper gets this causal direction backwards. This is not a minor framing issue: the stated motivation for F-SAT — that models depend on imperceptible high-frequency features that attackers can easily manipulate — is built on an incorrect reading of the authors' own figure. The method may still be effective for other reasons (vulnerability of high frequencies to attack, as separately shown in Figure 5(b)), but the paper's core narrative is unsupported as written. The authors should reframe the motivation around vulnerability rather than reliance.

- **Claim of state-of-the-art results is unsupported.** The paper states "Our method achieved state-of-the-art results across all three benchmarks" (line 229). However, all models (including baselines) are trained on DeepFakeVox-HQ and tested on ASVspoof2019 and WaveFake — this is a cross-dataset evaluation, not the standard benchmark protocol. Published SOTA numbers for ASVspoof2019 (e.g., RawNet3 trained on the ASVspoof2019 training set and evaluated under the standard LA protocol) are not cited or compared. The results show that the authors' full pipeline outperforms other architectures trained on the same dataset, which is a valid ablation but does not establish SOTA against published results on those benchmarks. To make such a claim, the paper must either use the standard training splits or explicitly compare to published results using the same evaluation protocol and acknowledge the trade-offs of a different training distribution.

### Minor

- **Reproducibility gaps in attack specifications.** The paper describes F-SAT's iterative update (Eq. 9) but does not specify the attack algorithm used (PGD? FGSM? C&W?), the norm constraint (L∞? L2?), the number of iterations K, or the step size α for the experiments in Figures 6–7 and Tables 4–5. The hyperparameter analysis (Figure 8) states ε=0.01 for one experiment but omits these other critical details. Robustness claims depend entirely on these parameters.

- **Missing training configuration details.** The paper does not specify the optimizer, learning rate, number of epochs, batch size, train/validation split strategy, or training seed(s) for any of the baselines or the proposed method. This limits reproducibility.

- **Single-run results without confidence intervals.** All reported accuracies are single values with no indication of variance across multiple runs or seeds. For test sets of ~3k–6k samples, a difference of 1–2 percentage points may not be statistically significant.

- **Metric choice limits comparability.** The paper reports only per-class accuracy. The field standard for speaker verification / deepfake detection benchmarks (ASVspoof) is equal error rate (EER), and AUC is common for detection tasks. Adding these metrics would facilitate direct comparison with prior work.

### Trivial

- **Figure 2 caption terminology.** The caption refers to "center frequency" for what should be a cutoff frequency on a high-pass filter. This is ambiguous and should be corrected.

- **"40 vocoders" vs. "top seven TTS models."** Table 1 lists 40 vocoders in the training set, but the text (line 94) mentions only 7 TTS models by name. The relationship between "vocoders" (Table 1) and the described TTS models is unclear and should be reconciled.

- **Overclaim in conclusion.** The concluding sentence claims to "pioneer robust training for detecting fake audio for the first time," which is overstated given existing work on adversarial training for audio (cited in the paper's own related work section, lines 61–63).

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation not fully articulated by the paper is that the method works *despite* the flawed motivation. F-SAT targets high frequencies (4–8 kHz) for adversarial training, and it works well — but the stated reason (models "depend on" high frequencies) is contradicted by the paper's own high-pass filter experiment. A more consistent reading is: (i) primary discriminative features for deepfake detection lie below 4–6 kHz (the high-pass filter shows this), (ii) high frequencies are a *weakness* because they can be attacked without affecting those core features, and (iii) F-SAT inoculates against attacks at this vulnerability while leaving the core low-frequency features untouched. The paper would be stronger if it made this distinction explicit rather than conflating vulnerability with reliance.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the motivation.** Replace the claim that models "depend on high-frequency features" with a vulnerability-based argument: high frequencies are easily perturbed by attackers yet unimportant for human perception, so protecting them via selective adversarial training is beneficial. Reconcile this with what the high-pass filter experiment actually shows.
2. **Remove or qualify the SOTA claim.** Either report results under standard benchmark protocols (ASVspoof2019 LA), or explicitly frame the results as cross-dataset evaluation and drop the claim of achieving SOTA on the original benchmarks.
3. **Specify all attack parameters** (algorithm, norm, iterations, step size) and standard training hyperparameters in the main text or a clearly marked appendix.
4. **Add EER/AUC** to the main results table, and report results from at least 3 random seeds.
