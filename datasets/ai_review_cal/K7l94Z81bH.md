## Summary

This paper proposes RLD3, a multi-agent reinforcement learning algorithm for designated driver dispatch that addresses three sparsity challenges (dataset sparsity, feedback sparsity, and cross-effect sparsity) through group-sharing network parameters, a dense neighborhood potential reward, and a window-lasting policy ensemble. The authors build a simulator from real-world Hangzhou data and compare against DRL and optimization baselines, with ablation studies that isolate each component.

## Strengths

- **Tailored Dec-POMDP formulation for designated driving** (Section 3.1): The formulation explicitly captures the unique "hub-and-spoke" order structure, slow driver movement, and heterogeneous constraints that distinguish designated driving from prior ride-hailing Dec-POMDPs. This is the first such formalization for this specific problem domain.

- **Group-sharing architecture with ablation confirmation** (Section 3.2, Table 2): Drivers are grouped by cost conditions, sharing parameters and replay buffers within each group. The ablation study directly shows that removing this structure degrades performance for both MADDPG and RLD3, providing clear evidence that the design improves learning under data sparsity and driver heterogeneity.

- **Dense neighborhood potential reward with causal evidence** (Section 3.3, Table 2): The inverse-root potential reward (Eq. 4) provides dense feedback at every timestep based on distances to nearby orders. The ablation confirms that removing it reduces completed orders and destabilizes training (much larger value-function loss), demonstrating its role in overcoming feedback sparsity.

- **Window-lasting interaction mechanism with ablation support** (Section 3.4, Table 2): The time-averaged action over the last W idle periods captures the lasting distributional impact of slow-moving drivers. The ablation shows that omitting this significantly hurts performance, confirming its value over single-step interaction models.

- **Real-world simulator** (Section 4.1): Built from Hangzhou datasets with ~3,000 drivers and ~13,000 orders per day, providing a realistic testbed beyond synthetic environments common in prior MARL fleet management work.

- **Empirical superiority over multiple baselines** (Figure 4, Table 1): RLD3 consistently outperforms four DRL baselines (DDPG, MADDPG, MAMFRL, RND) and two optimization-based methods (Max-throughput, MPC) on completed orders while maintaining smaller moving distances, with the gains persisting on IID generalization tests.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Experimental scale mismatch undermines the sparsity motivation.** The paper's central narrative is that designated driving suffers from *extreme* sparsity: ~3,000 drivers and ~13,000 orders/day in the real data (ratio ≈ 1 driver per 4.3 orders). Yet the experiments are conducted with only **50 drivers and 500 orders** (ratio ≈ 1 driver per 10 orders). This more-than-doubles the orders-per-driver ratio, making the problem *denser* than the real setting along this axis, not sparser. The core technical contributions (potential reward, window-lasting) are motivated by sparsity, but evaluating on a drastically downscaled and comparatively denser environment does not demonstrate that these techniques are effective at realistic sparsity levels. The paper provides no justification for this downsampling and no discussion of how the reported benefits might or might not transfer to the actual scale. This is a significant gap between the paper's motivating premise and its experimental evidence.

### Minor

- **Group construction is not specified.** The paper states that drivers "can be classified into several types based on their cost conditions" (line 94) and sets the group number M=5, but never describes how groups are actually assigned—whether via thresholds on mileage limits, k-means on driver features, or some other procedure. The future work section acknowledges the desire to make grouping trainable (line 254), confirming this is currently an open design choice. Without this detail, the "heterogeneity" claim is difficult to assess or reproduce.

- **No sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters.** Several design choices are made without justification or ablation: window size W=60 (≈30 min), group number M=5, potential reward exponent of -0.5. The ablation study compares the presence/absence of each component but never varies the parameters within a component. This makes it hard to know whether performance is brittle or robust to these choices.

- **Temporal difference regularization term (H(a,a')) is introduced but not ablated.** The critic loss includes a regularization term H(a,a') = ‖a−a'‖₂ to smooth driver trajectories (line 153). This is a design choice whose effect is not isolated in the ablation study—only "w/o potential reward" and "w/o cost" are tested. An ablation comparing the full model with and without this term would clarify its importance.

- **Baseline hyperparameter fairness is unclear.** All DRL baselines use the same architecture (2×64 hidden layers), batch size, and learning rate (line 198). While common practice, no hyperparameter search is reported for the baselines, so it is unclear whether the comparison reflects method quality or suboptimal tuning of the baselines. This concern is somewhat mitigated by the fact that the same architecture clearly works for RLD3 (which shares the same base), but a statement about per-baseline tuning would strengthen confidence.

### Trivial

- **Optimization baselines are underspecified.** Max-throughput and MPC are cited but not described in sufficient detail to assess whether they are properly instantiated for the designated driving setting. A brief summary or specific reference to the implementation would improve reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Scaling experiments:** Run the method at an intermediate scale (e.g., 500 drivers, 5,000 orders) or at a scale where the orders-per-driver ratio matches the real data (~4.3:1) to directly test whether the techniques address sparsity at the claimed level. If computational constraints prevent this, an explicit discussion of the trade-off would help.
- **Per-seed results or visible confidence intervals:** If the tables/figures do not already display variance, showing per-seed results or error bars would allow readers to assess statistical significance, especially given only 4 seeds.
- **OOD generalization:** The IID generalization test uses the same initial driver states and underlying order distribution. A true out-of-distribution test (changed hotspot patterns, different city zones) would strengthen claims of robustness.
- **Group-formation ablation:** Compare the manual grouping used (unspecified) against alternatives (random assignment, k-means on driver features) to verify that the grouping scheme is meaningful.

## Suggestions

1. **Address the scale mismatch directly.** Acknowledge the downsampling from 3,000→50 drivers and 13,000→500 orders, explain the rationale (computational limits, convergence stability), and provide at least one experiment at an intermediate scale (e.g., 500 drivers, 2,000–5,000 orders) or with a sparsity ratio comparable to the real setting (~4.3 orders/driver). Even a single scalability point showing that the benefits of the components persist under sparser conditions would substantially strengthen the paper's central narrative.
2. **Specify the group-construction procedure** (even if simple, e.g., thresholds on mileage limits) and add a brief sensitivity analysis for group number M.
3. **Add a brief sensitivity analysis** for window size W and, if possible, the potential reward exponent, to show that performance is not brittle to these choices.
4. **Include an ablation for the H(a,a') regularization term** to isolate its contribution.
5. **Clarify whether baselines were tuned** and if so, report the search range and best settings.
