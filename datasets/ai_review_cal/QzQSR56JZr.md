## Summary

This paper introduces MALLS (34K NL-FOL pairs generated by GPT-4) and LogicLLaMA (a LLaMA-7B model fine-tuned with LoRA) for natural language to first-order logic translation. The key methodological contribution is a novel SFT+RLHF training framework: the model is first fine-tuned on synthetically perturbed FOL rules with ground-truth chain-of-thought correction steps, then further refined via RLHF using a logical equivalence (LE) metric (with BLEU) as the reward signal. On the FOLIO benchmark, LogicLLaMA with RLHF CoT correction achieves 0.849 LE vs. GPT-4 5-shot at 0.855, and outperforms GPT-3.5 5-shot (0.767), at a fraction of the cost.

## Strengths

- **Novel SFT+RLHF framework using synthetic perturbations to generate CoT training data (Section 4.2.1).** The paper identifies that ground-truth CoT steps are unavailable for real GPT-3.5 outputs and addresses this by systematically perturbing ground-truth FOL rules (Table 2; 12 atomic operations across label change, insert, delete) to create synthetic correction trajectories. The perturbation process includes negative examples ("No changes needed," 20% probability) to penalize over-correction. This is a concrete methodological advance for training corrective models without human-annotated CoT steps.

- **Large, diverse NL-FOL dataset MALLS (34K pairs) from GPT-4 with a careful prompting pipeline (Section 3).** Table 1 shows MALLS is substantially larger and more diverse than FOLIO (2K) and LogicNLI (12K), with 22.7K vocabulary size, 16.1 avg NL words, and 4.6 avg literals. The dynamic prompting pipeline (N-gram frequency tracking, FOL configuration prompts, break-down prompt for over-complicated predicates) demonstrably produces more diverse and structurally varied FOL rules than existing datasets.

- **Competitive performance at reduced cost.** Table 3 shows RLHF CoT correction (0.849 LE) approaching GPT-4 5-shot (0.855 LE) on FOLIO, while significantly outperforming GPT-3.5 5-shot (0.767). The Direct Translation mode (0.818 LE) also beats GPT-3.5 5-shot. The paper reports pricing ($0.002/1K tokens for GPT-3.5 vs. $0.03–$0.06/1K for GPT-4), supporting the cost-efficiency claim.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The most severe criticism raised — that the LE metric is fundamentally unsound for FOL with quantifiers — does not invalidate the paper's core claims when examined carefully. The LE metric is a propositional truth-table approximation applied to FOL formulas. Quantifiers remain present in the formula structure when the truth table is computed; the limitation is that the domain is implicitly treated as single-element. This means the metric would not distinguish ∀x P(x) from ∃x P(x) when the literal set is identical. However, all methods (GPT models and LogicLLaMA variants) are evaluated with the same metric, so relative comparisons remain meaningful. Additionally, the paper already acknowledges the challenge (C2) and the greedy binding strategy for mismatched literal sets. The metric is imperfect and overclaimed (see below), but not "fatal" in the sense of invalidating all comparative results.

### Major

- **The paper overclaims the LE metric by calling it a "logical equivalence solver" (lines 196, 287) when it is a propositional approximation that does not fully handle quantifier semantics.** The metric treats each literal (e.g., `Country(x)`, `InEU(x)`) as an atomic Boolean proposition and computes truth-table overlap. This approach implicitly assumes a single-element domain, collapsing the distinction between ∀ and ∃ when they bind the same literal set. The paper should transparently discuss (1) that this is an approximate similarity measure, not a rigorous FOL equivalence check, and (2) under what conditions it could give misleading scores. The core comparative results (LogicLLaMA vs. GPT-3.5/4) are still informative because the same metric is applied uniformly, but the absolute LE scores should not be interpreted as true logical equivalence rates.

- **No ablation of the reward components or the mixing ratio ω.** The combined reward is LE (ω=0.7) + BLEU (1-ω=0.3), but the paper does not ablate (a) LE-only, (b) BLEU-only, or (c) different ω values. Without this, the individual contributions of each component to the final RLHF performance cannot be assessed, and the choice ω=0.7 is not justified.

### Minor

- **No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests reported.** Given that the gap between RLHF CoT (0.849) and Naive Correction (0.840) or GPT-4 0-shot (0.799) is relatively small, the stability of these numbers is unclear. Single-run evaluation is common in LLM work due to API costs, but at minimum a bootstrap or discussion of variance would strengthen the claims.

- **Limited comparison with non-LLM neural NL-FOL systems.** The paper compares only against GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. Several neural NL-FOL translation systems are cited in Related Work (e.g., Hahn et al. 2022, Lu et al. 2022) but are not included as baselines. While re-implementing these may be non-trivial, their absence makes it harder to contextualize LLM-based vs. traditional semantic parsing approaches.

- **No human evaluation to validate that LE scores correlate with human-judged correctness.** Given the LE metric's limitations, a small-scale human evaluation on a subset of FOLIO would strengthen the validity of the reported scores.

- **The "break-down prompt" (line 106) for over-complicated predicates is not quantitatively evaluated.** It is unclear how many GPT-4 generations required this prompt, how often it improves quality, or whether it sometimes introduces errors.

### Trivial

- The LE metric could more accurately be described as "truth-table similarity" or "propositional approximation of logical equivalence" rather than "logical equivalence" throughout the paper.

## Nice-to-Haves

## Novel Insights

The two reviews offer competing framings of the LE metric problem. The harsh critic treats it as a fatal, theory-level flaw that invalidates all results; the strength finder treats it as a principled contribution. The genuine insight from reconciling them is that the paper has a *disconnect between rhetoric and reality* in the LE metric: the name and framing ("logical equivalence solver") imply a rigorous formal check, while the actual computation is a pragmatic propositional approximation. This disconnect is real and should be fixed, but it does not undermine the paper's practical contributions (dataset, training framework, relative performance comparisons). None of the other reviewer disagreements yield qualitatively new observations beyond what the paper itself already states.

## Suggestions

1. **Rename the metric.** Replace "logical equivalence (LE)" with a more honest name such as "truth-table similarity (TTS)" or "propositional equivalence approximation (PEA)." Add a paragraph explicitly discussing what the metric captures and what it misses (quantifier semantics, domain dependence). This single change would dissolve the strongest criticism.
2. **Add an ablation study of the reward components.** Report LE-only, BLEU-only, and the mixed reward at ω=0.7 and one or two other values (e.g., ω=0.5, ω=0.9). This would clarify the contribution of each component.
3. **Report bootstrapped confidence intervals** for the main FOLIO results, or at minimum acknowledge that differences <0.01 may be within noise.
4. **Consider adding a small-scale human evaluation** on a random subset of FOLIO (50–100 examples) to validate that LE scores align with human judgments of translation correctness.
5. **Quantify the "break-down prompt" effectiveness** by reporting how many GPT-4 generations triggered it and a qualitative assessment of whether it improved quality.
