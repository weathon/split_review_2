## Summary

This paper introduces a controlled experimental framework using strings generated by formal grammars (random and hierarchical) to study the distinction between memorization (learning by rote) and generalization (learning with understanding) in LLMs. By fine-tuning Pythia-1B, Llama3-8B, and Mistral-Nemo-12B on grammar-generated strings and tracking training/test loss divergence, the paper identifies two phases (generalization and memorization) that overlap, shows that the two learning modes are at odds (memorization harms generalization and vice versa), and demonstrates that lower-entropy distributions are easier to generalize to but harder to memorize. The framework is methodologically clean and the research question is well-motivated.

## Strengths

1. **Clean, well-controlled experimental framework.** The use of formal grammars (random and hierarchical) to generate training and test data is a genuinely useful methodological contribution. It allows precise control over distributional properties, ensures training data is unseen during pretraining, and provides ground-truth test sets from the same distribution — none of which is possible with natural language. Figure 1 cleanly demonstrates that the framework separates grammar learning from random memorization.

2. **Operational definition of learning phases.** The paper defines the start of memorization (test loss exceeding training loss by >5%) and end of generalization (minimum test loss) in a concrete, reproducible way. Figure 2 shows these markers consistently across three model families and two dataset sizes, providing a practical tool for studying learning dynamics.

3. **Demonstration of adversarial interplay between memorization and generalization.** Figure 4 shows that (a) once the memorization phase begins, further training reduces training loss while increasing test loss, and (b) switching to a new dataset causes the model to forget previously memorized data (with a transient "re-generalization" to the test set). The latter sub-observation — that test loss briefly decreases when switching datasets — is the more novel element and deserves further analysis.

4. **Entropy results are the strongest contribution.** Section 5 systematically varies entropy through three independent methods (alphabet size, oversampling, production rule skew) and consistently finds that lower-entropy distributions are easier to generalize to but harder to memorize, and vice versa for higher-entropy distributions (Figure 5). This finding holds across all three model families, making it the paper's most robust and informative result.

5. **Validation across model families.** Results are shown for Pythia-1B, Llama3-8B, and Mistral-Nemo-12B, spanning an order of magnitude in parameter count. The consistency of the observed phenomena across architectures and scales strengthens the empirical basis.

## Weaknesses

### Major
None. The core claims are supported by the experiments, and no verified weakness invalidates them.

### Minor

1. **The 5% divergence threshold for defining "start of memorization" is used without robustness analysis.** The paper relies on a single threshold (test loss > 1.05 × training loss) to identify when memorization begins. No justification is given for this specific value, and the overlap between generalization and memorization phases could be partially an artifact of this choice. The paper should show that the qualitative conclusions (phase overlap, ordering of phase boundaries across conditions) hold for a range of thresholds (e.g., 2%, 10%).

2. **Section 4's "surprising" framing overlooks known phenomena.** The observation that memorizing new data causes forgetting of previously memorized data is presented as surprising ("This result is surprising and points to potential differences between human and machine cognition"). However, this is broadly consistent with catastrophic forgetting effects studied extensively in continual learning. The paper would benefit from contextualizing this finding within existing knowledge and focusing analytical attention on the more genuinely novel sub-observation: the brief "re-generalization" (test loss dip) when switching datasets, which is mentioned but not deeply analyzed.

3. **The paper uses pretrained models without discussing how pretraining shapes the results.** The paper fine-tunes Pythia, Llama, and Mistral on grammar strings but does not discuss how pretrained representations (e.g., token-level priors, learned positional patterns) might influence the observed dynamics. For instance, models already have priors about character distributions and sequential patterns that could help or hurt grammar learning. A brief discussion of this limitation would strengthen the paper.

4. **The random grammar setting conflates "understanding" with distribution matching.** For the random grammar (length-64 uniform strings), generalization on the test set largely reflects matching the marginal distribution rather than learning structured rules. Calling any improvement on test data "learning with understanding" overinterprets what is essentially distribution matching. The paper would benefit from acknowledging this and focusing the "understanding" claim on the hierarchical grammar, where structural generalization is meaningful.

5. **Forgetting and entropy experiments use small training sets (n=8).** Section 4 (sequential memorization) and Section 5 (entropy) use only 8 training samples. While this is appropriate for demonstrating the phenomena, it is unclear whether the qualitative trends hold for larger training sets. Adding even one larger-scale condition (e.g., n=64 or n=256) for the forgetting experiment would substantially strengthen the claims.

6. **The re-generalization effect (test loss dip when switching datasets) may be a small effect; statistical significance is not reported.** In Figure 4, the test loss appears to decrease briefly when switching to the second dataset. The magnitude of this dip is modest, and no significance test or variance estimate is provided. Without this, it is unclear whether the effect is reliable or noise.

7. **Tone toward prior work is unnecessarily confrontational.** The paper states that prior works "erred" (line 33) and describes their definitions as "overly conservative/liberal" without acknowledging that those definitions were designed for specific purposes (e.g., Carlini et al.'s 50-token accuracy threshold was designed for high-precision privacy auditing, not for characterizing learning dynamics). A more measured discussion would improve the paper without weakening its contribution.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Robustness analysis across multiple thresholds for defining memorization onset (2%, 5%, 10%).
- At least one experiment with a larger training set (n ≥ 64) in the forgetting and entropy settings to test scalability.
- A small-scale natural-language analog (e.g., an artificial language with known syntax) to partially bridge the gap to real data.
- Statistical significance tests or error bars for the re-generalization observation.
- Discussion of how pretraining may affect the observed dynamics.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's observation that the "re-generalization" phenomenon (brief test loss decrease when switching datasets in Section 4) is the more genuinely novel element of the sequential memorization experiment, rather than the forgetting itself, is insightful and not fully exploited by the paper. The entropy results remain the strongest contribution and are relatively understated in the paper's own framing. A second observation: the paper's demonstration that two models with identical training loss can be in fundamentally different learning phases (Figure 3) cleanly exposes a gap in recollection-based auditing approaches that the paper could leverage more explicitly as a practical caution rather than a theoretical impossibility.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a robustness analysis** for the 5% threshold used to define memorization onset. Show that qualitative conclusions hold across 2%, 5%, and 10% thresholds.
2. **Reframe the forgetting result** (Section 4) with appropriate context from continual learning, and shift analytical emphasis to the re-generalization behavior as the novel finding.
3. **Soften the language** around prior work ("erred," "impossible") to acknowledge that different definitions serve different purposes. This would not weaken the paper's contributions.
4. **Add a discussion** of how using pretrained models (rather than training from scratch) might influence the results.
5. **Clarify what "understanding" means** for the random grammar case (where it primarily reflects distribution matching) vs. the hierarchical grammar (where it reflects structural generalization).
