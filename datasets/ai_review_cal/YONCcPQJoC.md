## Summary

The paper proposes fine-tuning LLMs with auxiliary tasks that involve synthetic intermediate planning steps (summary, outline, key information), enabling single-turn generation of structured long documents. To overcome the scarcity of training data for these intermediate steps, the authors use LLMs to generate synthetic intermediate writing data from existing full articles. Experiments on SciNews and Wikipedia datasets show consistent improvements in ROUGE scores over fine-tuning without intermediate steps, and human side-by-side evaluation shows a strong win rate on SciNews (3.60 W/L ratio).

## Strengths

- **Consistent gains across two diverse datasets and the most important baseline**: Table 1 shows that FT w/ I outperforms fine-tuning without intermediate steps (FT w/o I) on both SciNews (RLsum 44.14 → 46.61, +2.47) and Wikipedia (RLsum 41.28 → 45.67, +4.39) across all four ROUGE variants. The FT w/o I baseline is the critical ablation — it isolates the effect of adding planning steps to the fine-tuning pipeline — and the gains are clear and consistent.

- **Human SxS evaluation shows substantial perceived-quality improvements on SciNews**: Table 4 reports expert human SxS on 50 SciNews items with an overall W/L ratio of 3.60, with particularly strong wins in verifiability (7.75) and coherence & organization (4.25). These ratios are far from the 1.0 tie line, suggesting perceptually meaningful differences.

- **Generality across base models**: Table 6 replicates the main result on Gemini 1.5 Flash, showing the same pattern (R1 45.38 → 46.53, RLsum 42.62 → 43.77), confirming the benefit is not specific to one model.

- **Clean ablation of training mixtures**: Table 2 systematically compares different combinations of training tasks and inference configurations, demonstrating that the two-task mixture ($x_i → y_i$; $x_i → \bz_i ⊕ y_i$) with inference that also outputs intermediate steps works best, and showing that adding the third task ($x_i ⊕ \bz_i → y_i$) yields diminishing returns — an informative negative result.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

None that threaten the paper's core claims. The method's central thesis — that adding synthetic planning steps through auxiliary training tasks improves single-turn generation — is well-supported by the experimental evidence.

### Minor

- **The sinusoidal scoring function g(·) in Algorithm 1 is never defined**: The paper states it uses "a sinusoidal scoring function g(·) derived from empirical observations" (Section 4.2) to compute LengthScore, but the actual function is not specified. Since the entire synthetic data construction pipeline for the auxiliary tasks depends on this scoring function to select high-quality intermediate steps, the pipeline is not fully reproducible. This is the most substantive reproducibility gap in the paper.

- **Human evaluation sample size is modest and lacks significance reporting**: The human SxS uses only 50 items per dataset with no confidence intervals, significance tests, or inter-annotator agreement reported. The SciNews W/L of 3.60 is strong enough that the conclusion is unlikely to flip with a larger sample, but the Wikipedia W/L of 1.56 is much closer to parity and could easily be within noise. The paper's abstract states "clear wins in organization, relevance, and verifiability" without qualification, which overstates the Wikipedia evidence.

- **The Wikipedia human evaluation uses a suboptimal model variant**: For the Wikipedia human SxS, the paper selects the three-task training mixture even though Table 2 shows the two-task mixture achieves notably higher ROUGE scores (RLsum 45.67 vs. 43.92). The paper says the three-task mixture is "comparable," but the two-task mixture is clearly better across all four metrics. This choice means the Wikipedia human evaluation likely underestimates the method's true potential on that dataset.

- **No analysis of synthetic intermediate step quality**: The paper does not evaluate whether the synthetic summaries, outlines, and key information generated by the pipeline are actually good — e.g., a human evaluation of the synthetic steps against human-written ones, or an ablation where the scoring function is removed. Since the method's effectiveness rests on these synthetic steps being reasonable training signals, some validation of the data quality would strengthen the paper.

- **Abstract's "single auxiliary task" framing is imprecise**: The abstract states "Our main novelty lies in a single auxiliary task," but Section 4.3 introduces "two auxiliary fine-tuning tasks" ($x_i → \bz_i ⊕ y_i$ and $x_i ⊕ \bz_i → y_i$). While one could charitably read "single auxiliary task" as "a single type of auxiliary supervision" (planning), the wording is inconsistent with the actual method and should be corrected.

### Trivial

- The paper does not report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests for any metric (ROUGE scores, human SxS, auto SxS). For the main ROUGE results this is less concerning because the gains are consistent across configurations, but it would improve rigor.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Non-RAG multi-turn baseline**: The paper positions single-turn generation as a simpler alternative to multi-turn systems. While the comparison in Table 3 (single-turn vs. the same model used sequentially) is informative, a comparison against a simple two-stage baseline (e.g., prompt the model to generate an outline, then prompt it to write the article from the outline) would more directly test whether the single-turn planning approach adds value beyond explicit multi-turn decomposition. This is not a weakness that invalidates the core claim — the FT w/o I baseline already establishes the value of the planning training — but it would strengthen the paper's framing.

- **Qualitative analysis**: A case study showing an input, the generated intermediate steps, and the final article would help readers understand what the model learns to do internally.

## Novel Insights

Both reviews independently identify the same key tension: the paper's empirical story is clear and coherent (planning training → better generation), but some framing and presentation choices weaken the narrative. The harsh critic's "missing baseline" critique is overblown given the paper's actual scope, but the concerns about the underspecified scoring function and the modest human evaluation sample are well-founded and point to genuine areas for improvement. An interesting observation is that the three-task mixture hurts performance — the paper speculates this is because "writing an article given the outlines is a relatively trivial task" — but this suggests the synthetic intermediate steps are most useful as a training signal when the model must generate them itself, not when they are provided as input.

## Suggestions

1. **Define g(·) in Algorithm 1** or replace it with a simple well-specified heuristic (e.g., a Gaussian centered on a target length ratio). This is the single change that would most improve reproducibility.

2. **Add significance testing to the human evaluation** (e.g., a binomial test against the null of equal quality) and report the raw win/tie/loss counts alongside the ratios. Clarify that the abstract's "clear wins" language applies primarily to SciNews, not Wikipedia.

3. **Revisit the Wikipedia human evaluation**: Either use the best-performing two-task mixture, or explain more clearly why the three-task variant was chosen despite lower ROUGE scores.

4. **Invest 2–3 sentences on validating the synthetic data**: E.g., a small human rating of whether the extracted outlines/summaries are faithful, or a comparison of selected vs. randomly-picked candidates on downstream task performance.

5. **Correct the "single auxiliary task" phrase in the abstract** to match Section 4.3 where two auxiliary tasks are described.
