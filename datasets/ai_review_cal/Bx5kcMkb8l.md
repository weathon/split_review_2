## Summary

This paper proposes using LLMs with manually designed or automatically generated medical prompts for PROM (premature rupture of membranes) risk prediction from a 7,199-participant cohort with 78 factors. The main contributions are: (1) a hierarchical prompt design with Chain-of-Thought, (2) automatic prompt generation via MLM-driven, Bio-QA, and hybrid pipelines augmented with a Shapley-value-based factor interaction map, and (3) evaluation across 10+ open-source LLMs. The paper reports 79% accuracy with all 78 factors and 96% accuracy with a 40-factor subset, claiming superiority over supervised baselines.

## Strengths

1. **Broad evaluation across diverse LLM architectures**: The paper compares its approach across 10+ open-source models spanning different sizes (7B–405B), architectures (dense, MoE), and medical specializations (Meditron, BioMistral, Med42, PMC-Llama, etc.) under zero-shot, few-shot, and fine-tuned settings. This multi-model scope is more comprehensive than typical single-model evaluations in prompt-engineering papers (Section 4.4, lines 185, 197).

2. **Automatic prompt generation pipelines that reduce reliance on manual expert annotation**: Section 3.4–3.5 introduces three methods for generating prompts without manual effort: MLM-driven (using PubMedBERT), Bio-QA-based (using OpenBioLLM), and a hybrid. The paper reports that autogenerated prompts (especially hybrid and MLM-driven) approach the performance of manually designed prompts, which addresses a practical bottleneck in deploying LLMs for cohort analysis (lines 213–216, Figure 3).

3. **Data efficiency advantage over classical logistic regression**: Figure 4 compares the LLM-based method with logistic regression across varying training sample sizes, showing the proposed method maintains higher accuracy with fewer samples — a practical benefit for medical settings with limited labeled data (lines 205–208).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

1. **Only accuracy is reported on a substantially imbalanced medical dataset, making the headline performance numbers uninterpretable.** The dataset has 1,483 PROM cases out of 7,199 participants (≈20.6% prevalence, line 176). A trivial classifier that always predicts "no PROM" achieves 79.4% accuracy. The paper's flagship 78-factor result (79% accuracy, line 4) is *indistinguishable from (in fact slightly below)* this no-skill baseline. The 40-factor result (96%) is well above baseline, but without any per-class metrics — sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, AUC, F1 — it is impossible to assess whether the model usefully identifies PROM cases or merely fits the majority class. The paper uses the term "risk assessment at the screening level" (line 4), but for a medical screening task, reporting only accuracy is a severe methodological gap. No mention of sensitivity, specificity, AUC, or any other standard medical evaluation metric appears anywhere in the paper (verified via grep). This is a verifiable, paper-as-written issue that undermines the paper's core quantitative claims.

### Major

2. **The 40-factor (96%) to 78-factor (79%) accuracy drop is undiscussed and contradicts the paper's central thesis.** The paper's title and motivation ("No Factor Left Behind") argue for including all available factors, yet performance degrades sharply when moving from 40 to 78 factors. The paper neither analyzes why this happens (overfitting? poor prompt scaling? low-quality low-frequency factors?) nor even acknowledges this apparent contradiction. This gap goes to the core of the contribution (lines 4, 202–205).

3. **The method is underspecified: no concrete example of a full prompt is provided.** The prompt template is described via equations and high-level descriptions (Eq. 1, lines 84–87; Eq. 5, lines 147–150; Eq. 6, lines 155–158), but the reader never sees a single, complete prompt for an actual participant. Figure 5 is described as an "Auto-prompt generation showcase" (line 216) without any accompanying description or analysis of its content. How the Shapley interaction scores (Eq. 2–3) are rendered as natural language text within the prompt is described only as "add[ing] the feature interaction map for each participant" (line 131), without illustration. This level of abstraction makes it difficult to assess the method's soundness or reproduce it.

4. **Key experimental design choices are missing from the main text.** No train-test split or cross-validation strategy is reported (Section 4.2–4.3). No standard deviations or confidence intervals are provided for any of the accuracy numbers. The ablation study investigating hierarchical prompt vs. CoT is referenced as Table S16 in the appendix (line 223), which is stripped and not present in the main paper — meaning the only quantitative analysis of the core design choice is absent from the body of the paper.

5. **The comparison to "supervised baselines" is partially opaque.** The paper names the compared models (Meditron-7B, BioMistral-7B, PMC-Llama-7B, Phi3.5 MoE — line 197), contrary to the critic's claim that they are unnamed. However, the paper does not compare against standard strong tabular-data methods (XGBoost, Random Forest, Elastic Net) that are known to handle high-dimensional features well, limiting the significance of the claim that the LLM approach "surpasses the supervised baseline by a large margin" (line 18). The only non-LLM baseline is logistic regression (Figure 4), which is compared on data efficiency, not absolute performance with all 78 factors.

### Minor

6. **Related work sections on RAG (Section 2.4) and Toolformer (Section 2.5) describe techniques that are not clearly used in the proposed method.** The paper claims to "build upon" RAG by "embedding information from the cohort population" and to "utilize" Toolformer to incorporate ML-based information, but the actual method does not implement retrieval augmentation or API-calling tools. These sections read as tangential and do not clearly connect to the contributions.

7. **The MLM prompt template "The [Value] of an [Factor] is [MASK]" appears grammatically ill-formed.** Substituting typical values (e.g., "high school" for [Value], "education level" for [Factor]) yields unnatural text ("The high school of an education level is [MASK]"), which raises questions about the quality of the generated prompts. This does not threaten the core method but suggests the prompt generation process may produce low-quality text for some factors.

### Trivial

8. The paper refers to "logistical regression" instead of "logistic regression" (Figure 4 caption, line 208). Minor presentation issues include: "supervised fine-tuning" misspelled as "superviesd fine tune" (line 185), an extra comma in "inference,," (line 91), and the abstract's ambiguous phrasing "(78 factors)" / "(40 factors)" which could be read as reference citations rather than factor counts.

## Suggestions
1. **Report clinically meaningful metrics for the primary task**: sensitivity, specificity, AUC, PPV, NPV, and confusion matrices. These are essential for any medical screening task and would resolve the core ambiguity about whether the reported accuracy reflects real predictive value or majority-class fitting.
2. **Provide a concrete, worked example** of a single participant's full prompt under each of the four methods (manual, MLM-driven, Bio-QA, hybrid), along with the actual output showing where Shapley interaction scores appear in the text.
3. **Analyze the 40→78 factor accuracy drop**: identify which added factors degrade performance, whether prompt length is a bottleneck, and whether the degradation can be mitigated through prompt engineering or feature selection.
4. **Add standard tabular baselines** (XGBoost, Random Forest, Elastic Net) with and without feature selection, using identical train/test splits, to support the claim that the LLM approach is advantageous for high-dimensional cohort data.
5. **Report train-test splits and confidence intervals/standard deviations** for all quantitative results to establish statistical reliability.
