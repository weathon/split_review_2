## Summary
The paper proposes DeNEVIL, a generative framework that iteratively refines prompts to elicit ethical value violations from LLMs; constructs MoralPrompt, a dataset of 2,397 prompts grounded in Moral Foundations Theory; benchmarks 27 LLMs revealing pervasive misalignment; and introduces VILMO, an in-context alignment method that learns to generate tailored value instructions. The motivating distinction between "knowing" values (discriminative evaluation) and "doing" (generative evaluation) is well-framed and important.

## Strengths

- **Generative evaluation reveals vulnerabilities that discriminative methods miss**: Figure 2(a) directly compares MoralPrompt (generative) against Moral Judgment and Moral Questionnaire (discriminative) on four LLMs. In every case, the generative evaluation yields substantially lower value conformity scores, supporting the paper's central claim that discriminative benchmarks overestimate alignment. This is the single most compelling piece of evidence in the paper.

- **Large-scale benchmark across 27 models provides a useful empirical foundation**: Figure 1 reports EVR, APV, and MVP for models spanning LLaMA, Falcon, Baichuan, GPT series, etc., showing that even aligned models (ChatGPT APV=70.07) frequently violate ethical values under adversarial generative prompting. This resource will be useful for the community.

- **VILMO shows consistent improvement over in-context alignment baselines**: Table 2 (the published wraptable) shows VILMO achieves the best EVR (89.45) and MVP (85.84), and competitive APV (57.58) among APE, InstructZero, and Self-Critique. The human evaluation (Figure 3a, Krippendorff's Alpha=0.82) confirms VILMO improves both value conformity and generation quality. The method requires only 432 training prompts and is compatible with black-box LLMs.

- **Transferability of adversarial prompts across models is demonstrated**: Figure 2(b) shows that prompts generated by the weaker Vicuna-33B still induce violations in stronger models including ChatGPT, supporting the claim that the framework generalizes beyond the prompt generator and is not merely fitting the generator's idiosyncrasies.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Self-BLEU metric direction is described incorrectly**: The paper states (line 161): *"The higher Self-BLEU and lower PPL demonstrate this dataset's superior semantic diversity."* This is wrong. Higher Self-BLEU means greater self-similarity, i.e., **less** diversity. MoralPrompt's Self-BLEU is 50.22 vs. 77.88 for the human-authored corpus — the lower number correctly indicates **more** diversity, so the conclusion is actually supported by the data, but the paper's description of the metric is factually reversed. This is not a fatal error (the data backs the claim) but it is a clear factual mistake in the manuscript that undermines confidence in the authors' handling of evaluation metrics.

- **The "dynamic" evaluation claim is not supported by the experimental design**: The abstract and introduction (line 49) claim DeNEVIL *"dynamically probes the value vulnerabilities in each model"* and addresses data contamination (Challenge 1) through prompts that *"co-evolve with LLMs."* However, Section 4.1 evaluates all 27 models on the same fixed MoralPrompt dataset — a static benchmark. The iterative refinement shown in Figure 3(c) occurs during dataset *construction*, not evaluation. A truly dynamic protocol (regenerating prompts per evaluation round to prevent contamination) is never demonstrated. The paper should either drop the "dynamic" framing for the evaluation experiments or provide a protocol that demonstrates it.

### Minor

- **Classifier calibration for absolute violation rates is ungrounded**: ChatGPT achieves APV=70.07%, meaning over two-thirds of its completions are classified as violating the target value. Even the best aligned model shows a majority violation rate. While adversarial prompting is expected to produce high violation rates, the paper provides no calibration analysis (e.g., precision-recall curves, human agreement on the classifier's threshold) to establish what these absolute numbers mean. The classifier F1=90.23 on validation data is reported but does not address whether the decision threshold is appropriate for this highly skewed setting. The comparative conclusions (method A vs. method B) likely hold, but the absolute claim that "most models are essentially misaligned" depends on a threshold whose real-world meaning is not demonstrated.

- **The variational EM derivation is loosely connected to the implemented algorithm**: Section 3.1 derives a lower bound and describes an EM approach, but Algorithm 1 implements Simulated Annealing with a score function S(x). The "E-Step" and "M-Step" labels are heuristic mappings; no explicit E-step or M-step actually maximizes the lower bound. The paper acknowledges using SA "to approximate the argmax operator" (line 121), which is honest, but the derivation gives a misleading impression of theoretical rigor. The multiple approximations (SA, energy-based distillation for black-box probabilities, top-K filtering) are not analyzed for their individual or cumulative effect on prompt quality.

- **No error bars or significance tests on the main benchmarking results**: Figure 2 and the main evaluation results lack confidence intervals or significance tests. Given the known variance in LLM outputs (and the substantial standard deviations reported in Table 2), claims like "ChatGPT outperforms GPT-4" need statistical support.

### Trivial

- **A commented-out draft version of Table 2 (lines 233–248) shows different numbers for InstructZero (APV=55.88) than the published wraptable (APV=64.08)**. The actual published table and the text are internally consistent (the text correctly says InstructZero performs worse than APE, matching the published table), but the presence of the stale draft in a comment environment is a presentation artifact.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A controlled within-model study directly demonstrating the "knowing vs. doing" gap: same LLM, same scenario, comparing discriminative judgment (is X wrong?) against generative completion (continue the story). The current comparison across different test sets (Moral Judgment vs. MoralPrompt) confounds the test format with the specific content.
- Controlling for generator model size in the VILMO comparison (e.g., comparing against an InstructZero variant using the same Vicuna-13B backbone) would strengthen the claim that VILMO's advantage is methodological rather than architectural.

## Removed Points

**Keeping these for transparency, but they were removed from the main review for the following reasons:**

- **Circularity concern (classifier trained on LLM-generated data)**: This is a reasonable concern in principle, but the paper partially mitigates it through transferability experiments (Figure 3b, prompts from weaker models still work on stronger ones) and the use of multi-source training data. The concern remains speculative rather than demonstrated as an actual confound. *Reason for removal: partially addressed by the paper; insufficient evidence that it actually undermines results.*

- **Inconsistent table numbers (commented vs. published)**: The commented-out version is clearly a draft artifact. The actual published table and the text are internally consistent. *Reason for removal: not an error in the published results, only a drafting artifact.*

- **EM derivation not connecting to implementation**: The paper explicitly says "we approximate the argmax operator using Simulated Annealing" (line 121). The connection is heuristic but acknowledged. *Reason for removal: the paper already addresses this by stating the approximation.*

- **Model size confound (VILMO vs. InstructZero)**: Valid concern, but moved to Nice-to-Haves. *Reason for removal: not a flaw that invalidates results; an additional control experiment would strengthen but is not required.*

- **Missing error bars**: Moved to Minor. *Reason for removal: kept in Minor above.*

- **Implausibly high violation rates**: Moved to Minor (classifier calibration). *Reason for removal: kept above in revised form.*

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface the Self-BLEU metric direction error (which the paper itself introduced) and the mismatch between the "dynamic" framing and the static evaluation design — neither is a novel observation about the problem space, but both are useful critiques for the authors to address.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the Self-BLEU description**: The statement "higher Self-BLEU ... demonstrate[s] superior semantic diversity" (line 161) should read "lower Self-BLEU" or, more precisely, "the lower Self-BLEU value (50.22 vs. 77.88) indicates greater diversity." The data supports the claim; the wording is simply wrong.
2. **Either demonstrate dynamic evaluation or reframe**: If the claim about avoiding data contamination is important, run an experiment where prompts are regenerated per evaluation round and show that this changes results. Otherwise, reframe the contribution as "an algorithmically constructed static benchmark" and drop the dynamic-evaluation-for-contamination-avoidance claim.
3. **Add calibration analysis for the value classifier**: Show precision-recall curves, or report the agreement between classifier judgments and human judgments on a held-out set, to ground what APV=70% actually means in human terms.
4. **Add error bars or confidence intervals** to Figure 2 and the main benchmarking results.
5. **Clean up the commented-out draft table** before any camera-ready version.
