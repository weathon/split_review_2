## Summary

This paper proposes a potential-based motion planning method using diffusion models as energy-based models. The core idea is to learn a trajectory-level potential function via denoising score matching on a dataset of solved motion plans, then optimize trajectories by following the learned energy gradient through the diffusion reverse process. The authors demonstrate three contributions: (1) a diffusion-based learned potential framework for motion planning, (2) strong empirical results outperforming classical and learned planners on success rate and collision checks in 2D, 7D, and 14D environments, and (3) compositional generalization by summing learned potentials, enabling zero-shot transfer to environments with more obstacles, hybrid static+dynamic constraints, and real-world multi-agent scenes.

## Strengths

- **Large and consistent margins over both classical and learned planners** (Figure 3). The method achieves the highest success rate across all three environments (2D, 7D, 14D) while requiring up to two orders of magnitude fewer collision checks than BIT* and roughly 7× fewer than MπNet in Dual KUKA, with competitive or better planning time. This directly supports the core effectiveness claim.

- **Compelling compositional generalization** (Figure 4, Figure 5, Table 2). The paper shows that potentials from models trained on only 6 obstacles can be combined to handle up to 11 obstacles, with success rates that degrade gracefully while baselines collapse. Composition also works across heterogeneous constraints (static + dynamic obstacles, Table 2: 96.6% success vs. 70.4% for SIPP). These experiments convincingly demonstrate that compositionality is a genuine, useful property of the learned potentials.

- **Effectiveness in high-dimensional spaces** (Figure 3, Table "Motion Refining"). The method maintains meaningful success rates (~47% before refinement, ~70-80% after) in 14D Dual KUKA, where all baselines fall sharply (BIT* ~30%, MπNet ~10%), demonstrating scalability that prior learned planners lack.

- **Motion refining scheme with clear benefit** (Table 2). The proposed refinement (adding noise to collision sections and re-denoising) consistently and substantially boosts success rates, e.g., from 71.3% to 90.0% in KUKA (R=3) and from 45.5% to 69.8% in Dual KUKA, with diminishing returns as R increases, as expected.

### Major

- **Training data generation procedure is not specified (Section 3.2, Section 4).** The paper trains on "a dataset of solved motion planning problems" but never states how these solved trajectories are obtained. Are they generated by an optimal/sampling-based planner? By a human demonstrator? By optimization? This is the single most important missing detail: if the data comes from BIT* or another expensive planner, the method is effectively amortizing that planner's output; if from a suboptimal source, the learned potentials may bake in suboptimalities. This gap affects how all main results should be interpreted. (Lines 108, 360; the paper only says "randomly generated environments that follow the same procedural generation pipeline as the training environments" for test cases, but says nothing about training trajectory generation.)

- **Probabilistic completeness argument is informal and lacks necessary justification (Section 3.5).** The proof (Eq. 6–8) asserts that "∀ q_{1:T}, f_θ(q_{1:T}) > 0" as a fact without any justification. For a learned diffusion model with finite capacity and finite training data, whether the learned density has full support is a nontrivial claim. Additionally, the argument that a τ-interval of valid trajectories exists within J_c is stated without connecting it to the specific topology of the motion planning problem. While the conclusion (asymptotic completeness) may hold in principle, the current presentation is a sketch, not a rigorous proof. This section overstates its theoretical grounding. (Lines 265–293.)

- **Missing network architecture and conditioning details essential for reproducibility.** The paper does not specify: (a) the network architecture for E_θ, (b) how the obstacle configuration C is parameterized and fed into the network, (c) the noise schedule β_s, or (d) the diffusion constants γ and σ²_s. These are not trivial hyperparameters but core design decisions. The paper mentions "classifier-free guidance scale of 2.0" (line 129) but does not describe the base architecture.

### Minor

- **Composition approach attribution could be clearer (Section 3.3).** The method of composing potentials by summing gradients (Algorithm 1) directly follows the compositional diffusion framework of Du et al. (2023, "Reduce, Reuse, Recycle"). While that work is cited for the training objective (line 112), the paper does not explicitly credit it for the composition mechanism itself. The prose ("we can likewise form a composite potential function...") could give the impression that composition is a novel methodological contribution of this paper, whereas the novelty lies in the application to motion planning. This overstates the methodological novelty.

- **The "local minima avoidance" claim conflates the learned potential with the diffusion process (Section 4.1).** The comparison against RMP (handcrafted potentials) shows that the learned potential produces better energy landscapes, but it does not isolate the benefit of the diffusion annealing procedure versus simply having a better learned energy. An ablation comparing non-annealed Langevin dynamics on the learned E_θ (without the multi-scale diffusion schedule) would clarify this. The claim as stated is not incorrect, but it is imprecise about the source of the benefit.

- **"Before" success rates in Table 2 vary across R conditions for the same environment.** For Maze2D, "Before" rates are 96.3 (R=3), 95.3 (R=5), and 95.8 (R=10). If the same 100 test environments (with 20 start/goal pairs each) are used across all R conditions, these should be identical. The paper should clarify whether different random seeds were used for the initial sampling, or whether different test splits were used. (Lines 385–391.)

- **MPD underperformance is not discussed (Table 1, concave results).** MPD (Carvalho et al. 2023), another diffusion-based motion planner, achieves only 44.4% success on concave environments versus the method's 100%. The paper reports this but does not analyze why — whether due to different conditioning mechanisms, training data, or obstacle representation. This comparison would strengthen the paper if discussed. (Lines 427, 436.)

- **Real-world experiments lack quantitative metrics (Section 4.4).** Only qualitative trajectory visualizations are shown for the ETH/UCY dataset. Given that the dataset provides ground-truth trajectories, reporting success rate, collision rate, or displacement error would substantially strengthen the real-world claims.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation comparing the diffusion-based sampling against direct gradient descent on the learned E_θ (without the multi-scale schedule) would isolate the benefit of the annealing procedure for avoiding local minima.

- Path quality metrics (path length, smoothness, clearance) beyond success rate would provide a more comprehensive comparison against baselines, especially BIT* (which produces rough paths).

2. **Provide the network architecture and conditioning mechanism** — describe the architecture of E_θ, how obstacle set C is encoded (point cloud? set encoder? occupancy grid?), and list the diffusion noise schedule.
3. **Remove or substantially revise Section 3.5** — either replace the informal "proof" with a proper theoretical treatment acknowledging assumptions, or reframe it as an intuitive plausibility argument rather than a formal proof.
4. **Clarify the "Before" rates in Table 2** — explain why they vary across R conditions, or report a single "Before" rate with refinement improvements at each R.
5. **Add a brief discussion of why MPD underperforms** — this would strengthen the analysis of the proposed method's advantages.
6. **Acknowledge the composition attribution more explicitly** — credit Du et al. (2023) for the compositional gradient summing mechanism alongside the training objective.
