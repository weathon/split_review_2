## Summary

This paper introduces LIPS, a neuro-symbolic framework for proving Olympiad-level inequalities in Lean. The core design principle is a clean division of labor: **scaling tactics** (applying known inequality lemmas like AM-GM, which involve non-equivalent transformations from a finite set) are handled by symbolic enumeration with CAD-based pruning, while **rewriting tactics** (equivalent transformations like rearranging terms, which form an infinite space) are generated by LLMs. Goal selection uses two-stage filtering (heuristic symbolic scores for homogeneity and decoupling, then LLM ranking). Evaluated on 161 competition problems, LIPS solves 56/61 Olympiad-level problems (ChenNEQ+MO-INT) within 90 minutes, significantly outperforming CAD (41) and other baselines, and generates human-readable, formally verified Lean proofs.

## Strengths

- **Principled and well-motivated decomposition of tactics.** The paper identifies a fundamental difference between scaling tactics (finite lemma set, non-equivalence-preserving → enumerable and prunable by counterexample search) and rewriting tactics (infinite space, equivalence-preserving → requires algebraic intuition). This analysis (Section 3) cleanly drives the architecture and is a genuine insight for the domain.

- **State-of-the-art performance on a challenging benchmark with a large gap.** On 61 Olympiad-level problems (ChenNEQ+MO-INT), LIPS proves 56 within 90 minutes, versus 41 for the best symbolic baseline (CAD). On the full 161-problem testbed, LIPS surpasses CAD (59.0%) and MMA (57.1%) by 14–24 percentage points. These results represent a clear empirical advance, not an incremental one.

- **Two-stage goal selection is novel and empirically justified.** The combination of symbolic filtering (DC/HM scores) with LLM chain-of-thought ranking (Section 4) demonstrably improves search efficiency: LIPS averages 15.75 search loops, only 2.17× the oracle's 7.25 loops (Figure 3). This is a practical contribution that addresses the scalability issue of feeding many goals into an LLM.

- **Robustness across multiple LLMs.** The ablation in Section 5.2 (RQ3) shows that alternative LLMs (Mathstral 7B, LLaMA-3 8B, DeepSeek-chat V2.5) achieve comparable performance in neural ranking, and only a mild drop in rewriting generation (Figure 5). This demonstrates the framework is not brittle or tied to a single API.

- **Honest and substantive limitations section.** Section 7 clearly acknowledges the manual effort required for tactic formalization and scopes the domain to elementary algebraic inequalities, which is appropriate for the contribution level.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The core claim about division of labor is not tested by a cross-ablation.** The paper argues that scaling tactics should be symbolic (finite, prunable) and rewriting tactics should be neural (infinite, needs intuition). This is plausible and the combined system works well. However, there is no experiment that tests the alternative assignments — e.g., generating scaling tactics with an LLM (while still pruning with CAD), or generating rewriting tactics with symbolic pattern matching. Without this, the partition of labor is an informed design choice supported by the system's overall success, but not empirically isolated as the *cause* of the improvement. The contribution does not collapse without this ablation (the combined system is still novel), but the paper's strongest conceptual claim is under-evidenced.

### Minor

- **The symbolic filtering scores (DC and HM) are introduced without validation against simpler alternatives.** Section 4.1 defines decoupling as mean variables per term and homogeneity as variance of degree. These are intuitive but ad hoc. The paper includes a robustness experiment on filtered set size (8–16) showing the threshold is not sensitive, but never compares the *ordering* imposed by DC/HM against trivial baselines (e.g., filtering by term count, random filtering, or the equality-condition check from AIPS). The scores may well be useful, but the evidence for them specifically is absent.

- **Exact per-dataset success numbers for the 567NEQ subset are not reported in the text.** The paper states "overall results are presented in Table 1" (which is an image and unreadable), and reports overall success rates (59.0% CAD, 57.1% MMA, LIPS surpassing by 14–24%), but the absolute count or rate specifically on the 100-problem 567NEQ subset is absent from the text. This is a small reporting gap.

- **The DSP baseline achieving 0% success without discussion of any tuning effort.** The paper reports that DSP (GPT-4o generating natural language proofs then autoformalizing) achieves zero proofs. While not surprising for Olympiad-level inequalities, the paper does not discuss whether any attempts were made to improve the pipeline (multiple tries, few-shot examples, self-correction). This does not undermine the main results (which rely on comparisons with AIPS and CAD/MMA), but it weakens the presentation of the baseline set.

- **Statistical variability from the LLM component is not addressed.** The paper does not state whether LLM calls use temperature=0 (deterministic) or higher temperatures (stochastic), and reports only single-run results without confidence intervals. If the LLM introduces randomness, the reader cannot assess the stability of the reported numbers. This is a standard expectation even in theorem-proving papers that use LLMs.
