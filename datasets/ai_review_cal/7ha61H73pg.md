## Summary

This paper proposes ILA (Identifying Important Layers for LLM Alignment), a method that learns binary masks for each LoRA weight matrix to identify which components are most critical during alignment fine-tuning. The key empirical findings are: (1) importance rankings are highly consistent across diverse alignment datasets (Alpaca-GPT4, LIMA, No Robots) with ~90% Jaccard overlap, (2) freezing ~25% of the least important modules can improve downstream performance over full LoRA, and (3) fine-tuning only 10–30% of important modules achieves near-full performance while reducing compute. The paper connects these findings to the hypothesis that alignment primarily adjusts presentation style rather than knowledge.

## Strengths

- **Consistent importance ranking across diverse alignment datasets.** The paper reports ~90% Jaccard overlap (at the top-75% threshold) when comparing importance rankings from Alpaca-GPT4 (52K GPT-4 generated examples), LIMA (1K human-curated), and No Robots (10K human demonstrations) — datasets that differ substantially in size, source, and content. This consistency is the paper's most compelling empirical result and directly supports its central claim.

- **Freezing unimportant modules improves performance, validated against meaningful baselines.** The paper shows that freezing the 25% least important modules (by ILA's ranking) consistently outperforms both full LoRA and full fine-tuning on MMLU, Hellaswag, MT-Bench, and Vicuna across Llama 2-7B and Mistral-7B. The ablation study confirms that random freezing, freezing the first K modules, or freezing the last K modules all fail to outperform the baseline — showing ILA's selection is non-trivial.

- **Selective fine-tuning (10–30% of important modules) achieves competitive performance with significant efficiency gains.** Fine-tuning only the top 30% of important modules matches full LoRA performance, with only minor degradation at 10–20%. The integration with QLoRA (30–75% of modules) further demonstrates practical resource savings.

- **Low overhead for the importance-search stage.** Stage 2 (learning the binary masks) requires only ~11 minutes of additional training (128 batches on Llama 2-7B), making the method practical for routine use.

- **Cross-dataset intersection yields better importance estimates.** The ablation showing that taking the intersection of unimportant modules across multiple datasets produces better results than any single-dataset ranking is a clever practical insight.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Terminology ambiguity between "layers" and "weight matrices" undermines the paper's framing.** The paper's title, abstract, and running narrative refer to identifying "important layers," but the method actually learns one binary mask per individual LoRA weight matrix (W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o, W_up, W_down, W_gate, W_head). For Llama 2-7B, |γ_t| = 225 (line 253), whereas the model has only 32 transformer layers. A reader naturally expects "layer importance" to mean which of the 32 transformer blocks matter most. The paper never clarifies this granularity choice or analyzes whether important modules cluster within certain transformer layers. This conflation makes it difficult to interpret whether the findings are about specific modules (e.g., all W_up matrices are important) or specific transformer blocks (e.g., layers 20-25 are important). The claim that "layer importance is consistent across datasets" may simply reflect that FFN-type modules are consistently more important than attention modules (as the pilot study already suggests).

- **No statistical significance or variance reporting across experiments.** All results are presented as point estimates (Tables 2, 3, 9, etc., referenced via \input commands). The text states that ILA "consistently outperformed" baselines without reporting standard deviations, confidence intervals, or number of seeds. Given that improvements over LoRA are described in qualitative terms without magnitude, it is impossible for a reader to assess whether these reflect real gains or optimization noise. This is the most significant methodological gap.

### Minor

- **Importance estimation is a post-hoc heuristic without formal grounding.** The method trains LoRA normally, then learns a binary mask while freezing LoRA parameters by optimizing the same training loss. Theorem 1 shows that the learned masks are stable across consecutive checkpoints but does not relate the mask to any principled notion of parameter importance (e.g., sensitivity, ablation effect, Fisher information, or influence). The empirical validation (ablation studies, cross-dataset consistency) provides indirect support, but the method's theoretical status is weaker than the paper's presentation suggests.

- **Jaccard similarity at the top-75% threshold is a lenient measure.** The paper reports ~90% overlap by defining the top 75% of 225 modules (≈169 items) as "important." Two random large subsets of this size would have substantial overlap by chance. Reporting overlap at tighter thresholds (e.g., top 10%, top 30%) would provide a much stronger signal of consistency. The current threshold makes the 90% figure less impressive than it initially appears.

- **Freezing 25% of unimportant modules as the single tested percentage.** The performance-enhancement experiments only freeze "approximately 25%" of modules (line 210). No ablation varies this percentage for the performance claim, so it is unclear whether 25% is optimal, whether more or less freezing would be better, or whether the improvement is sensitive to this choice.

- **AdaLoRA comparison with limited hyperparameter search.** The paper acknowledges (line 214) that only "a narrow range of the hyperparameter t_r" was explored for AdaLoRA. While this admission is honest, it weakens the comparison and the claim that ILA outperforms AdaLoRA.

- **Single GPT-4 judge for conversational evaluation.** The paper uses GPT-4 to score responses directly (line 182) following the standard MT-Bench implementation. While this follows community practice, direct scoring is known to be less reliable than pairwise preference judgments, and the paper does not report inter-rater agreement or use multiple judges. This limits the strength of conclusions drawn from conversational metrics.

### Trivial

- The definition of ε-stability (Definition 1, line 86–91) references expectation over the alignment dataset but the practical implementation uses a single batch — the practical approximation could be clarified.
- Theorem 1's bound (line 144–148) involves constants (Q, R, L₁, L₂) that are not estimated or instantiated in the experiments, making it purely qualitative.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Analyze whether important modules cluster in certain transformer layers (e.g., are W_up modules in the last 5 layers more important than those in the first 5?). This would directly address the granularity ambiguity and turn it into a real insight.
- Compute importance overlap at multiple thresholds (top 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%) to strengthen the consistency claim.
- Report results with 3+ seeds and error bars for the main tables.
- Cross-validate the "single search suffices" claim by computing the ranking on LIMA and applying it to No Robots (currently only the reverse is shown).
- Analyze which types of weight matrices (W_up vs. W_q vs. W_k, etc.) are consistently important across datasets.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface one genuinely novel perspective not fully developed in the paper: the granularity conflation actually masks what could be a stronger finding. If the 225 LoRA modules are broken down by type (W_up, W_down, W_gate, W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o, W_head) and by transformer layer index, the consistency claim could be decomposed into: (a) are FFN-type modules always more important than attention-type modules? and (b) within each type, are specific transformer layers consistently more important? The current analysis lumps all module types together, so the "consistent ranking across datasets" finding may collapse a more nuanced story into a single number. Separating these dimensions would likely strengthen the paper's contribution.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the granularity throughout the paper.** Replace "layers" with "LoRA modules" or "weight matrices" where appropriate, and explicitly state that each of the 225 units is one LoRA module (not one transformer layer). If possible, add an analysis of whether important modules concentrate in certain transformer layers.
2. **Report results with multiple seeds and standard deviations** for all main tables (freezing claim, efficiency claim).
3. **Add Jaccard overlap at tighter thresholds** (top 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%) to strengthen the consistency evidence.
4. **Vary the freezing percentage** (e.g., 10%, 25%, 40%) for the performance-enhancement experiments.
5. **Consider pairwise GPT-4 comparisons** or report agreement statistics for the conversational evaluation.
6. **Strengthen the theoretical connection** between the learned mask and a principled importance notion (e.g., validate by measuring the effect on loss when the module is actually ablated).
