## Summary

This paper introduces *conceptual tools* (cognitive constructs such as knowledge sources or dialogue strategies) as a distinct category from functional tools, and proposes Think-Plan-Execute (TPE), a multi-persona prompting framework that decomposes dialogue response generation into Thinker (internal status analysis), Planner (tool ordering), and Executor (response composition). The method is evaluated on three dialogue datasets (FoCus, CIMA, PsyQA) against supervised baselines and CoT-based unsupervised methods.

## Strengths

- **Introduction of conceptual tools as an extension of the tool-learning paradigm.** The paper explicitly defines and operationalizes "conceptual tools" (cognitive strategies, knowledge sources) as distinct from functional tools (calculators, retrievers), broadening the tool-augmented LLM landscape to dialogue scenarios requiring reasoning about user emotion, tutoring tactics, etc. (Section 1, examples in Figure 1). This framing is well-motivated and provides a useful conceptual contribution.

- **Consistent empirical gains on the multi-source FoCus dataset.** On FoCus, TPE (ChatGPT) achieves Avg.B 23.47 and F1 36.95, outperforming the strongest unsupervised baseline ReWOO (21.05 Avg.B, 34.45 F1) by statistically meaningful margins (Table 1). This is the cleanest positive result in the paper.

- **Explainable and decomposable framework design.** The Think-Plan-Execute separation makes each step (user state analysis → tool ordering → response composition) transparent and independently inspectable, as illustrated in Figure 2. This design choice has face validity for the stated goals of explainability and controllability.

- **Demonstration of emergent compositional strategies.** The analysis in Section 5.2 shows that TPE with GPT-4 generates novel combined strategies (e.g., "correction question") at a 27% rate, whereas the observation-dependent baseline ReAct rarely produces such compositions. This provides qualitative evidence that the framework encourages non-imitative planning (Figure 4 / strategy distribution analysis).

- **Ablation confirms the Thinker's retrieval benefit.** Removing the internal status generated by the Thinker from the retrieval query causes a ~3% drop across all metrics on FoCus, empirically validating that the module enriches semantic information (Section 5.1, Figure 3 right panel).

- **Systematic in-context learning analysis.** Table 3 dissects contributions of strategy descriptions, examples, and demonstrations, yielding non-obvious findings (e.g., adding strategy examples hurts performance while adding full demonstrations improves F1). This provides actionable insights for prompt engineering.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Unexplained 17-point CIMA F1 gap undermines the "superior performance" claim.** In Table 1, TPE (ChatGPT) achieves F1 36.42 on CIMA while ReAct achieves 53.13 — a gap of ~17 points. The paper acknowledges this implicitly ("5 out of 6 evaluation metrics") but in the multi-strategy dialogue section (line 166) states "TPE demonstrates superior performance over these competitive baselines" without addressing this large negative result. The conclusion (line 258) similarly claims "superior performance over existing methods on three datasets." The F1 is a standard lexical overlap metric on CIMA, and a gap of this magnitude requires explanation: does TPE select wrong strategies, or right strategies with poor linguistic surface form? The paper does not analyze this. While TPE wins on CIMA sBLEU and BERTScore, the F1 gap is severe enough that blanket claims of "superior" performance are misleading without qualification.

- **Efficiency claims are made without any supporting evidence.** Section 3.2 (line 104) lists "Efficiency: less token consumption or computation cost" as a distinctive attribute of TPE. Section 4.2 (line 164) contrasts ReAct's "elevated costs" with TPE. Yet the paper provides **zero quantitative evidence**: no token counts, no latency measurements, no cost comparisons with baselines. This claim is asserted but not substantiated, making it unverifiable.

- **Evaluation metrics do not directly measure the core contribution.** The paper argues TPE improves *compositional reasoning* over conceptual tools, producing more explainable, controllable, and better-planned responses. Yet the evaluation relies entirely on n-gram overlap metrics (BLEU, F1, Rouge-L, BERTScore). These do not measure: (a) whether the correct strategy was selected (strategy-level precision/recall), (b) whether the reasoning in the Thought step is sound, or (c) whether the response is actually more explainable or controllable. The CIMA dataset has ground-truth strategy annotations, enabling direct plan-accuracy evaluation, but the paper only reports descriptive strategy distributions (Figure 3) rather than a quantitative comparison of plan correctness against baselines. This creates a gap between what the paper claims to improve and what it actually measures.

- **Overclaimed results on PsyQA.** On PsyQA (Table 2), TPE's performance is marginal. With ChatGPT, TPE is second-best on all three metrics (Avg.B 16.19 vs ReAct 16.03 — gap of 0.16; F1 33.06 vs ReAct 33.63; D-1 44.81 vs Cue-CoT 45.32). With GPT-4, TPE wins Avg.B and F1 but loses D-1 by 6.6 points (41.30 vs CoT 47.91). The paper accurately says "consistently delivers strong performance" (line 198) but the conclusion claims "superior performance over existing methods on three datasets" — which overstates the PsyQA evidence.

### Minor

- **No human evaluation of response quality.** Given the focus on explainability, controllability, and strategy adherence — properties n-gram metrics capture poorly — a human evaluation of response naturalness and strategy appropriateness would substantially strengthen the contribution. Its absence leaves a key aspect of the claimed improvements unvalidated.

- **The "only exception at Rouge.L on FoCus" framing is imprecise.** Line 162 states "compelling improvement for TPE over both supervised and unsupervised methods with the only exception at Rouge.L on FoCus." But CIMA F1 (36.42 vs ReAct's 53.13) is also an exception, at least for the "over both supervised and unsupervised" claim. The subsequent clarification ("5 out of 6 metrics") is accurate, but the first sentence overclaims.

- **"TBD" in Table 3 is undefined.** The ablation table (line 235) uses "TBD" as the method name for the full TPE without explanation.

- **No statistical significance or variance reporting.** Temperature was set to 0, but retrieval (FoCus) and inherent model behavior still introduce variability. Reporting results from multiple runs or trials would improve reliability assessment.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Direct evaluation of planning accuracy**: The CIMA dataset has ground-truth strategy labels. Reporting strategy-level precision/recall for TPE vs. baselines would directly test whether TPE actually plans better, not just whether its responses have higher n-gram overlap.
- **Human evaluation**: Even a small-scale study rating response appropriateness, naturalness, and strategy adherence would substantially strengthen the evidence.
- **Token cost / latency comparison**: Providing quantitative efficiency metrics (tokens per response, inference cost) would substantiate the efficiency claim and contextualize trade-offs vs. observation-dependent methods like ReAct.
- **Ablation of the Thinker vs. Planner vs. decomposition structure**: The current ablation shows that removing internal status hurts retrieval, but not how much each component contributes separately.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic and strength finder both surface the same core tension: the paper's framework (conceptual tools + TPE) is well-motivated and the FoCus results are strong, but the evaluation does not consistently support the strength of the claims being made. The most novel observation emerging from the reviews is the asymmetry in TPE's performance depending on task type — on multi-source tasks (FoCus) the gains are clear, but on multi-strategy tasks (CIMA, PsyQA) the results are mixed and sometimes worse than simpler baselines. This suggests TPE's value may lie more in its structured retrieval and dependency planning (which matters for multi-source) than in its strategy decomposition per se. The strategy distribution analysis showing emergent combined strategies (27% with GPT-4) is a genuinely interesting finding that the paper under-exploits — it could be the basis for a more direct claim about TPE enabling compositional tool use, if backed by human judgment of whether those novel combinations are actually appropriate.

## Suggestions

1. **Acknowledge and analyze the CIMA F1 gap.** Conduct error analysis: does TPE select the wrong strategies, or the right strategies with poor surface form? Compare strategy sequences generated by TPE vs. ReAct against ground-truth strategy annotations (which CIMA provides). This is the single most impactful addition for a revision.

2. **Provide efficiency data.** Report average token consumption per response for TPE and key baselines (ReAct, ReWOO). Without this, the efficiency claim should be dropped.

3. **Tone down overclaims.** Replace "superior performance over existing methods on three datasets" with precise, qualified language (e.g., "competitive performance with notable gains on FoCus, and mixed but promising results on multi-strategy datasets"). This is a simple but important fix.

4. **Report strategy-level accuracy.** For CIMA, directly compare the precision/recall of strategy selection between TPE and baselines against the ground-truth strategy sequence. This would directly evaluate the planning contribution the paper claims.
