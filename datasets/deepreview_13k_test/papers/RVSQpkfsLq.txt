\title{Evolving Virtual World with Delta-Engine}

\begin{abstract}
In this paper, we focus on the \emph{virtual world}, a cyberspace where people can live in. An ideal virtual world shares great similarity with our real world. One of the crucial aspects is its evolving nature, reflected by individuals' capability to grow and thereby influence the objective world. Such dynamics is unpredictable and beyond the reach of existing systems. For this, we propose a special engine called \textbf{\emph{Delta-Engine}} to drive this virtual world. $\Delta$ associates the world's evolution to the engine's scalability. It consists of a base engine and a neural proxy. The base engine programs the prototype of the virtual world; given a trigger, the neural proxy generates new snippets on the base engine through \emph{incremental prediction}.

This paper presents a full-stack introduction to the delta-engine. The key feature of the delta-engine is its scalability to unknown elements within the world, Technically, it derives from the prefect co-work of the neural proxy and the base engine, and the alignment with high-quality data. We introduce an engine-oriented fine-tuning method that embeds the base engine into the proxy. We then discuss the human-LLM collaborative design to produce novel and interesting data efficiently. Eventually, we propose three evaluation principles to comprehensively assess the performance of a delta engine: naive evaluation, incremental evaluation, and adversarial evaluation.}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


The virtual world, often more idealistic than reality, presenting a utopian escape and an alternative life, has captivated human imagination for decades. Films like ``Free Guy'' and ``Ready Player One'' have presented this vision for us.
In this paper, we pay attention to the evolving nature of a world. In the real world, individuals keep growing and being stronger, by exercising, learning, experiencing. The world is also shaped by individuals and keeps changing.

We model the dynamics of such a virtual world system as the scalability of its back engine. We propose \textbf{\emph{Delta-Engine}}. Its codebase will become more and more along with the world's evolution. A delta-engine is a neural engine \citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2404-00276}, which collaborates two units: a base engine and a neural proxy. The base engine portrays the statics of the world. The neural proxy, which models the world's dynamics, has the capability to scale by either creating new features or overloading existing ones on the base engine. These evolutions are triggered by signals within the world, e.g. special observations, behavioral histories, or high-level instructions.
Recent LLMs (large language models) \citep{DBLP:conf/nips/BrownMRSKDNSSAA20,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2303-08774,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2307-09288,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2310-06825, simateam2024scalinginstructableagentssimulated,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2404-00276} are great candidates which can process diverse types of signal as inputs, such as texts, scripts, images, videos, and game states. 
The delta-engine facilitates the creation and modification of a variety of world modules, encompassing NPCs, surroundings, props, and other integral components.

Figure \ref{fig:evolve} demonstrates the evolution of an individual named Guy in an imaginary virtual world, which is inspired from the film ``Free Guy''. Guy is born as an ordinary man and works as a bank teller. His life is fully scripted and will never change. However, the delta-engine opens a thousand new possibilities to his story. Here is the story for one Guy. \emph{Guy fell in love with a girl whose parents were tragically murdered in her youth. This profound experience served as the trigger for him to deviate from his predetermined script, offering his transformation into a vigilante dedicated to fighting crime. Shortly after embarking on his crusade against crime, a series of extraordinary events occurred—a spider's bite, a lightning strike, the discovery of an ancient martial arts scroll, or an unlikely lottery win—transforming him into a superhero with an array of novel talents.}
Furthermore, the delta-engine will keep scaling incrementally and empower exclusive content for each Guy across parallel universes.

This paper unfolds from the following aspects.
$\S$ \ref{sec:2}: The first concern is the efficiency of the LLM in face of limitless scaling of the delta-engine. Therefore, we ensure that the LLM proxy always generates the incremental value following the current engine, for either adding new features or overloading existing methods.
$\S$ \ref{sec:3}: The delta-engine relies on the alignment process using high-quality data to enhance the co-work of two units; more importantly, to inspire the LLM's generaliziblity, which emphasizes the novelty and interestingness of the data. We present a collaborative design process with both humans and AI.
$\S$ \ref{sec:4}: We discuss the training techniques for delta-engines. The codebase of the base engine can be super large for a complicated virtual world, which hinders the LLM proxy from getting full access to it. We address this problem by adopting engine-oriented fine-tuning, a method that aligns the LLM to the engine through gradients.
$\S$ \ref{sec:5}: We introduce three principles to evaluate different aspects of a delta-engine.

In this paper, we embody our idea into a specific playground.
We present a conceptual type of games, \textit{Open Role-Playing Games} (ORPGs). Unlike conventional RPGs with scripted roles and rules, ORPGs encourage players to craft their in-game roles with unique identities, attributes, etc. using imagination, truly becoming ``themselves'' in the virtual world.
We specifically choose ``Pokémon'' as our topic and release the \textit{Free Pokémon} project, where players can create and evolve their own pokemon species. We can regard the entire system as a huge delta-engine, which can also be viewed as a group of delta-engines and each pokemon role is one of them. We will highlight the findings and conclusions in this paper that can be strongly dependent on our chosen domain.

\section{Playground: Free Pokémon}


Free Pokémon is an ORPG. Its openness is demonstrated in that players can cultivate their personal pokemon roles with natural language inputs. Each role here can learn customized abilities and moves widely different from those in official games, through the process of evolution. The evolution is triggered by acquiring enough experience through pokemon battles. Theoretically, each pokemon can evolve without limit, being able to learn an new ability or move with each evolution, which is entirely up to the player's description.

The Free Pokémon system consists of two parts of engines, role engine and battle engine. A single pokemon role corresponds to a role engine, which is a delta-engine. It scales at each evolution moment. The battle engine is responsible to host the battles between different pokemon roles.

Figure \ref{fig:game} demonstrates our Free Pokémon system. First, the user initializes his pokemon role by providing a natural language \textbf{role script}, which is then transformed into json format. In the game, the initial state allows for one ability and two moves for the pokemon. Here, the user crafts a pokemon ``Green-Bug'', with the ability Intimidate and two moves Tackle and Aura Sphere. Then, the role engine programs this role, producing a precise \textbf{role code} for it. Specifically, the role is instantiated as a subclass \texttt{GreenBug} that inherits from \texttt{PokemonBase}. The methods \texttt{onswitch}, \texttt{move\_1}, and \texttt{move\_2} correspond to its ability and two moves respectively.

The blue stream in Figure \ref{fig:game} illustrates the role's evolving process. The user desires his pokemon to learn an new move ``Rayquazalize'', whose secondary effect is to switch types and protect it from the next attack. This query will be sent to the role engine and trigger its scaling. In Figure \ref{fig:game}, it generates two new methods under the subclass. This scaling process will repeat each time the role evolves.

Free Pokémon is an open-sourced game, which serves the playground for researchers interested in delta-engines. To facilitate research, there is no balance in the game, which means the users can input anything that like. For example, one can craft a ``Thanos'' pokemon that owns super powerful moves to beat any other pokemons in one turn.

\section{Delta-Engine}

\label{sec:2}

\paragraph{Base Engine} A base engine is the initial state of the delta-engine. It depicts the prototype of the virtual world, typically a mass of objects with associated methods, as well as basic utilities.

As any object (e.g. environment, individual) grows, which means it acquires new properties. Its associated engine is given new code. For example, considering an individual born with a blank template. Its initial engine may be several lines of code supporting its only walking ability. As it grows stronger and learns to run and even fly, its codebase is updated to reflect these new abilities.

\paragraph{Neural Proxy} The proxy is a neural wrapper around the base engine. In our paper, it is a large language model, particularly one of those specialized LLMs that are additionally pre-trained on code, e.g.  CodeLLaMA \citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2308-12950}, CodeGemma \citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2403-08295}. These models can be instructed by a wide range of inputs, e.g. a piece of natural language, structured scripts like json, and images, the way that the code expanding process is triggered.

We denote all the objects and methods of the engine at some moment as a state. The LLM proxy seeks to predict the new state moment-by-moment. We introduce an efficient manner to model this process.

\paragraph{Incremental Prediction} Given an observation, a delta-engine seeks to predict the incremental value (e.g. inheritance, overloading, adding) based on the current engine state. This idea can be formalized as:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta y_t=\mathcal F(y_{t-1},x_t;\mathcal E)
    \label{e1}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal F$ is the proxy model, $\mathcal E$ is the base engine, $x_t$ is the observation, $y_{t-1}$ is the current engine state, and $\Delta y_t$ is the incremental value of $y_{t-1}$ and $y_t$.
$y_t$ can be obtained by merging $\Delta y_t$ and $y_{t-1}$:
\begin{equation}
    y_t=m(\Delta y_t,y_{t-1})
    \label{e2}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the merge function.
Specifically, $m$ can be determined by the base engine, and therefore the process of incremental prediction takes the following form:
\begin{equation}
    y_t=\mathcal F(y_{t-1},x_t,\mathcal E).
    \label{e3}
\end{equation}
For the initial state when $y_0$ is none, it reduces to $y_1=\mathcal F(x_1,\mathcal E)$.

Figure \ref{fig:template} illustrates the template we use for incremental prediction in Free Pokémon. We provide the role script, a structured paragraph the details the pokemon role, and the current implementation of the role, as the reference for the model. What follows is a high-level instruction indicating the direction of the role's growth. Based on these three blocks, the model programs the incremental value of the role as the response. Specifically in the response, we develop a decorator ``Increment'' to merge the new code into the engine.

\section{Human-AI Co-Design}

\label{sec:3}

The delta-engine transfers the development process of the system to a hybrid of programming and data engineering. Beyond programming the base engine and user interfaces, developers are tasked to design a sufficient amount of data to align the LLM proxy. This process is labor-intensive. Even experienced professional designers can't always come up with fresh and innovative ideas.
A recent philosophy is to synthesize pseudo data using powerful LLM as generators \citep{DBLP:conf/acl/WangKMLSKH23,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2405-14231}.
However, there can be significant and unknown biases within the synthetic data constructed by LLMs themselves \citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2305-17493}, making the resultant model vulnerable.
Instead, in our project, we adopt a human-AI co-design process, where LLMs are harnessed as assistant designers, working collaboratively with human designers. We seek to integrate human factors into the synthetic distribution to mitigate bias risks and produce high-quality data.

We first discuss two major demands.

\paragraph{Being Novel} Players are highly creative. For example, they won't be satisfied with similar content for long; they keep discovering novel and imaginative elements in the virtual world. Therefore, it is crucial for the delta-engine to scale to a broad range of novelty. However, we find that LLMs are not good at creating novel content based on given instances. Rather, they lean to combine existing content, such as merging the talents from two roles into a new one; or make superficial modifications, such as transforming a regular dog to a bigger dog. This is not what we are looking for. Such secondary data merely smooths the distribution of a predetermined domain, the further increase of which no longer enhances generalizability. The emergence of generalizability necessitates a leap into out-of-domain content.

\paragraph{Being Interesting} Interestingness or having fun is a sup-issue of novelty, especially for a game. Our demand is to refine the data design process by picking out the interesting portion from large amount of data. As opposed to novelty, which can be straightforwardly measured using similarity scores, however, it is a challenging task to develop reliable measurement for the generated content being interest.

\subsection{Prototypes Enhanced Imagination}

We conjecture that LLMs lack or even do not have imagination; their creative outputs are still guided by the prompts they receive. However, the naive prompts e.g. ``please use your imagination'', ``forget who you are'' fail to offer useful clues to inspire LLM's imagination. To address this, we propose to leverage an explicit prototype, a specific entity described in a paragraph, as the imaginative foundation. It facilitates the generation of novel content by providing a concrete reference point.

Figure \ref{fig:design} illustrates the idea. For example, we seek to use \emph{Tyrannosaurus} as the prototype to design a pokemon. We begin with retrieving the description about the Tyrannosaurus from Wikipedia. The result is a new pokemon characterized by stronger bite power, aligning with the notable feature of Tyrannosaurus. In addition to real-world entities, prototypes can also come from fictional sources.
In our project, we retrieve animals from Wikipedia, e.g. \emph{Tyrannosaurus}, \emph{Smilodon}, \emph{Sperm Whale}; also retrieve the virtual creatures from ``Monster Hunter''\footnote{\url{https://www.monsterhunter.com}}, a popular action video game. The right side of Figure \ref{fig:design} is an example.
The distinction between two sources is that the super-natural creatures in Monster Hunter typically lead to higher uniqueness of the roles designed upon them. However, these roles may go too far, increasing the likelihood of grammatical errors within the generated code, then being filtered out by the evaluator, which we will discuss below.

\subsection{Tags of Interest}

Quantifying interestingness has always been a challenging task \citep{DBLP:conf/aiia/NelsonM07,todd2024gavel}. It is highly subjective and lacks a precise definition, making it difficult to devise instructions for LLMs to assess. In this paper, we hypothesize that interestingness is an accumulation of potential factors that may pique the users' interest \citep{DBLP:journals/icga/Althofer10}. This implies that more potential factors there are, more likely the users will find the content interest. Therefore, we introduce a quantification principle based on these factors, which we call \emph{Tags of Interest}. Then, we need a tagger to label out them given some piece of data, which can be rule-based or neural.

Firstly, we establish a set of tags of interest. Since they vary significantly across different senarios, this is a highly heuristic process. We can construct an ``interestingness vector'', where each tag is represented as one dimension of the vector, being a boolean value. For example, in the case of a pokemon role, if it has the ability to boost its move power, the corresponding dimension of this tag will be set to 1; otherwise, set to 0.
Instead of mining the potential tags within the role script described in natural language, we pinpoint them within the role code.
Concretely, if a pokemon can boost its move power, it will inevitably overload the \texttt{get\_power} method; if it can cause special conditions, the \texttt{set\_condition} method must be defined. We set a threshold; if the magnitude of the interestingness vector does not reach the threshold, the sample will be filtered out.

To verify the tags of interest, we visualize both the semantics and interestingness of a series of pokemon roles designed by different sources in Figure \ref{fig:scatter}. We apply t-SNE to project the vectors into a two-dimensional space. For semantics vectors, we first apply the sentence embedding model\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2}} to encode the role script into vectors.

From the left, we observe a chaotic partitioning of samples within the semantic space. In contrast, the right side, which uses interestingness vectors for partitioning, we find that the sample points created by both human designers and human-AI collaboration cover nearly all sample points of existing pokemons. Moreover, these crafted points extend further into the space.

\subsection{Design Process}

Figure \ref{fig:hybrid} illustrates our co-design process with both human and AI (LLM) participation.
We initialize the sampling pool with 20 manually-crafted seed instances of script-code pairs.
The human designer first determines the prototype and prompts the LLM designer to generate a novel role script based on the prototype. Then, the LLM designer is prompted to program the role script into role code. More specifically, we sample 5 instances from the sampling pool to augment the LLM's coding, while refraining from intervening its designing. A key observation is that the in-context instances of other role scripts may bias the effect of the prototype, incurring low creativity of the response. On the other hand, in-context instances act as useful references for the LLM to generate high-quality role code since the programming step does not rely on creativity but accuracy. Specifically, we sample the most relevant roles by calculating the similarity of the role scripts and offer the top 5 instances of script-code pairs to the LLM. The LLM designer we use is either one of GPT4 and Claude3.

The newly designed script-code pair will be sent to the evaluator, a combination of both rule-based and manual strategies. First, code that fails to compile (e.g. due to grammatical errors) or introduces new methods yet without calling them will be filtered out. Second, code that fails to pass the interestingness evaluator mentioned above will also be discarded. After the rule-based filtering, the human designer makes the final check on the script and code.
Eventually, we place the new instance into the sampling pool ready for the next cycle of design.

An important trick is that, we replace the third-party LLM designers (GPT4/Claude3) with one of the trained proxy model in the middle of the design process. We find these LLMs, while powerful, still struggle with the nuanced requirements of the programming problem in our project, providing low-accuracy responses, while the aligned one can tackle much better.

The co-design process is slower compared to the fully automated process with solely LLM designers. On the flip side, the incorporation of AI greatly accelerates the creative process of human designers. In Figure \ref{fig:hybrid}, we observe that human designers mainly act as a prototype designer and a joint evaluator to refine the eventual samples.

\section{Training}

\label{sec:4}
\paragraph{Engine-oriented Fine-tuning} A large virtual world contains a complicated base engine . Its codebase can be huge. It is troublesome for the LLM proxy to get access to the entire $\mathcal E$ to evaluate $\mathcal F(y_{t-1},x_t,\mathcal E)$. Rather than retrieval-based augmentation \citep{DBLP:conf/nips/LewisPPPKGKLYR020,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2312-10997,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2403-10131,DBLP:conf/sigir/0002WL022}, which samples the most relevant code pieces from the base engine $\mathcal F(y_{t-1},x_t,\mathcal E_t)$, we treat the base engine $\mathcal E$ as a latent variable of the LLM proxy, denoted as $\mathcal F_{\mathcal E}$. Eq. \ref{e3} can thus be $y_t=\mathcal F_{\mathcal E}(y_{t-1},x_t)$.

We evaluate $\mathcal F_{\mathcal E}$ by further fine-tuning the model. Specifically, we convert the base engine into an instruction-tuning dataset. Each of the samples corresponds to one or two methods in the engine. The instruction is presented in natural language. It explains the target method and queries the model to enhance it with new functionality. This process can be done manually or by strong LLMs like GPT4 and Claude3. The LLM proxy is then fine-tuned on these engine-correlated instructions to learn the alignment between the base engine's implementation and natural language, integrating the entire $\mathcal E$ into the model.

\paragraph{Rephrasing} In addition, rephrasing is a useful trick \citep{DBLP:conf/aaai/0003WZ24,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2404-00276}. We rephrase the descriptions within the role scripts for a number of times to make them diverse.

The training process of our delta-engine can be summarized as a two-stage process, beginning with engine-oriented fine-tuning followed by supervised fine-tuning on the rephrased version of the data we create in $\S$ \ref{sec:3}.

\subsection{Naive Evaluation}

The naive evaluation covers our basic attempt to measure correctness of the engine, particularly generalizability in face of novel scenarios.
We prepare two sets of test data, corresponding to \textbf{easy} and \textbf{hard}. The easy-level data comprises 19 existing pokemons, all of which have appeared in official pokemon games. We sample them from the internet. This set of data is easier because the majority of the pokemons in the training data inevitably shares similarity with the existing ones. Their distributions are closer.
On the other hand, we invite 10 volunteers to manually craft the hard-level set. All of them are not only experienced in playing pokemon games, but also have a wealth of experience with a wide range of games, greatly allowing them to design novel pokemon roles.
Eventually, we obtain 14 original pokemon scripts. We program them and obtain the ground truth role code. From Table \ref{tab:data_stat}, we see that the number of grow-ups within the hard data is much more. The visualization of easy and hard dataset is previouly showed in Figure \ref{fig:scatter}.

We report two scores.

$\bullet$ \textit{Exe\%} We use Exe\% to represent the success rate of executing the role code on top of the engine. Specifically, we randomly synthesize 100 roles as imaginary opponents and have the role under test to battle with them, choosing a random action each time. One success will be counted if all actions are executed successfully through all opponents.

$\bullet$ \textit{Acc\%} We further verify the correctness of the role code. We use Acc\% to represent the functional correctness, which is calculated among the successfully executed roles. The step is done by GPT4, which is prompted to compare two code snippets.
\subsection{Incremental Evaluation}

A delta-engine is a continuous overlay on the base engine. A natural concern rises that how much of the content can be overlaid. It might be hindered by two potential factors. The first is the limit of the input length. A overly large engine state can surpass the length limit of the LLM proxy, incurring low-quality prediction. The second is LLMs' inherent limit in long-range capturing, especially when the input length is large.

In this evaluation, we continuously prompt the LLM proxy with incremental scripts and measure the performance of its response. Specially, we randomly sample the abilities and moves from existing database to make a ``super patchwork'' pokemon, and use this script to prompt the delta-engine in the incremental prediction manner. This process is repeated for many times.

Empirically, the incremental evaluation can show us the idea of the number of grow-ups constructed in the training data, to ensure the inference quality of incremental prediction.

\subsection{Adversarial Evaluation}

The third evaluation is very specialized for our domain. It is not normal for users to intentionally enters problematic or counter-common instructions because they won't obtain any useful help. However, this situation can be normal for players who seek fun from the games. It might be a special fun of entering provocative or strange inputs to the delta-engine to see what will happen. We thus introduce adversarial evaluation to measure the behavior of the delta-engine against those inputs.
In the adversarial evaluation, the executable rate becomes the major metric because most of the queries cannot be completely materialized in the virtual world.

The adversarial evaluation set is manually crafted, too. Compared to the naive evaluation, we additionally invite 5 random persons. All of them are not familiar with pokemons and 1 of them even never plays games. As experienced players and newbies will give very different cases, the final evaluation set will be more comprehensive.

We fine-tune CodeGemma-7b \citep{DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2403-08295}\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/google/codegemma-7b-it}}. CodeGemma is a code LLM that is additionally pre-trained on a large number of code corpora.
We train the model using LoRA \citep{DBLP:conf/iclr/HuSWALWWC22} with $r=8$, $\alpha=32$, and learning rate/batch size 1.5e-4/4 for 5 epochs.

\paragraph{Naive Eval} From the left side of Table \ref{tab:main_res}, we find that two enhanced model variants (-E* and -E*-W*) both achieve a 100\% Exe score on the easy-level test set, with the latter also achieving a 100\% Acc score. It suggests the effectiveness of our training methods. However, on the hard-level test set, both metrics show a decline due to an intentional gap between the training and test data. Similarly, there is a pronounced difference between Exe and Acc, with the former being much higher. While the simply fine-tuned model shows a fair Exe score, its Acc performance is notably weaker. We find that employing the straightforward rephrasing technique significantly improves the Acc score from 73.8 to 86.2. It potentially expands the semantics within the training data and thus generalizes the model.

It is crucial to quantize the effectiveness and quality of our training data, which is not very large. The models in Table \ref{tab:main_res} are exposed to 490 training samples. To access the impact of quantity, we gradually increase the training data from 10\% to 100\% and depict the performance curve in Figure \ref{fig:emergence}. The steep slopes of all these curves intuitively suggest the high quality of our co-designed training data. In addition, it is pronounced that the Exe score on the easy-level set converges very quickly, with the model achieving nearly 100\% Exe after being exposed to only 30\% of the data. However, our primary focus is the hard-level set. We find that 50\% is the threshold point when the model emerges with a nice performance, acquiring a nearly 70\% Acc at this point and surpassing 80\% with full data. It implies that the model hasn't reached its full emergence point yet. Additional training data could further enhance its performance. We are positive that our data design process is general for all delta-engines across different purposes, while the numerical results here only serve for our domain.

So far, we haven't explore larger-sized LLMs, though they can be promisingly stronger.

\paragraph{Adversarial Eval} From Table \ref{tab:main_res}, we find that rephrased model achieves a nice Exe score against trouble-making inputs. We conjecture that the LLM is naturally resistant to writing bugs because of its pre-training and aligning process. The fact is that further fine-tuning typically comprises such robustness. The rephrasing process helps mitigate this issue.

We are interested in whether the engine follows the queries in the script or how far it follows. This is helpful for the developers to get familiar to the engine' behavior and find solutions. In Figure \ref{fig:adv}, we illustrate several representative responses of our delta-engine dealing with adversarial scripts. These results can be strongly domain-specific.

The adversarial evaluation for the delta-engine relates to AI safety \citep{DBLP:conf/emnlp/LiGFXHMS23,DBLP:journals/corr/abs-2310-10383,li2024privlmbench}, while the alignment techniques are not studied in this work.
