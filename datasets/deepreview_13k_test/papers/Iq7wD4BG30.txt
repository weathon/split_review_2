\title{Learning Utilities from Demonstrations in Markov Decision Processes}

\begin{abstract}
   Our goal is to extract useful knowledge from demonstrations of behavior in
  sequential decision-making problems. Although it is well-known that humans
  commonly engage in \emph{risk-sensitive} behaviors in the presence of
  stochasticity, most Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) models assume a
  \emph{risk-neutral} agent. Beyond introducing model misspecification, these
  models do not directly capture the risk attitude of the observed agent, which
  can be crucial in many applications.
   In this paper, we propose a novel model of behavior in Markov Decision
   Processes (MDPs) that explicitly represents the agent's risk attitude through
   a \emph{utility} function.
   We then define the Utility Learning (UL) problem as the task of inferring the
   observed agent's risk attitude, encoded via a utility function, from
   demonstrations in MDPs, and we analyze the partial identifiability of the
   agent's utility.
   Furthermore, we devise two provably efficient algorithms for UL in a
   finite-data regime, and we analyze their sample complexity. We conclude with
   proof-of-concept experiments that empirically validate both our model and our
   algorithms.
 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}


 

The ultimate goal of Artificial Intelligence (AI) is to construct artificial
rational autonomous agents \citep{russel2010ai}. Such agents will interact with
each other and with human beings to achieve the tasks that \emph{we} assign to
them. In this vision, a crucial feature is being able to correctly model the
\emph{observed} behavior of other agents. This allows a variety of applications:
$(i)$ \emph{descriptive}, to understand the intent of the observed agent
\citep{russell1998learning}, $(ii)$ \emph{predictive}, to anticipate the
behavior of the observed agent (potentially in new scenarios)
\citep{arora2020survey}, $(iii)$ \emph{normative}, to imitate the observed agent
because they are behaving in the ``right way'' \citep{osa2018IL}.

Nowadays, Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) provides the most popular and
powerful models, i.e., simplified representations, of the behavior of the
observed agent, named ``expert''. Under the so-called ``reward hypothesis''
\citep{sutton2018RL}, that has been recently re-interpreted in terms
of properties of preferences over trajectories
\citep{shakerinava2022utility,bowling2023settlingrewardhypothesis}, IRL
algorithms construct \emph{reward functions} representing the objectives and the
desires of the expert. Depending on the application, different models can be
adopted. For instance, \citet{ng2000algorithms} considers the expert as an exact
expected return maximizer, while \citet{Ramachandran2007birl} and
\citet{ziebart2008maxent} assume that the probability with which
actions and trajectories, respectively, are played is proportional to their fraction of
optimality (i.e., of expected return).

All these models assume that the expert is a \textit{risk-neutral} agent, i.e.,
an agent interested in the maximization of the \textit{expected} return.
However, there are many scenarios in which rational agents
\citep{foellmer2004stochastic}, as well as humans
\citep{kahneman1979prospect,Kreps1988NotesOT}, adopt \textit{risk-sensitive}
strategies in the presence of stochasticity. In the most general case, agents
are not only interested in the \textit{expected} return, but in the full
\emph{distribution} of the return \citep{bellemare2023distributional}. Popular
examples in this context include agents who aim to maximize the
\textit{expected} return while trying to minimize the variance
\citep{mannor2011meanvarianceoptimizationmarkovdecision,
dicastro2012policygradientsvariancerelated}, agents interested in the
optimization of the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR)
\citep{rockafellar2000cvar}, or in rewards volatility
\citep{bisi2019rewardvolatility}. IRL models, thus, incur in mis-specification,
which can crucially affect the descriptive, predictive, and normative power of
the inferred reward function
\citep{skalse2023misspecificationinversereinforcementlearning,skalse2024quantifyingsensitivityinversereinforcement,
chan2021human}.

\textbf{Related works.}~~To overcome this limitation, some authors have analyzed
the \emph{risk-sensitive IRL} problem
\citep{ratliff2017inverse,majumdar2017risk,cao2024inferenceutilitiestimepreference},
in which either the learner is provided with the reward function of the expert
and it must infer some parameters representing the risk attitude, or the learner
must infer both the reward function and the risk attitude from the given
demonstrations
\citep{ratliff2017inverse,majumdar2017risk,chen2019active,cheng2023eliciting}.
Nevertheless, these works suffer from major limitations that prevent the
adoption of the proposed algorithms in real-world applications. For instance,
they either make demanding assumptions (e.g., Boltzmann policies like in
\citet{ratliff2017inverse} and \citet{cao2024inferenceutilitiestimepreference},
which hypothesize the expert to play each action exactly proportionally to its
$Q$-value), or consider rather limited settings (e.g., the ``prepare-react
model'' of \citet{majumdar2017risk}, that imposes too much structure in the
expert's behavior and in the environment's dynamics).

An analogous line of research focuses on the problem of learning the risk
attitude of an agent from demonstrations in certain decision-making settings
other than Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)
\citep{Chajewska2001learning,shukla2017learningvideo,bai2020influencediagram}.
Even though the powerful model of von Neumann-Morgenstern (vNM) \textit{utility
functions} \citep{vnm1947theory,Kreps1988NotesOT} is adopted for representing
the risk attitude of the expert, these works only focus on ``coarse'' sequential
decision-making settings like decision trees \citep{Chajewska2001learning}, that
do not provide the rich expressivity of MDPs (there is no notion of reward
function). A more detailed analysis, along with additional related works, is
\textbf{Our proposal.}~~In this paper, we formalize, characterize, and analyze
the problem of inferring the \emph{risk attitude} of an agent, encoded with a
utility function, from demonstrations of behavior in MDPs. The main
contributions of this paper are listed below. The proofs of all results are in
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep, topsep=-2pt]
   \item Motivated by a real-world example, we propose a simple yet powerful
   \emph{model} of behavior in MDPs, that separates the objective (reward)
   from the risk attitude (utility) of an agent (Section \ref{sec: motivation
   and problem setting}).
   \item We introduce \emph{Utility Learning} (UL) as the problem of
   \emph{inferring the risk attitude} of an agent in MDPs, and we characterise
   the partial identifiability of the expert's utility (Section \ref{sec:
   utility learning}).
   \item We present and theoretically analyze two novel
   algorithms, \caty and \tractor, for efficiently solving the Utility Learning
   problem with finite data (Section \ref{sec: online utility learning}).
   \item We conclude the paper with proof-of-concept experiments that serve
   as an empirical validation of both the proposed model and the presented
   algorithms. (Section \ref{sec: experiments}).
\end{itemize}



\section{Preliminaries}


The main paper's notation is below. Additional notation for the
\textbf{Notation.}~~For any $N \in \Nat$, we write
$\dsb{N}\coloneqq\{1,\dotsc,N\}$. Given set $\cX$, we denote by $\Delta^\cX$ the
probability simplex on $\cX$. Given $\cX\subseteq\RR^d,y\in\RR^d$, we define
$\Pi_{\cX}(y) \coloneqq \argmin_{x\in\cX}\|y-x\|_2$.
A real-valued function $f:\RR\to\RR$ is \emph{$L$-Lipschitz} if, for all
$x,y\in\RR$, we have $|f(x)-f(y)|\le L|x-y|$. $f$ is \emph{increasing} if, for
all $x<y\in\RR$, it holds $f(x)\le f(y)$, and it is \emph{strictly-increasing} if
$f(x)< f(y)$.
The probability distribution that puts all its mass on $z\in\RR$ is denoted
by $\delta_z$ and is called the \emph{Dirac delta}. We represent probability
measures on finite support as finite mixtures of Dirac deltas.

\textbf{Markov Decision Processes (MDPs).}~~A tabular episodic Markov Decision
Process (MDP) \citep{puterman1994markov} is a tuple
$\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r}$, where $\cS$ and $\cA$ are the finite state
($S\coloneqq|\cS|$) and action ($A\coloneqq|\cA|$) spaces, $H$ is the time
horizon, $s_0\in\cS$ is the initial state, $p:\SAH\to\Delta^\cS$ is the
transition model, and $r:\SAH\to [0,1]$ is the \emph{deterministic} reward
function.
The interaction of an agent with $\cM$ generates trajectories. Let
$\Omega_h\coloneqq (\cS\times\cA)^{h-1}\times\cS$ be the set of
state-action trajectories of length $h$ for all $h\in\dsb{H+1}$, and
$\Omega\coloneqq \Omega_{H+1}$.
A deterministic
\emph{non-Markovian} policy $\pi=\{\pi_h\}_{h\in\dsb{H}}$ is a sequence of
functions $\pi_h:\Omega_h\to\cA$ that, given the history up to stage $h$, i.e., 
$\omega=\tuple{s_1,a_1\dotsc,s_{h-1},a_{h-1},s_h}\in\Omega_h$,
prescribes an action.
A \emph{Markovian} policy $\pi=\{\pi_h\}_{h\in\dsb{H}}$ is a sequence of
functions $\pi_h:\cS\to\cA$ that depend on the current state only.
We use $g:\bigcup_{h\in\{2,\dotsc,H+1\}}\Omega_h\to[0,H]$ to denote the return
of a (partial) trajectory $\omega\in\Omega_h$, i.e., $g(\omega)\coloneqq\sum_{h'\in\dsb{h-1}}
r_{h'}(s_{h'},a_{h'})$.
With abuse of notation, we denote by $\P_{p,r,\pi}$ the probability distribution
over trajectories of any length induced by $\pi$ in $\cM$ (we omit $s_0$ for
simplicity), and by $\E_{p,r,\pi}$ the expectation w.r.t.
$\P_{p,r,\pi}$.
We define the \emph{return distribution} $\eta^{p,r,\pi}\in\Delta^{[0,H]}$ of
policy $\pi$ as
$\eta^{p,r,\pi}(y)\coloneqq\sum_{\omega\in\Omega:\,g(\omega)=y}\P_{p,r,\pi}(\omega)$
for all $y\in[0,H]$. The set of possible returns at $h\in\dsb{H+1}$ is
$\cG^{p,r}_h \coloneqq\{y\in[0,h-1]\,|\,\exists \omega\in\Omega_h,\exists
\pi:\,g(\omega)=y\wedge \P_{p,r,\pi}(\omega)>0\}$, and
$\cG^{p,r}\coloneqq\cG^{p,r}_{H+1}$. We remark that $\cG^{p,r}_h$ has finite
cardinality for all $h$.
The performance of policy $\pi$ is given by $J^\pi(p,r)\coloneqq
\E_{p,r,\pi}[\sum_{h=1}^H r_{h}(s_h,a_h)]$, and note that
$J^\pi(p,r)=\E_{G\sim\eta^{p,r,\pi}}[G]$. We define the optimal
performance as $J^*(p,r)\coloneqq \max_\pi J^{\pi}(p,r)$, and the optimal policy
as $\pi^*\in\argmax_\pi J^{\pi}(p,r)$.

\textbf{Risk-Sensitive Markov Decision Processes (RS-MDPs).}~~A Risk-Sensitive
Markov Decision Process (RS-MDP) \citep{wu2023risksensitive} is a pair
$\cM_U\coloneqq\tuple{\cM,U}$, where
$\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r}$ is an MDP, and $U\in\fU$ is a utility function
in set $\fU\coloneqq\{U':[0,H]\to[0,H]\,|\,U'(0)=0,U'(H)=H\wedge U'\text{ is
strictly-increasing and continuous}\}$. Differently from
\citet{wu2023risksensitive}, w.l.o.g., our utilities satisfy $U(H)=H$ to settle
the scale.
The interaction with $\cM_U$ is the same as with
$\cM$, and the notation described earlier still applies, except for the
performance of policies.
The performance of policy $\pi$ is $J^\pi(U;p,r)\coloneqq\E_{p,r,\pi}[ U(\sum_{h=1}^H
r_h(s_h,a_h))]$, and note that
$J^\pi(U;p,r)=\E_{G\sim\eta^{p,r,\pi}}[U(G)]$. We define the optimal
performance as $J^*(U;p,r)\coloneqq \max_\pi J^{\pi}(U;p,r)$, the optimal policy
as $\pi^*\in\argmax_\pi J^{\pi}(U;p,r)$, and the set of optimal policies for
$\cM_U$ as $\Pi^*_{p,r}(U)$.

\textbf{Enlarged state space approach.}~~ In MDPs, there always exists a
\emph{Markovian} optimal policy \citep{puterman1994markov}, but in RS-MDPs this
does not hold.
The \emph{enlarged state space approach} \citep{wu2023risksensitive} is a
method, proposed by \citet{bauerle2014more}, to compute an optimal policy in a
RS-MDP.
Given RS-MDP {\thickmuskip=1mu
\medmuskip=1mu \thinmuskip=1mu $\cM_U=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r,U}$}, we construct the
\emph{enlarged} state space MDP
$\fE[\cM_U]=\tuple{\{\cS\times\cG^{p,r}_h\}_{h\in\dsb{H}},\cA,H,\tuple{s_0,0},\fp,\fr}$,
with a different state space $\cS\times\cG^{p,r}_h$ at each
$h$.\footnote{Actually, \citet{bauerle2014more} use state space
$\cS\times\RR_{\ge 0}$, while \citet{wu2023risksensitive} use $\cS\times[h-1]$
for all $h\in\dsb{H}$. Instead, we consider sets $\cS\times\{\cG^{p,r}_h\}_h$ to capture the
minimal size required.}
For every $h\in\dsb{H}$ and {\thickmuskip=2mu
\medmuskip=1.5mu$(s,y,a)\in\cS\times\cG^{p,r}_h\times\cA$}, the
reward function $\fr$ is {\thickmuskip=2mu
\medmuskip=1.5mu$\fr_h(s,y,a)=U(y+r_h(s,a))\indic{h=H}$}, while the dynamics
$\fp$ assigns to the next state {\thickmuskip=2mu
\medmuskip=1.5mu$(s',y')\in\cS\times\cG^{p,r}_{h+1}$} the
probability: {\thickmuskip=2mu
\medmuskip=1.5mu$\fp_h(s',y'|s,y,a)\coloneqq p_h(s'|s,a)\indic{y'=y+r_h(s,a)}$}.
In words, the state space is enlarged with a component that keeps track of
the cumulative reward in the original RS-MDP, and the reward $\fr$, bounded in
$[0,H]$,
provides the utility of the accumulated reward at the end of the episode.
A Markovian policy $\psi=\{\psi_h\}_{h\in\dsb{H}}$ for $\fE[\cM_U]$ is a
sequence of mappings $\psi_h:\cS\times\cG^{p,r}_h\to\cA$. Being an MDP, we adopt
for $\fE[\cM_U]$ the same notation presented earlier for MDPs, by replacing
$p,r,\pi$ with $\fp,\fr,\psi$.
Let $\psi^*$ be the optimal \emph{Markovian} policy for $\fE[\cM_U]$.
Then, Theorem 3.1 of \citet{bauerle2014more} shows that the (non-Markovian)
policy $\pi^*$, defined for all $h\in\{2,\dotsc,H\}$ and {\thickmuskip=1mu
\medmuskip=1mu \thinmuskip=1mu$\omega\in\Omega_h$} as
$\pi^*_h(\omega)\coloneqq
\psi^*_h(s_h,\sum_{h'\in\dsb{h-1}}r_{h'}(s_{h'},a_{h'}))$, and
{\thickmuskip=1mu
\medmuskip=1mu \thinmuskip=1mu $\pi^*_1(s_0)=\psi^*_1(s_0,0)$}, is optimal for $\cM_U$.

\textbf{Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL).}~~IRL aims to recover the reward function of an expert agent from demonstrations
of behavior \citep{russell1998learning}.
In the literature (e.g.,
\citet{ng2000algorithms,ziebart2008maxent,Ramachandran2007birl}), various
assumptions are made on how the expert's policy $\pi^E$ is generated from the
expert's MDP $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r^E}$. Given the expert's MDP without
reward $\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p}$, the expert's policy $\pi^E$, and the specific
assumption considered, the IRL objective is to recover the reward $r^E$.

\textbf{Miscellaneous.}~~For $L>0$, we write $\fU_L\coloneqq\{U\in\fU\,|\,U$ is
$L$-Lipschitz$\}$. For any finite set $\cX\subseteq[0,H]$ we define
$\overline{\fU}^{\cX}\coloneqq\{\overline{U}\in[0,H]^{|\cX|}\,|\, \exists
U\in\fU,\,\forall x\in\cX:\, \overline{U}(x)=U(x)\}$, and
$\overline{\fU}_L^\cX\coloneqq\{\overline{U}\in\overline{\fU}^\cX\,|\,\exists
U\in\fU_L,\,\forall x\in\cX:\, \overline{U}(x)=U(x)\}$.
We will denote by $\cM_{\overline{U}}$ some RS-MDPs with
$\overline{U}\in\overline{\fU}^{\cX}$.
 


\section{Motivation and Problem Setting}

\label{sec: motivation and problem setting}

 In this section, we begin by motivating the need for a more expressive model of behavior
 in MDPs. Next, we propose a risk-aware model and we justify it. We conclude
 with some observations.
 
 \textbf{Existing models for representing behavior.}~~Our goal is to develop
 an algorithm, that permits to learn a ``\emph{good}'' model of behavior of an
 agent from demonstrations in an MDP.
 In this context, the most common models present in the literature enforce
 a structure made of \textbf{two} components: $(i)$ a \emph{reward function}, that
 represents the objective of the agent, and $(ii)$ a \emph{planning method},
 that describes \emph{how the behavior of the agent is generated given its
 objective}.
 Crucially, the planning method is assumed to be \emph{known},\footnote{Indeed,
 \citet{armstrong2019occamsrazorinsufficientinfer} have demonstrated that ``it
 is impossible to uniquely decompose a policy into a planning algorithm and
 reward function'', but we need to impose some structure to the problem.} thus,
 all the information about the behavior must hold inside the reward (the
 objective) that can be \emph{learned}. Popular examples include IRL
 \citep{ng2000algorithms},
 entropy-regularized IRL,
 \citep{ziebart2008maxent}, and Bayesian IRL
 \citep{Ramachandran2007birl}.
 
 \textbf{Limitations.}~~Our insight is that these models are \emph{not expressive enough} to model human
 behavior in the presence of stochasticity in many common situations, as shown in
 the following example.

  \end{tikzpicture}

\opencutright
 \begin{example}
  \label{example: non markovian policy}
  \begin{cutout}{2}{6cm}{0cm}{8}
  Consider the MDP on the side, where you can reach state $s$ having already
  earned either $0$\texteuro\ or $100$\texteuro\ (in this example reward is
  money). From $s$, you can take either the ``risky'' action $a_{\text{risky}}$,
  that provides you with 200\texteuro\ with probability (w.p.) $1/2$ or
  0\texteuro\ otherwise, or the ``safe'' action $a_{\text{safe}}$, that provides
  you always with $50$\texteuro\ . \emph{What action would you play in state
  $s$?} Risk-averse \citep{kahneman1979prospect} people might go with
  $a_{\text{safe}}$ when landed on $s$ with $0$\texteuro\ , and with
  $a_{\text{risky}}$ otherwise, while risk-seeking people might always go with
  $a_{\text{risky}}$. Simply put, the current state $s$ is not sufficient for
  predicting behavior, because people decide to take risks depending on how
  much money (i.e., reward) they earned so far.
\end{cutout}
 \end{example}

 In other words, people might exhibit a \emph{non-Markovian} behavior dependent
 on both the state \emph{and} the cumulated rewards, which is not contemplated
 by the aforementioned IRL models.\footnote{Re-modelling the MDP including the
 reward into the state would make the optimal policy Markovian. Yet, this
 mathematical device would alter the state representation, preventing reward
 transfer to new environments.}

 \textbf{Our proposal.}~~% \paragraph{Our insight.}
  We propose to \emph{explicitly} represent the risk attitude by constructing a
  model with \textbf{three} components: $(i)$ a \emph{reward function}, i.e.,
  the objective; $(ii)$ a \emph{utility function}, i.e., the risk attitude,
  $(iii)$ a (known) \emph{planning method}, i.e., how the behavior of the agent
  is generated given its objective \emph{and its risk-attitude}.
 Formally, we model the expert as an optimal agent in a RS-MDP:
 {\thinmuskip=1mu
 \medmuskip=1mu
 \thickmuskip=2mu
 \begin{equation}\label{eq: model expert rs mdp}
   \pi^E\in\textcolor{vibrantBlue}{\argmax\limits_{\pi}
   \E}_{p,r,\pi}
   \Big[
   \textcolor{brightGreen}{\bm{U}}\Big(\sum\limits_{h=1}^H
   \textcolor{vibrantOrange}{\bm{r}}_h(s_h,a_h)\Big)
   \Big],
 \end{equation}}
 where $(i)$ $\textcolor{vibrantOrange}{\bm{r}}$ is the reward, $(ii)$
 $\textcolor{brightGreen}{\bm{U}}\in\fU$ is the utility, and $(iii)$ the
 principle of \textcolor{vibrantBlue}{maximization of the expected utility} is
 the planning method.
 There are many arguments that support this model:
 \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep, topsep=-2pt]
   \item it generalizes the IRL model of \citet{ng2000algorithms} by modelling
   the risk attitude through $U$;
   \item it is justified by
   the famous expected utility theory \citep{vnm1947theory};\footnote{The set of
   prizes is $\cG^{p,r}$, and each policy $\pi$ is a choice that induces a
   lottery $\eta^{p,r,\pi}$ over prizes.}
   \item it explains the existence of non-Markovian optimal policies (see
   \citet{bauerle2014more});
   \item the corresponding planning problem enjoys practical tractability
   \citep{wu2023risksensitive}.
 \end{enumerate}
 
 \textbf{Some considerations.}~~ If the utility $U$ is linear, the RS-MDP $\cM_U$
  admits a \emph{Markovian} optimal policy.
Otherwise, the more $U$ deviates from linearity, the more non-Markovian policies
  \emph{may} outperform Markovian policies, which may incur in a finite loss of
  performance, as shown below.

  \begin{restatable}{prop}{loseinperformancewithmarkovianity}
    \label{prop: lose in performance with markovianity}
    There exists a RS-MDP with horizon $H=4$ in which the difference between the
    optimal performance and the performance of the best Markovian policy is $0.5$.
   \end{restatable}
   
   Next, we observe that also any deterministic RS-MDP
   admits an optimal \emph{Markovian} policy. Intuitively, in absence of risk (i.e., stochasticity)
   the utility function plays no role.

  \begin{restatable}{prop}{envdeterministic}
    \label{prop: env deterministic}
    Given any RS-MDP with deterministic transition model $p$ and reward function
    $r$, if the utility $U$ is increasing, then, there exists a Markovian
    optimal policy.
  \end{restatable}

  Finally, if we restrict to Markovian policies, we note that non-stationarity
  (i.e., the dependence of the policy on the stage $h$) and stochasticity (i.e.,
  if the policy prescribes a lottery over actions instead of a single action)
  can improve the performance even in stationary environments. Intuitively, they
  permit to consider larger ranges of return distributions w.r.t. stationary
  deterministic policies.
  \begin{restatable}{prop}{policynonstationary}
    \label{prop: policy non stationary}
    There exists a RS-MDP with stationary transition model and reward in which
    the best \emph{Markovian} policy is non-stationary, and the best
    \emph{stationary Markovian} policy is stochastic.
  \end{restatable}

 \textbf{Learning from demonstrations under the new model.}~~In Section
 \ref{sec: motivation and problem setting}, we described a model that
 parametrizes the behavior of an agent through two components: a reward $r$,
 and a utility $U$. Given demonstrations of behavior in an MDP, Eq. \eqref{eq:
 model expert rs mdp} defines three different learning problems:
 \begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, noitemsep, topsep=-2pt]
   \item \textbf{Utility Learning (UL)}: given $r$, learn $U$.
   \item \textbf{Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL)}: given $U$, learn $r$.
   \item \textbf{IRL + UL}: learn both $r$ and $U$.
 \end{enumerate}
 Problem 3 is the most interesting (and challenging), because it makes the least
 assumptions,
 while Problem 2 has been extensively studied in literature
 when $U$ is linear \citep{ng2000algorithms} (but not in detail for
 other choices of $U$).
 In this paper, we focus on Problem 1, which we name
 \emph{Utility Learning (UL)}, for two reasons. First, there exist relevant
 applications of UL per se (see the last part of this section). Second,
 understanding UL represents a significant step toward solving Problem 3.
 
 \textbf{Partial identifiability in utility learning.}~~In the \emph{exact} UL
 setting (where $s_0,p,\pi^E$ are known), by definition, we are given a policy
 $\pi^E$ and an MDP $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H, s_0,p,r}$, and the goal is to find
 the expert's utility function $U^E\in\fU$ that satisfies
 $J^*(U^E;p,r)=J^{\pi^E}(U^E;p,r)$, i.e., that makes $\pi^E$ optimal in RS-MDP
 $\cM_{U^E}$.
Does the knowledge of $\pi^E$ and $\cM$ suffice to \emph{uniquely} identify
 $U^E$? Analogously to IRL
 \citep{skalse2023invariancepolicyoptimisationpartial}, the answer is
 \emph{negative}, as shown in the following example.

 \begin{example}
  \label{example: UE partially identifiable}
  Let $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r}$ be the MDP in Fig. \ref{fig: example fs}
  (left), where $H=2,r_1(s_0,a_1)=1,r_1(s_0,a_2)=0.5$, and all other values of
  $p,r$ are drawn in the figure. Note that $\cG^{p,r}=\{0,1,1.5,2\}$. Let
  $\pi^E$ be the expert policy, that prescribes $a_1$ in $s_0$. Then, a utility
  $U\in\fU$ makes $\pi^E$ optimal for $\cM_U$ if playing $a_1$ is better than
  playing $a_2$: $J^{\pi^E}(U;p,r)=0.1U(2)+0.5U(1.5)+0.4U(1)\ge
  0.8U(1.5)+0.2U(1)$.
  Thus, all the utilities $U\in\fU$, that assign to $G=1,G=1.5$ any of the
  values coloured in blue in Fig. \ref{fig: example fs} (middle), are
  equally-plausible candidates to be $U^E$.
 \end{example}
 
 Example \ref{example: UE partially identifiable} shows that $U^E$ is just
 \emph{partially identifiable} from demonstrations. In particular, we cannot uniquely
 identify the value of $U^E$ at points in the set $\cG^{p,r}$, and we do not
 have information on $U^E$ at the other points $[0,H]\setminus\cG^{p,r}$.
 Similarly to \citet{metelli2023towards}, we formalize the
 set of utilities ``compatible'' with $\pi^E$ in $\cM$ by introducing the notion

 provide a more explicit expression of the feasible utility set.}
 \begin{defi}[Feasible Utility Set]
  \label{defi: fs}
  Let $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H, s_0,p,r}$ be an MDP, and let $\pi^E$ be the
  (potentially non-Markovian) expert policy.
  The \emph{feasible utility set} $\cU_{p,r,\pi^E}$ contains all the utilities
  that make $\pi^E$ optimal for RS-MDP $\cM_U$. Formally: $
  \cU_{p,r,\pi^E}\coloneqq\{U\in\fU\,|\,$\scalebox{0.9}{$J^{\pi^E}$}$(U;p,r)=J^*(U;p,r)\}.$
   \end{defi}
 
 \textbf{Usage and transferability of utilities.}~~UL is a problem setting for inferring the risk attitude of an agent.
 Once learned, we might ``use'' the computed utility $\widehat{U}$ for $(i)$
 \emph{predicting} the behavior of the expert in a new environment, $(ii)$
 \emph{imitating} the expert, or $(iii)$ \emph{assessing} how valuable a certain
 policy is from the viewpoint of the expert.
 However, due to partial identifiability, $\widehat{U}$ cannot be close to
 $U^E$ more than the worst utility in the feasible set $\cU_{p,r,\pi^E}$. Is this
 ``ambiguity'' tolerated by the applications above? The following
 propositions answer \emph{negatively} for all $(i),(ii)$, and $(iii)$ of them, but,
 fortunately, Proposition \ref{prop: multiple demonstrations} shows that adding
 more data can solve the issue.

 Let us begin with $(i)$. In our model, a utility $\widehat{U}$ permits to
 predict the behavior of an agent with utility $U^E$ in a new MDP $\cM'$ if
 $\widehat{U}$ and $U^E$ induce in $\cM'$ the same optimal policies.
 Nevertheless, \emph{not} all the utilities in the feasible set satisfy this
 property for all the possible MDPs, as shown below:

 \begin{restatable}[Transfer to a new transition
   model]{prop}{proptransferabilityp}\label{prop: transferring utilities p} 
   There exist two MDPs $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r},\cM'=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p',r}$,
  with $p\neq p'$, for which there exists a policy $\pi^E$ and a pair
   of utilities $U_1,U_2\in\fU$ such that: $U_1,U_2\in\cU_{p,r,\pi^E}$ and
$\Pi_{p',r}^*(U_1)\cap\Pi_{p',r}^*(U_2)=\{\}$.
\end{restatable}

\begin{restatable}[Transfer to a new reward]
{prop}{proptransferabilityr}\label{prop: transferring utilities r}
  There exist two MDPs $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r},\cM'=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r'}$,
  with $r\neq r'$, for which there exists a policy $\pi^E$ and a pair
  of utilities $U_1,U_2\in\fU$ such that: $U_1,U_2\in\cU_{p,r,\pi^E}$ and
  $\Pi_{p,r'}^*(U_1)\cap\Pi_{p,r'}^*(U_2)=\{\}$.
  \end{restatable}

  Intuitively, we are saying that transferring the learned utility $\widehat{U}$ to an
  MDP with a different reward or transition model might cause it to
  induce optimal policies other than those induced by $U^E$ there.

  Consider now $(ii)$. To perform a meaningful imitation, due to the practical
  difficulty of computing optimal policies, we require that any policy with an
  almost optimal performance for $\widehat{U}$ has also a ``good'' performance
  for $U^E$, but this does not always hold:

  \begin{restatable}{prop}{propimitation}
  \label{prop: imitating the expert}
  There exists an MDP $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r}$
  and a policy $\pi^E$
    for which there exists a pair of utilities $U_1,U_2\in\cU_{p,r,\pi^E}$
    such that, for any $\epsilon\ge 0$ smaller than some constant, there
    exists a policy $\pi_\epsilon$ such that
    $J^*(U_1;p,r)-J^{\pi_\epsilon}(U_1;p,r)=\epsilon$ and
    $J^*(U_2;p,r)-J^{\pi_\epsilon}(U_2;p,r)\ge 1$.
    \end{restatable}

Finally, concerning $(iii)$, the fact that $\widehat{U}$ and $U^E$ provide close
 values of performance for all the policies seems a desirable requirement, i.e.,
 asking for small {\thickmuskip=2mu \medmuskip=1.5mu
 \thinmuskip=1.5mu$d_{p,r}^{\text{all}}(U^E,\widehat{U})\coloneqq \max_{\pi}
 \big|J^\pi(U^E;p,r)-J^\pi(\widehat{U};p,r)\big|$} \cite{zhao2023inverse}.
We note that closeness under some norm implies closeness in $d^{\text{all}}$:

\begin{restatable}{prop}{propbounddall}
  Consider an arbitrary MDP with transition model $p$ and reward function
  $r$. Then, for any pair of utilities $U_1,U_2\in\fU$, it holds that
  $d^{\text{all}}_{p,r}(U_1,U_2)\le
  \max_{G\in\cG^{p,r}}|U_1(G)-U_2(G)|$.
\end{restatable}
Nonetheless, \emph{not} all the utilities in the feasible set are close to each
other in $d^{\text{all}}_{p,r}$ distance:
 
 \begin{restatable}{prop}{propnobounddall}
   \label{prop: no bound d all}
   There exists an MDP $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r}$ and a policy $\pi^E$ for
      which there exists a pair of utilities $U_1,U_2\in\cU_{p,r,\pi^E}$ such
      that $d^{\text{all}}_{p,r}(U_1,U_2)= 1$.
 \end{restatable}
 
Intuitively, Propositions \ref{prop: transferring utilities p}-\ref{prop:
 imitating the expert}, \ref{prop: no bound d all}, prove that demonstrations of
 behavior in a \emph{single} MDP do not provide enough information on $U^E$ to
 obtain guarantees for applications $(i),(ii)$, $(iii)$. Instead, the following
 result shows that demonstrations in \emph{multiple} environments permit to
 mitigate this issue.
 
 \begin{restatable}[Multiple demonstrations]{prop}{propmultipledemonstrations}
 \label{prop: multiple demonstrations}
   Let $\cS,\cA,H$ be, respectively, any state space, action space, and horizon,
   satisfying $S\ge 3,A\ge2,H\ge 2$, and let $U^E\in\fU$ be any utility. If, for
   any possible dynamics $s_0,p$ and reward $r$, we are given the set of
   \emph{all} the
   deterministic optimal policies of the corresponding RS-MDP $\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,
   s_0,p,r,U^E}$, then we can \emph{uniquely} identify utility $U^E$.
 \end{restatable}

 Simply put, Proposition \ref{prop: multiple demonstrations} says that it
 suffices to observe the set of optimal policies induced by the expert utility
 in a certain set of MDPs for uniquely retrieving $U^E$. Its proof is
 constructive.



\section{Online Utility Learning with Generative Model}

 \label{sec: online utility learning}
 
 In the previous section, we have analyzed UL in the \emph{exact} setting. Here,
 we introduce a more realistic setting for UL, and we describe two efficient
 algorithms with theoretical guarantees to address it.

 We consider the \emph{online UL} problem setting with multiple
 demonstrations,\footnote{Asking for multiple demonstrations permits to
 alleviate the partial identifiability issues (Proposition \ref{prop:
 multiple demonstrations}).} which we now define.
 Let $U^E\in\fU$ be the utility function of the expert. Consider $N$ MDPs
 $\cM^i=\tuple{\cS^i,\cA^i,H,s_0^i,p^i,r^i}$, indexed by $i\in\dsb{N}$, that
 share the same horizon $H$. For each $\cM^i$, we \emph{know}
 $\cS^i,\cA^i,H,s_0^i,r^i$,
we have access to a \emph{generative sampling oracle} \citep{azar2013minimax}
 for the transition model $p^i$, which, given any triple $s,a,h$, returns a
 sample $s'\sim p^i_h(\cdot|s,a)$, and we are given a \emph{dataset}
 $\cD^{E,i}=\{\tuple{s_1^j,a_1^j,s_2^j,\dotsc,s_H^j,a_H^j,s_{H+1}^j}
 \}_{j\in\dsb{\tau^{E,i}}}$
 of $\tau^{E,i}$ trajectories collected by executing expert policy $\pi^{E,i}$,
 which is optimal for the RS-MDP $\cM^i_{U^E}$.
 Informally, the goal is to find $U^E$.

 \subsection{Challenges and Our Solution}\label{sec: our solution}
 To develop efficient algorithms for learning utilities \emph{in practice}, some
 challenges must be addressed.

 \textbf{Curse of Dimensionality.}~~Approximation techniques are needed for
  computing optimal policies in RS-MDPs (the enlarged state space is too large
  $|\cG^{p,r}_h|\propto (SA)^{(h-1)}\;\forall h$ \citep{wu2023risksensitive}),
  for storing return distributions (whose support may grow exponentially in the
  horizon $|\cG^{p,r}|\propto (SA)^H$ \citep{bellemare2023distributional}), and
  for storing utilities in $\fU$ (defined over the interval $[0,H]$).

  \textbf{Finite Data.}~~Some quantities of interest
  (i.e., policies and transition models) are not known exactly, but they must be
  \emph{estimated} from samples, introducing an estimation error.

  \textbf{Partial Identifiability.}~~Even in the exact setting,
  demonstrations of behavior are ``explained'' \emph{equally well} by
  \emph{infinitely} many utilities; thus, it is not clear which utility an
  algorithm should output.

 

 To address these challenges, our algorithms (Sections \ref{sec: caty}-
 \ref{sec: tractor}) adopt the following approaches.

 \textbf{Curse of Dimensionality.}~~We combine the $(i)$ \emph{discretization}
 approach in \citet{wu2023risksensitive} for the enlarged state space, with the
 $(ii)$ \emph{categorical representation} of \citet{bellemare2023distributional}
 for return distributions. Moreover, we consider $(iii)$ \emph{discretized}
 utilities.
 
 $(i)$ Fix parameter $\epsilon_0>0$, define sets
 $\cR\coloneqq\{0,\epsilon_0,2\epsilon_0
 ,\dotsc,\floor{1/\epsilon_0}\epsilon_0\}$,
 $\cY_h\coloneqq\{0,\epsilon_0,2\epsilon_0
 ,\dotsc,\floor{(h-1)/\epsilon_0}\epsilon_0\}$ as the
 $\epsilon_0$-coverings of $[0,1]$ and $[0,h-1]$ for all $h\in\dsb{H+1}$, and let
 $\cY\coloneqq\cY_{H+1},d\coloneqq|\cY|$.
 Intuitively, note that the summation of $h$ values in $\cR$ provides a value in
 $\cY_{h+1}$ for all $h$.
 Therefore, for any $i\in\dsb{N}$, let \scalebox{0.9}{$\overline{\cM}^i_{U^E}
 $}$\coloneqq\tuple{\cS^i,\cA^i,H,s_0^i,p^i,\overline{r}^i,U^E}$ be the RS-MDP
 with reward $\overline{r}^i$, obtained from $r^i$ as
 $\overline{r}^i_h(s,a)\coloneqq \Pi_{\cR}[r^i_h(s,a)]$ for all $s,a,h$.
 In this manner, the sets of partial returns of \scalebox{0.9}{$\overline{\cM}^i
 $} satisfy \scalebox{0.9}{$\cG^{p^i,\overline{r}^i}_h$}$\subseteq
 \cY_h\subseteq\cY$ for all $h$, thus the MDP
 $\fE[$\scalebox{0.9}{$\overline{\cM}^i_{U^E}$}$]$ has a state space with
 cardinality at most $Sd\le \cO(SH/\epsilon_0)$, which is no longer exponential
 in the horizon.
 $(ii)$ Denote as $\cQ\coloneqq\{q\in\Delta^{\dsb{d}}\,|\,
 \sum_{j\in\dsb{d}}q_j\delta_{y_j}\}$ the set of parametric probability
 distributions supported on $\cY$, where $y_1\coloneqq 0,y_2\coloneqq
 \epsilon_0,\dotsc,y_d\coloneqq \floor{H/\epsilon_0}\epsilon_0$ represent the
 \emph{ordered} items of set $\cY$.
 We construct the categorical representation $\text{Proj}_\cC(\eta)\in\cQ$ of an
 arbitrary (return) distribution $\eta\in\Delta^{[0,H]}$ through the operator
 $\text{Proj}_\cC$, defined in Eq. \eqref{eq: definition proj c}.
$(iii)$ We approximate utilities $U\in\fU$ with vectors
$\overline{U}\in\overline{\fU}\coloneqq$\scalebox{0.9}{$\overline{\fU}^\cY$} so
that $\overline{U}(y)=U(y)$ for all $y\in\cY$.
 
 In this way, we work with tractable approximations whose complexity is
 controlled by parameter $\epsilon_0$.
 
 \textbf{Finite Data.}~~We introduce the notion of \emph{utility
 compatibility} to cope with finite data.
 With multiple demonstrations, the true utility $U^E$ satisfies the \emph{hard}
 constraints \scalebox{0.9}{$J^{\pi^{E,i}}$}$(U^E;p^i,r^i)=J^*(U^E;p^i,r^i)$ for all
 $i\in\dsb{N}$. However, with finite data, our \emph{estimate} of
 \scalebox{0.9}{$J^{\pi^{E,i}}$}$(U^E;p^i,r^i)-J^*(U^E;p^i,r^i)$ might be \emph{different from
 zero} for some $i$, thus, we might get wrong in recognizing $U^E$ as the true
 expert's utility. Crucially, collecting more (but still finite) data does
 \emph{not} guarantee to obtain \emph{exactly} zero.
 Drawing inspiration from \citet{lazzati2024compatibility}, we relax these
 ``hard'' requirements by introducing a ``soft'' notion of constraints
 satisfaction, which we name \emph{utility compatibility}:

 \begin{defi}\label{defi: compatibility}
  Given MDP $\cM=\tuple{\cS,\cA,H,s_0,p,r}$ and policy $\pi^E$, the
 \emph{(non)compatibility} $\overline{\cC}_{p,r,\pi^E}:\fU\to[0,H]$ of utility
 $U\in\fU$ with $\pi^E$ in $\cM$ is: $\overline{\cC}_{p,r,\pie}(U)\coloneqq
 J^*(U;p,r)-$\scalebox{0.95}{$J^{\pi^{E}}$}$(U;p,r)$.
 \end{defi}
 Thanks to utility compatibility, we can \emph{quantify} the extent to which a
 utility $U$ is \emph{(non)compatible} with the (multiple) demonstrations by
 computing $\max_{i\in\dsb{N}}\overline{\cC}_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U)$.

 \textbf{Partial Identifiability.}~~We \emph{propose} to develop two \emph{practical} algorithms to fully
 characterize a set of utility functions:
 $(i)$ A \emph{utility classifier},\footnote{The notion of reward classifier can
 be found in \citet{lazzati2024compatibility}. We extend it to utilities.} that
 ``defines'' the boundaries of the set,
 and $(ii)$ a \emph{utility extractor}, that extracts a utility from
 the set.

 For a given accuracy threshold $\Delta>0$, define the set of
 $\Delta$-compatible utilities as:
 $\cU_\Delta\coloneqq\{U\in\fU\,|\,\max_{i\in\dsb{N}}\overline{\cC}_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U)
 \le\Delta\}$. $(i)$ We define a \emph{utility classifier} algorithm as a
 procedure that takes in input a utility $U\in\fU$, and outputs a boolean saying
 whether $U\in\cU_\Delta$ or not. Intuitively, being the input utility
 arbitrary, such algorithm permits to characterize the entire set $\cU_\Delta$.
 Furthermore, $(ii)$ we define a \emph{utility extractor} algorithm as a
 procedure that outputs an arbitrary utility $U$ from set $\cU_\Delta$.
 

 \subsection{\caty (\catylong)}
 \label{sec: caty}

 \caty is a \emph{utility classifier} algorithm. It classifies utilities $U$
 w.r.t. $\cU_\Delta$ by estimating the (non)compatibility
 \scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\cC}^i(U)\approx \cC_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U)$} for all
 $i\in\dsb{N}$, and, then, checking if $\max_{i \in
 \dsb{N}}$\scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\cC}^i(U)\le\Delta$}.
 As other algorithms \citep{jin2020RFE,lazzati2024compatibility}, \caty
 comprises two phases: an \emph{exploration} phase (Algorithm \ref{alg: caty
 exploration}), where we compute estimates $\{\widehat{p}^i\}_i$ by collecting
 $\tau^i$ samples from the generative model of each \scalebox{0.9}{$\cM^i$}, and
 a \emph{classification} phase (Algorithm \ref{alg: caty classification}), that
 takes in input a utility {\thinmuskip=1mu \medmuskip=1mu \thickmuskip=1mu
 $U\in\fU$}, estimates $\{\widehat{p}^i\}_i$, and datasets {\thinmuskip=1mu
 \medmuskip=1mu \thickmuskip=1mu$\{\cD^{E,i}\}_i$}, to construct estimates
 $\{$\scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\cC}^i$}$(U)\}_i$ for classifying $U$.

 Specifically, at Line \ref{line: discretize U}, we discretize the utility $U$.
 Next, for all $i\in\dsb{N}$, we construct estimates
 $\widehat{J}^{E,i}(U)\approx J^{\pi^{E,i}}(U;p^i,r^i)$ and
 $\widehat{J}^{*,i}(U)\approx J^*(U;p^i,r^i)$ as follows. At Line \ref{line: erd

 \approx\text{Proj}_\cC(\eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}) \approx
 \eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}$ through the \texttt{ERD} (Estimate Return
 Distribution) subroutine (Algorithm \ref{alg: erd}), and dataset $\cD^{E,i}$,
 while at Line \ref{line: est JE caty} we compute $\widehat{J}^{E,i}(U)$.
 At Line \ref{line: planning caty}, we approximate the optimal performance
 $J^*(U;p^i,r^i)$ in RS-MDP $\cM^i_U$ with the optimal performance
 $\widehat{J}^{*,i}(U)\coloneqq J^*(\overline{U};\widehat{p}^i,\overline{r}^i)$
 in RS-MDP
 $\fE[$\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\overline{\cM}}^i_{\overline{U}}$}$]\coloneqq
 \tuple{\cS^i,\cA^i,H,s_0^i,\widehat{p}^i,\overline{r}^i,\overline{U}}$, which
 is computed through \emph{value iteration} in the enlarged state space MDP
 $\fE[$\scalebox{0.8}{$\widehat{\overline{\cM}}^i_{\overline{U}}$}$]$ using the
 \texttt{PLANNING} subroutine (Algorithm \ref{alg: planning}).

 \caty enjoys the following guarantee: 
 \begin{restatable}{thr}{thrupperboundcatyoneu}
   \label{thr: caty upper bound 1 u}
   Let $\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1)$, and let $\cU$ be a subset of $\fU_L$
   containing the utilities to classify. If we set $\epsilon_0=
   \epsilon^2/(72HL^2)$, and if it holds that, for all $i\in\dsb{N}$:
   \begin{align*}
    & 
    \scalebox{0.9}{$ \displaystyle
    \text{if }|\cU|=1:\qquad
     \tau^{E,i}\le
     \widetilde{\cO}\Big(\frac{H^2}{\epsilon^2}\log\frac{N}{\delta}\Big),
     \qquad\tau^i\le\widetilde{\cO}\Big(\frac{SAH^4}{\epsilon^2}
     \log\frac{SAHNL}{\delta\epsilon}\Big), 
     $} 
     \\
     & 
     \scalebox{0.9}{$ \displaystyle
     \text{else }:\qquad\tau^{E,i}\le
     \widetilde{\cO}\Big(\frac{H^4 L^2}{\epsilon^4}\log\frac{HNL}{\delta\epsilon}\Big),
     \qquad\tau^i\le\widetilde{\cO}\Big(\frac{SAH^5}{\epsilon^2}
     \Big(S+\log\frac{SAHN}{\delta}\Big)\Big), 
     $}
   \end{align*}
   then, w.p. at least $1-\delta$, \caty \emph{correctly} classifies all the
   $U\in\cU$ that satisfy either $\max_{i}\overline{\cC}_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U)
   <\Delta-\epsilon$ (inside $\cU_\Delta$) or
   $\max_{i}\overline{\cC}_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U) >\Delta+\epsilon$ (outside
   $\cU_\Delta$).
 \end{restatable}
 Some observations are in order.
 First, note that $\Delta$ is arbitrary in $[0,H]$, and the sample complexity
 does not depend on it.
 If we have one utility to classify $|\cU|=1$, then $\propto S$ queries to the
generative model suffice instead of $\propto S^2$.
Note that $\epsilon_0$ represents a trade-off between
approximation and estimation error.
If we re-normalize utilities so that $U(H)=1$, then some $H$ terms in the bounds
disappear.
Intuitively, the Lipschitzianity assumption is necessary for approximating
\emph{continuous} utilities $U\in\cU$ with vectors in
$\overline{\fU}$.
Finally, observe that we can restrict the range of (non)compatibility
$[\Delta-\epsilon,\Delta+\epsilon]$ where \caty can make mistake with high
probability (w.h.p.) by collecting more data.
 
\subsection{\tractor (\tractorlong)}
\label{sec: tractor}

For simplicity, let $\overline{\fU}_L\coloneqq\overline{\fU}_L^\cY$ for $L>0$,
and let $\underline{\fU},\underline{\fU}_L,
\overline{\underline{\fU}},\overline{\underline{\fU}}_L,\underline{\cU}_\Delta$
be the analogous of, respectively, $\fU,\fU_L,
\overline{\fU},\overline{\fU}_L,\cU_\Delta$, but containing \emph{increasing}
functions instead of \emph{strictly-increasing} functions.\footnote{Note that,
for defining $\underline{\cU}_\Delta$, we extend also the definition of
(non)compatibility (Def. \ref{defi: compatibility}) to utilities in
$\underline{\fU}$.}

\tractor is a \emph{utility extractor} algorithm. For any $\Delta>0$, it aims to
 extract a utility $U$ from $\underline{\cU}_\Delta$ by performing \emph{online
 gradient descent} in the space of discretized $L$-Lipschitz utilities
 $\underline{\fU}_L$.
 It comprises two phases: an \emph{exploration} phase, that coincides with that
 of \caty (Algorithm \ref{alg: caty exploration}) and aims to compute estimates
 $\{\widehat{p}^i\}_i$ using $\{\tau^i\}_i$ samples, and an \emph{extraction}
 phase (Algorithm \ref{alg: tractor}), that takes in input estimates
 $\{\widehat{p}^i\}_i$, and datasets $\{\cD^{E,i}\}_i$, to construct a utility
 $\widehat{U}\in\overline{\underline{\fU}}_L$ to return.

Specifically, starting from
$\overline{U}_0\in\overline{\underline{\fU}}_L$,
we compute a sequence $\{\overline{U}_1,\dotsc, \overline{U}_T\}$ of utilities
in $\overline{\underline{\fU}}_L$ through an \emph{online projected gradient
descent} scheme, where the gradient $g_t$ is computed at Line \ref{line: compute
gt tractor}, and the update is carried out at Line \ref{line: gd update tractor}
with projection onto $\overline{\underline{\fU}}_L$.
Intuitively, we aim to minimize function $\max_{i\in\dsb{N}}
\overline{\cC}_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U)\le\sum_i
\overline{\cC}_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U)$ over set $\underline{\fU}_L$ (we upper
bound the max with the sum to work with gradients instead of subgradients), but
computing the gradient $\nabla_{U} \sum_{i\in\dsb{N}}
\overline{\cC}_{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}}(U) =\sum_i (\nabla_{U}
J^*(U;p^i,r^i)-\eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}})$ is not simple.
Thus, analogously to \citet{syed2007game,schlaginhaufen2024transferability}, we
replace {\thinmuskip=1mu \medmuskip=1mu \thickmuskip=1mu $\nabla_{U}
J^*(U;p^i,r^i)$} with {\thinmuskip=1mu \medmuskip=1mu \thickmuskip=1mu $\nabla_{U} J^{\pi^{*,i}_t}(U;p^i,r^i)
=\eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{*,i}_t}$}, where $\pi^{*,i}_t$ is the (fixed) optimal policy
in RS-MDP $\cM^i_{U_{t}}$ ($U_t\in\underline{\fU}_L$ satisfies
$U_t(y)=\overline{U}_t(y)$ for all $y\in\cY$), and we prove convergence.
Therefore, Lines \ref{line: erd tractor},
\ref{line: for2 tractor}-\ref{line: end for2 tractor}
approximate {\thinmuskip=1mu \medmuskip=1mu \thickmuskip=1mu $\sum_i(\widehat{\eta}_t^i-\widehat{\eta}^{E,i})\approx \sum_i
(\eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{*,i}_t}-\eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{E,i}})$} for all $t$.
In particular, Lines \ref{line: planning tractor}-\ref{line: line compute etati
tractor} approximate $\eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{*,i}_t}$ by passing through
{\thinmuskip=1mu \medmuskip=1mu \thickmuskip=1mu
$\widehat{\eta}^i_t\approx\eta^{\widehat{p}^i,\overline{r}^i,\widehat{\pi}^{*,i}_t}
\approx \eta^{p^i,r^i,\pi^{*,i}_t}$}, where $\widehat{\pi}^{*,i}_t$ is the
optimal policy for the RS-MDP {\thinmuskip=1mu \medmuskip=1mu \thickmuskip=1mu
$\widehat{\cM}^i_{\overline{U}_t}\coloneqq\tuple{\cS^i,\cA^i,H,s_0^i,
\widehat{p}^i, \overline{r}^i,\overline{U}_t}$}.
At Line \ref{line:
planning tractor} we compute through \emph{value iteration} (\texttt{PLANNING}
subroutine, Algorithm \ref{alg: planning}) the optimal policy
\scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\psi}^{*,i}_t$} for MDP
$\fE[$\scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\cM}^i_{\overline{U}_t}$}$]$.
Then, at Line \ref{line: rollout tractor},
we collect the return of
$K$ trajectories obtained by executing \scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\psi}^{*,i}_t$} in MDP
$\fE[$\scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\cM}^i_{\overline{U}_t}$}$]$ (\texttt{ROLLOUT} subroutine, Algorithm
\ref{alg: rollout}), which is equivalent to
playing $\widehat{\pi}^{*,i}_t$ in $\scalebox{0.9}{$\widehat{\cM}^i_{\overline{U}_t}$}$.
Finally, at Line \ref{line: line compute etati tractor}, we use
this data to compute the \emph{empirical} estimate $\widehat{\eta}^i_t$.
\tractor enjoys the following guarantee:
\begin{restatable}{thr}{thrupperboundtractor}\label{thr: tractor upper bound}

    Let $\epsilon,\delta\in(0,1), L>0$, and assume that
    $U^E\in\underline{\fU}_L$.
  If we execute \tractor with parameters $\epsilon_0=\epsilon^2/(80N^2L^2H),T\ge
   \cO\big(N^4H^4L^2/\epsilon^4\big),
   K\ge\widetilde{\cO}\big(
   N^2H^2\log\frac{NHL}{\delta\epsilon}/\epsilon^2\big),
   \alpha=\sqrt{\floor{H/\epsilon_0}-1}H/(2N\sqrt{T})$, an arbitrary
   $\overline{U}_0\in\overline{\underline{\fU}}_L$, and if it holds that, for
   all $i\in\dsb{N}$:
   \begin{align*}
      \scalebox{0.9}{$  \displaystyle
      \tau^{E,i}\ge 
      \widetilde{\cO}\Big(
        \frac{H^4N^4L^2}{\epsilon^4}\log\frac{NHL}{\delta\epsilon}
      \Big),\qquad
      \tau^i\ge
      \widetilde{\cO}\Big(
        \frac{N^2SAH^5}{\epsilon^2}\Big(
        S+\log\frac{SAHN}{\delta}  
        \Big)
      \Big), 
      $}
  \end{align*}
   then, w.p. at least $1-\delta$, for any $\Delta\ge\epsilon$, \tractor

\end{restatable}

\section{Conclusion}


In this paper, we proposed a novel descriptive model of behavior in MDPs, we
formalized the UL problem as that of learning the risk attitude of an agent from
demonstrations, and we characterised the partial identifiability of the expert's
utility. In addition, we have described two provably efficient algorithms for
estimating the compatibility of a utility with demonstrations, and for
extracting a compatible utility. They have been empirically validated through
two proof-of-concept experiments.

\textbf{Future directions.}~~ This paper opens up many important questions. To
quantify the model mis-specification, to use function approximation, to conduct
an empirical study on the horizon used by humans for planning
\citep{carton2016humanplanninghorizon}, to combine demonstrations with other
feedbacks \citep{jeon2020rewardrational},
to learn both $r$ and $U$, to extend imitation learning approaches (e.g., GAIL
\citep{ho2016generativeadversarialimitationlearning}) or the maximum entropy
framework with utilities, to improve the model in Eq. \eqref{eq: model expert rs
mdp} with negative rewards and prospect theory \citep{kahneman1979prospect}, and
many others.

\emph{We believe that most of the IRL literature shall be
extended under the proposed, more expressive, framework to construct more
accurate algorithms for IRL and UL.}
