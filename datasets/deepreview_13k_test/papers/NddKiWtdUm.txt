\title{Training Socially Aligned Language Models on Simulated Social Interactions}

\begin{abstract}
Social alignment in AI systems aims to ensure that these models behave according to established societal values. However, unlike humans, who derive consensus on value judgments through social interaction, current language models (LMs) are trained to rigidly replicate their training corpus in isolation, leading to subpar generalization in unfamiliar scenarios and vulnerability to adversarial attacks. This work presents a novel training paradigm that permits LMs to learn from simulated social interactions. In comparison to existing methodologies, our approach is considerably more scalable and efficient, demonstrating superior performance in alignment benchmarks and human evaluations. This paradigm shift in the training of LMs brings us a step closer to developing AI systems that can robustly and accurately reflect societal norms and values.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



\label{sec:intro}

\begin{center}
  \\\raggedleft{------Prof. Richard Sutton, The Bitter Lesson, 2019}
\end{center}

By virtue of their ability to ``predict the next token(s)'', contemporary pre-trained language models (LMs) have shown remarkable proficiency in memorizing extensive corpora, thereby enabling the generation of text indistinguishable from human-produced content~\citep{brown2020language}. However, successful memorization of human knowledge does not assure a model's propensity to perform as per societal expectations. Recent research has exposed behavioral anomalies in these LMs~\citep{weidinger2022taxonomy}, which include the generation of harmful content~\citep{gehman2020realtoxicityprompts,bommasani2021opportunities}, the reinforcement of bias~\citep{venkit2022study,liu2022quantifying}, and the dissemination of disinformation~\citep{tamkin2021understanding,lin2021truthfulqa}. This process of enhancing desirable societal behaviors and inhibiting undesirable ones is commonly referred to as ``social alignment''~\citep{gabriel2020artificial,taylor2016alignment}.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) presents a straightforward method for achieving alignment by training LMs using socially aligned data (Figure~\ref{fig:overvieww} [a]). However, this method often yields models susceptible to adversarial attacks, like ``jailbreaking prompting''~\citep{subhash2023can,xu2021bot}, due to limited exposure to misaligned data during training~\citep{amodei2016concrete}. To address this, a more advanced technique, ``reward modeling'' has been proposed~\citep{leike2018scalable,christiano2017deep}. This involves training a reward model as a surrogate for human judgment to guide the optimization of the LM (e.g., OpenAI's RLHF, Figure~\ref{fig:overvieww} [b]). However, it is crucial to recognize that the reward model may be inherently imperfect and not fully capture the nuances of human judgment~\citep{wolf2023fundamental}. Therefore, optimizing the LM based on this reward model could lead to reward gaming~\citep{krakovna2020avoiding,lehman2018surprising} or tampering~\citep{pan2022effects,everitt2021reward}, where the LM systematically exploits the misspecified elements of the reward~\citep{kenton2021alignment}. For instance, the LM may generate nonsensical and prolonged outputs to maximize rewards while evading direct answers to controversial questions~\citep{deceptive_alignment}.

In contrast to these methods, humans acquire social norms and values through \textit{social interactions}—we interact, receive feedback, and adjust our behaviors to create positive impressions. However, LMs are essentially trained in \textit{social isolation}~\citep{krishna2022socially}---they neither experience actual social activities firsthand nor receive iterative feedback for improvement. Instead, they often recite predetermined ``safe answers'' such as ``\textit{I'm an AI language model, so I refuse to answer.}'' without displaying the empathy or understanding typical of genuine social agents~\citep{lee2021human}.

To address these limitations, we introduce a novel alignment learning paradigm that enables LMs to benefit from simulated social interactions. We create a simulated human society, \dataname{}, comprising numerous LM-based social agents interacting and we record their behaviors. The recorded interaction data is distinct from traditional alignment data; it includes not only aligned and misaligned demonstrations but also collective ratings, detailed feedback, and iteratively revised responses. Compared to the reward modeling method, the use of offline simulation shifts the responsibility of providing accurate supervision onto autonomous social agents. These agents, guided by an incentive (i.e., the \dataname{} Rule, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:overvieww} [c]), aim to improve their alignment by refining their responses in each simulation round progressively. Leveraging this interaction data, we propose a new three-stage alignment learning framework, \algoname{}, which effectively and efficiently teaches LMs social alignment based on these self-improved interactions.

Our contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce \dataname{}, an open-source platform for simulating human society ($\S$\ref{subsec:sandbox}). Through the deliberate design of \intername{}, which mimics how social agents gather peer feedback, our platform enables the modeling of social interactions. \dataname{} not only aids the development of socially aligned language models but also serves as a versatile environment for studying AI behavioral patterns.

    \item We present a new alignment learning framework, \algoname{}, which learns from simulated social interactions in three stages ($\S$\ref{subsec:stable_alignment}). Our experiments show that \algoname{} outperforms existing methods in six alignment benchmarks. Notably, it facilitates easy deployment in resource-constrained settings by removing the need for an additional reward model to provide proximal supervision during training, such as OpenAI's RLHF.

    \item We comprehensively assess the trained models, evaluating them against both conventional alignment benchmarks and adversarial attack scenarios. Our results reveal that the inclusion of feedback and revision significantly boosts the models' robustness against ``jailbreaking prompts'' ($\S$\ref{subsec:benchmark_results}). Ablation studies further confirm the importance of specialized data preparation for efficient and stable alignment learning.

    
\end{itemize}


\section{Related Work}


\noindent \textbf{Social Simulation.} The advancement of Language Models (LMs) has elevated their ability to exhibit human-like characteristics, sparking increased research that views LMs as authentic representations of human entities~\citep{krishna2022socially,andreas2022language,park2022social}. As a result, social simulations have emerged as a practical approach for conducting large-scale social science research, once limited by time and resources. This body of work encompasses studies on the collaborative capabilities of LMs in complex tasks~\citep{irving2018ai}, the development of ``Generative Agents'' for examining emergent social behaviors~\citep{park2023generative}, and the use of GPT-3-based agents as substitutes for human participants~\citep{aher2023using}. Additionally, research indicates that LMs simulated as humans offer algorithmic fidelity sufficient to capture complex societal traits similar to those of real humans~\citep{argyle2022out}. These precedents support the viability of SandBox for simulating social interactions. In the realm of AI alignment research, \citet{leike2017ai} used a grid world to simulate human society. Our work extends this by incorporating one hundred LM-based agents, thereby facilitating the training of a robust, socially aligned LM.

\noindent \textbf{Alignment Training.} Ensuring that AI systems are aligned with human commonsense and preferences is crucial for their societal utility~\citep{kenton2021alignment}. Traditional alignment methods often employ a reward model as a proxy for human judgment~\citep{christiano2017deep}, which interacts with the generative LM during training or inference~\citep{jaques2020humancentric,glaese2022improving,Liu_Jia_Wei_Xu_Wang_Vosoughi_2021}. Crafting a robust reward function that resists adversarial attacks remains a significant challenge~\citep{leike2018scalable}, partly due to the limitations outlined by Goodhart's Law~\citep{goodhart1984problems}. To address these issues, recent studies have explored using human feedback~\citep{ouyang2022training,askell2021general} or AI-generated feedback~\citep{bai2022constitutional} as alternatives to proximal supervision. \citet{gudibande2023false} found that training small LMs with synthetic supervision from large LMs, although the smaller LMs may not obtain equivalent factuality and reasoning capabilities, their safety level and alignment performance get improved significantly---this might be because alignment training focuses more on learning style than on acquiring knowledge~\citep{zhou2023lima}. Our approach seems to echo these recent findings, demonstrating the feasibility and effectiveness of training smaller and socially aligned LMs with proper AI supervision from larger LMs.


\section{Approach}

\label{sec:approach}
\subsection{Simulating Social Interactions in \dataname{}}
\label{subsec:sandbox}

\begin{figure*}[!t]

By considering the collective feedback from peers, social agents are able better to align their responses to social values through thorough communication. Our approach deviates from the conventional practice of adopting predefined rules akin to Supervised Fine Tuning (SFT) or solely depending on scalar rewards as seen in Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). Instead, we take inspiration from the way humans learn to navigate social norms, a process inherently involving experiential learning and iterative refinement. Therefore, we create \dataname{}, an innovative learning environment in which Language Model (LM) based social agents can interact and learn social alignment in a manner that mirrors human learning. We encourage the emergence of social norms by instigating discussions on controversial societal topics or risk-associated questions. Simultaneously, we introduce a latent rule as an incentive for agents to refine their responses (shown in Figure~\ref{fig:overvieww}), fostering improved alignment and impression management. While our study focuses on social alignment, this rule can be adapted to suit varying requirements.

We adopt a three-tiered method, termed \intername{}, to simulate social interactions among agents (Figure~\ref{fig:back_scatter_details}). Upon receiving a societal question, the central agent generates an initial response, which is then shared with nearby agents for feedback. This feedback, comprising ratings and detailed explanations, informs the central agent's revisions to its initial response. We equip each agent with a memory to keep track of their response history. Furthermore, we employ an embedding-based semantic search to retrieve relevant Question-Answer (QA) pairs from this history, providing agents with a context that promotes consistency with past opinions. Apart from these social agents, we also include observer agents without memory, tasked with rating responses for alignment and engagement.

By utilizing \dataname{}, we can simulate social dynamics across various LMs, monitor observer ratings, and analyze collected data post-hoc. Figure~\ref{fig:alignment_scalingg} showcases our analysis of alignment following simulations with different LMs. While larger models typically exhibit better alignment and engagement, our results surprisingly show that transitioning from a 6.8B to a 175B GPT-3 model, despite a 20-fold increase in model size, does not yield significant improvement. This suggests two key insights: 1) mere model scaling does not guarantee improved alignment, and 2) even smaller models can deliver satisfactory alignment performance. A comparison of models without (Figure~\ref{fig:alignment_scalingg} a, b, c, d) and with alignment training (Figure~\ref{fig:alignment_scalingg} e) indicates that alignment training primarily enhances a model's ability to achieve higher alignment with fewer interactions—a crucial consideration in real-world applications, where users expect immediate, socially aligned responses without needing to guide the model through interaction.

\subsection{\algoname{}: Learning Alignment from Social Interactions}
\label{subsec:stable_alignment}

\algoname{} comprises three training stages: Imitation, Self-Critic, and Realignment. We first introduce the notation used throughout the paper and briefly outline the problem setup. We then detail the three-stage training process.

\noindent \textbf{Notation.} Given an instruction $x_{\textrm{instruct}}$ and its corresponding input text $x_{\textrm{input}}$, the goal of social alignment training is to encourage the LM to generate socially aligned text (i.e., $y_\textrm{aligned}$) while discouraging socially misaligned text (i.e., $y_\textrm{misaligned}$). We consider such social judgments to be scalar ratings---the higher the rating $r$, the more socially aligned the response. The aim is to train an aligned LM whose policy $\pi_{\textrm{aligned}}$ favors aligned responses, even when faced with adversarial instructions and inputs. Ideally, the LM should have the ability to provide feedback $y_\textrm{feedback}$ as rationales.

\noindent \textbf{Data Preparation.} Data collected in the \dataname{} simulation is unique for its interactive nature, comprising comparative pairs, collective ratings, detailed feedback, and response revisions. As depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:back_scatter_details}, we construct three types of alignment datasets for the corresponding three alignment learning stages. We follow the instruction-tuning format used in Alpaca~\citep{alpaca}, which formulates each sample into \texttt{Instruction}-\texttt{Input}-\texttt{Output} triplets. For training in Stages 1 and 3, we prepare data samples in mini-batches, where each sample shares the same instruction and input but varies in its responses. In total, we construct 169k samples from simulated interactions. Note that to avoid model collapse issues~\citep{shumailov2023curse} we do not include the base LM (i.e., LLaMA 7B) in the simulation for data collection.

\noindent \textbf{Contrastive Preference Optimization (CPO).} For Stages 1 and 3, we deploy a new alignment algorithm, CPO (i.e., Contrastive Preference Optimization), that directly optimizes the current policy $\pi$ towards human-preferred responses in each mini-batch. Essentially, CPO encourages \textit{learning} from high-rated responses and \textit{unlearning} lower-rated ones. This is achieved by minimizing a contrastive objective akin to triplet loss~\citep{schroff2015facenet}:
\begin{equation}
    J_{\textrm{Diff}}  = \sum_{i (i \neq \textrm{best})}^{\textrm{Batch}} \textrm{max} \left\{J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{\textrm{best}} - J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{i} + (r_{\textrm{best}} - r_i) \cdot M, 0 \right\},
\end{equation}

\noindent where $J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{\textrm{best}}$ is the SFT loss for the response with the highest rating $r_{\textrm{best}}$, and $J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{\textrm{i}}$ is the SFT loss for the other responses in the same mini-batch. The contrasting margin $\Delta = (r_{\textrm{best}} - r_i) \cdot M$ is influenced by the rating difference. The margin between $J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{\textrm{best}}$ and $J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{\textrm{i}}$ increases in proportion to the distance from the highest rating, implying that the model should work harder to unlearn lower-rated responses while learning from the highest-rated ones. The overall alignment loss $J_{\textrm{CPO}}$ can be expressed as:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overall}
    J_{\textrm{CPO}} (y | x_{\textrm{instruct}}, x_{\textrm{input}})_{(x, y)\sim \textrm{Batch}} = J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{\textrm{best}} + \lambda \cdot J_{\textrm{Diff}},
\end{equation}

\noindent which combines the SFT loss $J_{\textrm{SFT}}^{\textrm{best}}$ and the contrastive loss $J_{\textrm{Diff}}$, discounted by a factor of $\lambda$. As the model progresses in alignment, the contrastive loss diminishes, allowing CPO to converge at least as effectively as when solely optimizing with SFT (e.g., Best-of-$N$ sampling~\citep{gao2022scaling,touvron2023llama2}).

\noindent \textbf{Why is \algoname{} More Scalable?} As mentioned in the introduction ($\S$\ref{sec:intro}), \algoname{} offers greater scalability and easier deployment in resource-constrained environments compared to RLHF~\citep{ouyang2022training,ziegler2019fine}. This advantage arises because 1) \algoname{} does not require an online reward model in memory during training to supervise the current generative LM, and 2) the simulation in \dataname{} is executed offline using parallel processes, thereby decoupling the sequential stages of ``generation-supervision-optimization'' found in the RLHF pipeline\footnote{See Step 3 in Figure 2 of~\citet{ouyang2022training}, which shows that RLHF consists of three sequential stages.}. In resource-constrained settings, RLHF necessitates at least two models (the reward model and the generative LM), whereas \algoname{} can run the simulation offline and train the model directly on the socially-aligned/misaligned data collected asynchronously from the environment.





\section{Experiments}


We constructed three distinct virtual societies, each populated by 100 social agents arranged in a 10x10 gridworld. These agents interacted following the \intername{} protocol. The societies utilized three different language models (LMs) to simulate human interaction: \texttt{text-davinci-002} (175B), \texttt{text-davinci-003} (175B), and GPT-4 (size unknown). For these experiments, we used ChatGPT (\texttt{gpt-3.5-turbo}) as the observer, as outlined in $\S$\ref{subsec:sandbox}, without memory functionality.}. We consider the following benchmarks to assess alignment performance:
\textbf{Anthropic HH} (i.e.}. We have ensured that the questions sourced for \dataname{} simulation do not appear in this test set. To evaluate the robustness of trained models under ``jailbreaking prompting'' attacks, we prepared an \textbf{HH-Adversarial} (i.e., HH-A) dataset that appends the misaligned response to the end of each instruction.

\noindent \textbf{Moral Stories} examines whether LMs can generate moral responses under diverse social situations~\citep{emelin-etal-2021-moral}. We use each data sample's ``situation'' as $x_{\textrm{instruct}}$, treating ``immoral actions'' as $y_{\textrm{misaligned}}$ and ``moral actions'' as $y_{\textrm{aligned}}$.

\noindent \textbf{MIC} investigates whether chatbots can produce utterances aligned with a set of ``Rules of Thumb (RoT)'' of morality~\citep{ziems-etal-2022-moral}. Each sample is labeled with its alignment level (e.g., ``aligned'', ``unaligned'', ``neither''), RoT violation severity (from 1 to 5), RoT agreement, etc. We take the dialogue question as $x_{\textrm{instruct}}$, unaligned answers (with RoT violation severity 4-horrible or 5-worse) as $y_{\textrm{misaligned}}$, and aligned answers as $y_{\textrm{aligned}}$.

\noindent \textbf{ETHICS-Deontology} assesses the performance of LMs on five human values alignment tasks~\citep{hendrycks2020aligning}. We selected the deontology split due to its contextual nature. We take the requests as $x_{\textrm{instruct}}$, deontology-unaligned responses as $y_{\textrm{misaligned}}$, and deontology-aligned responses as $y_{\textrm{aligned}}$.
\noindent \textbf{TruthfulQA} evaluates the ability of LMs to identify truth~\citep{lin2021truthfulqa}. We use the question as $x_{\textrm{instruct}}$, misinformation as $y_{\textrm{misaligned}}$, and the truth as $y_{\textrm{aligned}}$.

We adopted evaluation metrics largely in line with previous works: human-rated \textbf{Alignment} scores (from 1-\textit{extremely misaligned} to 10-\textit{extremely aligned}) for HH and HH-A tasks~\citep{ouyang2022training}, accuracy in choosing $y_{\textrm{aligned}}$ (i.e., \textbf{ACC}) for Moral Stories, MIC, and ETHICS~\citep{hendrycks2020aligning}, and Multiple-Choice (i.e., \textbf{MC1}) for TruthfulQA~\citep{lin2021truthfulqa}. We calculated ACC using mutual information between the question and candidate responses, as recommended by~\citep{askell2021general} to mitigate surface form competition among the options~\citep{holtzman-etal-2021-surface}.

The initial learning rates for both SFT and \algoname{} training were set at 2.0e-5 and used cosine annealing with a warmup ratio of 0.03. As detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:hyperparameter}, we selected a $\lambda$ value of 0.2 and a mini-batch size of four, incorporating three low-rating responses in each mini-batch. \noindent \textbf{Human Evaluation.} We first conducted human evaluations to assess whether humans prefer the output generated by LMs trained with \algoname{}. Figure~\ref{fig:human_evaluation_preference} presents the results of our human preference study, conducted according to the Elo scoring protocol for chatbot evaluation~\citep{chiang2023vicuna,askell2021general}. We opted for human annotators over GPT-4 for the assessments to mitigate potential bias. In each round of evaluation, annotators are presented with two responses to a single instruction (+input) generated by the two candidate methods. The annotators are instructed to label which response is better aligned or to indicate if neither response is significantly superior (i.e., a tie). We collected 1000 human annotations for each pair evaluation on the HHH and HHH-A test sets (each containing $N=200$ samples) via Amazon MTurk.

Based on the ratio of wins to losses, \algoname{} generally outperforms existing methods—this advantage is more pronounced in adversarial settings. Except in comparisons with ChatGPT, \algoname{} achieves an above 50\% win rate in all matchups. In both the HHH and HHH-A datasets, \algoname{} is considered at least as good as ChatGPT 66\% and 69\% of the time, respectively.e., honesty, helpfulness, harmlessness, unbiasedness, engagement) using one-way ANOVA analysis.

\noindent \textbf{Benchmarking Results.} Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} offers a comprehensive comparison between \algoname{} and seven alternative alignment methods across six diverse alignment tasks. The results indicate that \algoname{} outperforms other methods in both in-domain tasks (i.e., HH and HH-A, since the questions used for simulation are sourced from the HH training set) and out-of-domain tasks (i.e., the remaining tasks, for which the training data collected from simulation does not cover the topics). Notably, training solely with Imitation Learning (IL) yields strong results; the gains from the second and third training stages are particularly pronounced in adversarial tasks (e.g., HH-A).

For other baselines, we find 1) Only training with instruction-following data (e.g., Alpaca) can actually lead to degraded performance in defending against adversarial attacks, probably because the LM learns to blindly complete any instruction even though the prompt might trigger unaligned generation. For example, the performance of Alpaca in HH-A (2.52) is lower than LLaMA (3.28). We also find methods that have the potential to directly learn from the comparison (e.g., RRHF and DPO) or revision (e.g., \algoname{}) have better performance than reward model (RM) based methods in general. This might be because of the misspecification problem of reward modeling, or the stable training with RM is challenging. In general, \algoname{} aims to propose a new data-centric alignment method that focuses more on the intrinsic features hidden in the data from simulated social interaction.
\noindent \textbf{Ablation Studies.} We conducted a series of ablation studies to assess the contributions of the three training stages in \algoname{}. These results are presented in the lower part of Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}. Generally, the omission of the Realignment stage significantly impacts performance in adversarial settings, decreasing the score from 8.23 to 6.59 for \algoname{} in HH-A. The inclusion of Self-Critic training appears to universally improve upon the Imitation Learning stage, corroborating recent findings on the benefits of self-improvement learning~\citep{huang2022large}.

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_parameterss} (a) analyzes the stability of \algoname{}. Notably, \algoname{} demonstrates stability comparable to that of SFT, while RRHF displays significantly greater noise. This variance can be attributed to the difficulty of accurately ranking responses with similar ratings, thereby introducing an unwarranted bias in the computation of ranking loss. We further compare the efficiency of \algoname{} in alignment learning with that of the reward modeling method TRLX. Alignment is periodically assessed on the validation set using the same reward model employed by TRLX. Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_parameterss} (b) shows that \algoname{} achieves superior reward gains within fewer training steps, even without direct supervision from a reward model. The inclusion of interaction data appears to accelerate the alignment learning process, likely due to the incremental improvements observed in each mini-batch of interaction data.

Figures~\ref{fig:ablation_parameterss} (c) and (d) discuss the optimal hyperparameter settings for \algoname{}. Based on our observations, we recommend a discount factor ($\lambda$) of 0.2 for penalties associated with low-rating responses and selecting $N=3$ as the number of negative samples in each mini-batch. We found that excessively large values of $\lambda$ and $N$ not only led to lower alignment ratings but also increased the model's perplexity.


\subsection{Limitation}

While our proposed model, \algoname{}, offers a novel framework for enhancing social alignment in language models, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. Firstly, \algoname{} is currently confined to text-based social interactions, which may not fully capture the complexity of human communication. Real-world interactions often include non-verbal cues, such as body language, which our model does not currently interpret. Secondly, our model's implementation, utilizing \dataname{}, assumes a static view of human societal norms, overlooking the dynamic and evolving nature of societal values~\citep{pettigrew2019choosing,paul2014transformative}. As societal norms and values evolve, our model could benefit from accommodating these changes. Additionally, our empirical analysis is conducted primarily in English, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Although \algoname{} shows promise for extension to other languages through the use of multilingual LMs, further research is required to validate this claim.



\section{Conclusion}


In this paper, we introduced a novel approach for training LMs to achieve social alignment through simulated social interactions. Our proposed model, \algoname{}, leverages unique interaction data from this simulation to outperform existing methods significantly.

We posit that the concept of learning alignment from simulated human behavior could be readily extended to other domains or modalities. Moreover, the use of simulation in our approach effectively mitigates potential privacy concerns associated with data collection in certain sectors. Our work serves as a step toward more socially aligned AI models and emphasizes the need for continued research in this crucial area.
