\title{Deep graph kernel point processes}

\begin{abstract}
    Point process models are widely used for continuous asynchronous event data, where each data point includes time and additional information called ``marks'', which can be locations, nodes, or event types. This paper presents a novel point process model for discrete event data over graphs, where the event interaction occurs within a latent graph structure. 
    Our model builds upon Hawkes's classic influence kernel-based formulation in the original self-exciting point processes work to capture the influence of historical events on future events' occurrence. The key idea is to represent the influence kernel by Graph Neural Networks (GNN) to capture the underlying graph structure while harvesting the strong representation power of GNNs. Compared with prior works focusing on directly modeling the conditional intensity function using neural networks, our kernel presentation herds the repeated event influence patterns more effectively by combining statistical and deep models, achieving better model estimation/learning efficiency and superior predictive performance. Our work significantly extends the existing deep spatio-temporal kernel for point process data, which is inapplicable to our setting due to the fundamental difference in the nature of the observation space being Euclidean rather than a graph. We present comprehensive experiments on synthetic and real-world data to show the superior performance of the proposed approach against the state-of-the-art in predicting future events and uncovering the relational structure among data.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}



{\small{\bf Keywords:}
    Point processes over graphs, Deep kernel, Graph neural networks
}
\label{sec:introduction}

Asynchronous discrete event data, where each data point includes time and additional information called ``marks'', are ubiquitous in modern applications such as crime \citep{zhu2022spatiotemporal}, health care \citep{wei2023granger}, earthquake events \citep{ogata1988statistical, zhu2021imitation}, traffic modeling \cite{zhu2021spatio}, and so on. In contemporary applications, the collection of discrete events often reveals an underlying latent graph structure, leading to the widespread adoption of models incorporating graph structures for various purposes. Point processes over latent graphs are a popular model for such data, where the graph nodes can be introduced to capture event marks, such as locations or event types.

Classic one- and multi-dimensional temporal, self-exciting point process models introduced by Hawkes \citep{hawkes1971spectra} leverage an event influence kernel function to capture the impact of historical events on future events' occurrence. The influence kernel takes an exponentially decaying form, often not expressive enough to capture complex influence mechanisms. Recently, there have been many successes in deep point process models that represent the influence kernel using neural networks for temporal-only kernels \citep{zhu2021deep}, spatio-temporal kernels \citep{okawa2021dynamic}, and non-stationary kernels \citep{dong2022spatio, zhu2022neural}. Such works achieve competitive performance through efficient modeling of the influence kernel compared to point processes that model the conditional intensity function using neural networks. The key lies in that modeling event influence through a kernel captures the repeated influence patterns more effectively by combining statistical and deep models, thus achieving better model estimation/learning efficiency and superior predictive performance. 

Despite much success in prior work on deep kernel modeling of point processes, there has been limited work in exploiting underlying graph structures of multi-dimensional point processes when characterizing the influence kernel. Meanwhile, recent developments in Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) offer potential solutions to modeling deep graph kernels with point process data by harvesting their representation power. However, how to properly adopt GNNs into point processes while preserving the statistical model interpretability remains an open question.

This paper presents a novel point process model with a deep graph influence kernel for discrete event data over graphs. The graph kernel is represented by a properly encoded GNN architecture to capture the underlying dynamics of event influence. Moreover, the kernel architecture proposes a unified framework for integrating various GNN structures with point processes via localized graph filter basis functions.
We also present a computationally efficient and flexible learning scheme by considering two types of loss functions: the commonly used maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and a new least-square estimation (LSE) scheme. We can allow general types of non-stationary, multi-hop influence (which can be negative), and a log-barrier penalty in the loss function ensures the non-negative constraint for the conditional intensity function. 

The modeling of the deep graph influence kernel also enables the characterization of dynamic graph influence through asynchronous discrete point process observations. The proposed kernel can capture the influence among discrete events originating from various nodes at different times with the encoded GNN architectures. The temporal relationships of these events can further represent the time-evolving interactions among graph nodes. Meanwhile, leveraging the power of Graph Attention Networks (GATs) \citep{velickovic2017graph}, our kernel can recover the underlying graph structure through the learned attention weights between nodes.


 

Our work significantly extends the existing deep spatio-temporal kernel for point process data, which cannot be applied in our setting since the observation space is fundamentally different: the spatio-temporal point processes are for events that occurred in physical Euclidean space, and point processes over graphs represent vicinity using nodes and edges. 
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}

    \item Our proposed method explicitly models the influence kernel in point processes via GNNs instead of typical intensity-based models. This permits greater expressivity of inter-event-category contributions, including non-stationary, multi-hop exciting, and inhibiting effects. Furthermore, the deep graph kernel can capture dynamic graph influence, yielding clear information about the relational structure in graph point processes.

    \item  The localized graph filter basis allows the deep kernel to go beyond simple distance-based influence for graphs representing events in space, providing a model structure for non-spatial graphs such as traffic or social networks. Meanwhile, a larger class of GNN models can be incorporated within our framework, enabling broader versatility in real-world applications.

    \item Comprehensive experiments demonstrate the benefits of including latent graph structures in the deep kernel modeling over the state-of-the-art in simulated and real data settings. Our method applies to a wide array of point process data settings, including events generated by infrastructural, climatic, and social phenomena.
    
\end{enumerate}



\subsection{Related Works}
There are various deep learning point process models based on {\it modeling the conditional intensity function} using neural networks (rather than the influence kernel), such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs)~\citep{du2016recurrent,mei2017neural}. Due to advances in attention models for sequential data modeling~\citep{vaswani2017attention}, RNN approaches have been surpassed by self-attention-based approaches \citep{zhang2020self, zuo2020transformer}. These RNN and self-attention methods provide expressive models for the conditional intensity; however, they often lack statistical interpretability coming from the original ``influence kernel'' approach, and they do not consider graph structures.
Modeling point processes with graph information has been explored in early attempts. Classical multivariate Hawkes processes \citep{reinhart2018review} assume a parametric form of the conditional intensity. A recent work \citep{fang2023group} develops the novel Group Network Hawkes Process that can account for heterogeneous nodal characteristics using a parametric network model. Our approach differs from parametric point processes by assuming a deep graph-based influence kernel.
Many modern approaches \citep{pan2023graphinformed, yang2021transformer, zhang2021learning, zuo2020transformer} adopt non-graph neural networks incorporated with the graph structure, rather than directly incorporating GNN structures in the models. 
For example, A-NHP \citep{yang2021transformer} and THP-S \citep{zuo2020transformer} adopt attention-based mechanisms with additional constraints on the learned attention weights posted by the graph structure, which differs from the Graph Attention Network (GAT) \citep{velickovic2017graph}. These approaches mainly consider the single-hop, adjacency-based influence over the latent graph.
The Geometric Hawkes Process (GHP) \citep{shang2019geometric} integrates graph convolution RNNs with the Hawkes process to estimate the parameters of the conditional intensity function with a parametric (exponentially decaying) kernel, achieving enhanced model expressiveness. 
In our work, incorporating GNN architectures in the influence kernel without any parametric constraints permits the flexible and interpretable recovery of complex event dependency, such as multi-hop influence mechanisms over the graph topology.
Our work is related to GNNs, which have seen wide applications in areas including temporal phenomena on graphs \citep{longa2023graph, wu2020comprehensive}.
Graph convolutions incorporate spatial or spectral convolutions based upon the adjacency matrix or graph Laplacian~\citep{bruna2013spectral}. An early attempt applying spatial convolutions is the Diffusion-Convolutional Neural Network (DCNN)~\citep{atwood2016diffusion}. Other spatial-based approaches include attention models ~\citep{brody2021attentive, dwivedi2020generalization, velickovic2017graph}. Prototypical spectral convolutions are utilized in Chebnet~\citep{defferrard2016convolutional} and graph convolutional networks (GCN) \citep{kipf2016semi}, with recent extensions including auto-regressive moving average (ARMA) spectral convolutions~\citep{bianchi2021graph} and the Simple Spectral Graph Convolution (SSGC)~\citep{zhu2021simple}. Modern approaches incorporate both local and global features, such as L3Net~\citep{cheng2020graph} and General, Powerful, Scalable (GPS) Graph Transformer~\citep{rampavsek2022recipe}. 


Prior work has been done aiming to infer graph influence or structures given observed data, such as spectral and graph signal processing methods via optimization of the graph Laplacian under certain assumptions~\cite{xia2021graph}.
Deep learning methods and GNNs have also been employed in graph structure learning~\cite{chen2020iterative,gao2020exploring, zhu2021survey}. The components of the adjacency matrix can be treated as learnable parameters, subject to downstream tasks~\cite{fatemi2021slaps, jin2020graph}.
The utilization of point process data in graph structure/influence learning has been explored \citep{xu2016learning, zhou2013learninga, zhou2013learningb}. The interaction between two nodes is represented by a learnable scalar, which may not be expressive enough to capture the complexity of the interactions, especially in modern applications. 
Our model can learn complicated and dynamic nodal interactions by adaptively learning the graph influence kernel from point process observations, facilitated by a carefully crafted framework that enables the incorporation of various GNN architectures.


\section{Background}


\paragraph{Self-exciting point process.} A self-exciting point process \citep{reinhart2018review} models the occurrence of time-stamped discrete events that depend on the observed history. Consider a simple temporal point process that only models event times. Let $\mathcal{H} = \{t_1, \dots, t_n\}$ be an observed event sequence, where $t_i \in [0, T] \subset \mathbb{R}$ is the time of the $i$-th event. We denote the history before a given time $t$ as $\mathcal{H}_t = \{t_i|t_i < t\}$. The conditional intensity of events is defined as
$
    \lambda(t) =  \lim_{\Delta t \downarrow 0} \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{N}([t, t+\Delta t])|\mathcal{H}_t\right] / \Delta t,
$
where the counting measure $\mathbb{N}$ is defined as the number of events occurring in $[t, t+\Delta t]$. For notational simplicity, we omit the dependency of history $\mathcal{H}_t$ in $\lambda(t)$. The well-known Hawkes process \citep{hawkes1971spectra} models the self-excitation effect from history in an additive manner. The conditional intensity function is defined as
\[
    \lambda(t) = \mu + \sum_{t^\prime \in \mathcal{H}_t}k(t^\prime, t),
    \label{eq:intensity-with-kernel}
\]
where $\mu$ is the background intensity and $k$ is the so-called influence kernel measuring the effects of historical events. 

In a \textit{marked point process}, each event is associated with an additional attribute called \textit{mark} denoted by $v \in V$. The mark represents specific characteristics of the event and can be either continuous or categorical, such as event location or event type. Let $\mathcal{H} = \{(t_i, v_i)\}_{i=1}^{n}$ and $\mathcal{H}_t = \{(t_i, v_i)|t_i < t\}$ be the observed event sequence and history before time $t$, respectively. The conditional intensity with influence kernel $k$ can be written as:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda(t, v) = \mu + \sum_{(t^\prime, v^\prime)\in \mathcal{H}_t}k(t^\prime, t, v^\prime, v).
    \label{eq:conditional-intensity-with-kernel}
\end{equation}
The influence kernel is crucial when learning the conditional intensity $\lambda(t, v)$ from event sequences. 
A standard and simplified way \citep{reinhart2018review} is to represent the kernel $k(t', t, v', v)$ to be a product of spatial interaction with temporal kernel in the form of $a_{v, v'}f(t-t')$, where the coefficient $a_{v, v'}$ captures the influence of node $v'$ on $v$ through a graph kernel, and $f$ is a stationary temporal kernel.

\paragraph{Graph convolution.} Graph convolutions in GNNs \citep{wu2020comprehensive} extend the convolution strategy to graphs. It falls into two categories: spectral- and spatial-based models. Spectral graph convolutions introduce graph filters $g_\theta$ based on the full eigen-decomposition of the graph Laplacian. The graph signal $X$ is convoluted by $X \ast_G g_\theta = Ug_\theta U^TX$, where $U$ is the matrix of the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian ordered by eigenvalues. For instance, in Spectral Convolutional GNNs \citep{bruna2013spectral}, the graph filter $g_\theta = \Theta_{i, j}$ contains a set of learnable parameters that characterize the relations between node pairs. On the other hand, spatial-based graph convolution is performed by information propagation along edges. The weight matrix in each layer is constructed based on the node's spatial relations (\textit{i.e.}, adjacency matrix). Either the localized filter or the weight matrix plays a pivotal role in capturing the nodal dependencies.



\section{Point processes on graphs}

\subsection{Problem definition}

We aim to develop a point process model for the occurrence of multiple types of events within a latent graph structure.
Let $G = (V, E)$ denote the underlying graph, where each node $v \in V$ represents one event type. An undirected edge connecting nodes $u$ and $v$ indicates the existence of potential interaction between type-$u$ and type-$v$ events. Note that the edges merely suggest the support of possible inter-event-category interactions without dictating the directions.

Consider a set of event sequences $\mathcal{S} = \{\mathcal{H}^{1}, \mathcal{H}^{2}, \dots, \mathcal{H}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\}$, where each $\mathcal{H}^s = \{(t_i^{s}, v_i^{s})\}_{i=1}^{n_s}$ is a collection of events $(t_i^{s}, v_i^{s})$ occurring on node $v_i^s$ at time $t_i^s$. Our proposed graph point process is expected to (i) jointly predict the times and types of forthcoming events based on the observed historical data and (ii) provide an interpretable understanding of the event generation process by revealing the interdependences among multiple types of events and uncovering the latent graph structure with no prior information.
Toward this end, we adopt the statistical formulation of conditional intensity in \eqref{eq:conditional-intensity-with-kernel} and introduce an influence kernel built on localized graph filters in GNNs, aiming to explicitly characterize the complicated contributing relationship between any binary event pair (\textit{e.g.}, excitation, inhibition, or other dynamic influences).

\subsection{Deep temporal graph kernel}

Modeling the multi-dimensional influence kernel $k$ for intricate event dependency is crucial yet challenging. To go beyond simple parametric forms of the kernel while maintaining the model efficiency, we represent the multi-dimensional kernel by taking advantage of the kernel singular value decomposition (SVD) \citep{mercer1909xvi, mollenhauer2020singular}. 
Specifically, the influence kernel $k(t^\prime, t, v^\prime, v)$ in \eqref{eq:intensity-with-kernel} is decomposed into basis kernel functions as follows:
\begin{equation}
    k(t', t, v', v) = \sum_{d=1}^D \sigma_d g_d(t', t-t^\prime) h_d(v', v),
\end{equation}
where $\{g_d, h_d\}_{d=1}^{D}$ are sets of basis kernels in terms of event time and type, respectively. The scalar $\sigma_d$ is the corresponding weight (or ``singular value'') at each rank $d$.
Instead of directly learning the multi-dimensional event dependency, we simplify the task by ``separately'' modeling specific modes of event dependency over time or graph using different basis kernels. It is worth noting that the weighted combination of basis kernels covers a broad range of non-stationary influence kernels used in point processes, and our kernel $k$ is not decoupled over time and graph space.
The temporal basis kernels are carefully designed to capture the heterogeneous temporal dependencies between past and future events. First, the parametrization of temporal kernels $\{g_d\}_{d=1}^{D}$ using displacements $t-t^\prime$ instead of $t$ provides us a low-rank way to approximate general kernels \citep{dong2022spatio}. To proceed, we approximate $\{g_d\}_{d=1}^{D}$ using shared basis functions:
\[
    g_d(t^\prime, t-t^\prime) = \sum_{l=1}^{L}\beta_{dl}\psi_l(t^\prime)\varphi_l(t-t^\prime), \quad \forall d = 1, \dots, D.
\]
Here $\{\psi_l, \varphi_l: [0, T] \to \mathbb{R}\}_{l=1}^{L}$ are two sets of one-dimensional basis functions characterizing the temporal impact of an event occurring at $t^\prime$ and the pattern of that impact spread over $t-t^\prime$. The scalar $\beta_{d, l}$ is the corresponding weight. The basis functions $\{\psi_l, \varphi_l\}_{l=1}^{L}$ are represented by independent fully connected neural networks, leveraging their universal approximation power.

\subsection{Graph basis kernel with localized graph filters}
\label{sec:graph-basis-kernel}

We develop a novel framework for the graph basis kernels by leveraging the localized graph filters in graph convolution to extract informative inter-event-category patterns from graph-structured data. Specifically, the basis kernels $\{h_d\}_{d=1}^{D}$ are represented as follows:
\[
    h_d(v^\prime, v) = \sum_{r=1}^{R}\gamma_{dr}B_r(v^\prime, v), \quad \forall d = 1, \dots, D,
\]
where $\{B_r(v^\prime, v): V \times V \to \mathbb{R}\}_{r=1}^{R}$ are $R$ bases of localized graph filters, and $\gamma_{dr}$ is the corresponding weight for each $B_r$. The bases can be constructed from a spatial or a spectral approach, corresponding to two categories of commonly seen graph convolutions. 

Formally, the influence kernel $k$ can be represented as:
\begin{equation}
    k(t^\prime, t, v^\prime, v) = \sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\alpha_{rl}\psi_l(t^\prime)\varphi_l(t-t^\prime)B_r(v^\prime, v),
    \label{eq:kernel-formulation}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_{rl} = \sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sigma_d\beta_{dl}\gamma_{dr}$.
To showcase the model flexibility of our graph basis kernel to incorporate various GNN structures, we implement two examples of \textit{L3Net} \citep{cheng2020graph} and \textit{GAT} \citep{velickovic2017graph} in our numerical experiments (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). L3Net provides a unified framework for both spatial- and spectral-based graph convolutions, and GAT can predict the presence or absence of an edge in a graph.
Note that our framework is compatible with general GNN layer types, such as those in \textit{Chebnet} \citep{defferrard2016convolutional} and \textit{GPS Graph Transformer} \citep{dwivedi2020generalization}.


The benefits of our design for the influence kernel $k$ are as follows.
(i) The kernel enables the adoption of various spectral and spatial filter bases. The combination of $R$ bases allows us to represent complex local and global patterns of inter-node influences with great model expressiveness. 
(ii) Our framework substantially reduces the number of model parameters to $\mathcal{O}(RC|V|)$ for modeling graph-structured point process data with $|V|$ event types, while classic multivariate point processes and other neural point processes typically require more than $\mathcal{O}(|V|^2)$ parameters. Here $C$ represents the average local patch size \citep{cheng2020graph}. In practice, we have $C, R \ll |V|$ when dealing with sparse graphs and considering only up to $o$-hop influence (commonly 2 or 3), which significantly improves the scalability of our model when applied to large graphs. 

{\it Choice of the network hyperparameters.} In practice, we can treat $\alpha_{rl}$ as one learnable parameter and the model parameter $D$ is absorbed.

\subsection{Model estimation}
\label{sec:model-estimation}

Previous studies of point processes primarily use two types of loss function for model estimation, including the (negative) log-likelihood function (NLL) \citep{dong2023non, reinhart2018review} and the least square loss function (LS) \citep{bacry2020sparse, cai2022latent}.
We present the incorporation of these two losses in our framework, which is not specific to a particular choice of loss and, in fact, can be quite general. 
\paragraph{Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL).} The model parameters can be estimated by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of observing event sequences $\mathcal{S}$ on $[0, T] \times V$ \citep{reinhart2018review}, which is expressed as:
\[
    \ell_{\text{NLL}}(\theta):= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|} \left(
    \sum_{v \in V}\int_0^T\lambda(t, v)dt
    -
    \sum_{i=1}^{n_s} \log \lambda\left(t_i^s, v_i^s\right)
    \right ).
\]
Note that the model parameter $\theta$ is incorporated into the intensity function $\lambda$. This approach is equivalent to the maximum likelihood estimation approach, and the log-likelihood indicates how well the model fits the occurrences of events.

\paragraph{Least Square (LS) loss.} Another approach to the point process estimation is based on least square loss \citep{hansen2015lasso} of observed sequences $\mathcal{S}$:
\[
    \ell_{\text{LS}}(\theta):= \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|} \left(\sum_{v \in V}\int_{0}^{T}\lambda^2(t, v)dt - \sum_{i=1}^{n_s}2\lambda(t_i^s, v_i^s)\right).
\]
The least-square loss function can be derived from the empirical risk minimization principle \citep{geer2000empirical}. Intuitively, we expect the integral of intensity over infinitesimal time intervals containing event times to be approximately one and approximately zero during non-event times. 

\noindent Since negative values of the influence kernel are allowed for learning inhibiting effects, an additional constraint is required for ensuring the non-negativity of the conditional intensity function. To achieve this, we leverage the log-barrier method for optimization in point processes \citep{dong2022spatio}, which maintains the model interpretability while being computationally efficient. The final objective function to be minimized is formulated as 
\[
    \begin{aligned}
        &\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(\theta) := \ell_{\text{NLL}}(\theta) + \frac{1}{w}p(\theta, b) \\ \text{and} \quad &\min_{\theta} \mathcal{L}_{\text{LS}}(\theta) \ \ := \ell_{\text{LS}}(\theta) + \frac{1}{w}p(\theta, b)
    \end{aligned}
\]
for two loss functions, respectively. Here $p(\theta, b) = - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{U}_{\text{bar}, t} \times V|}\sum_{s=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{U}_{\text{bar}, t}}\sum_{v \in V}\log(\lambda(t, v) - b)$ is the log-barrier function with regularization parameter $w >0$. The $\mathcal{U}_{\text{bar}, t}$ is 
a dense enough grid over $[0, T]$ that the intensity function is penalized on,
and $b$ is a lower bound of the intensity value, which will be tuned to 0, to ensure the initialization is within the feasible domain of the logarithm function. 


\section{Experiment}


In this section, we compare our method with a deep graph kernel, referred to as \texttt{GraDK}, with seven state-of-the-art point process methods on both synthetic and real data sets. 

\paragraph{Baselines.} We include two groups of baselines with distinctive model characteristics. Models in the first group treat the associated node information of each event as one-dimensional event marks without considering the graph structure, including (i) Recurrent Marked Temporal Point Process (\texttt{RMTPP})~\citep{du2016recurrent} that uses a recurrent neural network to encode dependence through time; (ii) Fully Neural Network model (\texttt{FullyNN})~\citep{omi2019fully} that models the cumulative distribution via a neural network; and (iii) Deep Non-Stationary Kernel \citep{dong2022spatio} with a 
low-rank neural marked temporal kernel (\texttt{DNSK-mtpp}).
The second group includes two models that encode the latent graph structure information when modeling event times and marks, including (iv) Structured Transformer Hawkes Process (\texttt{THP-S})~\citep{zuo2020transformer} and (v) Graph Self-Attentive Hawkes Process (\texttt{SAHP-G})~\citep{zhang2020self} with a given graph structure, which both use self-attention mechanisms to represent the conditional intensity.

\paragraph{Experimental setup.} We demonstrate the adaptability of the proposed method to various advanced GNN architectures and loss functions using three different architectures: (i) \texttt{GraDK} with L3Net and NLL (\texttt{GraDK+L3net+NLL}), (ii) \texttt{GraDK} with L3Net and LS (\texttt{GraDK+L3net+LS}), and (iii) \texttt{GraDK} with GAT and NLL (\texttt{GraDK+GAT+NLL}).

\subsection{Synthetic data}
\label{sec:synthetic-data}

We evaluate the efficacy of our model on five synthetic data sets generated by point processes with the following kernels and latent graph structures: (i) a non-stationary temporal kernel on a 3-node graph with negative influence; (ii) a non-stationary temporal kernel on a 16-node graph with 2-hop graph influence; (iii) an exponentially decaying temporal kernel on a 50-node graph with central nodes; (iv) a non-separable diffusion kernel on a 50-node graph with central nodes; and (v) an exponentially decaying temporal kernel on a 225-node graph.

\subsubsection{Known graph topology}

We demonstrate the exceptional performance of \texttt{GraDK} in modeling point process data by leveraging observed latent graph structures using synthetic data sets. These experiments aim to simulate scenarios where prior knowledge of the latent space is available.

\paragraph{Kernel and intensity recovery.} Figure~\ref{fig:16-node-synthetic-data-vis} contains the recovered graph kernel by each method for the synthetic data generated by the kernel on a 16-node ring graph with 2-hop influence. Our method and \texttt{DNSK} directly learn the kernel, and the graph kernel in \texttt{SAHP-G} is constructed as in the original paper \citep{zhang2020self} by computing the empirical mean of the learned attention weights between nodes. It is worth noting that our model learns an accurate representation, recovering the self-exciting and multi-hop influential structures in the kernel over the latent graph. At the same time, \texttt{SAHP-G} only captures the mutual dependencies within one-hop neighbors, restricted by their model formulation. Our methods also recover the event intensity successfully (the bottom row of Figure~\ref{fig:16-node-synthetic-data-vis}). The conditional intensities of \texttt{SAHP-G} and \texttt{DNSK}, however, either fail to capture the magnitude of this interdependence or do not accurately decay node dependence along the ring-structure connections.

\paragraph{Event dependency.} Our model exhibits exceptional performance in capturing sequential event dependencies. The second row of Figure~\ref{fig:16-node-synthetic-data-vis} visualizes the learned inter-event dependency given a testing sequence generated on the 16-node graph with 2-hop influence. The dependency between a prior and a future event is characterized by the influence kernel \eqref{eq:intensity-with-kernel} in \texttt{GraDK}, \texttt{DNSK}, and the true model. For \texttt{SAHP-G}, the event dependency is indicated by the scaled self-attention weight (Equation 10 \citep{zhang2020self}). While \texttt{SAHP-G} is capable of discovering long-term dependencies, the decaying influence of recent events is not represented. \texttt{DNSK} captures the decaying influence but fails on the long-term effects of certain event types. Our method learns both of these features resembling those of the true model. Similar results on event dependencies on other synthetic data sets are shown in the second row of Figure~\ref{fig:3-node-synthetic-data-vis}-\ref{fig:225-node-synthetic-data-vis}, indicating the superior performance of our model against \texttt{SAHP-G} and \texttt{DNSK}.

\paragraph{Predictive ability.} The superior predictive performance of \texttt{GraDK} is further demonstrated through a comprehensive evaluation. Apart from assessing the fitted log-likelihood ($\ell$) of the testing data, for each data set, we generate $100$ event sequences using each learned model (one of three independent runs) and provide two metrics \citep{juditsky2020convex}: (i) the mean absolute error of predicted event frequency (\textit{Time MAE}) compared to that in the testing data, and (ii) the Kullback–Leibler Divergence of predicted event types (\textit{Type KLD}), which compares the empirical distributions of event types (nodes) in the testing data and generated sequences. 
These metrics reflect the model's predictive capacity for future events, as opposed to individual event prediction accuracy, which tends to be noisy when applied to large graphs.
The quantitative results in Table~\ref{tab:synthetic-data-results} demonstrate that the \texttt{GraDK} method excels in fitting sequential data on a latent graph. It achieves the highest log-likelihood across all datasets and significantly outperforms all baseline methods in predicting future events, which holds immense importance within the domain of point process modeling.

\paragraph{Dynamic graph influence.} One salient feature of the proposed deep graph kernel is the capability of recovering dynamic interactions among graph nodes, \textit{i.e.}, the evolution of event influence over the graph. In Figure~\ref{fig:dynamic-kernel}, we visualized the true non-separable diffusion kernel used in the synthetic experiments (first row) and the learned kernel (second row) over the graph with the time lag $t-t'$ fixed at three values (the diffusion kernel is stationary; thus, we can fix $t'$ at an arbitrary value without impacting the results). The interactions among graph nodes vary over time, and by using our deep influence kernel, we can successfully recover the dynamic nodal influence out of discrete point process observations.
The quantitative results in Table~\ref{tab:synthetic-data-results} also showcase the superior model performance of \texttt{GraDK} to capture the dynamic and non-separable event influence.

\paragraph{Comparison of NLL and LS.} The empirical results indicate that both loss functions can be well-suited for learning graph point processes. In particular, models with LS show consistently better performance by a slight margin. 
\subsubsection{Unknown graph topology}

We now justify the capability of the proposed framework to recover the structure of the event dependency when we have no prior information about the latent graph structure. This is achieved by incorporating GAT into the graph kernel and uncovering the graph support through the learned attention weights. In particular, we exploit a fully connected graph support in the experiments of \texttt{GraDK+GAT+NLL}, assuming possible interactions between any pair of graph nodes. The estimated localized graph filters can indicate the knowledge about the node interactions that the model has learned from the data. As we can see in Figure~\ref{fig:16-node-synthetic-data-vis}, the recovered graph kernels and event dependencies by \texttt{GraDK+GAT+NLL} closely resemble the ground truth. The model's capability to capture the underlying patterns of node interactions or dependencies is crucial in various real-world applications in which one does not have access to the latent graph structure.

\subsection{Real data}
\label{sec:real_data_experiment}

We test the performance of our proposed approach on real-world point process data. Since the applications of graph point processes involve discrete events over networks with asynchronous time information, most of the traditional benchmark graph data sets are not applicable. In the following, we collect four publicly available data sets for numerical experiments: (i) traffic congestion data in Atlanta, (ii) Sepsis data by PhysioNet Challenge, (iii) wildfire data in California, and (iv) theft data in Valencia, Spain.

\subsubsection{Ablation study}
\label{sec:ablation-study}

For real-world applications involving discrete event data observed in geographic space, spatio-temporal point processes (STPPs) with Euclidean-distance-based influence kernels can be used. Nevertheless, through an ablation study where we compare \texttt{GraDK} with three STPP baselines, we showcase that our proposed graph point process model is more flexible and powerful in capturing the more complicated event influence across discretized locations (represented by nodes) that are distant but strongly influence each other. Such influences are captured by direct edges between nodes or through multiple hops, which can be harder to capture using spatio-temporal kernels.

We compare \texttt{GraDK} against three STPP baselines including (i) Multivariate Hawkes process (\texttt{MHP}) \citep{hawkes1971spectra}; (ii) Epidemic Type Aftershock Sequence (\texttt{ETAS}) model \citep{ogata1988statistical}; and (iii) Deep Non-Stationary Kernel with a low-rank spatio-temporal kernel (\texttt{DNSK-stpp}). Each baseline represents one type of approach for modeling the spatial effect of historical events. \texttt{MHP} discretizes the entire space by several geographic units and models the dependency among units using a spatial kernel matrix. On the contrary, \texttt{ETAS} and \texttt{DNSK-stpp} adopt parametric and neural-network-based spatial kernels and model the event dependencies over continuous Euclidean space, respectively. We estimate each of the four models with a fixed exponentially decaying temporal kernel on wildfire and theft data sets which are originally observed within Euclidean geographic space.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-study} presents the quantitative results of three metrics used in synthetic data sets for each model, which demonstrate the superior performance of \texttt{GraDK} in fitting the data and predicting future events. It is worth noting that the performance gain of our model does not rely on the increasing of parameters, indicating the model benefits of the proposed graph kernel framework.

Results in Table~\ref{tab:real-data-results} underscore the efficacy of the \texttt{GraDK} approach in acquiring knowledge about graph point processes across a diverse array of real-world domains. These settings cover diverse event dependency dynamics, as the influence mechanisms include infrastructure (roadways for traffic patterns), healthcare (Granger causality among Sepsis-associated derangements), nature (weather and climate for wildfire patterns), and social dynamics (criminal behavior for theft patterns). Despite the inherent complexity of these scenarios, our method excels in providing a robust framework capable of capturing the intricate dependencies and facilitating accurate predictions, demonstrated by the low Time MAE and Type KLD from our method in each setting, which is better than or comparable to the best baselines in each of the four real data sets.

Figure~\ref{fig:real-data-vis} visualizes the learned graph kernels by (a) \texttt{GraDK+L3net+NLL}, (b) \texttt{GraDK+GAT+NLL}, (c) \texttt{SAHP-G}, and (d) \texttt{DNSK} on the theft data. The third panel reveals that \texttt{SAHP-G} learns a noisy graph kernel, resulting in a conditional intensity that depends slightly on inter-event influence, thus learning a homogeneous Poisson process for each node with a relatively high likelihood. \texttt{DNSK} (last panel) fails to present meaningful or discernible patterns of self or inter-node dependencies. Lastly, \texttt{GraDK+GAT} captures intricate self-influence and inter-node dependencies, and \texttt{GraDK+L3net} recovers multi-hop event influence with the aid of the flexible graph kernel, indicating the complex inhomogeneous dynamics among data.

\subsubsection{Dynamic graph influence on Sepsis data}

We use the ICU time-series data for sepsis prediction to demonstrate the model capability of learning dynamic graph influence. Figure~\ref{fig:sepsis-dynamic-kernel} visualizes four snapshots of the influence learned by \texttt{GraDK} among different medical indices. The snapshots are arranged in an order with the increase of the time lag $t-t'$, showcasing the temporal evolution of the 
graph influence. As we can see, the excitation effects on Sepsis onsets by the observations of certain medical indices (\textit{e.g.}, RI, OCD, DCO, OD(vs), Inf(vs)) are stronger in the near future (\textit{e.g.}, at $t-t'=2$) and would decay over time. Some medical indices (\textit{e.g.}, Shock, and OD(Lab)) would have more interactions when the Sepsis does not occur. Similar decaying patterns exist among the inhibition effects between certain index pairs (\textit{e.g.}, OCD to EI, OCD to RI, Inf(Lab) to RI). Full names of the medical indices can be found in Table~\ref{tab:sepsis-medical-indices}.



\section{Conclusion}


We develop a novel deep kernel for graph point processes that can incorporate various GNN architectures through localized graph filters, which efficiently learn intricate and non-stationary event dynamics over latent graphs. The kernel modeling enhances interpretability, as one can parse the learned kernel to understand the dynamic event type interdependence. Our approach outperforms existing methods in terms of dependency recovery and event prediction in both simulation and real-data experiments. %, including large graph structures and those with multi-hop influence.
