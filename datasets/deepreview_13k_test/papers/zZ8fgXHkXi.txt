\title{h4rm3l: A Language for Composable Jailbreak Attack Synthesis}

\begin{abstract}
Despite their demonstrated valuable capabilities, state-of-the-art (SOTA) widely
deployed large language models (LLMs) still cause harm to society due to the inef-
fectiveness of their safety filters, which can be bypassed by prompt transformations
called jailbreak attacks. Current approaches to LLM safety assessment, which
employ datasets of templated prompts and benchmarking pipelines, fail to cover
sufficiently large and diverse sets of jailbreak attacks, leading to the widespread
deployment of unsafe LLMs. Recent research showed that novel jailbreak attacks
could be derived by composition, however, a formal composable representation for
jailbreak attacks, which among other benefits could enable the exploration of a
large compositional space of jailbreak attacks through program synthesis methods,
has not been previously proposed. We introduce h4rm3l, a novel approach
addressing this gap with a human-readable domain-specific language (DSL). Our
framework comprises: (1) The h4rm3l DSL, which formally expresses jailbreak
attacks as compositions of parameterized string transformation primitives. (2)
A synthesizer with bandit algorithms that efficiently generates jailbreak attacks
optimized for a target black box LLM. (3) The h4rm3l red-teaming software
toolkit that employs the previous two components and an automated harmful
LLM behavior classifier that is strongly aligned with human preferences. We
demonstrate h4rm3l’s efficacy by synthesizing a dataset of 2656 successful
novel jailbreak targeting 6 SOTA open-source and proprietary LLMs (GPT-3.5,
GPT-4o, Claude-3-sonnet, Claude-3-haiku, Llama3-8b, and Llama3-70b), and
by benchmarking those models against a subset of the synthesized attacks, and
previously published jailbreak attacks which were used as few-shot examples. Our
results show that h4rm3l’s synthesized attacks are diverse and more successful
than previously reported attacks, with success rates exceeding 90% on SOTA LLMs.
Warning: This paper and related research artifacts contain offensive and
potentially disturbing prompts and model-generated content.
\end{abstract}

\section{1 INTRODUCTION  }


While large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities, their safety remains a significant concern, particularly in the face of adversarial jailbreak attacks. These attacks bypass safety filters to enable the generation of objectionable content such as personally identifiable information, copyrighted materials, and toxic content, including assistance with crimes, misinformation, disinformation, harassment, or extremism (Gehman et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2023). Jailbreak attacks may also occur in non-adversarial settings: for example, accidental misspellings may inadvertently lead to a jailbreak and result in children’s exposure to disturbing content.  

Despite the urgent need to address these safety concerns, there is a lack of comprehensive benchmarks for the systematic evaluation of LLMs’ robustness to a large and diverse set of attacks. Such a benchmark could accelerate the process of red-teaming LLMs, understanding their behavior in adversarial and non-adversarial settings, and developing effective safety guardrails. Previous LLM safety benchmarking methods employ static or templated sets of illicit requests and adversarial prompts (Zou et al., 2023; Mazeika et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2023). However, these methods are insufficient because the space of known attacks is constantly expanding (Greshake et al., 2023; Yong et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2024), and both variations of known attacks (Shen et al., 2023)

and their composition (Wei et al., 2023) may result in successful novel attacks. Our work addresses this limitation by developing a dynamic LLM safety benchmark of composable jailbreak attacks.  

We propose a formal and composable representation of jailbreak attacks in a domain-specific language (DSL) that enables an expanded exploration of LLM vulnerabilities through program synthesis. Our proposed DSL represents jailbreak attacks as compositions of attack primitives, which are parameterized string-valued functions of strings (Figure 1). We also propose bandit-based fewshot program synthesis algorithms that leverage this programmatic representation to generate novel explainable attacks to which the target LLM is vulnerable. Finally, we propose a zero-shot harmful LLM behavior classifier that strongly aligns with human judgment and enables a scalable estimation of attack success rates (ASR). Our resulting system, $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ , therefore provides a valuable resources for assessing LLM vulnerabilities while generating resources for safety guardrail development.  

In summary, our contributions are: (i) a DSL for the formal expression of jailbreak attacks as compositions of parameterized string transformation primitives. (ii) Bandit-based few-shot program synthesis algorithms that generate novel jailbreak attacks with high ASRs on black-box state-of-theart (SOTA) LLMs. (iii) A dataset of 15,891 novel jailbreak attacks, including 2,656 attacks with estimated ASR between $40\%$ and $100\%$ on AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023), and qualitative analysis showing their diversity and specificity to the targeted LLMs. (iv) Safety benchmarking results for 6 SOTA LLMs with 82 jailbreak attacks, including 60 synthesized jailbreak attacks, and the 22 SOTA attacks used as few-shot examples. (v) Open-source automated black box LLM redteaming software for synthesizing novel targeted jailbreak attacks and benchmarking LLMs for safety. In our red-teaming experiments, $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ generated several jailbreak attacks which exceed $90\%$ ASR against SOTA proprietary LLMs such as Anthropic’s claude-3-sonnet, which previously had few known safety vulnerabilities, and OpenAI’s gpt4- $^{\prime}o$ , which was very recently released. We also show that the most effective attacks targeting one LLM are rarely as effective against the other LLMs, highlighting the need for targeted jailbreak attack synthesis methods such as h4rm3l.  



\section{2 RELATED WORK  }


There have been extensive research efforts towards an improved characterization of LLMs’ safety issues (Ganguli et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2023; Qi et al., 2023; Bianchi et al., 2024; R¨ottger et al., 2023; Chao et al., 2023, inter alia). A recent line of focus is the exploration of mechanisms by which jailbreak attacks bypass LLM safety filters. Several handcrafted attacks  

on black-box language models have been proposed, including the Base64 attack (Greshake et al., 2023), the refusal suppression attack, the prefix injection attack (Wei et al., 2023), the low-resource translation attack (Yong et al., 2023), the persuasion attack (Zeng et al., 2024), and the Disguise and Reconstruction attack (Liu et al., 2024). Wei et al. (2023) define jailbreak attacks on black-box LLMs as modifications on prompts for restricted behavior that cause the elicitation of on-topic responses from LLMs accessed solely through their chat interface, and show that the composition of individual attacks could result in higher attack success rates. Our work directly builds on this finding to propose the first formal and composable representation of black-box jailbreak attacks, and the first system to facilitate the automatic discovery of novel jailbreak attacks via program synthesis.   
h4rm3l significantly differs from prior efforts towards automated red-teaming and LLM safety benchmarking (Mazeika et al., 2024; Chao et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024a, inter alia). For example, Qiu et al. (2023) explored bias and harmful LLM behavior in performing tasks such as translation using structured templates. While h4rm3l enables such experimentation, it distinguishes jailbreak prompts from jailbreak attacks, which it characterize formally, and independently of particular illicit requests. h4rm3l enables unified reproducible LLM safety benchmarking, which is the goal of recently proposed systems such as HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) and JailbreakBench (Chao et al., 2024). However, h4rm3l’s formal composable representation of jailbreak attacks also enables the interpretable exploration of LLM vulnerabilities through composition and program synthesis. Our work is in line with prior efforts towards understanding the characteristics of attacks and learning from human jailbreakers (Yu et al., 2024b). However we approach this problem by proposing a language which represents attacks as composition of interpretable primitives abstractions.   
h4rm3l improves upon prior attempts to iteratively discover more attacks from an initial set of attacks such as LLM-Fuzzer (Yu et al., 2024a). However, h4rm3l differs because it represents attacks in a formal composable language that includes all string transformations, and hence all black-box attacks. Our synthesis approach is also different from LLM-Fuzzer, as it produces templates while our approach produces h4rm3l programs, which are compositions of primitives including templated primitives and primitives that can perform arbitrary computation such as Kang et al. (2023)’s payload splitting attack, which performs character-level manipulations of the input prompt
Program synthesis is the long-standing problem of generating programs that meet particular criteria (Manna and Waldinger, 1971; Gulwani et al., 2017). The success of LLM-based program synthesis has been reported for both domain-specific languages and general-purpose languages for applications ranging from database querying to compiler testing to data visualization and programming tasks (Austin et al., 2021; Poesia et al., 2022; Gu, 2023; Roziere et al., 2023). To the best of our knowledge, neither a domain-specific language for expressing composable jailbreak attacks nor the synthesis of jailbreak attacks in a formal language have been proposed before $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ .   
DSPy (Khattab et al., 2023) introduced a programming model that enables the declarative specification of LLM pipelines in terms of parameterized modules that optimize prompts, offering a substantial improvement over hand-crafted prompt templates. $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ differs as a language for composable parametric string transformation functions that can express arbitrary computation, including the invocation of auxiliary LLMs with specific prompts. $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ is also a system that employs program synthesis methods to generate optimized prompt transformations expressed in $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ .   
3 METHOD   
Modern LLMs are typically tuned to restrict unsafe behavior. However, prior work has found that particular string transformations on prompts, black-box jailbreak attacks, can bypass safety filters and enable restricted behavior in LLMs solely accessed via their prompting interface (Wei et al., 2023). In this section, we first propose to formally represent these transformations in h4rm3l, the domain-specific language presented in Section 3.1 that encodes string-to-string functions as programs that compose primitive transformations. Then, in Section 3.2, we formalize redteaming black-box LLMs as a h4rm3l program search problem with the objective of maximizing the ASR of generated attacks. Section 3.2 also presents our proposed bandit-based few-shot program synthesis algorithms towards this objective. Finally, in Section 3.3, we present our automatic ASR estimation method,

the Attack Success Rate $(A S R)$ of a jailbreak attack $p\in\mathcal{P}$ on black-box language model $L M$ as the average rate of harmful behavior demonstrated by $L M$ responses to malicious prompts transformed by $a$ (Equation $^{1}\!.$ ).  

Red-teaming a language model typically involves searching for potential attacks $p$ with high $A S R(p)$ . In $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ , we leverage program synthesis methods to automate this process, finding programs that maximize ASR. To this end, we employ an iterative LLM-based few-shot $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ program synthesis approach to generate a set of jailbreak attacks targeting a given black-box $1$ LLM.  

Our proposed program synthesis algorithms aim to maximize the ASR of synthesized programs targeting a particular LLM. In each iteration, an auxiliary LLM is prompted with $k_{e x a m p l e s}=15$ few-shot examples of programs selected from a pool of examples to generate proposals = 20 novel proposals which are scored and recorded (See generateProposals in Algorithm 1). $k_{i l l i c i t}\equiv5$ illicit prompts are sampled from the AdvBench dataset to evaluate the ASR of each proposal. Proposals with ASR above the pool average are added to the pool for subsequent iterations. We compare three program synthesis approaches that only differ in their few-shot example selection methods.

Random Bandits: This method uniformly selects examples from the pool of few-shot examples.   
Unlike this baseline method, the following two methods select examples more intelligently.  

ASR Rewarded Bandits: This method selects few-shot examples with probability $P(s,t,\lambda)$ sampled from a Beta distribution parameterized by an example’s ASR, $s$ , the current iteration $t$ , and the constant scalar hyperparameter $\lambda$ (Equation 2). The mean of the constructed distribution is the example’s ASR, but its variance decreases with $\lambda$ and $t$ . This design enables more exploration in the initial iterations and a controllably increased exploitation of high-ASR examples in later iterations.  

$$
P(s,t,\lambda)=B e t a\left(\lambda t\cdot s,\lambda t\cdot(1-s)\right)
$$

ASR Rewarded Bandits and Offspring ASR Rewarded Bandits outperform Random Bandits. Using only the low-level expression (LLE) of few-shot examples significantly degraded $\mu_{A S R}$ .  

Offspring ASR Rewarded Bandits: Rather than using examples’ ASR as a reward, this approach uses the ASR of their offspring: valid proposals generated when they served as a few-shot example. Each few-shot example is selected with probability $P(s^{o})$ from a distribution constructed with $s^{o}$ , the ASRs of the example’s offspring (Equation 3). Initially, $P(s^{o})=B e t a(1,1)$ , which produces a uniform selection of examples and greater exploration. In later iterations, offspring ASRs are interpreted as fractional Bernoulli trials, and aggregated to update $P(s^{o})$ , resulting in a posterior distribution that favors the exploitation of examples that have led to successful offspring.  

$$
P(s^{o})=B e t a\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{|s^{o}|}s_{i}^{o},1+\sum_{i=1}^{|s^{o}|}1-s_{i}^{o}\right)
$$  

Our program synthesis methods are described in Algorithm 1, which takes in a few-shot example sampling method, a list of primitives, a list of initial examples, a dataset of illicit prompts, and the number of iterations. This algorithm also uses the following hyperparameters: $k_{e x a m p l e s}=15$ (few-shot examples sample size per iteration), $k_{i l l i c i t}=5$ (illicit prompt sample size for scoring programs), and $\lambda$ , which scales the parameters of the Beta distribution $P(s,y,\lambda)$ used by our ASR rewarded bandit method for example selection. Note that $k_{e x a m p l e s}\,=\,15$ was chosen to fit the context window of the auxiliary LLM used for few-shot program synthesis. We chose $k_{i l l i c i t}=5$ to accelerate program synthesis at the expense of the quality of ASR estimates during attack synthesis.  

3.3 ESTIMATION OF ATTACK SUCCESS RATES  

Our GPT-4 based LLM behavior classifier employs the ternary taxonomy proposed by Wei et al. (2023), which categorizes LLM behaviors in responses to illicit requests as: Good Bot (The LLM issues a refusal of service message), Bad Bot (The LLM responds with harmful information that is clearly on topic for the original illicit request), or Unclear Bot (All other cases, including those in which the target LLM responds with irrelevant information). Our binary harm classifier ( $H$ in equation 1) categorizes the Bad Bot behavior as harmful, and the other two behaviors as not harmful. This classifier is used to estimate ASRs during program synthesis and for benchmarking LLMs for safety. To validate the classifier, two of the present authors annotated 100 Claude-3-haiku responses with $93\%$ ternary classification agreement resulting in 42 Good Bot, 22 Bad Bot, and 29 Unclear Bot retained ground truth annotations. The classifier achieved $88.3\pm2.1\%$ precision and $100\pm0\%$ recall for the Bad Bot class over 3 runs.

6 DISCUSSION   
$\mathtt{h4r m31}$ synthesizes successful and diverse target-specific attacks. $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ iteratively generates   
attacks optimized for a target LLM while learning successful patterns from each attempt. Our   
qualitative analysis showed the diversity of syntesized attacks, the sensitivity of their success to their   
literal expression, and their specificity to the targeted LLM. Although $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ generated several   
attacks that were widely successful, this evidence indicates the necessity for targeted attack synthesis.   
$\mathtt{h4r m31}$ enables reproducibility and interpretability in LLM safety research. It is an expressive   
human-readable language for defining, composing, synthesizing, applying and analysing prompt   
transformations that induce particular behaviors in LLMs. Datasets of $\ h4\,\mathrm{rm}31$ programs such as the   
ones we hereby release serve as basis for reproducible controlled experimentation and benchmarking.   
$\mathtt{h4r m31}$ performs an expanded exploration of jailbreak attacks. $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ represents attacks as   
compositions of string transformation primitives in a formal language. This approach contrasts with   
prior approaches, which often rely on static datasets of templated prompts or sets of attacks that are   
limited in number or diversity. $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ ’s representation allows its synthesizer to parameterize and   
compose jailbreak primitives in novel ways, thereby, exploring a larger and more diverse attack space.   
$\mathtt{h4r m31}$ ’s synthesizer can be guided with customized primitives and initial examples. Even   
though $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ produces attacks that are more successful than initial few-shot examples, its gen  
erations are influenced by the primitives and initial examples that are specified to the synthesizer.   
For instance $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ generated novel attacks that perform Morse Code, Pig Latin, and Rotation-13   
based obfuscation. While those of obfuscation schemes did not exist in our initial few-shot examples,   
they were likely inspired by our initial few-shot examples that employed base64-based obfuscation.   
The choice of primitive names may affect synthesis quality. We find that the careful selection   
of primitive names is important for performance, as they encode valuable priors, just like few-shot   
examples and the program synthesis prompt. RolePlayingDecorator, for instance, was named   
to specify the intent of various state-of-the-art jailbreak attacks, such as DAN, and AIM, which induce  

a malicious persona in the target model by appending a particular prefix or suffix to the original prompt. Naming this primitive AffixDecorator resulted in degraded synthesis performance.  

Synthesizer hyperparameter tuning. The $\lambda$ hyperparameter of our ASR Rewarded Bandit algorithm controls how fast the variance of the underlying Beta distribution decreases with synthesis iterations, and effectively balances the exploration of less successful attacks and the exploitation of more successful attacks as few-shot examples. Lower values of $\lambda$ will result in more diverse attacks at the expense of the average success rate of synthesized attacks. Future work could also introduce a similar hyperparameter in our Offspring ASR Rewarded Bandit algorithm. Higher values of $k_{i l l i c i t}$ will result in better ASR estimates, but slower iterations. $N_{e x a m p l e s}$ and $N_{p r o p o s a l s}$ should be adjusted to fit the maximum number of input/output tokens of the auxiliary LLM used for program synthesis.  

h4rm3l’s LLM behavior classifier can be adapted to custom content moderation policies. What constitutes harmful LLM behavior is nuanced and depends on philosophical, moral, and legal foundations as well as the specific terms of services under which the LLM is deployed. Our classifier is based on Wei et al. (2023)’s ternary taxonomy of Bot Behaviors, which we found superior given a dataset of prompts assumed to be illicit. Users can use an alternate dataset of illicit requests. Users can also adjust the validation prompt and even adhere to a different taxonomy of model behaviors, but should validate the classifier in their setting using human annotated examples of LLM responses.  

Anthropic applied additional safety filters. Some attacks achieved $100\%$ ASR on a handful of samples during our program synthesis runs targeting claude-3-sonnet-20240229 and claude-3-haiku20240307, but we could not benchmark their behavior under normal Anthropic use conditions, since Anthropic noticed the repeated harmful content generated by our experiments and applied additional safety fliters to our account. Our benchmarking results for those language models are results after that additional layer of protection and are not comparable to the other LLMs we benchmarked for safety. Our synthesized attacks still recorded $82\%$ ASR on claude-3-haiku and $44\%$ ASR on claude-3-sonnet.  

$\mathtt{h4r m31}$ is general and extends beyond the representation and synthesis of jailbreak attacks. It is a versatile language that can represent arbitrary compositions of parameterized string transformations, where each transformation can perform arbitrary code execution and invoke auxiliary systems, including LLMs. This general computation model can be applied to a wide range of problems, making $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ suitable for various domains. We note that synthesized $\mathtt{h4r m31}$ programs can contain arbitrary Python code, the execution of which may lead to security concerns. Although we did not encounter such issues in our experiment, we advise users to run $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ in an isolated sandbox.  



\section{7 CONCLUSION  }


We introduced $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ , a DSL that formally represents jailbreak attacks as compositions of parameterized string transformation primitives. This formal representation, coupled with bandit-based few-shot program synthesis algorithms, allows $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ to automatically generate diverse datasets of human-interpretable, compositional attacks that constitute vulnerabilities of any target black box LLM. Our experiments demonstrate that these synthesized attacks have higher success rates than previously reported attacks on six state-of-the-art LLMs, including $82\%$ on Claude-3-haiku, $90\%$ on Llama3-70b, and $94\%$ on GPT-4o. Our qualitative results showed the diversity of synthesized attacks and underscored the need for targeted jailbreak attack synthesis when benchmarking LLMs for safety. The $\ h4\,\mathrm{rm}31$ toolkit, with its DSL, synthesizer, and automated harmful LLM behavior classifier, provides useful resources for understanding LLM safety at scale and the deploying safer AI.  



\section{8 ETHICS STATEMENT  }


The $\ h4\,\mathtt{r m}31$ toolkit and associated dataset of synthesized jailbreak attacks were created for the purpose of assessing and improving the safety of large language models (LLMs). While this research aims to benefit AI safety, we acknowledge the ethical considerations and potential risks involved:
