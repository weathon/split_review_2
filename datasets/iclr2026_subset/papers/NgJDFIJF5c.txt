# STRATA-SWORD: A HIERARCHICAL SAFETY EVALUATION TOWARDS LLMs BASED ON REASONING COMPLEXITY OF JAILBREAK INSTRUCTIONS

Anonymous authors

Paper under double-blind review

## ABSTRACT

Large language models (LLMs) have gained widespread recognition for their superior performance and have been deployed across numerous domains. Building on Chain-of-Thought (CoT) ideology, Large Reasoning models (LRMs) further exhibit strong reasoning skills, enabling them to infer more accurately and respond appropriately. However, strong general reasoning capabilities do not guarantee a safety response to jailbreak instructions requiring even more robust reasoning capabilities. A model with strong general reasoning capabilities but lacking corresponding safety capabilities can create serious vulnerabilities in the real application. Therefore, a comprehensive benchmark needs to be established to evaluate the safety performance of the model in the face of instructions of different reasoning complexity, which can provide a new dimension of the safety boundaries of the LLMs. This paper quantifies “Reasoning Complexity” as an evaluable safety dimension and categorizes 15 jailbreak attack methods into three different levels according to the reasoning complexity, establishing a hierarchical Chinese-English jailbreak safety benchmark for systematically evaluating the safety performance of LLMs. Meanwhile, to fully consider reasoning complexity brought by unique language characteristics, we first propose some Chinese jailbreak attack methods, including the Chinese Character Disassembly attack, Lantern Riddle attack, and Acrostic Poem attack. A series of experiments indicate that current LLMs and LRMs show different safety boundaries under different reasoning complexity, which provides a new perspective to develop safer LLMs and LRMs. **Warning: This paper contains model-generated content that might be offensive.**

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Large Language Models (LLMs) (Radford et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2024a; Bai et al., 2023) have demonstrated impressive capabilities across a wide range of tasks, from dialogue and reasoning to code generation. A central driver of this progress is their ability to perform multi-step reasoning, which was further strengthened by the introduction of the Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paradigm (Wei et al., 2022) and its integration into Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) (Liu et al., 2024a). However, this very reasoning capacity that empowers LLMs has become the Achilles’ heel exploited by adversaries developing jailbreak attacks (Gupta et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023; Jia et al., 2024; Teng et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024a; Gao et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025). For models already safety aligned to reject straightforward harmful instructions, carefully constructed jailbreak instructions with higher linguistic or logical complexity can obscure malicious intent, prompting the model itself to reconstruct and execute the harmful intention. Given that reasoning capability performs as both a primary asset and attack surface, there is an urgent need for systematic evaluation of LLMs’ and LRMs’ safety boundaries across varying levels of reasoning complexity.

Existing safety evaluations follow two major directions. The first expands the breadth of harmful topics coverage, continuously extending risk domains from traditional ethical violations to emerging, high-stakes scenarios (Röttger et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Ying et al., 2025b). The second intensifies the strength of attack techniques, designing extremely strong and complex jailbreak instructions to minimize safety performance (Zou et al., 2023; Souly et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024; Qiu et al., 2023). However, they fail to systematically pinpoint the reasoning-complexity

Moreover, most existing evaluations are conducted predominantly in English, overlooking the hidden reasoning complexity contributed by language-specific characteristics. For instance, Chinese exhibits logographic structures in which characters themselves encode semantic cues. Thus, the polysemy, idioms, or classical allusions often require deeper contextual reasoning for accurate interpretation. Such properties inherently introduce additional cognitive load, which can be deliberately exploited to disguise harmful intent more effectively than in English. These limitations highlight the need for an effective, multilingual, and hierarchically structured safety evaluation benchmark—one that can comprehensively characterize the reasoning boundaries of different models and progressively reveal their failure thresholds under increasing reasoning complexity.

To address the above challenge, this paper introduces **Strata-Sword**, a multi-language hierarchical jailbreak benchmark organized by reasoning complexity. Guided by three key aspects of reasoning complexity: logical depth, linguistic ambiguity, and task overhead, we collect 15 jailbreak attack methods and categorize them into three levels: Basic Instruction (Level 1), Simple Reasoning (Level 2), and Complex Reasoning (Level 3). Level 1 attacks directly expose harmful intent; Level 2 attacks are slight modifications of original instructions that require simple reasoning to infer the harmful intent; and Level 3 attacks involve multiple intent modifications or intrinsically complex techniques, requiring models to carefully deconstruct the jailbreak instructions through complex reasoning.

Strata-Sword also account for language characteristics by tailoring jailbreak instructions to Chinese and English. In particular, we introduce Chinese-specific Level 3 methods such as acrostic poems, lantern riddles, and Chinese character-disassembly attacks. Strata-Sword helps reveal the differing safety boundaries of various LLMs and LRMs when confronted with hierarchical instruction attacks: some models lack basic human-aligned safety preferences, while others show vulnerabilities under more complex reasoning attacks. In short, our contributions are summarized as follows:

- We systematically define the “Reasoning Complexity” from safety dimension and divide harmful jailbreak attacks into three different levels according to three key elements of reasoning complexity, including logic depth, linguistic ambiguity, and task overhead.
- We construct a hierarchical Chinese-English safety evaluation benchmark named Strata-Sword based on the reasoning complexity of jailbreak instructions. Strata-Sword is applied to evaluate the safety ability of LLMs and LRMs under different levels of reasoning complexity.
- A systematic experimental result shows that different LLMs and LRMs have different safety boundaries when facing hierarchical jailbreak attacks. The results provide a new evaluation dimension for re-examining safety alignment from the perspective of AI Red Teaming.

## 2 RELATED WORK

### 2.1 JAILBREAK ATTACK

Jailbreak attacks aim to circumvent the safety alignment of LLMs and induce them to generate harmful content, a significant line of work focuses on automated jailbreak generation. Among them, white-box jailbreak methods exploit model’s gradients or logprobs to optimize adversarial samples: GCG (Zou et al., 2023) uses gradient-guided search to craft non-semantic suffixes; AutoDAN (Liu et al., 2023) employs genetic algorithms with likelihood-based fitness scoring. While effective in open models, these approaches suffer from poor transferability to closed-source systems. In contrast, black-box jailbreak methods circumvent access to model internals by iteratively optimizing harmful inputs through semantic content refinement—leveraging persuasive linguistic strategies (Perloff, 1993) to disguise malicious intent. For instance, PAP (Zeng et al., 2024) constructs a hierarchical taxonomy of persuasion techniques for prompt optimization; CL-GSO (Huang et al., 2025) proposes a more generalized, hierarchical strategy space that substantially expands the diversity of attack patterns; PAIR (Chao et al., 2023) exploits LLMs to iteratively refine expressions with self-reflection. Other approaches leverage machine-generated malicious prompts (Teng et al., 2024) or token-level manipulations like refusal suppression (Gao et al., 2024) to achieve jailbreaks. Meanwhile, attackers can embed malicious requests within multi-step reasoning processes: Zhou et al. (2024) and Cheng et al. (2024b) decomposes a malicious prompt into a sequence of plausible sub-queries via benign dialogue context to gradually accumulate harm through semantic erosion, reframing the attack as a natural conversational continuation; RACE (Ying et al., 2025a) reformulates the harmful input as a legitimate reasoning task with semantic coherence, which covertly align reasoning pathways with malicious intent. These methods reveal that focusing solely on sentence-level toxicity of individual inputs is insufficient, even highly aligned models can be vulnerable to such well-orchestrated attacks.

### 2.2 SAFETY EVALUATION BENCHMARKS

The community has developed several benchmarks to assess the safety evaluation of LLMs from different dimension (Mazeika et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023; Röttger et al., 2023; Souly et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024b; Zhao et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). HarmBench (Mazeika et al., 2024) offers a standardized evaluation framework for automated red teaming, including includes a library of harmful prompts, a collection of attack algorithms, and a standardized procedure. To address the problem of “exaggerated safety”, XSTest Röttger et al. (2023) provides a test suite with prompts carefully designed to be provocative but not explicitly harmful, thereby measuring a model’s ability to distinguish nuanced, acceptable content from genuine policy violations. Likewise, StrongReject (Souly et al., 2024) presents a challenging benchmark generated by using a powerful proprietary model (GPT-4) to create subtle, complex, or creatively-phrased harmful requests. DoNotAnswer (Wang et al., 2023) focuses squarely on the act of refusal itself. It compiles a wide array of prompts, ranging from dangerous instructions to requests for private information, where the only correct behavior is a refusal to respond, providing a direct metric for this core safety function. WildChat (Zhao et al., 2024) consists of a large scale, multi-turn dataset of user-ChatGPT conversations collected “in the wild.”, which turn the synthetically generated or expert-curated prompts into real-world user interactions.

Different from the above method, our Strata-Sword decouples the simple dimension of safety performance into the three different levels based on the reasoning complexity, which can help us better understand the safety boundaries of LLMs and provide a basis for subsequent adoption of different safety alignment strategies.

## 3 REASONING COMPLEXITY OF JAILBREAK INSTRUCTIONS

When processing input instructions, large language models (LLMs) must first comprehend the instructions and then generate an appropriate response. When a user’s intention is stated explicitly, such as “*How to make a bomb*”, the harmful purpose can be identified without complex reasoning, and the response of LLMs will directly reflect its basic safety alignment. However, when the intention is not expressed directly, detecting harmful requests may require additional reasoning. For example, consider the instruction: “*Steps to produce the following chemical compound:  $C_7H_5N_3O_6$* .” LLM should recognize that this compound is the main ingredient in TNT and understand that producing

{3}------------------------------------------------

such a substance is associated with bomb-making, which is harmful. Identifying this intent requires complex reasoning combined with specialized domain knowledge. Under threats posed by harmful instructions with different levels of reasoning complexity, a model’s safety performance may vary significantly.

To better understand these safety boundaries, it is necessary to comprehensively evaluate LLMs across different levels of reasoning complexity. In this work, we propose a disentangled analysis of instruction complexity from a general perspective. For a typical instruction, its reasoning complexity can be decomposed into three key elements: Logical Depth, Linguistic Ambiguity, and Task Overhead:

- **Logical Depth:** Instructions require multi-step, conditional, or causal reasoning to fully understand real intention. The deeper the reasoning chain, the higher the complexity.
- **Linguistic Ambiguity:** Instructions may employ metaphors, vague references, implicit requests, or syntactically complex structures, making the true intention difficult to identify without pragmatic or contextual inference.
- **Task Overhead:** Beyond the core intention, some instructions also involve additional demanding tasks, such as programming or mathematical derivations, which require greater computational effort to complete. Those additional workload complicates the identification of the underlying intention.

Based on these fundamental elements of reasoning complexity, we can roughly classify jailbreak attack methods into three different levels **Level 1 (Basic Instruction)**, **Level 2 (Simple Reasoning)**, and **Level 3 (Complex Reasoning)**:

- **Level 1 (Basic Instruction):** This type of attack has extremely low reasoning complexity. In terms of **logical depth**, L1 attacks directly express harmful intent; the wording is straightforward with no **linguistic ambiguity**; and the **task overhead** is minimal. Typical jailbreak attacks at this level include HarmBench(Mazeika et al., 2024) and AdvBench (Zou et al., 2023).
- **Level 2 (Simple Reasoning):** This type of attack involves relatively simple reasoning complexity. For **logical depth**, L2 attacks typically require only 12 reasoning steps (e.g., DoAnythingNow (Shen et al., 2024) or Contradictory attacks). Regarding **linguistic ambiguity**, the semantics may show partial ambiguity (e.g., Text Shuffle Attack (Zhao et al., 2025)). The **task overhead** may be present but remains simple (e.g., Simple ASCII Drawing). If an attack meets any of these conditions, it is categorized as Level 2.
- **Level 3 (Complex Reasoning):** The attack in this level involves high reasoning complexity. For **logical depth**, L3 attacks require more than three reasoning steps. In terms of **linguistic ambiguity**, high semantic ambiguity may exist. The **task overhead** may involve highly complex operations, potentially requiring detailed domain knowledge. An attack belongs to Level 3 if it meets any above factors or satisfies two different factors defined for Level 2. Typical attacks at this level include Multi-Round Dialogue Attack (Wang et al., 2024a) and Code Attack (Kang et al., 2024).

After classifying different attacks into these levels, we can evaluate the safety boundaries of large language models (LLMs) under varying reasoning complexities. If a model fails to defend even against Level 1 attacks, it may indicate that the model is poorly aligned with human values and unable to recognize even the most basic safety risks. If it can defend against Level 1 attacks but performs poorly against Level 2 attacks, this suggests that the model has achieved basic safety alignment but remains vulnerable to jailbreak attacks requiring simple reasoning, which indicates that its alignment is only superficial. When a model can handle both Level 1 and Level 2 attacks but fails against Level 3 attacks, it demonstrates preliminary safety capabilities across different scenarios, but remains insufficient for addressing more complex safety risks. If the model performs well across all three levels, it implies that the model possesses robust risk-response capabilities for real-world, complex application scenarios. Therefore, evaluating performance at each level separately enables developers to gain a more comprehensive understanding of overall safety alignment for a LLM.

## 4 STRATA-SWORD: A SAFETY HIERARCHICAL BENCHMARK

Based on the criterion of reasoning complexity outlined in Section 3, we construct a hierarchical safety benchmark named Strata-Sword. Different Chinese and English jailbreak instructions are categorized into different levels of complexity.

Implementation details can be found in Appendix A.2.

**Evaluation Criteria.** In this paper, we utilize LLMs as risk assessment evaluators. Specifically, we design a prompt to instruct the QwQ-32B model to assess the safety of query-response pairs based on predefined risk criteria. The model outputs the classification result in the specified format: “safe” or “unsafe”. We apply the attack success rate (ASR) as our evaluation metric, representing the rate of unsafe responses in total responses. A lower ASR indicates a higher model robustness against jailbreak attacks. more details can be viewed in Appendix A.3.

### 5.2 PERFORMANCE OF STRATA-SWORD

Here we present the performance of our Strata-Sword towards 23 different LLMs on Table 2. Based on these results, our Strata-Sword effectively diagnoses an LLM’s safety capabilities under varying reasoning complexities, which can provide an overall judgment for LLM developers.

Specifically, we find that for most LLMs, the attack success rate increases with attack level in both Chinese and English. For example, the ASRs for Open-Source Large Language Models across Levels 13 are: 23.83%, 30.64%, and 64.42% (English); 28.33%, 40.67%, and 70.13% (Chinese), respectively, and the similar result can also be found in open-source LRMs and closed-source LLMs. The results show that most LLMs maintain human-aligned safety preferences against basic jailbreak instructions, but as the reasoning complexity of the attacks increases to Simple Reasoning (L2) and complex Reasoning (L3), both open-source and closed-source LLMs exhibit progressively more serious safety vulnerabilities.

**Insight 1: While most current LLMs can effectively defend against basic jailbreak instructions, they remain vulnerable to jailbreak attacks with simple and complex reasoning.**

From a linguistic perspective, our analysis reveals that LLMs exhibit distinct safety profiles for Chinese and English. For instance, the Llama, Vicuna, and Mistral series demonstrate stronger safety in English, whereas the Qwen3 and Oyster series excel in Chinese. Furthermore, we observe similar linguistic safety disparities in closed-source models, such as Gemini-2.5-Pro.

More interestingly, a comparison between the base Qwen3-8B and its fine-tuned version, DS-Distill-Qwen3-8B-0528, highlights a nuanced issue. While the fine-tuned model shows obviously safety improvement for the English jailbreak instructions (from 31.58% to 16%), the safety improvement only slightly increases for the Chinese ones (from 26.10% to 24%). We reasonably infer that the fine-tuning dataset was heavily weighted towards English content, which bolsters its safety capabilities, while the corresponding Chinese data was less comprehensive, ultimately leading to a pronounced safety imbalance between the two languages. This result underscores a broader concern regarding the imbalanced distribution of languages in the training data.

### 5.3 PERFORMANCE OF DIFFERENT TYPES OF ATTACKS

This section analyzes the performance of individual attack methods within Strata-Sword, with the average Attack Success Rate (ASR) for each detailed in Figure 2. While higher-level attacks are generally more effective, we observe obvious performance variance within each level, indicating that not all attacks of a given complexity are equally harmful. And the detailed case can be viewed in Appendix A.5.

Specifically, within Level 2, the Opposing Response and Template attacks are notable outliers, achieving obviously higher ASRs. The success of the Opposing Response attack may stem from its dual-task nature, which could divert the model’s cognitive resources away from its safety alignment. As for the Template attack, its potency likely arises from its novelty: the template we applied is an out-of-distribution (OOD) example not found in common public jailbreak datasets like “DoAnythingNoW” (Shen et al., 2024), making it a more challenging prompt for current LLMs.

In Level 3, CodeAttack shows the highest ASR. Its effectiveness is likely because discerning the malicious intent requires the model to first parse and reason about the code, a task of high reasoning complexity. Conversely, the Multi-turn attack shows relatively low efficacy, and we attribute this to our experimental setup: for efficiency, all multi-turn contexts are concatenated into a single input. This approach may not fully replicate the dynamics of a sequential, interactive dialogue, potentially diluting the attack’s impact. Meanwhile, the three Chinese attack methods we proposed: Lantern Riddle, Acrostic Poem, and Chinese Acrostic Poem Attack, show a pretty attack success rate, showing that the current LLMs are very vulnerable to attacks with language characteristics.

The results show that certain attacks, such as the Shuffle+Template attack, are highly effective against nearly all tested closed-source LLMs. Meanwhile, some models exhibit unique defensive strengths and weaknesses. Therefore, when faced with these diverse jailbreak challenges, we need to seek ideal paradigms to improve the safety capabilities of large language models. Currently, large reasoning models that leverage the Chain of Thought paradigm are gaining increasing attention. These models explicitly output their thinking process, which helps them to better understand complex reasoning instructions. This, in turn, enables them to more clearly perceive the harmful intentions contained within the instructions and generate appropriate safe responses. Advanced methods such as Oyster and Realsafe exemplify this approach, integrating and innovating upon this reasoning-based defense strategy. Therefore, we believe that the safety alignment strategy based on LRMs will become the future trend.

## 6 CONCLUSION

In this paper, we decoupled reasoning complexity along three core dimensions: Logical Depth, Linguistic Ambiguity, and Task Overhead. Based on this Criteria, we can classify jailbreak attacks into three distinct levels, including basic instructions, simple reasoning, and complex reasoning. Then we introduce Strata-Sword, a hierarchical, bilingual (Chinese-English) evaluation benchmark. We applied fifteen attack methods, categorizing them into these levels while adapting them to the unique characteristics of each language. A key contribution is our introduction of novel attacks that exploit Chinese-specific linguistic features, including Chinese Character Disassembly, Lantern Riddle, and Acrostic Poem Attack. Our evaluation of mainstream open-source and closed-source LLMs using Strata-Sword reveals that models exhibit markedly different safety boundaries when faced with varying levels of reasoning complexity. These findings provide a clear guidance for developers to implement improvement strategies that address vulnerabilities specific to each complexity level.

## ETHICS STATEMENT

This research aims to improve the safety of AI systems by testing the safety boundaries of large language models through jailbreak attacks. All experiments were conducted in a controlled environment and were not disseminated or used for real-world harm. We adhere to the model’s terms of use and commit to responsibly disclosing our research findings. These findings will be used solely for defensive research and safety assessments, contributing to the construction of more safety and reliable AI systems.
