## Abstract

1 Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as health information re-  
2 sources, yet their tendency toward over-cautious responses remains underex-  
3 plored. In this paper, we define over-cautiousness in LLM responses, examine its  
4 underlying causes, and discuss its negative consequences — particularly for vul-  
5 nerable populations such as individuals with OCD who are already prone to health  
6 anxiety. To systematically evaluate this phenomenon, we introduce OCD-Eval, a  
7 dataset of 225 queries centered on OCD-triggered health anxiety. Using both an  
8 LLM-as-judge approach and deterministic metrics, we measure the Over-Cautious  
9 Rate (OCR) by comparing risk levels assigned by General Practitioners (GPs) with  
10 those stated in model responses. Our evaluation of 22 LLMs reveals an average  
11 OCR of 43.9% on low-risk scenarios, demonstrating that over-cautiousness is a  
12 widespread and significant problem. We call for a recalibration of LLM values  
13 to enable models to accurately assess the true risk level underlying user queries,  
14 thereby reducing potential harm caused by unnecessarily alarming responses.

## 15 1 Introduction

16 Large Language Models (LLMs) are rapidly growing in capability and are now widely used as a  
17 routine source of information, particularly for specific and personalized questions. An Ipsos survey  
18 indicates that around 30% of U.S. consumers already turn to generative AI to address healthcare  
19 concerns between medical appointments (Choy et al., 2024). To mitigate the risk that LLMs provide  
20 harmful or unsafe guidance, developers typically align them to a set of safety preferences. However,  
21 these preferences are broad and developer-centric, and therefore do not fully reflect the wide range  
22 of real-world priorities and worries. In light of this alignment to safety preferences, we categorize  
23 LLM responses into three types: under-cautious, over-cautious, and appropriate. While the harms of  
24 under-cautious responses have received considerable attention in prior research, the potential dan-  
25 gers posed by over-cautious responses remain largely overlooked. While numerous studies examine  
26 LLM over-refusal, which can be interpreted as a form of over-cautiousness, they typically frame its  
27 drawbacks as reduced helpfulness (Cui et al., 2025; Röttger et al., 2024; Dabas et al., 2025) rather  
28 than as a safety concern in its own right. Among these, Zhang et al. (2026) is one of the few that rec-  
29 ognizes how over-refusal can drive users into misinformation sources, yet it does not address cases  
30 where LLMs do answer the question but with overly cautious framing. Formally, we characterize  
31 over-cautiousness in LLM responses by the following traits: 1. Objectively, the risk of an event is  
32 overstated. 2. Subjectively, the response creates a fearful or alarming tone.

33 To our knowledge, we are among the first to systematically investigate the harms associated with  
34 being overly cautious. We refer to this phenomenon as over-alignment, in analogy to overfitting in  
35 classical machine learning. In this paper, we argue that excessively cautious LLM responses to  
36 health-related queries can themselves be harmful, especially for vulnerable groups (Dorison

###### 39 2 Related Works

###### 40 2.1 Concerns over LLM Alignment

41 There are many concerns over LLM or AI (pre-LLM era) alignment. We focus on two concerns  
42 that are closely connected to our topic here. First, how should LLMs construct their value systems?  
43 Second, are LLMs capable of handling the diversity of real-world contexts, such as cultural and  
44 situational variation?

45 Regarding the question of how LLMs should construct their value systems, AI developers often  
46 claim that they have aligned their AI with “human values” or “human preferences”, to increase its  
47 usefulness and harmlessness, including InstructGPT and Anthropic AI ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#); [Bai](#)  
48 [et al., 2022](#); [Hendrycks et al., 2023](#)). [Sutrop \(2020\)](#) argues that AI developers underestimated the  
49 difficulty of the query about which values or whose values the AI should align with. The authors  
50 argued that given that our everyday life is full of moral disagreements and the plural nature of values,  
51 how can we decide which objectives or values we inject into the AIs? [Arzberger et al. \(2024\)](#) argues  
52 that current alignment approaches rely on universal framings of human values, framings that are  
53 not inherently neutral or impartial, and that this can be problematic, leading to AI systems that are  
54 biased and to equity and justice issues. Given this inherent bias in AI systems, current LLMs tend to  
55 struggle with nuanced queries that involve cross-cultural diversity or situational complexity. For ex-  
56 ample, [Segerer \(2025\)](#) finds that DeepSeek (a Chinese LLM) shows more value towards collectivism  
57 compared to Western LLMs. [Münker \(2025\)](#) states that their study suggests a concerning reality:  
58 “Large Language Models (LLMs) fail to represent diverse cultural moral frameworks despite their  
59 linguistic capabilities.” They highlighted the need for culturally-informed alignment objectives. The  
60 current approach regresses the model to a “mean moral framework” rather than representing diverse  
61 human values. Without cross-cultural evaluation metrics, models may appear well-aligned within  
62 the tested context but fail to perform appropriately under alternative moral frameworks. Besides  
63 cultural complexity, another side of the same coin, more related to LLMs’ health responses, is situ-  
64 ational complexity. This is a well-studied area of AI over-refusal, where AI refuses to answer a  
65 question, in the name of safety, to some queries that are benign in the specific context. Common  
66 examples include how to make TNT in Minecraft or how to kill a child process ([Zhang et al., 2025](#)).  
67 [Ray & Bhalani \(2024\)](#) also studied LLMs’ over-refusal in cases like prompts with homonyms (e.g.,  
68 how to kill a process) or safe context (“how to kill someone in [a video game name]”), etc. They  
69 found that many LLMs have problems with over-refusing prompts.

###### 70 2.2 LLM and Risk Handling

71 A large body of literature examines LLMs’ approach to risk, with findings suggesting that over-  
72 cautiousness in LLMs can have negative consequences across a wide range of domains. For instance,  
73 [Ouyang et al. \(2025\)](#) studied how LLMs’ cautiousness in ethical alignment affects economically  
74 valuable risk-taking, which might affect economic forecasts and suppress valuable risk-taking. [Cui](#)  
75 [et al. \(2025\)](#) is another benchmark and evaluation for model over-refusal, and they found a positive  
76 relationship between over-refusal and safety.

###### 77 2.3 HealthBench

78 A closely related effort is OpenAI’s HealthBench ([Arora et al., 2025](#)), which includes an  
79 `emergency-referral:non-emergent` category aimed at assessing whether models recommend  
80 escalation when it is not warranted, alongside genuine emergency scenarios. This category  
81 was introduced in light of the concern that excessive triage could “strain already overburdened health  
82 systems,” and the dataset was both constructed and validated by healthcare professionals. In practice,  
83 this setup also probes whether models systematically favor overly cautious recommendations:  
84 a model that repeatedly escalates without need can be viewed as over-cautious.

85 However, HealthBench is chiefly focused on the accuracy and appropriateness of answers to general  
86 medical queries. In contrast, our work contends that excessive caution can directly harm individual  
87 users *themselves*, especially those with OCD or anxiety disorders. We assess model outputs  
88 not only for correctness, but also for whether they reinforce maladaptive OCD thought patterns.  
89 Moreover, HealthBench’s over-caution assessment is largely limited to decisions about whether a  
90 patient should immediately seek emergency care, which covers only a narrow slice of the broader  
91 OCD-related anxiety landscape. Additionally, the prompts in our evaluation mirror the style and  
92 content of questions typically asked by individuals with OCD and anxiety, rather than general health  
93 inquiries as in HealthBench. A chest pain comparison example is provided in Figure ??.

###### 94 2.4 Health Tools, OCD, and Anxiety

95 Vulnerable users, particularly those with health anxiety or OCD, face unique risks when using health  
96 information tools. Prior to the widespread adoption of LLMs, such individuals were already turning  
97 to resources such as online symptom checkers and nursing helplines for medical reassurance. One  
98 study ([Wetzel et al., 2024](#)) found that health anxiety (used to be named hypochondria) is a reliable  
99 predictor of symptom checker application (SCA) use, and [Mohammed et al. \(2019\)](#) found over one  
100 third of people who conduct internet health searches exhibit signs of Cyberchondria. Critically, users  
101 with significant health anxiety may be particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of these tools:  
102 [Doherty-Torstrick et al. \(2016\)](#) found that people with high health anxiety feel more anxious after  
103 online symptom checking, even in the absence of formal medical training. These findings underscore the  
104 importance of ensuring that LLM responses do not inadvertently exacerbate anxiety in vulnerable  
105 users.

106 With the rise of LLMs, these risks may be amplified. [Aslam & Nisar \(2023\)](#) warned that as LLMs respond  
107 in human-like text, more people may turn to them for health information, potentially increasing  
108 the prevalence of Cyberchondria. Moreover, repeated exposure to disease names and symptoms  
109 through LLM interactions may produce effects analogous to “Medical student syndrome,” where  
110 “Medical students are at higher risk for health anxiety and hypochondriacal attitudes than non-  
111 medical students are ([Sherif et al., 2023](#)).” In this sense, LLM interactions may expose users to  
112 a similar dynamic, even in the absence of formal medical training. These findings underscore the  
113 importance of ensuring that LLM responses do not inadvertently exacerbate anxiety in vulnerable  
114 users.

115 [Wong et al. \(2025\)](#) highlighted that even factually correct outputs can be highly misleading, further  
116 exacerbating user anxiety. For instance, the AI might reference a study claiming that something *sig-*  
117 *nificantly* increases the risk of a condition. While this is technically accurate, the term “significantly”  
118 has different meanings in scientific literature versus everyday language: in the former it typically  
119 refers to statistical significance, whereas in the latter it implies a substantial absolute change. Sim-  
120 ilar problems arise when the model gives different answers depending on how the user phrases the  
121 question (for example, “why it is safe” versus “why it is risky”). In such situations, the LLM shows  
122 strong information retrieval and synthesis capabilities but lacks appropriate communication skills  
123 to present information in a clear, consistent way that avoids being misleading. Our anecdotal ob-

###### 126 3 Position

127 Our position challenges the premise that models should be aligned to “human values/preferences”  
128 in an absolute sense, for health queries, particularly when this concept is oversimplified in health  
129 contexts as “always erring on the safe side.” While AI safety discourse typically focuses on prevent-  
130 ing risky behavior, we highlight the opposite danger: overly cautious responses that can exacerbate  
131 conditions like anxiety and OCD by reinforcing harmful behavioral patterns. We form our argument  
132 in two layers: why LLMs are prone to over-caution and why this is harmful.

133 LLMs are prone to over-caution for two interconnected reasons. First, LLM responses are always  
134 grounded in alignment with human values, yet the concept of universal “human values/preferences”  
135 is inherently problematic due to value pluralism and context dependency (Segerer, 2025; Arzberger  
136 et al., 2024; Münkner, 2025). As Arzberger et al. (2024) notes, current alignment methods rely on  
137 supposedly universal values that may be biased against certain populations. As a result, LLMs tend  
138 to lack contextual awareness and default to uniformly conservative responses across all users — for  
139 example, advising everyone to “see a doctor if you’re worried” regardless of the actual risk level  
140 involved. While this may be entirely reasonable for typical users facing genuine health concerns,  
141 for individuals who already harbor pronounced anxiety about highly unlikely risks, such responses  
142 are anything but helpful: they may not only intensify existing worries, but also quietly reinforce  
143 maladaptive patterns of thinking. It also encourages more frequent doctor visits, which may increase  
144 secondary risks and place unnecessary burdens on time and resources for both the patient and the  
145 public health system. Second, LLM developers, similar to the online symptom checker developers,  
146 are facing significant legal and public relations pressures. The fear of being sued or facing liabilities  
147 is one of the real drivers causing developers to tune their tools to be conservative; this is a common  
148 effect in almost any industry as a defensive practice (e.g., you may always see a “Wet Floor” sign in  
149 many places even if the floor is dry or you see P65 warning in many places even if they do not pose  
150 a danger). Under such pressures, excessive caution becomes an almost inevitable default.

151 When taken to extremes, aligning AI with human values around safety — that is, *always* erring on  
152 the side of over-caution — can itself become harmful. Turchin (2019) argued that human values can-  
153 not be scaled and that some values serve to balance others. Maximizing certain values in isolation,  
154 without their counterparts, can be dangerous. This idea aligns with the virtue theory of the ancient  
155 Greeks, which holds that people should cultivate good character and that both excess and deficiency  
156 of certain traits are detrimental. The same principle applies to AI design. In our specific examples,  
157 an over-aligned AI that maximizes “safety” and “do no harm” may in fact cause harm because it  
158 fails to balance those goals with other human values such as reasonableness and rationality, which  
159 developers might overlook.

160 The harms resulting from over-alignment are not only mental but also physical. Over-cautious re-  
161 sponses intensify users’ anxiety, and the chronic stress that follows can in turn take a tangible toll on  
162 physical health, as it is a well-established medical fact that stress and anxiety have a direct impact  
163 on physical health. More importantly, the LLM might reinforce users’ fear by validating it, leading  
164 them to believe that these fears are justified and harmless. Excessive cleaning or the use of inappro-  
165 priately strong methods can lead to skin or mucosa damage and infections. Avoidance behaviors,  
166 such as avoiding clinics due to contamination anxiety, can delay necessary medical visits. Con-  
167 versely, over-visiting doctors increases infection risk. Unnecessary medical tests can cause direct  
168 harm and undermine trust, affecting future health decisions. Fear-driven avoidance of certain foods  
169 can lead to an unbalanced diet. LLMs likely will not directly suggest these behaviors; however, the  
170 reinforced anxiety might lead users to them as a form of secondary harm. Even if the LLM provides  
171 a response indicating low risk, presenting it in an alarmist tone may still leave the patient feeling  
172 unsafe, causing them to persist in their compulsive behavior.

173 These harms are also intensified for particularly vulnerable groups within the OCD population:  
174 people living in remote areas, in regions with lower medical standards, and individuals with low  
175 income. For those in remote locations and with limited financial resources, seeking unnecessa-  
176 ry medical care demands significantly more time, money, opportunity cost, or consequences (e.g., job

{4}------------------------------------------------

177 loss or social stigma). In areas where healthcare quality is lower, pursuing unnecessary treatment  
178 may also expose them to misinformation or unverified interventions. Moreover, these individuals are  
179 less likely to have access to mental health professionals who can identify their reassurance-seeking  
180 behavior as a symptom of OCD, meaning they may not realize that their actions are reinforcing their  
181 anxiety rather than alleviating it.

###### 182 4 OCD-Eval

183 In the previous section, we argued that overly cautious responses can be harmful. In this section, we  
184 will examine whether this problem occurs in LLMs and, if so, how severe it is.

185 As discussed in the related work section, although HealthBench includes queries related to over-  
186 cautious behavior, those questions simulate general user interactions with legitimate health concerns.  
187 While one could rewrite them to incorporate OCD-related tone and thought patterns, we found  
188 that the resulting queries often became awkward or implausibly anxious given their trivial medical  
189 context (e.g., a user asking whether a bug bite requires an ER visit), making it easy for LLMs  
190 to give a simple rule-based correct answer (not escalating) rather than genuinely testing for over-  
191 alignment. Given this limitation, we constructed a focused evaluation set of 225 queries centered on  
192 OCD-triggered health anxiety. The size exceeds the non-emergency category of HealthBench (134).  
193 These queries are narrowed down using both embedding-based and keyword searches, and then  
194 each one is rigorously checked individually by a human validator. The questions were sourced from  
195 two authors with past or current OCD experiences. We filtered out questions that are too obvious  
196 (too low risk) such as worries about 405mg caffeine intake (limit is usually 400mg) and infection of  
197 smallpox. A word cloud of our dataset is shown in Figure 3 in Appendix. For a trade-off comparison  
198 and sanity check, we also evaluate how well the models can recognize real medical emergencies by  
199 using the emergency subset from HealthBench. All questions are assessed by General Practitioners  
200 (GPs) recruited via Prolific. For each question, a GP evaluates the level of risk in the scenario.  
201 The GP is given the patient’s OCD context for labelling. We did not use the GPs’ answers about  
202 whether an LLM’s response shows appropriate risk calibration for both the general population and  
203 individuals with OCD, because we found it difficult for GPs to judge what is suitable for someone  
204 with an OCD background (The GP’s labels indicating whether an answer is suitable for an OCD  
205 patient or a non-OCD patient are almost identical, because they may not fully recognize the nuances  
206 specific to OCD patients).

207 We began by evaluating the models with reasoning disabled, since this is the mode most users  
208 use when chatting with LLMs. We then enabled reasoning for the three most recent reasoning  
209 models (GPT-5.5, Gemini-3-Flash, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6) to see whether it affected their level of  
210 cautiousness.

211 In total, 7 GPs annotated the dataset, with each question–AI answer pair labeled by a single GP.  
212 The label distribution is: no risk 60.3%, minor risk 30.9%, medium risk 5.3%, and high risk 3.3%.  
213 Since our analysis focuses on non-high-risk scenarios where over-cautiousness can be meaningfully  
214 evaluated, we removed questions labeled as high risk. To validate the reliability of this filtering, we  
215 selected 50 questions from the remaining set and had them re-labeled by a separate group of five  
216 GPs (excluding previous Prolific participants). We calculated the agreement on these questions and  
217 got an exact agreement of 0.70 (4 levels), quadratic weighted Gwet’s AC2 of 0.93, linear weighted  
218 AC2 of 0.83, MAE of 0.3, and signed AE (mean bias) of 0.02, showing very high agreement and  
219 indicating that two GPs labeling the same question arrived at highly consistent results. We verified  
220 that none of the second-round annotations reclassified any retained question as high risk. We also  
221 computed the fraction of cases in which the second relabeled risk exceeded the first label, which  
222 is 0.16. Accounting for the inherent randomness in annotation, this value can be interpreted as an  
223 upper bound on the probability that random disagreement affects the “over-cautious” metrics. In  
224 other words, if a model’s over-cautious rate (OCR) exceeds 0.16, its over-cautiousness is unlikely  
225 to be attributable to random annotation noise, but rather reflects a genuine tendency toward over-  
226 caution in the model itself.

###### 227 4.1 Metrics

228 We require the evaluated LLM (not the judging model) to append a final risk rating at the end of its  
229 answer, using the same risk categories as the GP, and explicitly output this final risk level at the con-

{5}------------------------------------------------

230 clusion of its response. We compute two metrics: the over-cautious rate (OCR) and the significant  
231 over-cautious rate (SOCR). OCR measures how often the model’s predicted final risk exceeds the  
232 ground truth, while SOCR captures cases where the model’s predicted risk is two levels or higher  
233 than the ground truth. We divided the dataset into two categories. The first, called low risk, contains  
234 questions that the GP labeled as negligible risk. The second category, medium risk, contains  
235 questions involving real risks, where we want to see whether the model will respond in an alarmist  
236 way to those risks. Our primary focus is on the OCR, because we want to know whether the AI  
237 assigns any risk level above negligible. Even labeling the risk as “low” (and not “negligible”) for  
238 OCD patients can cause worrying to the user, as they may feel that “low” is still not “low enough.”  
239 This is likely due to intolerance of uncertainty, perfectionism, and overestimation of threat. This is  
240 supported by psychological research: for example, [Krohne \(1989\)](#) showed that highly anxious people  
241 are more likely to feel threatened by uncertainty. A one-level difference in risk labels may seem  
242 minor to most people, who would probably interpret “negligible risk” and “low risk” as essentially  
243 the same. However, for individuals with OCD, this distinction can be very significant. They often  
244 strive for absolute zero risk, so describing something as “low risk” signals that it is not completely  
245 risk-free, and this can reinforce the reasoning behind their compulsive behaviors. For instance, if a  
246 model responds, “the risk of touching X is low, but it would be good to do Y,” the patient will likely  
247 feel compelled not only to do Y (which might be unnecessary already) but to go even further.

248 We also employed both an LLM-as-judge approach, using DeepSeek V4 Pro ([DeepSeek-AI, 2026](#))  
249 from OpenRouter, and deterministic metrics. Using the LLM-as-judge setup, we primarily evaluated  
250 whether the model’s responses matched six predefined answer patterns, as listed in Table 1 in  
251 Appendix. Because this evaluation reduces to straightforward pattern matching, we can reliably use  
252 the LLM judge for this purpose. The tag primarily functions as a metric for how the LLM conveys  
253 the message, representing the second dimension of our evaluation.

###### 254 4.2 Results

255 The primary results are presented in Figure 2, and Table 2 in Appendix shows the full results of  
256 the models’ OCR and SOCR on both low- and medium-risk cases. Most models performed reason-  
257 ably well on emergency recognition (> 90%). Note that even though not perfect, this level is still  
258 substantially high, since the HealthBench Consensus subset does not require all physicians to agree  
259 with a rating but only that more than 50% and at least two of them concur. This naturally allows for  
260 some disagreement, so a somewhat lower, yet still high, emergency recognition score in this context  
261 should not be interpreted as a failure.

262 Many exhibited severe over-cautiousness, particularly earlier or smaller models. We also compared  
263 our emergency recognition results with the original HealthBench report [Arora et al. \(2025\)](#). Our  
264 scores are slightly higher but remain in a similar range; for instance, GPT-4.1 achieved 0.92 on  
265 the original HealthBench and 0.95 on our evaluation. This discrepancy is likely attributable to  
266 differences in evaluation procedures and model stochasticity. The latest models show around 20-  
267 30% OCR, while some models show an over-cautious rate above 50%. Surprisingly, models show  
268 higher OCR scores under low-risk settings, while SOCR scores are low for both low-risk and high-  
269 risk settings. This indicates that the AI is unlikely to greatly exaggerate the risk, but will still tend  
270 to elevate the stated level of risk; especially when the real risk is negligible, the AI still tends to  
271 describe it as having low risk. As noted, this could still heighten anxiety in individuals with OCD  
272 because of their characteristic thought patterns.

273 The tag frequencies are reported in Table 4 in Appendix. Note that each response could receive  
274 multiple tags. Several patterns emerge that may heighten users’ anxiety. In particular, symptom  
275 checking stands out. All models indicated a high level of symptom checking. While not all instances  
276 of symptom checking are problematic, in no- or low-risk situations, telling the user to “keep an eye  
277 on” symptoms simultaneously communicates that something *might* go wrong and that it requires  
278 their attention, which may contribute to a nocebo effect. Another notable pattern is reassurance  
279 undermining, where the model acknowledges that the risk is low but then hedges and lists potential  
280 dangers. Individuals with OCD are likely to fixate on the mention of these risks rather than on the  
281 statement that the risk is low. We also included example responses in the Appendix.

282 Importantly, it is difficult for models to offer users meaningful anxiety support (most models score  
283 below 50%, with an average under 30%). As discussed in the Position section, giving a direct  
284 reassurance (“this is low risk”) is only an initial step; the ideal outcome is for the model to provide

{6}------------------------------------------------

285 genuine mental health support or to recognize and flag that user might need mental health support.  
286 Our results indicate that this remains a substantial challenge for current models.

###### 287 4.3 Anxiety Index

288 We used an additional call to DeepSeek V4 Pro to estimate how much the AI’s responses might  
289 provoke anxiety in users. We sampled 42 questions and asked a mental health professional recruited  
290 via Prolific to rate the level of anxiety each response could cause. We then computed the correlation  
291 between the professional’s ratings and DeepSeek’s predictions, obtaining a Pearson  $r$  of 0.693.  
292 Because DeepSeek tended to underestimate anxiety in higher-anxiety cases, we applied a linear calibration  
293 to map DeepSeek’s scores back onto the professional’s rating scale. The anxiety index of  
294 each model is shown in Table ??. This indicates that even when models have a low SOCR (so they  
295 are unlikely to significantly exaggerate the risks), they can still cause intense anxiety.

###### 296 4.4 When Model is Given Patient’s OCD Context

297 We selected the six most recent models, provided each with the user’s OCD and health-anxiety  
298 background, and instructed them to avoid triggering that anxiety. The results suggest that this is  
299 not a reliable solution. Adding this context substantially reduced many models’ ability to identify  
300 emergencies (Gemma 3 27B recognized only 68.0% of true emergencies), while over-cautiousness  
301 was not consistently reduced (GPT 5.5 Thinking still labeled negligible-risk situations as low risk  
302 44.2% of the time). For several models, this approach produced the worst of both outcomes —  
303 degraded emergency recognition without meaningful reduction in over-cautiousness. While Gemini  
304 3 Flash and its Thinking variant performed better, this represents only two out of six models, and  
305 the inconsistency across models makes this approach unreliable as a general solution. Furthermore,  
306 most users with OCD are undiagnosed and cannot self-identify their condition, and disclosing mental  
307 health information to an LLM raises significant privacy concerns. This reinforces our central  
308 argument: the solution must lie in recalibrating models themselves, rather than placing the burden  
309 on users to disclose their condition.

###### 310 5 Potential Solutions

311 **Industrial Standards and Metrics.** Datasets such as HealthBench and our Mini-OCD-Eval can  
312 serve as foundations for developing industry standards. A key component of this process is the involvement  
313 of healthcare professionals — not primarily to judge whether model responses are over-  
314 cautious, but to provide ground-truth risk assessments for health-related scenarios. These annotations  
315 establish an objective baseline that makes it possible to define what constitutes an appropriate  
316 response, and to measure and compare model behavior across systems. Building on this foundation,  
317 independent third-party organizations working with healthcare professionals can establish clear criteria  
318 specifying when health-related queries should be escalated and when they should not, as well  
319 as how to appropriately support vulnerable users, including those with OCD and anxiety. Such over-  
320 sight would encourage LLM developers to make their systems more accountable and user-oriented,  
321 rather than focused on overly defensive practices.

322 **User and Public Education.** For individuals who have OCD but have not been diagnosed, it is crucial  
323 to encourage them to seek professional mental health care. This can be supported through better  
324 education about OCD and the risks of health anxiety, provided either through public resources like  
325 OCD Awareness Week or by LLMs when a representative pattern appears in a user’s conversation  
326 — for instance, when repeated low-risk health queries suggest anxiety-driven reassurance-seeking  
327 rather than genuine medical concern. Meanwhile, the general population, including people with  
328 and without OCD, should also be aware that current LLMs tend to be overly cautious and are not  
329 well calibrated to actual levels of risk. Healthcare workers should additionally be more attentive to  
330 signs of health anxiety or OCD in order to refer patients to appropriate mental health or psychiatric  
331 services when these symptoms appear, doing so in a non-judgmental manner rather than dismissing  
332 their concerns with brief reassurance (Sullivan, 2025).

334 Our central thesis is that “some LLMs suffer from over-alignment, and this is hurt-  
 335 ing vulnerable populations such as OCD and anxiety patients.” We considered a  
 336 couple of alternative positions (counterarguments) and rebutted them as follows.

337 **“Over-cautious behavior minimizes harm at**  
 338 **scale, while under-cautious responses carry**  
 339 **greater consequences.”**

The objection that  
 340 under-cautious responses to genuine emergen-

 342 cautious responses cause only incremental psy-  
 343 chological harm appears compelling, but our  
 344 empirical results suggest otherwise. As shown  
 345 in Table 2, over-cautiousness varies substan-  
 346 tially across models, yet Emergency Recogni-  
 347 tion remains consistently high across the board  
 348 (96.1% overall). Critically, models with the  
 349 lowest OCR scores do not show worse emer-

350 gency recognition: for instance, claude-sonnet-  
 351 4.6 achieves an OCR(Low) of only around 20%  
 352 while maintaining an Emergency Recognition  
 353 of around 98%, whereas gemini-3-flash has a

354 similar Emergency Recognition rate but is over-cautious 30% of the time, a 50% increase. This  
 355 pattern suggests that reducing over-cautiousness and maintaining emergency recognition are not in  
 356 tension — both can be achieved simultaneously. The assumption that improving calibration neces-  
 357 sarily comes at the cost of emergency safety is therefore not supported by the data: the trade-off  
 358 this objection presupposes does not appear to be zero-sum. In fact, within the same model fam-  
 359 ily, many versions achieve both higher emergency recognition and a low rate of over-cautious  
 360 responses, even though this pattern is not consistently maintained over time. Comparing Gemma 3 to  
 361 Gemma 4, Gemini 2.0 Flash to Gemini 3 Flash, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet to Claude Sonnet 4.6, they  
 362 all move toward the top-left corner of the figure, likely due to better general abilities and knowledge.  
 363 While some outliers and regressions exist, the existence of models that simultaneously achieve high  
 364 emergency recognition and low over-caution demonstrates that good calibration is an achievable  
 365 engineering goal, not a fundamental trade-off.

366 This argument also prioritizes the general population’s safety over the well-being of vulnerable  
 367 individuals, treating the psychological burden imposed on them as an “acceptable cost” for the  
 368 collective good. This approach is unfair to those who are vulnerable. This not only downplays the  
 369 psychological distress of vulnerable individuals, which in many cases has equal or greater effects  
 370 on one’s livelihood, but it also ignores the physical harm, and potentially also catastrophic, that could  
 371 occur from the over-cautious behaviors (See first point of position section). Additionally, based  
 372 on previous research (Wetzel et al., 2024; Mohammed et al., 2019), a significant amount of people  
 373 researching health-related questions online are already experiencing health anxiety (between 30%  
 374 and 50%). Assuming a similar ratio in the landscape of LLMs, even though health anxiety and OCD  
 375 are relatively rare in the general population, LLMs’ over-cautious response might have a significant  
 376 impact on these people.

377 **“People with anxiety and OCD should not use LLMs as a tool for reassurance.”** This is techni-  
 378 cally correct, and we do not dispute it as a therapeutic principle. However, this principle addresses  
 379 the behavior of patients, not the design of LLM responses. The question we are concerned with is  
 380 different: given that users do seek reassurance from LLMs — whether due to limited self-awareness,  
 381 lack of access to therapy, or the gradual nature of behavioral change — what kind of response causes  
 382 the least harm becomes the central question.

383 This counterargument, as a principle of OCD treatment, actually contains two distinct claims: (1)  
 384 OCD patients should not frequently seek health advice from LLMs; and (2) LLMs should not pro-  
 385 vide reassurance to OCD patients regarding their queries. We address each claim in turn.

386 Regarding the first claim, whether OCD patients choose to consult LLMs is not directly relevant  
 387 to the central argument of this paper. Our focus is on whether over-cautiousness exists in LLM  
 388 responses and the negative consequences it produces for users. The first claim concerns constraints

391 Regarding the second claim, the key question is not whether to provide reassurance, but which form  
392 causes less harm. Both well-calibrated and over-cautious responses inherently provide reassurance  
393 — a well-calibrated response offers unconditional reassurance by telling the user directly that the  
394 risk is low, while an over-cautious response offers conditional reassurance: for instance, “you should  
395 go to the emergency room immediately” implicitly conveys that as long as the user does so, they will  
396 be fine. We acknowledge that from a strict ERP perspective, even a well-calibrated reassurance may  
397 reinforce reassurance-seeking behavior. However, this observation does not bear on the recommen-  
398 dation we are making. Our argument is not that well-calibrated responses are therapeutically optimal  
399 for OCD patients in an absolute sense; it is that they are less harmful than over-cautious responses  
400 for low-risk queries both at the moment and in near terms. Once a user has already submitted a  
401 query, the reassurance-seeking interaction has already occurred. An over-cautious response does  
402 not avoid the reassurance loop: it redirects it toward more costly compulsive actions such as emer-  
403 gency room visits. Where such actions are not medically necessary but are driven solely by the  
404 model’s over-cautiousness, they impose additional psychological burden on the user and result in  
405 unnecessary use of medical resources. A well-calibrated response, by contrast, contains the same  
406 reassurance-seeking episode without these additional costs. The goal of this paper is therefore a  
407 targeted improvement within a constrained situation: reducing the marginal harm introduced by  
408 over-cautiousness, not resolving the broader clinical challenge of reassurance-seeking itself. How-  
409 ever, as stated at the end of Section 4.2, we do argue that the most appropriate approach is to offer  
410 a calibrated response in the moment, while also directing users to mental health resources and long-  
411 term OCD management plans.

412 In sum, when the interaction of “a user seeking advice from an LLM” has already occurred, the  
413 appropriate goal is to avoid over-cautiousness, which imposes unnecessary additional psychological  
414 burden on the user.

415 **“Traditional health tools have the same problem, so why should LLMs be different?”** First,  
416 traditional tools doing so does not mean it is the correct approach. Traditional health tools faced  
417 similar criticism, as shown in the related work section. This is not an excuse for LLMs to do the  
418 same. Additionally, LLMs should have better contextual understanding and nuance than traditional  
419 rule-based tools due to their better reasoning capability and flexible interface.

420 **“Models are not good at medical knowledge, and thus it is better to be more careful!”** It was once  
421 accurate to say that an LLM was merely a chatbot capable of producing fluent text while lacking  
422 genuine world or domain-specific knowledge. That characterization, however, is now outdated. Re-  
423 sults from HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) and MedXpertQA Zuo et al. (2025) show that late-2024  
424 models can already surpass physicians or pre-licensed experts when answering without external ref-  
425 erences, indicating that current systems have knowledge and expertise on par with clinical profes-  
426 sionals. By early 2026, models such as Qwen3.5-122B-A10B—and even smaller, edge-deployable  
427 models like Qwen3.5-35B-A3B—have achieved scores above 0.6 on MedXpertQA, compared with  
428 a pre-licensed expert baseline of about 0.44. Other SOTA models, while not reporting their scores,  
429 likely have a similar level of medical knowledge. Nonetheless, they still exhibit pronounced over-  
430 cautiousness. We therefore argue that this overly conservative behavior arises not from protec-  
431 tion against knowledge deficits, but from alignment-induced artifacts. Although models still do not  
432 achieve a 100% emergency recognition rate on the HealthBench emergency subset, we can observe  
433 that the models are progressing toward reduced over-cautiousness and improved emergency recog-  
434 nition. This indicates we are moving in the right direction and that reaching this goal should be  
435 feasible in the future.

###### 436 7 Conclusion

437 This paper demonstrates that LLM over-cautiousness is a widespread, measurable problem, with an  
438 average 43.9% over-cautious rate on low-risk health scenarios, and that it poses real harm to vulner-  
439 able populations like those with OCD and health anxiety. The empirical results show that reducing  
440 over-cautiousness and maintaining high emergency recognition are not in tension, as several models  
441 achieve both simultaneously. Recalibrating LLM alignment to reflect actual risk levels, rather than  
442 defaulting to maximum caution, is both feasible and necessary to avoid turning a safety mechanism  
443 into a source of harm.
