## Abstract

1 Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as health information re-  
2 sources, yet their tendency toward over-cautious responses remains underex-  
3 plored. In this paper, we define over-cautiousness in LLM responses, examine its  
4 underlying causes, and discuss its negative consequences — particularly for vul-  
5 nerable populations such as individuals with OCD who are already prone to health  
6 anxiety. To systematically evaluate this phenomenon, we introduce OCD-Eval, a  
7 dataset of 225 queries centered on OCD-triggered health anxiety. Using both an  
8 LLM-as-judge approach and deterministic metrics, we measure the Over-Cautious  
9 Rate (OCR) by comparing risk levels assigned by General Practitioners (GPs) with  
10 those stated in model responses. Our evaluation of 22 LLMs reveals an average  
11 OCR of 43.9% on low-risk scenarios, demonstrating that over-cautiousness is a  
12 widespread and significant problem. We call for a recalibration of LLM values  
13 to enable models to accurately assess the true risk level underlying user queries,  
14 thereby reducing potential harm caused by unnecessarily alarming responses.

## 15 1 Introduction

16 Large Language Models (LLMs) are rapidly growing in capability and are now widely used as a  
17 routine source of information, particularly for specific and personalized questions. An Ipsos survey  
18 indicates that around 30% of U.S. consumers already turn to generative AI to address healthcare  
19 concerns between medical appointments (Choy et al., 2024). To mitigate the risk that LLMs provide  
20 harmful or unsafe guidance, developers typically align them to a set of safety preferences. However,  
21 these preferences are broad and developer-centric, and therefore do not fully reflect the wide range  
22 of real-world priorities and worries. In light of this alignment to safety preferences, we categorize  
23 LLM responses into three types: under-cautious, over-cautious, and appropriate. While the harms of  
24 under-cautious responses have received considerable attention in prior research, the potential dan-  
25 gers posed by over-cautious responses remain largely overlooked. While numerous studies examine  
26 LLM over-refusal, which can be interpreted as a form of over-cautiousness, they typically frame its  
27 drawbacks as reduced helpfulness (Cui et al., 2025; Röttger et al., 2024; Dabas et al., 2025) rather  
28 than as a safety concern in its own right. Among these, Zhang et al. (2026) is one of the few that rec-  
29 ognizes how over-refusal can drive users into misinformation sources, yet it does not address cases  
30 where LLMs do answer the question but with overly cautious framing. Formally, we characterize  
31 over-cautiousness in LLM responses by the following traits: 1. Objectively, the risk of an event is  
32 overstated. 2. Subjectively, the response creates a fearful or alarming tone.

33 To our knowledge, we are among the first to systematically investigate the harms associated with  
34 being overly cautious. We refer to this phenomenon as over-alignment, in analogy to overfitting in  
35 classical machine learning. In this paper, we argue that **excessively cautious LLM responses to**  
36 **health-related queries can themselves be harmful, especially for vulnerable groups** (Dorison

###### 39 2 Related Works

###### 40 2.1 Concerns over LLM Alignment

41 There are many concerns over LLM or AI (pre-LLM era) alignment. We focus on two concerns  
42 that are closely connected to our topic here. First, how should LLMs construct their value systems?  
43 Second, are LLMs capable of handling the diversity of real-world contexts, such as cultural and  
44 situational variation?

45 Regarding the question of how LLMs should construct their value systems, AI developers often  
46 claim that they have aligned their AI with “human values” or “human preferences”, to increase its  
47 usefulness and harmlessness, including InstructGPT and Anthropic AI ([Ouyang et al., 2022](#); [Bai](#)  
48 [et al., 2022](#); [Hendrycks et al., 2023](#)). [Sutrop \(2020\)](#) argues that AI developers underestimated the  
49 difficulty of the query about which values or whose values the AI should align with. The authors  
50 argued that given that our everyday life is full of moral disagreements and the plural nature of values,  
51 how can we decide which objectives or values we inject into the AIs? [Arzberger et al. \(2024\)](#) argues  
52 that current alignment approaches rely on universal framings of human values, framings that are  
53 not inherently neutral or impartial, and that this can be problematic, leading to AI systems that are  
54 biased and to equity and justice issues. Given this inherent bias in AI systems, current LLMs tend to  
55 struggle with nuanced queries that involve cross-cultural diversity or situational complexity. For ex-  
56 ample, [Segerer \(2025\)](#) finds that DeepSeek (a Chinese LLM) shows more value towards collectivism  
57 compared to Western LLMs. [Münker \(2025\)](#) states that their study suggests a concerning reality:  
58 “Large Language Models (LLMs) fail to represent diverse cultural moral frameworks despite their  
59 linguistic capabilities.” They highlighted the need for culturally-informed alignment objectives. The  
60 current approach regresses the model to a “mean moral framework” rather than representing diverse  
61 human values. Without cross-cultural evaluation metrics, models may appear well-aligned within  
62 the tested context but fail to perform appropriately under alternative moral frameworks. Besides  
63 cultural complexity, another side of the same coin, more related to LLMs’ health responses, is situ-  
64 ational complexity. This is a well-studied area of AI over-refusal, where AI refuses to answer a  
65 question, in the name of safety, to some queries that are benign in the specific context. Common  
66 examples include how to make TNT in Minecraft or how to kill a child process ([Zhang et al., 2025](#)).  
67 [Ray & Bhalani \(2024\)](#) also studied LLMs’ over-refusal in cases like prompts with homonyms (e.g.,  
68 how to kill a process) or safe context (“how to kill someone in [a video game name]”), etc. They  
69 found that many LLMs have problems with over-refusing prompts.

###### 70 2.2 LLM and Risk Handling

71 A large body of literature examines LLMs’ approach to risk, with findings suggesting that over-  
72 cautiousness in LLMs can have negative consequences across a wide range of domains. For instance,  
73 [Ouyang et al. \(2025\)](#) studied how LLMs’ cautiousness in ethical alignment affects economically  
74 valuable risk-taking, which might affect economic forecasts and suppress valuable risk-taking. [Cui](#)  
75 [et al. \(2025\)](#) is another benchmark and evaluation for model over-refusal, and they found a positive  
76 relationship between over-refusal and safety.

###### 77 2.3 HealthBench

78 A closely related effort is OpenAI’s HealthBench ([Arora et al., 2025](#)), which includes an  
79 `emergency-referral:non-emergent` category aimed at assessing whether models recommend  
80 escalation when it is not warranted, alongside genuine emergency scenarios. This category  
81 was introduced in light of the concern that excessive triage could “strain already overburdened health  
82 systems,” and the dataset was both constructed and validated by healthcare professionals. In practice,  
83 this setup also probes whether models systematically favor overly cautious recommendations:  
84 a model that repeatedly escalates without need can be viewed as over-cautious.

85 However, HealthBench is chiefly focused on the accuracy and appropriateness of answers to general  
86 medical queries. In contrast, our work contends that excessive caution can directly harm individual  
87 users *themselves*, especially those with OCD or anxiety disorders. We assess model outputs  
88 not only for correctness, but also for whether they reinforce maladaptive OCD thought patterns.  
89 Moreover, HealthBench’s over-caution assessment is largely limited to decisions about whether a  
90 patient should immediately seek emergency care, which covers only a narrow slice of the broader  
91 OCD-related anxiety landscape. Additionally, the prompts in our evaluation mirror the style and  
92 content of questions typically asked by individuals with OCD and anxiety, rather than general health  
93 inquiries as in HealthBench. A chest pain comparison example is provided in Figure ??.

###### 94 2.4 Health Tools, OCD, and Anxiety

95 Vulnerable users, particularly those with health anxiety or OCD, face unique risks when using health  
96 information tools. Prior to the widespread adoption of LLMs, such individuals were already turning  
97 to resources such as online symptom checkers and nursing helplines for medical reassurance. One  
98 study ([Wetzel et al., 2024](#)) found that health anxiety (used to be named hypochondria) is a reliable  
99 predictor of symptom checker application (SCA) use, and [Mohammed et al. \(2019\)](#) found over one  
100 third of people who conduct internet health searches exhibit signs of Cyberchondria. Critically, users  
101 with significant health anxiety may be particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of these tools:  
102 [Doherty-Torstrick et al. \(2016\)](#) found that people with high health anxiety feel more anxious after  
103 online symptom checking, while the low health anxiety population feels more relief after online  
104 symptom checking.

105 With the rise of LLMs, these risks may be amplified. [Aslam & Nisar \(2023\)](#) warned that as LLMs re-  
106 spond in human-like text, more people may turn to them for health information, potentially increas-  
107 ing the prevalence of Cyberchondria. Moreover, repeated exposure to disease names and symptoms  
108 through LLM interactions may produce effects analogous to “Medical student syndrome,” where  
109 “Medical students are at higher risk for health anxiety and hypochondriacal attitudes than non-  
110 medical students are ([Sherif et al., 2023](#)).” In this sense, LLM interactions may expose users to  
111 a similar dynamic, even in the absence of formal medical training. These findings underscore the  
112 importance of ensuring that LLM responses do not inadvertently exacerbate anxiety in vulnerable  
113 users.

114 [Wong et al. \(2025\)](#) highlighted that even factually correct outputs can be highly misleading, further  
115 exacerbating user anxiety. For instance, the AI might reference a study claiming that something  
116 *significantly* increases the risk of a condition. While this is technically accurate, the term “sig-  
117 nificantly” has different meanings in scientific literature versus everyday language: in the former  
118 it typically refers to statistical significance, whereas in the latter it implies a substantial absolute  
119 change. Similar problems arise when the model gives different answers depending on how the user  
120 phrases the question (for example, “why it is safe” versus “why it is risky”). In such situations, the  
121 LLM shows strong information retrieval and synthesis capabilities but lacks appropriate commu-  
122 nication skills to present information in a clear, consistent way that avoids being misleading. Our

123 anecdotal observations also agree with this, suggesting that LLMs often highlight specific studies  
124 that support a given conclusion, treating peer-reviewed papers as an absolute gold standard, while  
125 overlooking other sources of disagreement and the nuances within the research. Two examples are

###### 127 3 Position

128 Our position challenges the premise that models should be aligned to “human values/preferences”  
129 in an absolute sense, for health queries, particularly when this concept is oversimplified in health  
130 contexts as “always erring on the safe side.” While AI safety discourse typically focuses on prevent-  
131 ing risky behavior, we highlight the opposite danger: overly cautious responses that can exacerbate  
132 conditions like anxiety and OCD by reinforcing harmful behavioral patterns. We form our argument  
133 in two layers: why LLMs are prone to over-caution and why this is harmful.

134 LLMs are prone to over-caution for two interconnected reasons. First, LLM responses are always  
135 grounded in alignment with human values, yet the concept of universal “human values/preferences”  
136 is inherently problematic due to value pluralism and context dependency (Segerer, 2025; Arzberger  
137 et al., 2024; Münker, 2025). As Arzberger et al. (2024) notes, current alignment methods rely on  
138 supposedly universal values that may be biased against certain populations. As a result, LLMs tend  
139 to lack contextual awareness and default to uniformly conservative responses across all users — for  
140 example, advising everyone to “see a doctor if you’re worried” regardless of the actual risk level  
141 involved. While this may be entirely reasonable for typical users facing genuine health concerns,  
142 for individuals who already harbor pronounced anxiety about highly unlikely risks, such responses  
143 are anything but helpful: they may not only intensify existing worries, but also quietly reinforce  
144 maladaptive patterns of thinking. It also encourages more frequent doctor visits, which may increase  
145 secondary risks and place unnecessary burdens on time and resources for both the patient and the  
146 public health system. Second, LLM developers, similar to the online symptom checker developers,  
147 are facing significant legal and public relations pressures. The fear of being sued or facing liabilities  
148 is one of the real drivers causing developers to tune their tools to be conservative; this is a common  
149 effect in almost any industry as a defensive practice (e.g., you may always see a “Wet Floor” sign  
150 in many places even if the floor is dry or you see P65 warning in many places even if they do not pose  
151 a danger). Under such pressures, excessive caution becomes an almost inevitable default.

152 When taken to extremes, aligning AI with human values around safety — that is, *always* erring on  
153 the side of over-caution — can itself become harmful. Turchin (2019) argued that human values can-  
154 not be scaled and that some values serve to balance others. Maximizing certain values in isolation,  
155 without their counterparts, can be dangerous. This idea aligns with the virtue theory of the ancient  
156 Greeks, which holds that people should cultivate good character and that both excess and deficiency  
157 of certain traits are detrimental. The same principle applies to AI design. In our specific examples,  
158 an over-aligned AI that maximizes “safety” and “do no harm” may in fact cause harm because it  
159 fails to balance those goals with other human values such as reasonableness and rationality, which  
160 developers might overlook.

161 The harms resulting from over-alignment are not only mental but also physical. Over-cautious re-  
162 sponses intensify users’ anxiety, and the chronic stress that follows can in turn take a tangible toll on  
163 physical health, as it is a well-established medical fact that stress and anxiety have a direct impact  
164 on physical health. More importantly, the LLM might reinforce users’ fear by validating it, leading  
165 them to believe that these fears are justified and harmless. Excessive cleaning or the use of inapp-  
166 propriately strong methods can lead to skin or mucosa damage and infections. Avoidance behaviors,  
167 such as avoiding clinics due to contamination anxiety, can delay necessary medical visits. Con-  
168 versely, over-visiting doctors increases infection risk. Unnecessary medical tests can cause direct  
169 harm and undermine trust, affecting future health decisions. Fear-driven avoidance of certain foods  
170 can lead to an unbalanced diet. LLMs likely will not directly suggest these behaviors; however, the  
171 reinforced anxiety might lead users to them as a form of secondary harm. Even if the LLM provides  
172 a response indicating low risk, presenting it in an alarmist tone may still leave the patient feeling  
173 unsafe, causing them to persist in their compulsive behavior.

174 These harms are also intensified for particularly vulnerable groups within the OCD population:  
175 people living in remote areas, in regions with lower medical standards, and individuals with low  
176 income. For those in remote locations and with limited financial resources, seeking unnecessary

180 less likely to have access to mental health professionals who can identify their reassurance-seeking  
181 behavior as a symptom of OCD, meaning they may not realize that their actions are reinforcing their  
182 anxiety rather than alleviating it.

###### 183 4 OCD-Eval

184 In the previous section, we argued that overly cautious responses can be harmful. In this section, we  
185 will examine whether this problem occurs in LLMs and, if so, how severe it is.

186 As discussed in the related work section, although HealthBench includes queries related to over-  
187 cautious behavior, those questions simulate general user interactions with legitimate health concerns.  
188 While one could rewrite them to incorporate OCD-related tone and thought patterns, we found  
189 that the resulting queries often became awkward or implausibly anxious given their trivial medical  
190 context (e.g., a user asking whether a bug bite requires an ER visit), making it easy for LLMs  
191 to give a simple rule-based correct answer (not escalating) rather than genuinely testing for over-  
192 alignment. Given this limitation, we constructed a focused evaluation set of 225 queries centered on  
193 OCD-triggered health anxiety. The size exceeds the non-emergency category of HealthBench (134).  
194 These queries are narrowed down using both embedding-based and keyword searches, and then each  
195 one is rigorously checked individually by a human validator. The questions were sourced from two  
196 authors with past or current OCD experiences. We filtered out questions that are too obvious (too  
197 low risk). A word cloud of our dataset is shown in Figure 3 in Appendix. For a trade-off comparison  
198 and sanity check, we also evaluate how well the models can recognize real medical emergencies by  
199 using the emergency subset from HealthBench. All questions are assessed by General Practitioners  
200 (GPs) recruited via Prolific. For each question, a GP evaluates the level of risk in the scenario.  
201 The GP is given the patient’s OCD context for labelling. We did not use the GPs’ answers about  
202 whether an LLM’s response shows appropriate risk calibration for both the general population and  
203 individuals with OCD, because we found it difficult for GPs to judge what is suitable for someone  
204 with an OCD background (The GP’s labels indicating whether an answer is suitable for an OCD  
205 patient or a non-OCD patient are almost identical, because they may not fully recognize the nuances  
206 specific to OCD patients). We began by evaluating the models with reasoning disabled, since this  
207 is the mode most users use when chatting with LLMs. We then enabled reasoning for the three  
208 most recent reasoning models (GPT-5.5, Gemini-3-Flash, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6) to see whether it  
209 affected their level of cautiousness.

210 In total, 7 GPs annotated the dataset, with each question–AI answer pair labeled by a single GP.  
211 The label distribution is: no risk 60.3%, minor risk 30.9%, medium risk 5.3%, and high risk 3.3%.  
212 Since our analysis focuses on non-high-risk scenarios where over-cautiousness can be meaningfully  
213 evaluated, we removed questions labeled as high risk. To validate the reliability of this filtering, we  
214 selected 50 questions from the remaining set and had them re-labeled by a separate group of five  
215 GPs (excluding previous Prolific participants). We calculated the agreement on these questions and  
216 got an exact agreement of 0.70 (4 levels), quadratic weighted Gwet’s AC2 of 0.93, linear weighted  
217 AC2 of 0.83, MAE of 0.3, and signed AE (mean bias) of 0.02, showing very high agreement and  
218 indicating that two GPs labeling the same question arrived at highly consistent results. We verified  
219 that none of the second-round annotations reclassified any retained question as high risk. We also  
220 computed the fraction of cases in which the second relabeled risk exceeded the first label, which  
221 is 0.16. Accounting for the inherent randomness in annotation, this value can be interpreted as an  
222 upper bound on the probability that random disagreement affects the “over-cautious” metrics. In  
223 other words, if a model’s over-cautious rate (OCR) exceeds 0.16, its over-cautiousness is unlikely  
224 to be attributable to random annotation noise, but rather reflects a genuine tendency toward over-  
225 caution in the model itself.

###### 226 4.1 Metrics

227 We require the evaluated LLM (not the judging model) to append a final risk rating at the end of its  
228 answer, using the same risk categories as the GP, and explicitly output this final risk level at the con-  
229 clusion of its response. We compute two metrics: the over-cautious rate (OCR) and the significant

{5}------------------------------------------------

over-cautious rate (SOCR). OCR measures how often the model’s predicted final risk exceeds the ground truth, while SOCR captures cases where the model’s predicted risk is two levels or higher than the ground truth. We divided the dataset into two categories. The first, called no-risk, contains questions that the GP labeled as negligible risk. The second category, medium risk, contains questions involving real risks, where we want to see whether the model will respond in an alarmist way to those risks. Our primary focus is on the OCR, because we want to know whether the AI assigns any risk level above negligible. Even labeling the risk as “low” (and not “negligible”) for OCD patients can cause worrying to the user, as they may feel that “low” is still not “low enough.” This is likely due to intolerance of uncertainty, perfectionism, and overestimation of threat. This is supported by psychological research: for example, [Krohne \(1989\)](#) showed that highly anxious people are more likely to feel threatened by uncertainty. A one-level difference in risk labels may seem minor to most people, who would probably interpret “negligible risk” and “low risk” as essentially the same. However, for individuals with OCD, this distinction can be very significant. They often strive for absolute zero risk, so describing something as “low risk” signals that it is not completely risk-free, and this can reinforce the reasoning behind their compulsive behaviors. For instance, if a model responds, “the risk of touching X is low, but it would be good to do Y,” the patient will likely feel compelled not only to do Y (which might be unnecessary already) but to go even further.

We also employed both an LLM-as-judge approach, using DeepSeek V4 Pro ([DeepSeek-AI, 2026](#)) from OpenRouter, and deterministic metrics. Using the LLM-as-judge setup, we primarily evaluated whether the model’s responses matched six predefined answer patterns, as listed in Table 1 in Appendix. Because this evaluation reduces to straightforward pattern matching, we can reliably use the LLM judge for this purpose. The tag primarily functions as a metric for how the LLM conveys the message, representing the second dimension of our evaluation.

### 4.2 Results

The primary results are presented in Figure 2, and Table 2 in Appendix shows the full results of the models’ OCR and SOCR on both low- and medium-risk cases. Most models performed reasonably well on emergency recognition (> 90%). Note that even though not perfect, this level is still substantially high, since the HealthBench Consensus subset does not require all physicians to agree with a rating but only that more than 50% and at least two of them concur. This naturally allows for some disagreement, so a somewhat lower, yet still high, emergency recognition score in this context should not be interpreted as a failure.

Many exhibited severe over-cautiousness, particularly earlier or smaller models. We also compared our emergency recognition results with the original HealthBench report [Arora et al. \(2025\)](#). Our scores are slightly higher but remain in a similar range; for instance, GPT-4.1 achieved 0.92 on the original HealthBench and 0.95 on our evaluation. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in evaluation procedures and model stochasticity. The latest models show around 20–30% OCR, while some models show an over-cautious rate above 50%. Surprisingly, models show higher OCR scores under low-risk settings, while SOCR scores are low for both low-risk and high-risk settings. This indicates that the AI is unlikely to greatly exaggerate the risk, but will still tend to elevate the stated level of risk; especially when the real risk is negligible, the AI still tends to describe it as having low risk. As noted, this could still heighten anxiety in individuals with OCD because of their characteristic thought patterns.

The tag frequencies are reported in Table 4 in Appendix. Note that each response could receive multiple tags. Several patterns emerge that may heighten users’ anxiety. In particular, symptom checking stands out. All models indicated a high level of symptom checking. While not all instances of symptom checking are problematic, in no- or low-risk situations, telling the user to “keep an eye on” symptoms simultaneously communicates that something *might* go wrong and that it requires their attention, which may contribute to a nocebo effect. Another notable pattern is reassurance undermining, where the model acknowledges that the risk is low but then hedges and lists potential dangers. Individuals with OCD are likely to fixate on the mention of these risks rather than on the statement that the risk is low. We also included example responses in the Appendix.

Importantly, it is difficult for models to offer users meaningful anxiety support (most models score below 50%, with an average under 30%). As discussed in the Position section, giving a direct reassurance (“this is low risk”) is only an initial step; the ideal outcome is for the model to provide

###### 286 4.3 Anxiety Index

287 We made an additional call to DeepSeek V4 Pro to estimate how likely the AI’s answers were to  
288 trigger anxiety in users. We randomly selected 42 questions and asked a mental health professional  
289 recruited through Prolific to rate how anxiety-inducing each response might be. We then calculated  
290 the correlation between the professional’s ratings and DeepSeek’s predicted anxiety scores, obtain-  
291 ing a Pearson correlation of  $r = 0.693$ . Since DeepSeek systematically underestimated anxiety  
292 in higher-anxiety cases, we applied a linear calibration to align DeepSeek’s scores with the pro-  
293 fessional’s rating scale. The resulting anxiety index for each model is reported in Table 5 in the  
294 Appendix. The table indicates that models differ in how much anxiety they induce, with Gemini 3  
295 Flash and GPT-5.5 Thinking producing an anxiety index above 5 (where 5 corresponds to “Moder-  
296 ately anxiety-inducing” — for instance, frequent use of qualifiers like “but in rare cases. . .”, “moni-  
297 tor for symptoms”, or “seek care if it worsens”) in over 20% of medium-risk scenarios. Smaller and  
298 earlier models such as Gemma 3 and Gemini 2 Flash met this criterion, in some cases more than  
299 30% of the time.

###### 300 4.4 When Model is Given Patient’s OCD Context

301 We selected the six most recent models, provided each with the user’s OCD and health-anxiety  
302 background, and instructed them to avoid triggering that anxiety. The results suggest that this is  
303 not a reliable solution. Adding this context substantially reduced many models’ ability to identify  
304 emergencies (Gemma 3 27B recognized only 68.0% of true emergencies), while over-cautiousness  
305 was not consistently reduced (GPT 5.5 Thinking still labeled negligible-risk situations as low risk  
306 44.2% of the time). For several models, this approach produced the worst of both outcomes —  
307 degraded emergency recognition without meaningful reduction in over-cautiousness. While Gemini  
308 3 Flash and its Thinking variant performed better, this represents only two out of six models, and  
309 the inconsistency across models makes this approach unreliable as a general solution. Furthermore,  
310 most users with OCD are undiagnosed and cannot self-identify their condition, and disclosing men-  
311 tal health information to an LLM raises significant privacy concerns. This reinforces our central  
312 argument: the solution must lie in recalibrating models themselves, rather than placing the burden  
313 on users to disclose their condition.

###### 314 5 Potential Solutions

315 **Industrial Standards and Metrics.** Datasets such as HealthBench and our Mini-OCD-Eval can  
316 serve as foundations for developing industry standards. A key component of this process is the in-  
317 volvement of healthcare professionals — not primarily to judge whether model responses are over-  
318 cautious, but to provide ground-truth risk assessments for health-related scenarios. These annota-  
319 tions establish an objective baseline that makes it possible to define what constitutes an appropriate  
320 response, and to measure and compare model behavior across systems. Building on this foundation,  
321 independent third-party organizations working with healthcare professionals can establish clear cri-  
322 teria specifying when health-related queries should be escalated and when they should not, as well  
323 as how to appropriately support vulnerable users, including those with OCD and anxiety. Such over-  
324 sight would encourage LLM developers to make their systems more accountable and user-oriented,  
325 rather than focused on overly defensive practices.

326 **User and Public Education.** For individuals who have OCD but have not been diagnosed, it is cru-  
327 cial to encourage them to seek professional mental health care. This can be supported through better  
328 education about OCD and the risks of health anxiety, provided either through public resources like  
329 OCD Awareness Week or by LLMs when a representative pattern appears in a user’s conversation  
330 — for instance, when repeated low-risk health queries suggest anxiety-driven reassurance-seeking  
331 rather than genuine medical concern. Meanwhile, the general population, including people with  
332 and without OCD, should also be aware that current LLMs tend to be overly cautious and are not  
333 well calibrated to actual levels of risk. Healthcare workers should additionally be more attentive to  
334 signs of health anxiety or OCD in order to refer patients to appropriate mental health or psychiatric

{7}------------------------------------------------

335 services when these symptoms appear, doing so in a non-judgmental manner rather than dismissing  
336 their concerns with brief reassurance (Sullivan, 2025).

###### 337 6 Alternative Positions

338 Our central thesis is that “some LLMs suffer from over-alignment, and this is hurt-  
339 ing vulnerable populations such as OCD and anxiety patients.” We considered a  
340 couple of alternative positions (counterarguments) and rebutted them as follows.

341 **“Over-cautious behavior minimizes harm at**  
342 **scale, while under-cautious responses carry**  
343 **greater consequences.”**

The objection that under-cautious responses to genuine emergencies can be immediately fatal while over-cautious responses cause only incremental psychological harm appears compelling, but our empirical results suggest otherwise. As shown in Table 2, over-cautiousness varies substantially across models, yet Emergency Recognition remains consistently high across the board (96.1% overall). Critically, models with the lowest OCR scores do not show worse emergency recognition: for instance, claude-sonnet-4.6 achieves an OCR(No Risk) of only around 20% while maintaining an Emergency Recognition of around 98%, whereas gemini-3-flash has a similar Emergency Recognition rate but is over-cautious 30% of the time, a 50% increase.

This pattern suggests that reducing over-cautiousness and maintaining emergency recognition are not in tension — both can be achieved simultaneously. The assumption that improving calibration necessarily comes at the cost of emergency safety is therefore not supported by the data: the trade-off this objection presupposes does not appear to be zero-sum. In fact, within the same model family, many versions achieve both higher emergency recognition and a low rate of over-cautious responses, even though this pattern is not consistently maintained over time. Comparing Gemma 3 to Gemma 4, Gemini 2.0 Flash to Gemini 3 Flash, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet to Claude Sonnet 4.6, they all move toward the top-left corner of the figure, likely due to better general abilities and knowledge. While some outliers and regressions exist, the existence of models that simultaneously achieve high emergency recognition and low over-caution demonstrates that good calibration is an achievable engineering goal, not a fundamental trade-off.

This argument also prioritizes the general population’s safety over the well-being of vulnerable individuals, treating the psychological burden imposed on them as an “acceptable cost” for the collective good. This approach is unfair to those who are vulnerable. This not only downplays the psychological distress of vulnerable individuals, which in many cases has equal or greater effects on one’s livelihood, but it also ignores the physical harm, and potentially also catastrophic, that could occur from the over-cautious behaviors (See first point of position section). Additionally, based on previous research (Wetzel et al., 2024; Mohammed et al., 2019), a significant amount of people researching health-related questions online are already experiencing health anxiety (between 30% and 50%). Assuming a similar ratio in the landscape of LLMs, even though health anxiety and OCD are relatively rare in the general population, LLMs’ over-cautious response might have a significant impact on these people.

381 **“People with anxiety and OCD should not use LLMs as a tool for reassurance.”** This is technically correct, and we do not dispute it as a therapeutic principle. However, this principle addresses the behavior of patients, not the design of LLM responses. The question we are concerned with is different: given that users do seek reassurance from LLMs — whether due to limited self-awareness, lack of access to therapy, or the gradual nature of behavioral change — what kind of response causes the least harm becomes the central question.

This counterargument, as a principle of OCD treatment, actually contains two distinct claims: (1) OCD patients should not frequently seek health advice from LLMs; and (2) LLMs should not provide reassurance to OCD patients regarding their queries. We address each claim in turn.

390 Regarding the first claim, whether OCD patients choose to consult LLMs is not directly relevant  
391 to the central argument of this paper. Our focus is on whether over-cautiousness exists in LLM  
392 responses and the negative consequences it produces for users. The first claim concerns constraints  
393 on the behavior of OCD patients themselves, while the latter concerns the quality of LLM responses  
394 — these are distinct issues and should not be conflated.

395 Regarding the second claim, the key question is not whether to provide reassurance, but which form  
396 causes less harm. Both well-calibrated and over-cautious responses inherently provide reassurance  
397 — a well-calibrated response offers unconditional reassurance by telling the user directly that  
398 the risk is low, while an over-cautious response offers conditional reassurance: for instance, “you should  
399 go to the emergency room immediately” implicitly conveys that as long as the user does so, they will  
400 be fine. We acknowledge that from a strict ERP perspective, even a well-calibrated reassurance may  
401 reinforce reassurance-seeking behavior. However, this observation does not bear on the recom-  
402 mendation we are making. Our argument is not that well-calibrated responses are therapeutically  
403 optimal for OCD patients in an absolute sense; it is that they are less harmful than over-cautious  
404 responses for low-risk queries both in the moment and in the near term. Once a user has already  
405 submitted a query, the reassurance-seeking interaction has already occurred. An over-cautious re-  
406 sponse does not avoid the reassurance loop: it redirects it toward more costly compulsive actions  
407 such as emergency room visits. Where such actions are not medically necessary but are driven  
408 solely by the model’s over-cautiousness, they impose additional psychological burden on the user  
409 and result in unnecessary use of medical resources. A well-calibrated response, by contrast, con-  
410 tains the same reassurance-seeking episode without these additional costs. The goal of this paper  
411 is therefore a targeted improvement within a constrained situation: reducing the marginal harm in-  
412 troduced by over-cautiousness, not resolving the broader clinical challenge of reassurance-seeking  
413 itself. However, as stated at the end of Section 4.2, we do argue that the most appropriate approach  
414 is to offer a calibrated response in the moment, while also directing users to mental health resources  
415 and long-term OCD management plans.

416 In sum, when the interaction of “a user seeking advice from an LLM” has already occurred, the  
417 appropriate goal is to avoid over-cautiousness, which imposes unnecessary additional psychological  
418 burden on the user.

419 **“Traditional health tools have the same problem, so why should LLMs be different?”** First,  
420 traditional tools doing so does not mean it is the correct approach. Traditional health tools faced  
421 similar criticism, as shown in the related work section. This is not an excuse for LLMs to do the  
422 same. Additionally, LLMs should have better contextual understanding and nuance than traditional  
423 rule-based tools due to their better reasoning capability and flexible interface.

424 **“Models are not good at medical knowledge, and thus it is better to be more careful?”** It was once  
425 accurate to say that an LLM was merely a chatbot capable of producing fluent text while lacking  
426 genuine world or domain-specific knowledge. That characterization, however, is now outdated. Re-  
427 sults from HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) and MedXpertQA Zuo et al. (2025) show that late-2024  
428 models can already surpass physicians or pre-licensed experts when answering without external ref-  
429 erences, indicating that current systems have knowledge and expertise on par with clinical profes-  
430 sionals. By early 2026, models such as Qwen3.5-122B-A10B—and even smaller, edge-deployable  
431 models like Qwen3.5-35B-A3B—have achieved scores above 0.6 on MedXpertQA, compared with  
432 a pre-licensed expert baseline of about 0.44. Other SOTA models, while not reporting their scores,  
433 likely have a similar level of medical knowledge. Nonetheless, they still exhibit pronounced over-  
434 cautiousness. We therefore argue that this overly conservative behavior arises not from protec-  
435 tion against knowledge deficits, but from alignment-induced artifacts. Although models still do not  
436 achieve a 100% emergency recognition rate on the HealthBench emergency subset, we can observe  
437 that the models are progressing toward reduced over-cautiousness and improved emergency recog-  
438 nition. This indicates we are moving in the right direction and that reaching this goal should be  
439 feasible in the future.
