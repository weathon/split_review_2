## Abstract

1 Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as health information re-  
2 sources, yet their tendency toward over-cautious responses remains underex-  
3 plored. In this paper, we define over-cautiousness in LLM responses, examine its  
4 underlying causes, and discuss its negative consequences — particularly for vul-  
5 nerable populations such as individuals with OCD who are already prone to health  
6 anxiety. To systematically evaluate this phenomenon, we introduce OCD-Eval, a  
7 dataset of 225 queries centered on OCD-triggered health anxiety. Using both an  
8 LLM-as-judge approach and deterministic metrics, we measure the Over-Cautious  
9 Rate (OCR) by comparing risk levels assigned by General Practitioners (GPs) with  
10 those stated in model responses. Our evaluation of 22 LLMs reveals an average  
11 OCR of 43.9% on low-risk scenarios, demonstrating that over-cautiousness is a  
12 widespread and significant problem. We call for a recalibration of LLM values  
13 to enable models to accurately assess the true risk level underlying user queries,  
14 thereby reducing potential harm caused by unnecessarily alarming responses.

## 15 1 Introduction

16 Large Language Models (LLMs) are rapidly growing in capability and are now widely used as a  
17 routine source of information, particularly for specific and personalized questions. An Ipsos survey  
18 indicates that around 30% of U.S. consumers already turn to generative AI to address healthcare  
19 concerns between medical appointments (Choy et al., 2024). To mitigate the risk that LLMs provide  
20 harmful or unsafe guidance, developers typically align them to a set of safety preferences. However,  
21 these preferences are broad and developer-centric, and therefore do not fully reflect the wide range  
22 of real-world priorities and worries. In light of this alignment to safety preferences, we categorize  
23 LLM responses into three types: under-cautious, over-cautious, and appropriate. While the harms of  
24 under-cautious responses have received considerable attention in prior research, the potential dan-  
25 gers posed by over-cautious responses remain largely overlooked. While numerous studies examine  
26 LLM over-refusal, which can be interpreted as a form of over-cautiousness, they typically frame its  
27 drawbacks as reduced helpfulness (Cui et al., 2025; Röttger et al., 2024; Dabas et al., 2025) rather  
28 than as a safety concern in its own right. Among these, Zhang et al. (2026) is one of the few that rec-  
29 ognizes how over-refusal can drive users into misinformation sources, yet it does not address cases  
30 where LLMs do answer the question but with overly cautious framing. Formally, we characterize  
31 over-cautiousness in LLM responses by the following traits: 1. Objectively, the risk of an event is  
32 overstated. 2. Subjectively, the response creates a fearful or alarming tone.

33 To our knowledge, we are among the first to systematically investigate the harms associated with  
34 being overly cautious. We refer to this phenomenon as over-alignment, in analogy to overfitting in  
35 classical machine learning. In this paper, we argue that excessively cautious LLM responses to  
36 health-related queries can themselves be harmful, especially for vulnerable groups (Dorison

###### 40 2.1 Concerns over LLM Alignment

41 There are many concerns over LLM or AI (pre-LLM era) alignment. We focus on two concerns  
42 that are closely connected to our topic here. First, how should LLMs construct their value systems?  
43 Second, are LLMs capable of handling the diversity of real-world contexts, such as cultural and  
44 situational variation?

45 Regarding the question of how LLMs should construct their value systems, AI developers often  
46 claim that they have aligned their AI with “human values” or “human preferences”, to increase its  
47 usefulness and harmlessness, including InstructGPT and Anthropic AI ([Ouyang et al., 2022; Bai](#)  
48 [et al., 2022; Hendrycks et al., 2023](#)). [Sutrop \(2020\)](#) concerns that AI developers underestimated  
49 the difficulty of the question about which values or whose values the AI should align with. The  
50 authors argued that given that our everyday life is full of moral disagreements and the plural na-  
51 ture of values, how can we decide which objectives or values we inject into the AIs? [Arzberger](#)  
52 [et al. \(2024\)](#) argues that current alignment approaches rely on universal framings of human values,  
53 framings that are not inherently neutral or impartial, and that this can be problematic, leading to  
54 AI systems that are biased and to equity and justice issues. Given this inherent bias in AI systems,  
55 current LLMs tend to struggle with nuanced queries that involve cross-cultural diversity or situa-  
56 tional complexity. For example, [Segerer \(2025\)](#) finds that DeepSeek (a Chinese LLM) shows more  
57 value towards collectivism compared to Western LLMs. [Münker \(2025\)](#) states that their study sug-  
58 gests a concerning reality: “Large Language Models (LLMs) fail to represent diverse cultural moral  
59 frameworks despite their linguistic capabilities.” They highlighted the need for culturally-informed  
60 alignment objectives. Current approach regresses the model to a “mean moral framework” rather  
61 than representing diverse human values. Without cross-cultural evaluation metrics, models may ap-  
62 pear well-aligned within the tested context but fail to perform appropriately under alternative moral  
63 frameworks. Besides cultural complexity, another side of the same coin, more related to LLMs’  
64 health responses, is situational complexity. This is a well-studied area of AI over-refusal, where AI  
65 refuses to answer a question, in the name of safety, to some queries that are benign in the specific  
66 context. Common examples include how to make a TNT in Minecraft or how to kill a child process  
67 ([Zhang et al., 2025](#)). [Ray & Bhalani \(2024\)](#) also studied LLMs’ over-refusal in cases like prompts  
68 with homonyms (e.g., how to kill a process) or safe context (“how to kill someone [a video game  
69 name]”), etc. They found that many LLMs have problems with over-refusing prompts.

###### 70 2.2 LLM and Risk Handling

71 A large body of literature examines LLMs’ approach to risk, with findings suggesting that over-  
72 cautiousness in LLMs can have negative consequences across a wide range of domains. For instance,  
73 [Ouyang et al. \(2025\)](#) studied how LLMs’ cautiousness in ethical alignment affects economically  
74 valuable risk-taking, which might affect economic forecasts and suppress valuable risk-taking. [Cui](#)  
75 [et al. \(2025\)](#) is another benchmark and evaluation for model over-refusal, and they found a positive  
76 relationship between over-refusal and safety.

###### 77 2.3 HealthBench

78 A closely related effort is OpenAI’s HealthBench ([Arora et al., 2025](#)), which includes an  
79 `emergency-referral:non-emergent` category aimed at assessing whether models recommend  
80 escalation when it is not warranted, alongside genuine emergency scenarios. This category  
81 was introduced in light of the concern that excessive triage could “strain already overburdened health  
82 systems,” and the dataset was both constructed and validated by healthcare professionals. In practice,  
83 this setup also probes whether models systematically favor overly cautious recommendations:  
84 a model that repeatedly escalates without need can be viewed as over-cautious.

85 However, HealthBench is chiefly focused on the accuracy and appropriateness of answers to general  
86 medical queries. In contrast, our work contends that excessive caution can directly harm individual  
87 users *themselves*, especially those with OCD or anxiety disorders. We assess model outputs  
88 not only for correctness, but also for whether they reinforce maladaptive OCD thought patterns.  
89 Moreover, HealthBench’s over-caution assessment is largely limited to decisions about whether a  
90 patient should immediately seek emergency care, which covers only a narrow slice of the broader  
91 OCD-related anxiety landscape. Additionally, the prompts in our evaluation mirror the style and  
92 content of questions typically asked by individuals with OCD and anxiety, rather than general health  
93 inquiries as in HealthBench. A chest pain comparison example is provided in Figure ??.

###### 94 2.4 Health Tools, OCD, and Anxiety

95 Vulnerable users, particularly those with health anxiety or OCD, face unique risks when using health  
96 information tools. Prior to the widespread adoption of LLMs, such individuals were already turning  
97 to resources such as online symptom checkers and nursing helplines for medical reassurance. One  
98 study ([Wetzel et al., 2024](#)) found that health anxiety (used to be named hypochondria) is a reliable  
99 predictor of symptom checker application (SCA) use, and [Mohammed et al. \(2019\)](#) found over one  
100 third of people who conduct internet health searches exhibit signs of Cyberchondria. Critically, users  
101 with significant health anxiety may be particularly vulnerable to the adverse effects of these tools:  
102 [Doherty-Torstrick et al. \(2016\)](#) found that people with high health anxiety feel more anxious after  
103 online symptom checking, even in the absence of formal medical training. These findings underscore the  
104 importance of ensuring that LLM responses do not inadvertently exacerbate anxiety in vulnerable  
105 users.

106 With the rise of LLMs, these risks may be amplified. [Aslam & Nisar \(2023\)](#) warned that as LLMs re-  
107 spond in human-like text, more people may turn to them for health information, potentially increas-  
108 ing the prevalence of Cyberchondria. Moreover, repeated exposure to disease names and symptoms  
109 through LLM interactions may produce effects analogous to “Medical student syndrome,” where  
110 “Medical students are at higher risk for health anxiety and hypochondriacal attitudes than non-  
111 medical students are ([Sherif et al., 2023](#)).” In this sense, LLM interactions may expose users to  
112 a similar dynamic, even in the absence of formal medical training. These findings underscore the  
113 importance of ensuring that LLM responses do not inadvertently exacerbate anxiety in vulnerable  
114 users.

115 [Wong et al. \(2025\)](#) highlighted that even factually correct outputs can be highly misleading, further  
116 exacerbating user anxiety. For instance, the AI might reference a study claiming that something *sig-*  
117 *nificantly* increases the risk of a condition. While this is technically accurate, the term “significantly”  
118 has different meanings in scientific literature versus everyday language: in the former it typically  
119 refers to statistical significance, whereas in the latter it implies a substantial absolute change. Sim-  
120 ilar problems arise when the model gives different answers depending on how the user phrases the  
121 question (for example, “why it is safe” versus “why it is risky”). In such situations, the LLM shows  
122 strong information retrieval and synthesis capabilities but lacks appropriate communication skills  
123 to present information in a clear, consistent way that avoids being misleading. Our anecdotal ob-

###### 126 3 Position

127 Our position challenges the premise that models should be aligned to “human values/preferences”  
128 in an absolute sense, for health queries, particularly when this concept is oversimplified in health  
129 contexts as “always erring on the safe side.” While AI safety discourse typically focuses on prevent-  
130 ing risky behavior, we highlight the opposite danger: overly cautious responses that can exacerbate  
131 conditions like anxiety and OCD by reinforcing harmful behavioral patterns. We form our argument  
132 in two layers: why LLMs are prone to over-caution and why this is harmful.

133 LLMs are prone to over-caution for two interconnected reasons. Firstly, LLMs responses are always  
134 grounded in alignment with human values, yet the concept of universal “human values/preferences”  
135 is inherently problematic due to value pluralism and context dependency (Segerer, 2025; Arztberger  
136 et al., 2024; Münker, 2025). As Arztberger et al. (2024) notes, current alignment methods rely on  
137 supposedly universal values that may be biased against certain populations. As a result, LLMs tend  
138 to lack contextual awareness and default to uniformly conservative responses across all users — for  
139 example, advising everyone to “see a doctor if you’re worried” regardless of the actual risk level  
140 involved. While this may be entirely reasonable for typical users facing genuine health concerns,  
141 for individuals who already harbor pronounced anxiety about highly unlikely risks, such responses  
142 are anything but helpful: they may not only intensify existing worries, but also quietly reinforce  
143 maladaptive patterns of thinking. It also encourages more frequent doctor visits, which may increase  
144 secondary risks and place unnecessary burdens on time and resources for both the patient and the  
145 public health system. Secondly, LLM developers, similar to the online symptom checker developers,  
146 are facing significant legal and public relations pressures. The fear of being sued or facing liabilities  
147 is one of the real drivers that developers are tuning their tools to be conservative; this is a common  
148 effect in almost any industry as a defensive practice (e.g., you may always see a “Wet Floor” sign in  
149 many places even if the floor is dry or you see P65 warning in many places even if they do not pose  
150 a danger). Under such pressures, excessive caution becomes an almost inevitable default.

151 When taken to extremes, aligning AI with human values around safety — that is, *always* erring on  
152 the side of over-caution — can itself become harmful. Turchin (2019) argued that human values can-  
153 not be scaled and that some values serve to balance others. Maximizing certain values in isolation,  
154 without their counterparts, can be dangerous. This idea aligns with the virtue theory of the ancient  
155 Greeks, which holds that people should cultivate good character and that both excess and deficiency  
156 of certain traits are detrimental. The same principle applies to AI design. In our specific examples,  
157 an over-aligned AI that maximizes “safety” and “do no harm” may in fact cause harm because it  
158 fails to balance those goals with other human values such as reasonableness and rationality, which  
159 developers might overlook.

160 The harms resulting from over-alignment are not only mental but also physical. Over-cautious re-  
161 sponses intensify users’ anxiety, and the chronic stress that follows can in turn take a tangible toll  
162 on physical health as it is a well-established medical fact that stress and anxiety have a direct impact  
163 on physical health. More importantly, the LLM might reinforce their fear by validating it, leading  
164 the user to believe that these fears are justified and harmless. Excessive cleaning or the use of inap-  
165 propriately strong methods can lead to skin or mucosa damage and infections. Avoidance behaviors,  
166 such as avoiding clinics due to contamination anxiety, can delay necessary medical visits. Con-  
167 versely, over-visiting doctors increases infection risk. Unnecessary medical tests can cause direct  
168 harm and undermine trust, affecting future health decisions. Fear-driven avoidance of certain foods  
169 can lead to an unbalanced diet. LLMs likely will not directly suggest these behaviors; however, the  
170 reinforced anxiety might lead users to them as a form of secondary harm. Even if the LLM provides  
171 a response indicating low risk, presenting it in an alarmist tone may still leave the patient feeling  
172 unsafe, causing them to persist in their compulsive behavior.

173 These harms are also intensified for particularly vulnerable groups within the OCD population:  
174 people living in remote areas, in regions with lower medical standards, and individuals with low  
175 income. For those in remote locations and with limited financial resources, seeking unnecessa-  
176 ry medical care demands significantly more time, money, opportunity cost, or consequences (e.g., job

{4}------------------------------------------------

177 loss or social stigma). In areas where healthcare quality is lower, pursuing unnecessary treatment  
178 may also expose them to misinformation or unverified interventions. Moreover, these individuals are  
179 less likely to have access to mental health professionals who can identify their reassurance-seeking  
180 behavior as a symptom of OCD, meaning they may not realize that their actions are reinforcing their  
181 anxiety rather than alleviating it.

###### 182 4 OCD-Eval

183 In the previous section, we argued that overly cautious responses can be harmful. In this section, we  
184 will examine whether this problem occurs in LLMs and, if so, how severe it is.

185 As discussed in the related work section, although HealthBench includes queries related to over-  
186 cautious behavior, those questions simulate general user interactions with legitimate health concerns.  
187 While one could rewrite them to incorporate OCD-related tone and thought patterns, we found  
188 that the resulting queries often became awkward or implausibly anxious given their trivial medical  
189 context (e.g., a user asking whether a bug bite requires an ER visit), making them easy for LLMs  
190 to give simple rule based correct answer (not escalating) rather than genuinely testing for over-  
191 alignment. Given this limitation, we constructed a focused evaluation set of 225 queries centered on  
192 OCD-triggered health anxiety. The size exceed the non-emergency category of HealthBench (134).  
193 These queries are narrowed down using both embedding-based and keyword searches, and then  
194 each one is rigorously checked individually by a human validator. The questions were sourced from  
195 two authors with past or current OCD experiences. We filtered out questions that are too obvious  
196 (too low risk) such as worries about 405mg caffeine intake (limit is usually 400mg) and infection of  
197 smallpox. A word cloud of our dataset is shown in Figure 3 in Appendix. For a trade-off comparison  
198 and sanity check, we also evaluate how well the models can recognize real medical emergencies by  
199 using the emergency subset from HealthBench.

200 We began by evaluating the models with reasoning disabled, since this is the mode most users  
201 use when chatting with LLMs. We then enabled reasoning for the three most recent reasoning  
202 models (GPT-5.5, Gemini-3-Flash, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6) to see whether it affected their level of  
203 cautiousness.

204 All questions are assessed by General Practitioners (GPs) recruited via Prolific. For each question,  
205 a GP evaluates the level of risk in the scenario. The GP is given the OCD context of patient for  
206 labelling. We did not use GP’s answer of whether an LLM’s response shows appropriate risk  
207 calibration for both the general population and individuals with OCD, because we consider it difficult  
208 for GP to judge what is suitable for someone with an OCD background, and their thresholds vary as  
209 well.

210 In total, 7 GPs annotated the dataset, with each question–AI answer pair labeled by a single GP.  
211 The label distribution is: no risk 60.3%, minor risk 30.9%, medium risk 5.3%, and high risk 3.3%.  
212 Since our analysis focuses on non-high-risk scenarios where over-cautiousness can be meaningfully  
213 evaluated, we removed questions labeled as high risk. To validate the reliability of this filtering, we  
214 selected 50 questions from the remaining set and had them re-labeled by a separate group of five GPs  
215 (excluding previous Prolific participants). We calculated the agreement on these questions and get a  
216 exact agreement of 0.70, quadratic weighted Gwet’s AC2 of 0.93, and linear weighted AC2 of 0.83,  
217 MAE of 0.3 and signed AE of 0.02, showing very high agreement, indicating that two GPs labeling  
218 the same question arrived at highly consistent results. We verified that none of the second-round  
219 annotations reclassified any retained question as high risk. We also computed the fraction of cases  
220 in which the second relabel risk exceeded the first label, which is 0.16. Accounting for the inherent  
221 randomness in annotation, this value can be interpreted as an upper bound on the probability that  
222 random disagreement affects the “over-cautious” metrics. In other words, if a model’s over-cautious  
223 rate (OCR) exceeds 0.16, its over-cautiousness is unlikely to be attributable to random annotation  
224 noise, but rather reflects a genuine tendency toward over-caution in the model itself.

###### 225 4.1 Metrics

226 We require the evaluated LLM (not the judging model) to append a final risk rating at the end of  
227 its answer, using the same risk categories as the GP, and explicitly output this final risk level at  
228 the conclusion of its response. We compute two metrics: the over cautious rate (OCR) and the

{5}------------------------------------------------

229 significant over cautious rate (SOCR). OCR measures how often the model’s predicted final risk  
230 exceeds the ground truth, while SOCR captures cases where the model’s predicted risk is two levels  
231 or higher than the ground truth. We divided the dataset into two categories. The first, called low  
232 risk, contains questions that the GP labeled as negligible risk. The second category, medium risk,  
233 contains questions involving real risks, where we want to see whether the model will respond in an  
234 alarmist way to those risks. Our primary focus is on the OCR, because we want to know whether  
235 the AI assigns any risk level above negligible. Even labeling the risk as “low” (and not “negligible”)  
236 for OCD patients can cause worrying to the user, as they may feel that “low” is still not “low  
237 enough.” This is likely due to intolerance of uncertainty, perfectionism, and overestimation of threat.  
238 A one-level difference in risk labels may seem minor to most people, who would probably interpret  
239 “negligible risk” and “low risk” as essentially the same. However, for individuals with OCD, this  
240 distinction can be very significant. They often strive for absolute zero risk, so describing something  
241 as “low risk” signals that it is not completely risk-free, and this can reinforce the reasoning behind  
242 their compulsive behaviors. For instance, if a model responds, “the risk of touching X is low, but it  
243 would be good to do Y,” the patient will likely feel compelled not only to do Y but to go even further.  
244 We employed both an LLM-as-judge approach, using Deepseek V4 Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) from  
245 OpenRouter, and deterministic metrics. Using the LLM-as-judge setup, we primarily evaluated  
246 whether the model’s responses matched six predefined answer patterns, as listed in Table 1 in Ap-  
247 pendix. Because this evaluation reduces to straightforward pattern matching, we can reliably use the  
248 LLM judge for this purpose. The tag primarily functions as a metric for how the LLM conveys the  
249 message, representing the second dimension of our evaluation.

###### 250 4.2 Results

251 The primary results are presented in Figure 2 and Table 2 in Appendix shows the full results of  
252 the model’s OCR and SOCR on both low and medium risk cases. Most models performed reason-  
253 ably well on emergency recognition (> 90%). However, many exhibited severe over-cautiousness,  
254 particularly earlier or smaller models. We also compared our emergency recognition results with  
255 the original HealthBench report Arora et al. (2025). Our scores are slightly higher but remain in  
256 a similar range; for instance, GPT-4.1 achieved 0.92 on the original HealthBench and 0.95 on our  
257 evaluation. This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in evaluation procedures and model  
258 stochasticity. Latest models are showing around 20-30% of OCR, while some models shows over  
259 50% over cautious rate. Surprisingly, the model shows higher OCR scores under low-risk settings,  
260 while SOCR scores are low for both low-risk and high-risk settings. This indicates that the AI is  
261 unlikely to greatly exaggerate the risk, but will still tend to elevate the stated level of risk, especially  
262 when the real risk is negligible, the AI still tends to describe it having low risk. As noted, this could  
263 still heighten anxiety in individuals with OCD because of their characteristic thought patterns.

264 The tag frequencies are reported in Table 4 in Appendix. Note that each response could receive  
265 multiple tags. Several patterns emerge that may heighten users’ anxiety. In particular, symptom  
266 checking stands out. All models indicated a high level of symptom checking. While not all instances  
267 of symptom checking are problematic, in no- or low-risk situations, telling the user to “keep an eye  
268 on” symptoms simultaneously communicates that something *might* go wrong and that it requires  
269 their attention, which may contribute to a nocebo effect. Another notable pattern is reassurance  
270 undermining, where the model acknowledges that the risk is low but then hedges and lists potential  
271 dangers. Individuals with OCD are likely to fixate on the mention of these risks rather than on the  
272 statement that the risk is low.

273 Importantly, most models rarely provide mental health support in their responses (Provide Anxiety  
274 Help rate under 50% across models, see Table 4). This raises a clinical dilemma: providing direct  
275 reassurance (e.g., “this is very low risk”) offers temporary relief but may reinforce the reassurance-  
276 seeking cycle central to OCD and health anxiety. However, we argue that reassurance is still prefer-  
277 able to alarming responses. An alarming response spikes the user’s immediate anxiety and drives  
278 them toward compulsive actions such as visiting the emergency room — and in doing so, it im-  
279 plicitly offers its own form of reassurance: the anxiety is temporarily resolved once the compulsive  
280 action is completed. In effect, the model replaces one reassurance-seeking loop with another that  
281 carries greater cost and distress. The optimal response, therefore, is to briefly acknowledge the  
282 low risk without excessive elaboration, while suggesting that the recurring pattern of worry itself

283 may benefit from professional mental health support. We also included example responses in the  
284 Appendix.

###### 285 4.3 When Model is Given Patient’s OCD Context

286 We selected the six most recent models, provided each with the user’s OCD and health-anxiety  
287 background, and instructed them to avoid triggering that anxiety. The results suggest that this is  
288 not a reliable solution. Adding this context substantially reduced many models’ ability to identify  
289 emergencies (Gemma 3 27B recognized only 68.0% of true emergencies), while over-cautiousness  
290 was not consistently reduced (GPT 5.5 Thinking still labeled negligible-risk situations as low risk  
291 44.2% of the time). For several models, this approach produced the worst of both outcomes —  
292 degraded emergency recognition without meaningful reduction in over-cautiousness. While Gemini  
293 3 Flash and its Thinking variant performed better, this represents only two out of six models, and  
294 the inconsistency across models makes this approach unreliable as a general solution. Furthermore,  
295 most users with OCD are undiagnosed and cannot self-identify their condition, and disclosing men-  
296 tal health information to an LLM raises significant privacy concerns. This reinforces our central  
297 argument: the solution must lie in recalibrating models themselves, rather than placing the burden  
298 on users to disclose their condition.

###### 299 5 Potential Solutions

300 **Industrial Standards and Metrics.** Datasets such as HealthBench and our Mini-OCD-Eval can  
301 serve as foundations for developing industry standards. A key component of this process is the in-  
302 volvement of healthcare professionals — not primarily to judge whether model responses are over-  
303 cautious, but to provide ground-truth risk assessments for health-related scenarios. These annota-  
304 tions establish an objective baseline that makes it possible to define what constitutes an appropriate  
305 response, and to measure and compare model behavior across systems. Building on this foundation,  
306 independent third-party organizations working with healthcare professionals can establish clear cri-  
307 teria specifying when health-related queries should be escalated and when they should not, as well  
308 as how to appropriately support vulnerable users, including those with OCD and anxiety. Such over-  
309 sight would encourage LLM developers to make their systems more accountable and user-oriented,  
310 rather than focused on overly defensive practices.

311 **More Professionals in Alignment.** We can include more health professionals in the alignment,  
312 designing specific training datasets, and when evaluating, focus on both over- and under-cautious.  
313 HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) has already addressed that emergency triage mistakes, both over-  
314 and underdiagnosis, could be harmful, and future alignment efforts should extend this principle to  
315 non-emergency, anxiety-related scenarios as well.

316 **User and Public Education.** For individuals who have OCD but have not been diagnosed, it is cru-  
317 cial to encourage them to seek professional mental health care. This can be supported through better  
318 education about OCD and the risks of health anxiety, provided either through public resources like  
319 OCD Awareness Week or by LLMs when a representative pattern appears in a user’s conversation  
320 — for instance, when repeated low-risk health queries suggest anxiety-driven reassurance-seeking  
321 rather than genuine medical concern. Meanwhile, the general population, including people with  
322 and without OCD, should also be aware that current LLMs tend to be overly cautious and are not  
323 well calibrated to actual levels of risk. Healthcare workers should additionally be more attentive to  
324 signs of health anxiety or OCD in order to refer patients to appropriate mental health or psychiatric  
325 services when these symptoms appear, doing so in a non-judgmental manner rather than dismissing  
326 their concerns with brief reassurance (Sullivan, 2025).

 343 this objection presupposes does not appear to be zero-sum. In fact, within the same model fam-

 345 sponses, even though this pattern is not consistently maintained over time. Comparing Gemma 3 to

 348 While some outliers and regressions exist, the existence of models that simultaneously achieve high  
 349 emergency recognition and low over-caution demonstrates that good calibration is an achievable  
 350 engineering goal, not a fundamental trade-off.

351 This argument also prioritizes the general population’s safety over the well-being of vulnerable  
 352 individuals, treating the psychological burden imposed on them as an “acceptable cost” for the  
 353 collective good. This approach is unfair to those who are vulnerable. This not only downplays the  
 354 psychological distress of vulnerable individuals, which in many cases has equal or greater effects on  
 355 one’s livelihood, but it also ignores the physical harm, and potentially also catastrophic, that could  
 356 occur from the over-cautious behaviors (See first point of position section). Additionally, based  
 357 on previous research (Wetzel et al., 2024; Mohammed et al., 2019), a significant amount of people  
 358 researching health-related questions online are already experiencing health anxiety (between 30%  
 359 and 50%). Assuming a similar ratio in the landscape of LLMs, even though health anxiety and OCD  
 360 are relatively rare in the general population, LLMs’ over-cautious response might have a significant  
 361 impact on these people.

362 **“People with anxiety and OCD should not use LLMs as a tool for reassurance.”** This is techni-  
 363 cally correct, and we do not dispute it as a therapeutic principle. However, this principle addresses  
 364 the behavior of patients, not the design of LLM responses. The question we are concerned with is  
 365 different: given that users do seek reassurance from LLMs — whether due to limited self-awareness,  
 366 lack of access to therapy, or the gradual nature of behavioral change — what kind of response causes  
 367 the least harm becomes the central question. This counterargument, as a principle of OCD treatment,  
 368 actually contains two distinct claims: (1) OCD patients should not frequently seek health advice

{8}------------------------------------------------

369 from LLMs; and (2) LLMs should not provide reassurance to OCD patients regarding their queries.  
370 We address each claim in turn.

371 Regarding the first claim, whether OCD patients choose to consult LLMs is not directly relevant  
372 to the central argument of this paper. Our focus is on whether over-cautiousness exists in LLM  
373 responses and the negative consequences it produces for users. The first claim concerns constraints  
374 on the behavior of OCD patients themselves, while the latter concerns the quality of LLM responses  
375 — these are distinct issues and should not be conflated.

376 Regarding the second claim, as discussed in Section 4.2, the key question is not whether to provide  
377 reassurance, but which form causes less harm. We acknowledge that from a strict ERP perspec-  
378 tive, even a well-calibrated reassurance may reinforce reassurance-seeking behavior. However, this  
379 observation does not bear on the recommendation we are making. Our argument is not that well-  
380 calibrated responses are therapeutically optimal for OCD patients in an absolute sense — it is that  
381 they are less harmful than over-cautious responses for low-risk queries. Once a user has already sub-  
382 mitted a query, the reassurance-seeking interaction has already occurred. An over-cautious response  
383 does not avoid the reassurance loop — it redirects it toward more costly compulsive actions such  
384 as emergency room visits. A well-calibrated response, by contrast, contains the same reassurance-  
385 seeking episode without the additional burden of unnecessary anxiety and compulsive behavior.  
386 The goal of this paper is therefore a targeted improvement within a constrained situation: reducing  
387 the marginal harm introduced by over-cautiousness, not resolving the broader clinical challenge of  
388 reassurance-seeking itself.

389 In sum, when the interaction of "a user seeking advice from an LLM" has already occurred, the  
390 appropriate goal is to avoid over-cautiousness, which imposes unnecessary additional psychological  
391 burden on the user.

392 **"Traditional health tools have the same problem, why LLMs should be different?"** Firstly, tradi-  
393 tional tools doing so does not mean it is the correct approach. Traditional health tools faced similar  
394 criticism, as shown in the related work section. This is not an excuse for LLMs to do the same. Ad-  
395 ditionally, LLMs should have better contextual understanding and nuance than traditional rule-based  
396 tools due to their better reasoning capability and flexible interface.

397 **"Models are not good at medical knowledge, and thus it is better to be more careful?"** It was once  
398 accurate to say that an LLM was merely a chatbot capable of producing fluent text while lacking  
399 genuine world or domain-specific knowledge. That characterization, however, is now outdated. Re-  
400 sults from HealthBench (Arora et al., 2025) and MedXpertQA Zuo et al. (2025) show that late-2024  
401 models can already surpass physicians or pre-licensed experts when answering without external ref-  
402 erences, indicating that current systems have knowledge and expertise on par with clinical profes-  
403 sionals. By early 2026, models such as Qwen3.5-122B-A10B—and even smaller, edge-deployable  
404 models like Qwen3.5-35B-A3B—have achieved scores above 0.6 on MedXpertQA, compared with  
405 a pre-licensed expert baseline of about 0.44. Other SOTA models, while not reported their scores,  
406 likely have similar level of medical knowledge. Nonetheless, they still exhibit pronounced over-  
407 cautiousness. We therefore argue that this overly conservative behavior arises not from protection  
408 against knowledge deficits, but from alignment-induced artifacts. Although on HealthBench emer-  
409 gency subset models still do not achieve a 100% emergency recognition rate, we can observe that  
410 the models are progressing toward reduced over-cautiousness and improved emergency recognition.  
411 This indicates we are moving in the right direction and that reaching this goal should be feasible in  
412 the future.

###### 413 7 Conclusion

414 This paper demonstrates that LLM over-cautiousness is a widespread, measurable problem, with an  
415 average 43.9% over-cautious rate on low-risk health scenarios, and that it poses real harm to vulner-  
416 able populations like those with OCD and health anxiety. The empirical results show that reducing  
417 over-cautiousness and maintaining high emergency recognition are not in tension, as several models  
418 achieve both simultaneously. Recalibrating LLM alignment to reflect actual risk levels, rather than  
419 defaulting to maximum caution, is both feasible and necessary to avoid turning a safety mechanism  
420 into a source of harm.
