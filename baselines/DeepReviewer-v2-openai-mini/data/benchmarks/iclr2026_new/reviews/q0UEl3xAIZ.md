## Summary
This paper applies Goal-Oriented Environment Inference (GOEI), a model-based Bayesian reinforcement learning algorithm, to the competitive two-player card game Hol's der Geier. GOEI learns a minimal "core" state representation sufficient for outcome prediction by using variational Bayesian inference with a Dirichlet process prior to automatically compress redundant observations. The authors train GOEI on games between a random strategy and the Nash equilibrium (NE) strategy, then test its performance against the NE opponent. The key result is that GOEI achieves near-NE-equivalent performance (median reward rate -0.010 at best hyperparameters) using only 452 states (2.9%) of the 15,542 possible observations in the restricted setting. The paper also analyzes which observation features are preserved via mutual information analysis, finding that information about hand cards is largely discarded while table-card and score-difference information is partially retained. The work provides a useful demonstration that GOEI's state reduction mechanism, previously validated only in abstract grid-world environments, can be effective in a more complex discrete competitive game.

## Strengths
**S1. Clear problem framing and relevant research question.** The paper addresses a well-motivated problem in explainable reinforcement learning: how to identify the minimal sufficient state representation (the "core") needed for optimal decision-making in competitive environments. The connection between state reduction and interpretability is clearly articulated.

**S2. Impressive quantitative result on state compression.** The central finding — that GOEI reduces the observation space from 15,542 to 452 states (2.9%) while maintaining near-Nash-equilibrium performance — is striking and provides strong evidence that the method can identify genuinely redundant information. This is the most compelling contribution of the paper.

**S3. Informative mutual information analysis.** The analysis in Section 4.2, which decomposes the reduced state representation by feature type, provides useful insight into what information is preserved versus discarded. The finding that hand-card information is largely eliminated while table-card and score-difference information is partially retained offers interpretable clues about the game's strategic structure.

**S4. Honest discussion of limitations.** The authors acknowledge the key limitation of separating environment inference from strategy optimization, and they candidly note that state reduction does not automatically produce verbal explanations. This intellectual honesty strengthens the paper's credibility.

**S5. Reproducible experimental setup.** The training protocol (3,000 epochs, 200 games per epoch, 21 seeds) is well-specified, and the hyperparameter settings are reported in detail. The evaluation against NE as a gold-standard benchmark is appropriate for a zero-sum game.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Strong and unvalidated assumption about opponent stationarity (major).** The method assumes that the opponent's action depends only on the current observation $o_t$ and is independent of game history (Section 3.1). This assumption is critical for the Markov property (Eq. 1) and the Bellman equation (Eq. 2), but it is not empirically validated. Real opponents in competitive games use history-dependent strategies, and the NE strategy is itself history-dependent because remaining card values change as cards are discarded. The paper does not discuss how this assumption limits the generalizability of the findings. The Discussion mentions interactive learning as a challenge but does not directly address the stationary opponent assumption.

**W2. Evaluation protocol creates an unrealistically favorable setup (major).** GOEI is trained on pooled games from two fixed strategies (Rand vs. NE) and tested against the same NE opponent it observed during training. This means (a) the training distribution includes games from the NE player's own perspective, providing direct information about optimal play, (b) the test opponent is identical to the opponent seen in training, and (c) GOEI is never tested against unseen strategies. The comparison with Q-learning is also somewhat skewed: Q-learning uses tabular representations over the full observation space, while GOEI's advantage comes largely from its state reduction mechanism. A fairer comparison would include Q-learning with state aggregation or function approximation.

**W3. Main performance claim lacks statistical rigor (major).** The paper states that GOEI achieves performance "indistinguishable" from the NE (reward rate ≃ 0), but the reported median for the best configuration (β=0.2, α=25) is -0.010 with quartiles [-0.012, -0.009], which does not include 0. The paper does not provide a statistical equivalence test or confidence interval analysis to support the "indistinguishable" claim. The learning curves (Figure 2A) show that GOEI approaches zero but remains slightly below it, even after 3,000 epochs. The claim should be qualified as "near-optimal" rather than "indistinguishable."

**W4. Contradictory interpretation of mutual information results (major).** Section 4.2 reports that information about the agent's hand (AH) and opponent's hand (OH) is "almost completely reduced" by GOEI, yet immediately claims these features are "likely to be crucial for learning a near-optimal strategy." This creates an unresolved logical tension: if AH and OH are crucial, how does GOEI succeed despite discarding them? The paper speculates that "required information is maintained in complex combinations," but this is not supported by the individual-feature mutual information analysis. The authors should either acknowledge that AH and OH may not be individually necessary (and explain why) or provide a mechanism for how the compressed state representation preserves their information through higher-order interactions.

**W5. Limited experimental scope (moderate).** The study is confined to a single game (5-card Hol's der Geier) with a single opponent strategy (NE). The game assumes full observability of both players' hand cards, which simplifies the problem relative to most real-world competitive scenarios. The hyperparameter search uses only 9 combinations (β∈{0.1,0.2,0.3}, α∈{11,25,50}) without systematic tuning methodology. Claims about scalability to larger games or broader applicability are speculative.

**W6. Algorithm description lacks intuitive explanation (moderate).** The GOEI method is presented in a technically dense manner (Section 3.2) without an intuition-first explanation. The core innovation — inferring a reverse model $p(a_t|s_{t+1},s_t)$ instead of the forward transition $p(s_{t+1}|a_t,s_t)$ — is attributed to "Bayes theory in which Bayesian inference is independent of the true causal direction," but this justification is not explained. Readers unfamiliar with the original GOEI paper (Takahashi et al., 2024) will struggle to understand why this approach is valid.

**W7. Abstract and introduction need structural improvement (minor).** The abstract is a single dense paragraph mixing background, method, and results without a clear problem-gap-solution-result logic. The introduction ends without a specific contribution summary or paper roadmap. The phrase "To our surprise" in the abstract is informal for a scientific publication.

**W8. Explainability goal is acknowledged as unmet (minor).** The Discussion candidly states that GOEI "could not give a verbal explanation of the reduced state representation more concretely than Figure 3." While the authors are honest about this limitation, it partially undermines the original motivation for developing GOEI (improving explainability through state reduction). The paper would benefit from discussing what additional steps are needed to bridge the gap between state reduction and human-interpretable explanations.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper demonstrates a meaningful extension of the GOEI framework from abstract grid-world environments to a competitive card game, achieving impressive state compression (2.9% of original observations) with near-NE performance. This provides useful evidence that the method can scale beyond toy domains. However, the evaluation has significant limitations that constrain the strength of the claims: the stationary opponent assumption is unvalidated, the training-test setup is unrealistically favorable, the main performance claim lacks statistical rigor, and the mutual information interpretation contains an unresolved contradiction. The experimental scope is limited to a single game with full observability, making broader claims about generalizability premature. The paper is technically sound within its stated scope but would benefit from addressing these weaknesses before the contribution can be fully assessed. The final score prioritizes research value (moderate — interesting state reduction result) and novelty (moderate — extension of existing method to new domain) as primary scoring dimensions, consistent with the evaluation criteria.
