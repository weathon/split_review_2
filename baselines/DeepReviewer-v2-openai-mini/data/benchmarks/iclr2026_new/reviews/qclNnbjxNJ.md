## Summary

This paper addresses a significant and genuinely underexplored challenge in interventional causal discovery: post-treatment selection, where samples are selectively included after interventions based on quality-control criteria. The authors show that post-treatment selection produces the same CI signature (variant marginal distribution with invariant conditional distribution) as direct causation, making it non-identifiable within existing frameworks that rely on interventional invariance patterns. To resolve this, they propose a new formulation extending the augmented DAG to explicitly model selection variables, define a fine-grained interventional Markov equivalence class ($\mathcal{FI}$-Markov equivalence) with a corresponding graphical representation called $\mathcal{F}$-PAG that uses novel edge marks (square, $\blacktriangle$) to distinguish causation from selection, and develop the $\mathcal{F}$-FCI algorithm that is proven sound and complete for identifying causal relations and post-treatment selection patterns among intervened variables. Empirical results on synthetic data (up to 25 variables, n=2000) and real-world gene perturbation data demonstrate precision improvements over baselines. The paper is technically rigorous, well-motivated, and addresses a problem with genuine practical relevance in genomics and clinical trials.

## Strengths
**1. Well-motivated and practically significant problem.** The paper identifies a genuine gap in interventional causal discovery: post-treatment selection is common in biological (single-cell genomics, clinical trials) and observational settings, yet existing frameworks like GIES, IGSP, and FCI-interven cannot distinguish it from direct causation. The motivating examples (Figure 1) are clear and compelling.

**2. Clean theoretical formulation.** The augmented DAG framework extended with selection variables provides a principled way to model post-treatment selection. Equation (1) correctly factorizes the joint distribution under selection conditioning, and Definition 1 formally integrates S and ψ into a unified graphical model. The three classes of statistical signals (interventional distribution changes, invariant relations, structural symmetries) provide a systematic taxonomy of CI patterns.

**3. Novel equivalence class with refined graphical representation.** The $\mathcal{FI}$-Markov equivalence class (Definition 2) and $\mathcal{F}$-PAG (Definition 5) are genuinely new contributions. The introduction of the square mark (□) and the $\blacktriangle$ edge type for inducing paths without direct causal links goes beyond standard PAG representation and enables distinguishing structures that were previously indistinguishable.

**4. Soundness and completeness guarantees.** Theorems 3 and 4 provide formal guarantees for the $\mathcal{F}$-FCI algorithm under oracle CI tests for the subgraph over intervened variables. This is a non-trivial theoretical contribution given the complexity of the selection-augmented causal graph.

**5. Empirical validation on synthetic and real data.** The simulations cover multiple sample sizes (500, 1500, 2000) and variable dimensions (10-25) under both hard and soft interventions, with 95% confidence intervals. The real-world application on Norman single-cell perturbation data demonstrates practical applicability.

## Weaknesses
**W1 (Critical) — Algorithm pseudocode has an unexecutable orientation step.** In Algorithm 1, Step 2.2 (lines 120-125 in the pseudocode box), every `if` condition checks the *identical* tuple `CIs == (⟂, ⟂, ⟂, ⟂)` but claims to produce different edge orientations (→, ↔, ∘→, etc.). As written, the pseudocode is logically inconsistent — only the first condition would ever match, and the remaining orientations are unreachable. This is almost certainly a formatting/copy-paste error where distinct CI pattern tuples should have been used. Since the entire algorithmic contribution hinges on correctly mapping CI patterns to orientations, this error makes the algorithm specification non-reproducible. The authors must provide the correct distinct CI-pattern tuples for each orientation case, consistent with Figure 4(i).

**W2 (Major) — Soundness/completeness guarantees are scoped to intervened variables only.** Theorems 3 and 4 formally guarantee soundness and completeness only for the subgraph over *intervened* variables. For non-intervened variables and edges between intervened and non-intervened nodes, the algorithm falls back to standard FCI orientation rules whose properties are not re-proven under the selection model. The paper's central claim (Title, Abstract) of "learning causal graphs with latent confounders and post-treatment selection" implies full-graph guarantees, but the formal results are limited. The authors should explicitly scope their soundness claims in the abstract and introduction, and discuss whether the interventional CI patterns provide any identifying power beyond FCI for non-intervened variables.

**W3 (Major) — Theorem 1 omits S conditioning in invariance/variability statements.** The second bullet (conditional invariance) and third bullet (marginal variability) of Theorem 1 omit $S$ from the conditioning set in the d-separation conditions. Since all derivations are conducted conditional on $S=1$, the formal statement should include $S$ to maintain consistency: $\psi_{I^{(k)}} \perp\!\!\!\perp_d X_A \mid X_B, S$ and $\psi_{I^{(k)}} \not\perp\!\!\!\perp_d X_A \mid S$ (without conditioning on $X_B$). The current omission could lead to incorrect CI test implementation when selection variables are present.

**W4 (Major) — Experimental evaluation lacks completeness in main text.** Only Precision and SHD are reported in the main text (Figure 6); Recall and F1 are deferred to Appendix D (Figure 10). For causal discovery, high precision can be achieved by being overly conservative (predicting few edges), and without recall the claim of "over 5% precision improvement" is incomplete. There is no ablation study isolating the key innovation (Type I inducing node detection in Step 2.3) from the baseline FCI skeleton procedure. The comparison against baselines would be more informative if tabulated with exact values across all configurations, rather than shown only in line plots.

**W5 (Major) — The Type I inducing node requirement is a critical practical limitation without empirical characterization.** The algorithm's key distinguishing power (distinguishing causation from selection) depends entirely on the presence of Type I inducing nodes. However, the paper provides no analysis — theoretical or empirical — of how restrictive this requirement is. What fraction of random DAGs with post-treatment selection contain Type I inducing nodes on the relevant paths? Do the real-world gene regulatory networks contain them? Without this characterization, readers cannot assess the practical applicability.

**W6 (Major) — S=1 notation ambiguity and selection assumptions.** The paper uses "S=1" without clarifying whether this conditions on the full selection vector (all S_i=1) or element-wise conditioning. The assumption that selection involves "at least two observed variables" is stated without justification. Single-variable post-treatment selection (e.g., filtering on one quality metric) is common in practice and excluded without clear rationale.

**W7 (Major) — Notation issues in Definition 1 and Definition 5.** (a) Definition 1 includes exogenous noise $\epsilon$ as a graph vertex, but $\epsilon$ never appears in any subsequent CI or identification statement, making its inclusion superfluous. (b) Definition 5 claims "eight types of edges" but the listing appears incomplete/duplicated, and the mark symbols (tail ⊣, arrowhead ▲) are inconsistent with later usage in the F-PAG illustrations. (c) The notation $\hat{X}_{\text{pag}(i)}$ in Equation (1) uses "pag" which collides with the "PAG" abbreviation for Partial Ancestral Graph used in Section 3.3.2.

**W8 (Minor) — $mathcal{F}$-FCL typo in Abstract.** The abstract writes $\mathcal{F}$-FCL instead of $\mathcal{F}$-FCI. While minor, this typo in the first mention of the algorithm name undermines confidence in careful proofreading.

**W9 (Minor) — Real-world evaluation is thin.** The gene regulatory network application (Section 5.2) reports results qualitatively (referring to Figure 13 and Appendix D.3) but provides no quantitative metrics (e.g., precision/recall against known regulatory links). The evaluation relies on enrichment analysis via Enrichr rather than direct comparison against ground-truth regulatory relationships.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a genuinely underexplored and practically significant problem (post-treatment selection in interventional causal discovery) with a well-motivated theoretical formulation. The $\mathcal{FI}$-Markov equivalence class and $\mathcal{F}$-PAG representation are substantial conceptual contributions that extend existing frameworks non-trivially. The soundness and completeness proofs (though scoped only to intervened variables) represent credible theoretical work.

However, the score is moderated by several significant issues: (1) the critical pseudocode error in Algorithm 1 Step 2.2 that makes the core orientation procedure unexecutable as written; (2) the scope limitation of the formal guarantees relative to the paper's broad claims; (3) incomplete experimental reporting (recall/F1 deferred to appendix, no ablation study for the key innovation); (4) notation ambiguities and missing assumptions that would affect reproducibility; and (5) the uncharacterized practical limitation of the Type I inducing node requirement. These weaknesses are fixable but must be addressed before the paper can be accepted.

The novelty/comparison conclusions are deferred for manual verification as external literature search was unavailable in this run (Retrieval-Disabled Mode). A manual literature check against related interventional causal discovery methods (GIES, IGSP, UT-IGSP, CDIS, JCI) is recommended to validate the claimed advantages.
