## Summary
This paper presents a large-scale interpretability analysis of DINOv2-B using a stable sparse autoencoder (RA-SAE) to extract a 32,000-concept dictionary. The study unfolds in three parts: (1) analyzing how three downstream tasks (classification, segmentation, depth estimation) recruit different concept subsets, revealing task-specific functional specialization; (2) characterizing the statistical and geometric properties of the learned concept space, finding departures from the idealized sparse near-orthogonal structure predicted by the Linear Representation Hypothesis (LRH); and (3) proposing the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis (MRH), where token embeddings behave as Minkowski sums of convex polytopes around archetypal landmarks, with multi-head attention providing a natural mechanism.

The paper's strengths lie in its systematic empirical methodology, the scale of the dictionary (32k concepts), and the novel conceptual proposal of MRH. However, several weaknesses temper the contributions: (a) causal claims about "Elsewhere" concepts and depth cue families lack strong discriminative evidence; (b) the MRH empirical evidence is suggestive but does not distinguish MRH from simpler alternative geometric models; (c) statistical quantification for geometric comparisons is missing; (d) the position analysis has methodological gaps; (e) the Discussion lacks a limitations section and draws unsupported implications; and (f) novelty claims (e.g., "largest interpretability demo") are unverifiable. Since external paper search is unavailable in this run, all novelty/comparison conclusions are deferred for manual verification.

The paper makes a genuine contribution by providing a detailed empirical map of DINOv2's internal concept space and by proposing a geometrically principled alternative to sparse coding. With revisions to strengthen causal evidence, add statistical rigor, and bound claims appropriately, this work could have significant impact on vision model interpretability.

## Strengths
1. **Systematic empirical methodology.** The paper presents a thorough, three-part analysis pipeline: SAE training → task-specific concept recruitment → geometric characterization → new hypothesis proposal. The logical flow is clear and each step builds on the previous one.

2. **Scale of the dictionary.** The 32,000-concept dictionary extracted from DINOv2-B is, to this reviewer's knowledge, one of the largest concept dictionaries obtained for a vision foundation model. The interactive visualization (to be released) is a valuable community resource that can enable further interpretability research.

3. **Novel conceptual proposal (MRH).** The Minkowski Representation Hypothesis offers a geometrically principled and mathematically well-defined alternative to the sparse near-orthogonal coding view. Defining representations as Minkowski sums of convex polytopes around archetypal landmarks is a creative synthesis linking attention mechanisms, convex geometry, and conceptual spaces theory. This provides a fresh perspective that could influence future work on representation geometry.

4. **Task-specific specialization findings.** The identification of "Elsewhere" concepts (classification), border concepts (segmentation), and monocular cue families (depth estimation) is empirically interesting and provides concrete insights into how DINOv2's internal features are deployed across different perceptual tasks. The observation that segmentation relies almost exclusively on boundary detectors forming a tight subspace is particularly striking.

5. **Falsifiability-conscious framing.** The paper explicitly identifies testable predictions of MRH (Propositions 1-2) and acknowledges that the empirical evidence is preliminary. This is more scientifically sound than claiming definitive confirmation.

6. **Causal perturbation approach for depth cues.** Using controlled image perturbations (median blur, edge-preserving smoothing, high-pass filtering) to isolate different depth cue families is a clever experimental design that goes beyond simple correlation-based analysis.

7. **Transparency about limitations.** The paper acknowledges several caveats, including the non-identifiability of Minkowski decomposition (Proposition 2) and that MRH evidence is preliminary. This scientific honesty is commendable.

## Weaknesses
### W1. MRH empirical evidence is preliminary and does not distinguish MRH from simpler alternatives [Major]
The three empirical tests for MRH (k-NN geodesics, Archetypal Analysis, Gram matrix block structure) are each consistent with MRH but also with simpler geometric explanations. (i) k-NN geodesics staying near the data support while straight-line interpolations leave it is expected for any data manifold with curvature, not specifically for Minkowski sums of polytopes. (ii) Archetypal Analysis matching SAE reconstruction with ~10 archetypes per image shows low-dimensional convex structure, but does not distinguish between a single polytope and the specific tiled MRH structure. (iii) The Gram matrix block structure is the most MRH-specific evidence, yet the paper does not quantify the block structure (e.g., modularity score) or compare against a null model with randomly permuted tile assignments. The paper frames MRH as the central conceptual contribution, but the evidence gap between the LRH departures and the specific MRH proposal remains large. *Fix: Add modularity quantification with permutation testing, compare against a single-polytope baseline, and explicitly state what evidence would falsify MRH.*

### W2. Statistical claims about concept geometry lack formal quantification [Major]
The paper makes multiple comparative geometric claims (higher coherence than Grassmannian baselines, sharper spectral decay, heavier-tailed inner products) based on visual inspection of figures without reporting confidence intervals, effect sizes, or significance tests. For example, "coherence is higher" is stated without mean pairwise cosine similarity values or their uncertainties. This weakens the paper's central argument that DINOv2's concept space departs substantially from LRH predictions. *Fix: Report mean ± SD of pairwise cosine similarities for observed vs. Grassmannian baselines, decay exponents for singular value spectra, and Hoyer score distributions across atoms. Include a compact supplementary table.*

### W3. Causal claims about "Elsewhere" concepts are insufficiently supported [Major]
The paper interprets "Elsewhere" concepts as implementing "learned negation" based on a causal masking experiment. However, the masking perturbation may introduce confounds (altered background texture, changed input distribution) that produce the observed effect without true negation. The paper itself acknowledges "another interpretation being distributed off-object evidence" but does not resolve this ambiguity. The claim of learned negation goes beyond what the current causal evidence supports. *Fix: (a) Add a control experiment comparing object removal to object insertion into blank contexts. (b) Use more cautious wording that explicitly states both interpretations remain viable. (c) Specify what experimental design would distinguish the two accounts.*

### W4. Proposition 1 conflates mathematical consistency with empirical implementation [Major]
Proposition 1 shows that multi-head attention outputs lie in the Minkowski sum of per-head convex sets — a mathematical observation that holds for any transformer. The paper presents this as evidence that "multi-head attention constructively realizes MRH," but this conflates two distinct claims: (i) attention is geometrically compatible with MRH (true by definition), and (ii) DINOv2's learned representations actually follow the specific tiled archetype structure of MRH (unestablished). The additional reachability condition ("if each head can realize any point of relint") is a strong assumption not verified for DINOv2. *Fix: Reframe Proposition 1 as showing MRH is geometrically compatible with attention, not that attention implements MRH. Add a clear statement that the empirical question is whether DINOv2's representations conform to MRH, which the preliminary experiments begin to address.*

### W5. Position analysis has methodological gaps [Major]
The argument that PCA structure persists beyond positional information relies on two steps: (1) linear position decoders show reduced accuracy in later layers, and (2) projecting orthogonally to a learned linear positional basis P preserves PCA structure. However, a linear decoder failing does not mean position is absent — it could be nonlinearly encoded. The orthogonal projection to P removes only linear positional structure. Nonlinear position encoding could survive both tests and contribute to the observed PCA smoothness. Since attention mechanisms are known to encode position nonlinearly (e.g., relative position biases, positional attention patterns), this is a plausible confound. *Fix: Add nonlinear position decoding (e.g., 2-layer MLP) across layers. Report both linear and nonlinear R^2. If nonlinear decoding succeeds, qualify the "beyond position" claim accordingly.*

### W6. Depth cue identification via perturbations lacks task-specific controls [Moderate]
Perturbation-based clustering (median blurring, edge-preserving smoothing, high-pass filtering) identifies three concept clusters interpreted as depth-specific cue families. However, these perturbations affect image content broadly; the observed clustering could reflect general responses to image statistics (e.g., high-frequency information, texture removal) rather than depth-specific processing. The paper does not apply the same perturbation protocol to classification or segmentation concepts as a control, so the depth-specific interpretation remains unvalidated. *Fix: Apply the same perturbation protocol to classification and segmentation concept groups. If the three clusters are depth-specific, they should not appear (or appear differently) for other tasks.*

### W7. Abstract and contribution list overclaim [Moderate]
The abstract claims "the largest interpretability demonstration for a vision foundation model to date" — an unverifiable scope claim. The contributions list states "we show that multi-head attention constructively realizes MRH" — conflating theoretical compatibility with empirical validation. These overclaims weaken reviewer trust and are unnecessary given the paper's substantive contributions. *Fix: Replace "largest" with a bounded factual statement. Reframe MRH claim as proposing a hypothesis with preliminary evidence, not a demonstrated fact.*

### W8. No variance or significance reporting for task-recruitment analyses [Moderate]
The task-specific concept recruitment analysis (Section 3) reports that classification draws from broader concept spans, that intra-task concepts are more aligned, and that task-specific eigenspectra decay faster, but all without variance estimates or significance tests. Given that the "top 100 concepts per head" selection is somewhat arbitrary and the cosine similarity comparisons could be influenced by the UMAP preprocessing, readers cannot assess the reliability of these patterns. *Fix: Report bootstrap confidence intervals for the span, alignment, and spectral decay measures. Show that findings are robust across different top-k thresholds (k=50, 200, 500).*

### W9. SAE inductive biases may shape observed geometry [Moderate]
The paper uses RA-SAE with non-negativity, sparsity (k=8), and convex-hull atom constraints to extract concepts, then measures geometric properties of the resulting dictionary as evidence against LRH. However, the SAE's constraints themselves impose structure on D — for example, the convex hull constraint D ∈ conv(A) restricts atoms to combinations of real activations, and the BatchTopK sparsity encourages distributed representations. These constraints could partially explain the observed departures (higher coherence, sharp spectral decay) without reflecting DINOv2's native activation geometry. The paper partially addresses this by examining model-native token geometry in Section 5, but the link between SAE-based and native geometry is not established quantitatively. *Fix: Compare SAE dictionary properties to those of a randomly initialized overcomplete dictionary projected through the same convex hull constraint. If similar geometric patterns appear, they may be artifacts of the constraint rather than properties of DINOv2's representations.*

### W10. Discussion lacks limitations and draws unsupported implications [Moderate]
The Discussion section (Section 7) does not include a limitations paragraph, omitting important caveats: the analysis is limited to DINOv2-B, SAE biases may influence findings, MRH evidence is preliminary, and all tasks use linear probes (which may not reflect actual downstream usage). Additionally, the implication that "extracting concepts from single layers is insufficient — proper decomposition requires signals from the entire network structure" is presented as a finding but is actually a theoretical deduction from Proposition 2 (non-identifiability), not an empirical result. The paper does not show that multi-layer approaches outperform single-layer ones. *Fix: Add a limitations paragraph. Replace unsupported implication with a conditional statement and an explicit call for future empirical validation.*

## Score
**Final Score: 6.5/10**

**Rationale:** This paper makes a genuine contribution by providing the most detailed empirical characterization to date of DINOv2's internal concept space and by proposing the Minkowski Representation Hypothesis as a geometrically principled alternative to sparse coding. The systematic three-part methodology, the scale of the dictionary (32k concepts), and the concrete task-specific findings (Elsewhere concepts, border concepts, depth cue families) represent meaningful advances for vision model interpretability.

However, several factors prevent a higher score. The central conceptual contribution (MRH) is supported by preliminary evidence that does not yet distinguish it from simpler alternative geometric models. Several key claims (causal interpretation of Elsewhere concepts, depth cue specificity, position-independent PCA structure) rely on analyses with plausible confounds or methodological gaps. Statistical quantification for geometric comparisons is absent, reducing the strength of the paper's central empirical argument. Overclaims in the abstract and contributions list weaken reviewer trust. The Discussion lacks a limitations section and draws an unsupported implication. External novelty verification was unavailable in this run due to API constraints, so comparative positioning against prior work remains unverified.

With revisions addressing the major weaknesses (especially W1-W5), the paper could strengthen to approximately 8/10. The core empirical contribution and the MRH proposal have the potential to influence the field, but the current evidence base is still preliminary relative to the strength of the claims. I encourage the authors to view the weaknesses as actionable revision targets that can substantially improve the paper's scientific impact.
