## Summary

This paper introduces CrowdFM, a foundation model for crowdsourced label aggregation. The core idea is to pre-train a bipartite graph neural network on a large corpus of synthetically generated crowdsourcing datasets, enabling zero-shot inference on unseen datasets without dataset-specific parameter estimation. The model uses a size-invariant initialization (all workers share an embedding, all tasks share an embedding) and attention-based message passing over worker-task-option triples, and it is pre-trained with a cross-entropy objective on data generated by a domain-randomized 3PL-based generator. Experiments on 22 real-world crowdsourcing benchmarks show that a single fixed CrowdFM model achieves 83.41% average accuracy, comparable to state-of-the-art per-dataset methods, while requiring no retraining. The paper also demonstrates downstream applications including worker ability estimation, task difficulty estimation, and compatibility-based task assignment.

The work addresses an important practical problem — eliminating dataset-specific training in crowdsourced label aggregation — and the experimental evaluation is reasonably extensive. However, the paper contains several instances of overclaiming (e.g., treating performance outcomes as a standalone contribution, claiming "consistent superiority" over methods that achieve higher average accuracy, describing moderate correlations as "strong"), and some key technical design choices are not adequately justified (e.g., the unusual self-attention formulation, the unidimensional-ability assumption in the synthetic generator). The novelty of the approach is tempered by the fact that individual components (bipartite GNN, attention, IRT-based generation) are established techniques, though their combination for cross-dataset crowdsourcing aggregation is new.

## Strengths
**S1. Important problem and practically motivated paradigm.** Eliminating dataset-specific training in crowdsourced label aggregation is a well-motivated goal with clear practical value. The paper correctly identifies the tension between Majority Voting (simple but inaccurate) and parameter-intensive methods (accurate but non-scalable), and proposes a foundation-model approach that bridges this gap.

**S2. Comprehensive evaluation across 22 datasets.** The experimental evaluation is one of the most extensive in the crowdsourcing aggregation literature, spanning diverse domains including NLP (RTE, Sentiment), computer vision (Bird, Dog, Face), and general benchmarks. The use of a Wilcoxon signed-ranks test provides statistical rigor for comparing against baselines.

**S3. Well-structured synthetic data generator.** The domain-randomized generator (3PL-based annotation model, heavy-tailed task assignment, randomized structural parameters) is carefully designed to produce diverse training scenarios. The ablation study confirms that the synthetic generator contributes meaningfully to performance (w/o SG drops from ~83% to ~78.5%), and the paper includes analysis comparing synthetic to real datasets (Appendix F).

**S4. Efficient zero-shot inference.** At 0.53 seconds average runtime per dataset, CrowdFM is orders of magnitude faster than per-dataset deep learning methods (e.g., LAA: 223s, TiReMGE: 27s, GOVERN: 95s) while achieving competitive accuracy. This efficiency-accuracy trade-off is the paper's strongest practical contribution.

**S5. Downstream task demonstrations.** Showing that the learned embeddings can be repurposed for worker assessment and task assignment (with only lightweight heads) extends the utility beyond label aggregation and aligns with the foundation model vision. The task-assignment experiment (Figure 5) is a novel and practical contribution.

**S6. Clean and reproducible methodology.** The paper's method sections are clearly written, with explicit equations for each component, a well-defined pre-training objective, and a provided code repository. The use of size-invariant initialization is an elegant solution to the variable-size problem.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Overclaiming: "consistent superiority" contradicts the reported evidence. (Major, Severity: High)**
*[Page 1 — Introduction: contribution list (C3); Page 6 — Comparison with dataset-specific methods]*

The paper claims "superior to state-of-the-art aggregation methods" (Contribution C3) and "consistent superiority of CrowdFM across the full set of datasets." However, Table 1 shows EBCC achieves higher average accuracy (84.08% vs 83.41%), and the Wilcoxon test finds no significant difference between CrowdFM and EBCC (p=0.90089). The "superiority" narrative is therefore misleading. "Competitive with" or "comparable to" would be accurate. Contribution C3 should be reframed as experimental validation of C1 and C2 rather than a standalone conceptual contribution.

*Fix:* Revise contribution C3 to describe experimental validation rather than superior performance. Replace "superior to" with "competitive with" throughout.

**W2. Unjustified attention mechanism design. (Major, Severity: High)**
*[Page 3-4 — Method: Attention-based encoder]*

Eqs (5-7) implement an unusual self-attention mechanism where each annotation triple $(w_i, t_j, a_{ij})$ produces both query $q_{ij}$ and key $k_{ij}$ from the same representation $h_{ij}^{(l)}$, then attention weights are softmax-normalized over all annotations incident to the same node. This is effectively a "self-importance weighting" rather than standard cross-attention. The design choice is not explained or compared to alternatives (e.g., using worker/task node embeddings as queries to attend over annotation keys). Without justification, the reader cannot assess whether this design is a meaningful contribution or an arbitrary choice.

*Fix:* Add an explanatory paragraph motivating why each annotation's own query-key dot product serves as an importance weight and why normalization across annotations of the same node is appropriate.

**W3. Missing ablations for core architectural claims. (Major, Severity: High)**
*[Page 9 — Ablation study]*

The ablation study tests (1) attention vs. mean aggregation and (2) the proposed synthetic generator vs. uniform random generator. However, the paper's central architectural claim (C1) — that explicitly modeling workers, tasks, and options as a bipartite graph is crucial — is never ablated. There is no comparison to a non-graph baseline (e.g., logistic regression on annotation statistics) or to a simpler GNN variant without explicit option nodes. The hyperparameter ablations (layers, dimensions) show monotonic improvement without identifying a plateau, making the "scalability" claim an untested extrapolation.

*Fix:* Add a non-graph baseline ablation (e.g., MLP on aggregated features). Report ablations with variance across multiple seeds. Extend the hyperparameter analysis to find the plateau point.

**W4. Unidimensional ability assumption in synthetic data generator. (Moderate, Severity: Medium)**
*[Page 3 — Method: 3PL annotation generation]*

The 3PL model assumes each worker has a single global ability parameter $\theta_i$. In practice, workers may have multi-dimensional expertise (e.g., good at labeling sentiment but poor at labeling factual accuracy). The paper does not discuss this limitation or its potential impact on generalization to real multi-topic datasets. The moderate real-world correlations (Pearson=0.449 for worker ability, Figure 4) may partially stem from this unidimensional assumption.

*Fix:* Acknowledge the unidimensional assumption as a limitation. Discuss how multi-dimensional IRT could extend the generator. Optionally, analyze per-topic ability correlation on multi-topic datasets.

**W5. Correlation strength overstatement in downstream tasks. (Moderate, Severity: Medium)**
*[Page 7-8 — Worker and Task Assessment]*

Figure 4 reports Pearson correlations of 0.449 (worker ability) and 0.606 (task difficulty) on real-world data. The text describes these as "strong positive correlation." However, values in the 0.45-0.6 range are conventionally described as moderate, not strong. Overstating the correlation quality inflates the apparent success of the downstream transfer. Additionally, only one dataset (Web) is tested for real-world assessment, limiting generalizability claims.

*Fix:* Replace "strong correlation" with "moderate positive correlation." Add at least one more dataset for real-world assessment validation.

**W6. Ambiguous accuracy improvement reporting. (Minor, Severity: Low)**
*[Page 5-6 — Main results]*

The paper states "average improvement of +1.64 percentage points" but does not report the standard deviation or interquartile range of improvements across the 22 datasets. The improvements range from -0.08 to +12.93 percentage points, indicating high variance. The text uses "%" and "percentage points" interchangeably, causing ambiguity. Also, "marginal drop on Senti of 0.08%" should be "0.08 percentage points."

*Fix:* Consistently use "percentage points" for absolute accuracy deltas. Report standard deviation of improvements. Clarify per-dataset variance.

**W7. Conclusion lacks limitations section. (Minor, Severity: Low)**
*[Page 9 — Conclusion]*

The conclusion summarizes findings but does not explicitly state the study's limitations (evaluation only on classification tasks, unidimensional ability assumption, potential sim-to-real gap, single-dataset downstream assessment). A dedicated limitation paragraph would improve scientific transparency.

*Fix:* Add a limitations paragraph before the future-work sentence, covering: (1) classification-only evaluation, (2) unidimensional ability assumption, (3) moderate downstream correlations, (4) potential distribution shift risks.

**W8. Related work is descriptive rather than comparative. (Minor, Severity: Low)**
*[Page 9 — Related Work]*

The Label Aggregation section lists existing methods but does not organize them along meaningful comparison axes (e.g., generative vs. discriminative, parametric vs. non-parametric, with vs. without worker modeling). The comparison with HyperLM is the only substantial contrast. A more structured related-work section would strengthen the positioning of CrowdFM.

*Fix:* Reorganize the related work into thematic categories (probabilistic models, deep embedding methods, cross-dataset approaches). For each category, explicitly state the key differences from CrowdFM.

**W9. Limited variance reporting across experiments. (Minor, Severity: Low)**

The main results (Table 1) report only average accuracy without standard deviations or confidence intervals. While the Wilcoxon test provides some statistical rigor, per-dataset variance across multiple runs (seeds) is not reported, making it impossible to assess result stability. The claimed improvements on some datasets (e.g., +0.04 for Fact, Trec, ZC_in) are extremely small and could be within noise range.

*Fix:* Report mean±std over at least 3 random seeds for key metrics. Flag small improvements (<0.5 percentage points) as within potential noise range.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** This paper tackles a meaningful and well-motivated problem — cross-dataset zero-shot label aggregation — with a technically sound approach combining bipartite GNNs, attention-based message passing, and synthetic data pre-training. The evaluation is extensive (22 datasets), and the efficiency-accuracy trade-off is practically compelling.

However, the score is constrained by three factors:

1. **Overclaiming (W1, W5):** The paper systematically overstates its empirical results (claiming "superiority" while EBCC has higher average accuracy; describing moderate correlations as "strong"). This weakens scientific credibility and would likely trigger significant reviewer pushback.

2. **Incomplete validation of architectural claims (W2, W3):** The core novelty (bipartite GNN with explicit worker-task-option modeling) is not isolated through ablation, and the unusual attention mechanism is not justified. Without these analyses, the reader cannot assess how much of the gain comes from the claimed architectural innovation versus established components.

3. **Unacknowledged limitations (W4, W7, W9):** The unidimensional ability assumption, limited variance reporting, and missing limitations section suggest the paper does not fully address its own boundary conditions.

The work has clear potential and the core direction (pre-trained GNN for crowdsourcing) is promising. Revision addressing the overclaiming, adding key ablations, and acknowledging limitations could substantially raise the score to 7-8/10.
