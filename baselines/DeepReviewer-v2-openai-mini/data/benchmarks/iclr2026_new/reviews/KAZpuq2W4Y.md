## Summary

This paper proposes HOMIL (Higher-Order Multi-Instance Learning), a WSI classification framework that extends attention-based MIL (ABMIL) by incorporating second-order statistical moments (covariance matrices) alongside the standard first-order attention-weighted pooling. To manage the computational cost of computing the covariance matrix over all patches, the authors employ DBSCAN clustering to group similar patches into variable-size clusters — fine-grained for rare pathological regions and coarse-grained for abundant normal tissue. First- and second-order representations are computed on cluster features, then fused via an attention mechanism for slide-level prediction. Experiments on CAMELYON16 (metastasis detection) and TCGA-NSCLC (lung cancer subtyping) show consistent improvements over nine MIL baselines in ACC, AUC, and F1, while maintaining competitive computational efficiency. The core ideas — using covariance to capture inter-feature variability and adaptive clustering for efficiency — are sensible and practically motivated. However, the paper has several significant shortcomings: (1) the covariance formulation lacks proper attention weighting and normalization, (2) the Conv1D-based covariance vectorization is needlessly complex and unablated, (3) no statistical significance tests are reported despite modest performance gains, (4) the ablation study is incomplete, and (5) key claims about robustness and state-of-the-art performance are over-extended relative to the evidence. Novelty verification is deferred to manual literature review due to retrieval unavailability in this run.

## Strengths
1. **Well-motivated conceptual contribution.** The idea of going beyond first-order (mean) aggregation to capture inter-feature variability via second-order moments is principled and directly addresses a known limitation of existing attention-based MIL methods. The connection between attention-weighted averaging and first-order moment estimation is clearly explained, making the extension to covariance matrices natural and accessible.

2. **Computational efficiency through adaptive clustering.** The use of DBSCAN to produce variable-resolution cluster representations is practically sensible. Unlike methods that process all patches uniformly (which is computationally expensive for WSIs with 10,000+ patches), HOMIL compresses redundant normal tissue into coarse clusters while preserving rare pathological regions in fine clusters. This design achieves significant speedups (310s vs. 7,200s for MambaMIL on CAMELYON16) without sacrificing accuracy.

3. **Comprehensive baseline comparison.** The paper compares against nine MIL baselines spanning different families (simple pooling, attention-based, transformer-based, state-space, and hierarchical), all implemented in a unified codebase with consistent data splits. This is a thorough empirical setup that reduces concerns about implementation unfairness.

4. **Ablation isolating two main components.** The ablation study (Table 3) clearly separates the contributions of the clustering module (CM) and the second-order moment module (SOM), demonstrating that both components contribute to the overall performance. The efficiency gains from clustering are also quantified.

## Weaknesses
### W1. Covariance formulation lacks attention weighting and normalization (Major)
The weighted covariance matrix in Section 4.3.3 (Eq. 9) is defined as **C = Σ_k g̃_k g̃_k^T**, where g̃_k = g_k - v^(1). This formulation has two problems. First, it is an unnormalized sum of outer products whose magnitude grows with the number of clusters K, making the second-order representation scale-dependent across slides with different K. Second, the attention weights a_k (computed in Section 4.3.1) are used only for the first-order representation but not re-applied to the covariance computation — uninformative and informative clusters contribute equally. This contradicts the paper's stated goal of emphasizing diagnostically important clusters. The authors should either use an attention-weighted covariance (**C = Σ_k a_k · g̃_k g̃_k^T**) or normalize by (K-1) and justify the choice. *Evidence: Page 5 - Method: second-order representation paragraph.*

### W2. No statistical significance tests despite modest gains (Major)
Tables 1 and 2 report ACC, AUC, and F1 as mean±SE over 5-fold CV, but no statistical significance tests (e.g., paired t-test, McNemar) are performed. This is critical because many improvements are small: AUC gains of 0.35% on CAMELYON16 and 0.83% on TCGA-NSCLC, with SE intervals that overlap with several baselines (e.g., HOMIL AUC 99.23±0.62 vs. S4MIL 99.02±0.87 on CAMELYON16). Without significance testing, the paper's central claim of "significantly improves the state-of-the-art performance" is unsubstantiated. The authors should add paired t-test results across the 5 folds for each baseline comparison, or report bootstrapped confidence intervals. *Evidence: Page 6-7 - Experimental Setup and Overall Performances.*

### W3. Ablation study is incomplete (Major)
The ablation (Table 3) covers only the clustering module (CM) and second-order moment (SOM). Several important design choices are not ablated: (a) the Conv1D-based covariance vectorization vs. simpler flatten+linear projection, (b) the attention-based fusion vs. concatenation or gated sum, (c) PCA dimension reduction (d'=32) vs. using full 512-dim features, (d) DBSCAN hyperparameter sensitivity (ε percentile, minPts) in the main text (only brief appendix mention). Without these ablations, readers cannot determine which design decisions are critical and which are incidental. The authors should add at least 2-3 additional ablation variants to support the claimed effectiveness of their design. *Evidence: Page 8 - Ablation Study.*

### W4. Covariance vectorization via Conv1D+max-pooling is overly complex and unprincipled (Major)
The procedure in Section 4.3.3 compresses the d×d covariance matrix C into a d-dimensional vector by: (i) convolving each row C_i with m-dimensional kernels, (ii) max-pooling the sequence, (iii) max-pooling across T kernels. This introduces three hyperparameters (m=64, T=4, max-pooling) without justification. Worse, two successive max-pooling operations discard most of the covariance information, which seems contradictory to the goal of "capturing feature correlations." A simpler approach — vectorizing the upper triangle and projecting to d dimensions via a linear layer — would be more interpretable and likely equally effective. The authors should either justify this design with an ablation study or replace it with a simpler alternative. *Evidence: Page 5 - Method: covariance vectorization.*

### W5. Overclaimed SOTA and robustness language (Moderate)
The abstract claims "significantly improves the state-of-the-art performance," and Section 5.3 states HOMIL "highlights robustness across task complexities." These claims are over-extended: the method is evaluated on only two binary classification datasets (metastasis detection and cancer subtyping), both from the same tissue/cancer domain. No multi-class, survival, or cross-scanner generalization experiments are performed. The SOTA claim is also problematic because not all competing methods may have had their hyperparameters optimally tuned for these datasets. The authors should replace global SOTA wording with bounded comparative statements and replace "robustness" with "consistent performance on two evaluated tasks." *Evidence: Page 1 - Abstract; Page 8 - Overall Performances paragraph.*

### W6. Discussion of fusion weights lacks analytical depth (Moderate)
Section 5.5 reports that α^(1) (first-order weight) stabilizes higher than α^(2), concluding the model "retains second-order statistics for complementary structural cues." However, this is purely descriptive: no variance across folds is reported, no correlation with slide-level heterogeneity is shown, and no analysis of when the second-order term changes predictions is provided. The conclusion that second-order information is "complementary" is therefore speculative. The authors should report fold-wise mean±std of α^(2) and show at least one qualitative example where the second-order representation alters the classification outcome. *Evidence: Page 8 - Discussion.*

### W7. Conclusion is too brief and lacks limitation discussion (Minor)
The Conclusion (Section 6) is three sentences summarizing what was done, with no mention of limitations, failure cases, or future work. This is a missed opportunity to bound claims and guide follow-up research. The authors should expand the conclusion to include one sentence on limitations (e.g., only two binary datasets evaluated) and one sentence on the most important next step. *Evidence: Page 8 - Conclusion.*

### W8. Notation inconsistency in covariance vectorization (Minor)
In Section 4.3.3, the kernel notation changes between "k_{i,t}" (line 85) and "k_{i,j}" (line 86) — the first index i seems to represent the kernel index, while elsewhere it represents the row index. This ambiguity could cause implementation confusion. The authors should standardize indexing: use k_t for kernels and C_{i,:} for rows. *Evidence: Page 5 - Method: Equation (10)-(11).*

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

*Rationale:* The paper presents a well-motivated and practically sensible extension to attention-based MIL by incorporating second-order moments and adaptive clustering. The core ideas are clear, and the experimental comparison against nine baselines on two datasets is thorough in scope. However, the score is limited by several significant concerns: (1) the covariance formulation has a technical flaw (lack of attention weighting and normalization) that undermines the claimed second-order aggregation, (2) no statistical significance testing is performed despite small performance margins and overlapping error bars, (3) the covariance vectorization design is overly complex and unablated, (4) the ablation study omits several critical design choices, and (5) the claims of state-of-the-art performance and robustness are over-extended relative to the two-dataset binary evaluation. Novelty cannot be fully assessed without external literature comparison (retrieval unavailable in this run). The paper has a solid conceptual foundation and useful empirical results, but the technical rigor of the analysis and the defensibility of the claims need substantial improvement before the contribution can be considered fully validated.

**Post-Revision Target: 7.5/10** — achievable if the authors add attention weighting to the covariance, include significance tests, complete the ablation study, simplify the vectorization design, and bound their claims to the evaluated settings.
