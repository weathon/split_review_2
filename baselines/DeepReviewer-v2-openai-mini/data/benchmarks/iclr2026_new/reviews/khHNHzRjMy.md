## Summary

This paper introduces EmoSign, a dataset of 200 American Sign Language (ASL) video clips annotated with sentiment ratings (7-point scale), intensity scores for 10 emotion categories, and open-ended descriptions of emotion cues from three Deaf native ASL signers. The authors also present baseline evaluations of four multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o, AffectGPT, Qwen2.5-VL-7B, MiniGPT4) under caption-only, video-only, and video+caption conditions for sentiment analysis, single-label emotion classification, and qualitative emotion cue grounding analysis.

**Core contributions (C1-C3):**
- C1: First dedicated dataset with fine-grained emotion and sentiment labels for ASL videos, annotated by Deaf native signers.
- C2: Documentation of qualitative descriptions of emotion cues from native signers' perspectives.
- C3: Baseline model performance benchmarks for sentiment and emotion classification in ASL, with modality ablation.

**Key findings:** Multimodal models perform poorly on visual-only emotion recognition from sign videos, show positive-emotion bias, and rely heavily on textual captions. The dataset fills a recognized gap in sign language emotion research.

**Novelty assessment:** Deferred — external literature verification was unavailable in this run (Retrieval-Disabled Mode). The FePh dataset comparison in the paper appears to be the closest prior work, and the authors' differentiation claims (full-body vs. cropped-face annotation, Deaf vs. hearing annotators, fine-grained vs. binary labels, qualitative cue descriptions) need independent verification.

**Primary weaknesses identified:** (1) Inter-annotator agreement uses different statistics (Krippendorff's alpha vs. Fleiss' kappa) for comparison with prior datasets; (2) Several negative emotion categories have near-chance agreement (alpha < 0.2), undermining benchmark ground truth; (3) Prompt structure inconsistency across models (GPT-4o multi-task vs. others single-task) is a confound for cross-model comparisons; (4) Small dataset size (200 utterances, 4 signers, 3 annotators) limits statistical power and generalizability; (5) VADER-based selection on English captions introduces potential misalignment with visual emotional content.

## Strengths
**1. Addresses a genuine and important research gap.** Emotion recognition in sign language is severely understudied compared to spoken languages, and the practical stakes are high (legal, medical, and accessibility contexts). The paper identifies a concrete gap — the absence of emotion-annotated ASL video resources — and provides a direct remedy. This is a well-motivated and timely contribution.

**2. Thoughtful dataset design with culturally aware annotation.** The decision to recruit Deaf native ASL signers (rather than hearing annotators or ASL learners) as annotators is a significant methodological strength. The three-layer annotation strategy (sentiment, emotion categories, open-ended cue descriptions) captures both quantitative and qualitative dimensions of emotional expression. The inclusion of confidence ratings and the option to skip distressing content demonstrates ethical consideration.

**3. Detailed documentation of emotion cues from native signers' perspective.** The qualitative themes (non-manual markers, modified signing parameters, contextual disambiguation) provide a rich descriptive account that goes beyond simple label-based datasets. This is potentially valuable for both linguistic research and model development. The dataset documentation of how specific facial expressions, head movements, and signing modifications map to emotional content is a distinctive contribution.

**4. Rigorous baseline evaluation with modality ablation.** The three-condition experimental design (caption-only, video-only, video+caption) is appropriate for diagnosing how models use different modalities. The selection of four diverse MLLMs provides reasonable coverage of current capabilities. The analysis of model biases and failure modes in Section 5.3 is particularly insightful — the observation that models interpret the same visual cue differently depending on whether the caption is available is a meaningful finding for the multimodal emotion recognition community.

**5. Transparent limitations section and community engagement.** The authors openly discuss the VADER-text mismatch issue and the single-source dataset constraint (ASLLRP). The months of community engagement, collaboration with Deaf experts, and commitment to open-source release demonstrate responsible research practices that align with recommendations for working with Deaf communities.

**6. Reproducibility-oriented decisions.** Using an existing well-documented corpus (ASLLRP) rather than acted emotional displays enables reproducibility and comparison with future studies. The public availability commitment (code and data after acceptance) further supports reproducibility.

## Weaknesses
### Major Weaknesses

**W1. Cross-statistic comparison of inter-annotator agreement (Page 4 — Dataset Post-Processing).** The paper reports Krippendorff's alpha for EmoSign labels but compares against Fleiss' kappa values reported by MELD (0.43) and IEMOCAP (0.48). These are different statistics with different mathematical formulations and expected ranges. Krippendorff's alpha is generally more conservative than Fleiss' kappa, meaning the comparison may overstate EmoSign's relative agreement. Additionally, several negative emotion categories (surprise_neg: 0.119, disgust: 0.166, frustration: 0.330) have near-chance agreement, which is a concern that is not adequately discussed in relation to benchmark validity. **Fix:** (a) Recompute comparable metrics or explicitly state the statistical incomparability; (b) Add a frank discussion of how low-agreement categories affect the reliability of the emotion classification benchmark (Table 4); (c) Consider reporting per-label sample sizes for the single-expression subset.

**W2. Prompt structure confound in model comparisons (Page 5 — Experimental Setup).** GPT-4o was evaluated with a unified multi-task prompt producing all three benchmark outputs at once, while AffectGPT, Qwen2.5-VL, and MiniGPT4 were evaluated with separate single-task prompts. This inconsistency means that observed performance differences across models could be partially attributable to prompt design rather than model capability. The paper does not acknowledge this as a confound. **Fix:** (a) Acknowledge the prompt confound explicitly and discuss its potential impact; (b) Run a controlled sub-experiment with GPT-4o using single-task prompts on a subset; (c) Qualify cross-model comparison conclusions accordingly.

**W3. Small sample size limits statistical reliability of benchmarks (Page 7 — Emotion Classification Results).** The single-expression evaluation set contains only 140 clips across 11 classes, meaning some classes may have very few test samples (e.g., anger appears in ~25 clips binarized overall, with only a subset being single-expression). No confidence intervals, bootstrap estimates, or multi-seed variance are reported. Without uncertainty quantification, the per-class accuracy numbers — some of which differ by only a few percentage points across conditions — cannot be evaluated for statistical significance. **Fix:** (a) Report per-class sample sizes for the single-expression subset; (b) Add confidence intervals via bootstrap; (c) Report results over multiple evaluation runs; (d) Consider merging low-frequency categories or using macro-averaged metrics with explicit error bars.

**W4. VADER-based selection on English captions may misalign with visual emotional content (Page 3 — Dataset Collection).** The dataset curation pipeline selects utterances based on the sentiment of their English text captions, not the visual signing content. The paper acknowledges this mismatch in Section 6 but does not quantify its impact. The artificial bimodal distribution (only 5 neutral clips) means the dataset does not reflect natural ASL emotion distributions, which limits the ecological validity of both the dataset and the benchmarks. **Fix:** (a) Add a quantitative analysis comparing VADER scores vs. annotator sentiment scores for the same utterances; (b) Explicitly bound claims about "emotion in sign language" to "emotion in sign language utterances whose English translations carry strong sentiment."

**W5. Qualitative analysis of emotion cue descriptions lacks methodological rigor (Page 4 — Section 3.4, C2).** The open-ended cue descriptions constitute a core contribution (C2), but the analysis is presented as an informal summary without: (a) a described qualitative methodology (thematic analysis? grounded theory?); (b) frequency or inter-coder reliability statistics; (c) any verbatim annotator quotes. This reduces the scientific value of an otherwise valuable qualitative resource. **Fix:** (a) Describe the qualitative coding methodology; (b) Include representative verbatim quotes; (c) Report theme frequencies; (d) Provide the full coded dataset in an appendix.

### Minor Weaknesses

**W6. Speculative bias attribution without evidence (Page 6 — Sentiment Analysis Results).** The observed positive-emotion bias is speculatively attributed to RLHF-based "helpful, harmless, honest" training without any test of this hypothesis. Alternative explanations (training data distribution, task ambiguity, model architecture) are not discussed. **Fix:** Present the HHH hypothesis as one of several possible explanations, not the primary interpretation.

**W7. Broader-implications overclaiming in introduction (Page 1 — Introduction).** The introduction claims the results "suggest a need for specialized visual encoders" and "motivate architectures that prevent text from overwhelming visual information," but the paper does not test either of these architectural claims — it only diagnoses the problem. **Fix:** Move broader implications to a dedicated discussion section or qualify them explicitly as untested hypotheses.

**W8. Emotion taxonomy justification gap (Page 3 — Annotation Construction).** The set of 10 emotion categories includes non-standard splits (surprise split by valence) and mixes basic emotions with complex states (worry, frustration, excited) without explicit justification. The confidence scale (0-100) is used for tie-breaking but its psychometric properties are not examined. **Fix:** Add rationale for the category selection and split decisions; analyze the relationship between confidence scores and agreement.

**W9. Conclusion introduces unsupported downstream claim (Page 8 — Conclusion).** The sentence "We hope that emotion-aware models would further improve the accuracy of sign language translation models" suggests a downstream benefit that is not tested or demonstrated in the paper. **Fix:** Replace with a bounded statement about what the dataset directly enables.

**W10. Limitations section covers only VADER and ASLLRP constraints, omitting key limitations (Page 8 — Limitations).** Missing limitations: limited signer diversity (4 signers), low inter-annotator agreement for negative emotions, prompt confound in model comparison, and lack of multi-label evaluation. **Fix:** Expand the limitations section to cover all major methodological constraints.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** EmoSign addresses a genuine and important research gap with a thoughtfully designed annotation protocol and culturally aware methodology. The dataset itself and the qualitative emotion cue descriptions represent a meaningful contribution to sign language research. However, the benchmark evaluation has several methodological weaknesses that reduce confidence in the reported results: the cross-statistic comparison of inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's alpha vs. Fleiss' kappa) is not statistically valid; the prompt structure confound between GPT-4o and the other models undermines cross-model comparisons; the small sample size (140 single-expression clips across 11 classes) limits statistical reliability; and the VADER-based selection on English captions introduces an unquantified bias. The novelty of the dataset relative to existing resources (particularly FePh) could not be independently verified in this run (Retrieval-Disabled Mode). With revisions addressing the major weaknesses — particularly the agreement analysis, prompt confound documentation, confidence intervals, and expanded limitations — the paper's score could increase substantially.
