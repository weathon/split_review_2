## Summary

This paper introduces Neon (Negative Extrapolation from self-traiNing), a post-processing method that improves generative models by exploiting the degradation caused by self-training on synthetic data. The core idea is striking in its simplicity: fine-tune a base generative model briefly on its own synthetic outputs (which predictably degrades quality), then extrapolate *away* from the degraded checkpoint via a parameter merge $\theta_{\text{Neon}} = (1+w)\theta_r - w\theta_s$ with $w>0$. The authors provide theoretical analysis showing that mode-seeking inference samplers (e.g., low-temperature sampling, classifier-free guidance) create anti-alignment between synthetic-data gradients and real-data population gradients, which makes negative extrapolation effective. The method is evaluated across four architecture families (diffusion, flow matching, autoregressive, few-step) on ImageNet, CIFAR-10, and FFHQ, achieving substantial FID improvements with minimal additional compute (typically <1%). On ImageNet-256, Neon elevates the xAR-L model from FID 1.28 to 1.02, which the authors report as state-of-the-art. The paper is well-structured, the core idea is novel and counterintuitive, and the experimental breadth is commendable. However, several key weaknesses — including overclaimed SOTA/universality language, a factual error in a figure caption, missing statistical variance reporting, and theoretical assumptions that are not fully acknowledged in the narrative — should be addressed before publication.

## Strengths
**S1 — Novel and Counterintuitive Core Idea.** The central insight — that self-training degradation is not just noise but a structured, anti-aligned signal that can be reversed via negative extrapolation — is genuinely creative. The method is remarkably simple: a single post-hoc parameter merge with no auxiliary models, no inference modifications, and no new real data. This conceptual clarity is a significant strength.

**S2 — Strong Theoretical Grounding.** The paper does not merely present an empirical trick; it provides a mathematical framework (anti-alignment via Theorem 1, mode-seeking sampler analysis via Theorem 2) that explains *why* the method works. While the theory has limitations (see W3, W4), the effort to ground the intuition in rigorous analysis elevates the paper substantially above a purely empirical contribution.

**S3 — Broad and Systematic Empirical Evaluation.** Neon is tested across four distinct architecture families (EDM diffusion, flow matching, autoregressive VAR/xAR, few-step IMM) and three datasets (ImageNet, CIFAR-10, FFHQ). The experiments cover multiple model scales (xAR-B vs xAR-L, VAR-d16 vs d30) and inference configurations. The ablation studies are thorough: transferability across architectures, sensitivity to synthetic data quality, base model quality requirements, and the CIFAR-10C control experiment.

**S4 — Impressive Practical Efficiency.** Neon consistently achieves FID improvements with <1% additional training compute and as few as 1k synthetic samples. The ImageNet-256 result (xAR-L: 1.02 FID with 0.36% overhead) is particularly striking. This efficiency makes the method attractive for practitioners who already have a trained model and want a low-cost improvement.

**S5 — Honest Discussion of Limitations and Scope.** The paper explicitly discusses when interpolation (not extrapolation) is needed (diversity-seeking samplers, Section 3.1 "When interpolation helps"), the role of finite synthetic dataset size (U-shaped performance curve), and the condition that anti-alignment holds only for near-optimal models. This intellectual honesty strengthens the paper's credibility.

**S6 — Transferability Discovery.** The finding that Neon's degradation signal transfers across architectures (e.g., flow matching data improving EDM) is practically important and opens new use cases (e.g., improving a model using synthetic data from a more efficient or accessible architecture).

## Weaknesses
**W1 — Missing Statistical Variance and Significance (Major).** The paper reports FID improvements without any variance estimates, confidence intervals, or significance tests. This is a critical gap because: (a) FID is a stochastic metric sensitive to random seeds; (b) many reported improvements are modest (e.g., 0.02-0.26 FID) and could be within evaluation noise; (c) the "40% data reduction" claim (annotation 17) relies on a 0.02 FID difference that is not statistically validated. *Fix:* Report mean±std over ≥3 seeds for all key results; add significance tests against baselines. (Annotation 15)

**W2 — Factual Error in Figure 4 Caption (Major).** The caption states "$w = -1$ corresponds to the model directly trained on synthetic data, i.e., $\theta_{\text{Neon}} = \theta_r$." This is incorrect: plugging $w=-1$ into Eq. (2) gives $\theta_{\text{Neon}} = \theta_s$, not $\theta_r$. This error could confuse readers interpreting the x-axis of Figure 4 and the precision-recall analysis. *Fix:* Replace $\theta_r$ with $\theta_s$ for $w=-1$ and verify all other figure captions for similar errors. (Annotation 8)

**W3 — Theoretical Assumptions Not Fully Disclosed in Narrative (Major).** The paper's central claim — that Neon works because synthetic gradients are anti-aligned with population gradients — is presented as a proven fact in the abstract and introduction, but the proof (Theorem 1, Theorem 2) relies on several non-trivial conditions: (a) near-optimal base model (small $\|\varepsilon\|_{H_d}$), (b) mode-seeking sampler structure, (c) smoothness and local convexity (or directional smoothness) of the loss, (d) spectral bounds on the preconditioned Hessian, (e) for diffusion/flow models, a curvature-density coupling assumption (A-MONO). These conditions are not previewed in the high-level narrative, giving an impression of universality that the theory does not fully support. *Fix:* Qualify the anti-alignment claim in abstract/Introduction with explicit reference to the required conditions; note that these conditions commonly hold in practice but are not guaranteed. (Annotations 4, 5, 7)

**W4 — "Prove Rigorously" Overstates Theoretical Contribution (Major).** Contribution C2 claims to "prove rigorously" that mode-seeking samplers create anti-alignment. The proof provides an upper bound ($s \leq ...$) with $O(\|\varepsilon\|_{H_d}^3)$ terms and unquantified constants, making it a qualitative bound rather than a rigorous, checkable guarantee. Theorem 2 further depends on the A-MONO condition for diffusion/flow models, which is itself an assumption verified only indirectly. *Fix:* Replace "prove rigorously" with "provide theoretical evidence" or "demonstrate theoretically" in the contribution list. (Annotation 5)

**W5 — Overclaiming Universality and SOTA Status (Major).** Multiple claims overreach the evidence: (a) "works across all architectures" (Section 2) — only tested on 4 families; GANs, VAEs, normalizing flows, energy-based models are untested. (b) "state-of-the-art FID of 1.02" (Abstract, Section 4.2) — the comparison scope is unclear (UCGM's 1.06 is cited but Table A.1 is in the removed appendix; concurrent methods are not listed). (c) "record-breaking" (Figure 1 caption) — promotional language without evidence. *Fix:* Replace "all" with "multiple architectures including..."; qualify SOTA as "to our knowledge" or "among compared methods under our evaluation protocol"; remove "record-breaking." (Annotations 1, 6)

**W6 — "40% Data Reduction" Claim Not Statistically Supported (Major).** The claim that "Neon can compensate for a 40% reduction in real training data" (Section 4.4) is based on FID 1.87 (30k+Neon) vs. 1.85 (50k baseline) — a 0.02 FID difference. Without confidence intervals, this difference is indistinguishable from evaluation noise. Additionally, the data reduction is only from 50k to 30k (20k absolute); more extreme reductions (e.g., 10k, 5k) would provide stronger evidence. *Fix:* Add confidence intervals; test smaller subsets; rephrase claim with appropriate uncertainty. (Annotation 17)

**W7 — Causal Mechanism Inference Overreach (Minor).** The paper interprets precision/recall changes as evidence of "redistributing probability mass from over-represented to under-represented modes" (Section 4.1). However, precision/recall metrics (Kynkaanniemi et al.) measure manifold coverage via k-NN, not probability density. The mechanistic interpretation is consistent with the data but not directly measured. *Fix:* Use softer causal language ("consistent with," "suggests") and acknowledge that direct density measurement would be needed for stronger claims. (Annotation 11)

**W8 — Speculative Future Work in Conclusion (Minor).** The conclusion raises two research questions (diversity-promoting samplers, optimal "bad" datasets) that have no grounding in the paper's experiments. These appear as speculation rather than motivated next steps. *Fix:* Replace with concrete future directions based on identified limitations (e.g., testing GANs, principled $w$ selection, iterative Neon). (Annotation 10)

**W9 — Missing Comparison to Step-Distillation Methods (Minor).** The few-step experiments (Section 4.3) claim Neon "halves inference cost" but do not compare against existing step-distillation techniques (e.g., consistency distillation, progressive distillation) that also achieve few-step generation. *Fix:* Add comparison or acknowledge this limitation. (Annotation 16)

**W10 — Grammatical Error in Finite |S| Paragraph (Minor).** The sentence "Our analysis assumes that the population synthetic gradients $r_s(\theta_r)$" is grammatically incomplete, lacking a predicate. This is a minor polish issue but appears in a central theoretical section. *Fix:* Complete the sentence. (Annotation 9)

**W11 — Introduction Dichotomy Oversimplifies Prior Work (Minor).** The Introduction (P2) frames prior work as either "simplistic" (self-training fails) or "complicated" (workarounds with overhead), omitting middle-ground approaches (data filtering, conservative fine-tuning, mixed training). This binary framing may overstate Neon's differentiation. *Fix:* Add a more nuanced classification. (Annotation 3)

## Score
**Final Score: 7/10**

**Rationale:** This is a strong paper with a genuinely novel idea, broad empirical validation, and a serious theoretical effort. The core contribution — turning self-training degradation into a corrective signal via negative extrapolation — is significant and practically useful. However, the score is constrained by several major weaknesses that need correction before the paper can be considered fully rigorous: (1) the SOTA and universality claims overreach the evidence, (2) missing statistical variance reporting undermines confidence in the reported gains, (3) a factual error in a figure caption needs correction, (4) the theoretical narrative oversells the strength of the proof by not disclosing key assumptions in high-level summaries, and (5) key empirical claims (e.g., "compensates for 40% data reduction") lack statistical support. These issues are fixable with relatively modest revisions — adding error bars, qualifying claims, correcting the caption, and tightening the narrative — which is why the paper still scores above 6. The research value (new knowledge about harnessing self-training degradation) and potential to change practice (post-hoc model improvement without new data) are clear. Top-venue publication potential is realistic after addressing these weaknesses.

**Scoring breakdown:**
- Novelty & Research Value: 8/10 — genuinely novel idea, clear practical significance
- Theoretical Rigor: 6/10 — solid framework but overstated and assumption-dependent
- Empirical Validation: 7/10 — broad coverage but missing statistical reliability
- Reproducibility: 6/10 — missing details (seeds, variance, Table A.1 in appendix)
- Presentation & Clarity: 7/10 — generally well-written but has overclaims and one factual error
