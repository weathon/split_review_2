## Summary
This paper introduces Ambig-SWE, an underspecified variant of SWE-Bench Verified, to evaluate how LLM-based coding agents handle incomplete user instructions in software engineering tasks. The authors propose a three-step decomposition of underspecificity resolution—detection, clarification, and integration—and evaluate six proprietary and open-weight models across three settings (Full specification, Hidden/underspecified, and Interactive). The key finding is that interaction can substantially improve resolve rates (up to 89% relative recovery of full-specification performance for Claude Sonnet 4), but most models default to non-interactive behavior unless explicitly prompted and struggle to distinguish well-specified from underspecified instructions. The paper provides a diagnostic framework for targeted evaluation of interactive agent capabilities.

**Strengths in brief:** The Ambig-SWE benchmark fills a genuine gap by providing controlled, paired full/underspecified issue variants. The three-step decomposition (detection→clarification→integration) enables diagnostic evaluation rather than holistic scoring. The cross-model comparison across proprietary and open-weight families provides practical insight into current model limitations.

**Core limitations in brief:** (1) The validity of LLM-generated underspecified issues vs. natural human underspecification is unvalidated. (2) The user proxy (GPT-4o) likely overestimates real-world interaction benefits. (3) Unequal interaction budgets (100 vs. 30 turns) confound cross-model comparisons. (4) The detection accuracy metric conflates underspecification awareness with prompt-following behavior. (5) The question-quality analysis relies on a cosine distance metric that may primarily measure verbosity rather than information relevance.

## Strengths
**S1 — Timely and well-motivated problem.** The study addresses a practically important gap: how LLM agents should handle underspecified instructions in complex software engineering tasks. Prior work has focused on ambiguity in isolated conversational settings, while real-world agentic tasks involve multiple interdependent specification gaps that emerge dynamically. The motivation is clearly grounded in deployment realities where user prompts are often incomplete.

**S2 — Clean experimental framework with three settings.** The Full / Hidden / Interaction design allows for principled measurement of how underspecificity affects performance and how interaction can recover lost capability. The paired full/underspecified issue construction enables within-issue comparisons, reducing variance from task difficulty differences.

**S3 — Three-step diagnostic decomposition.** Breaking underspecificity resolution into detection (RQ2), clarification quality (RQ3), and interactive problem-solving (RQ1) is a thoughtful analytical choice. This decomposition enables targeted diagnosis of where different models fail, which is more actionable than a single holistic score.

**S4 — Broad model coverage across families and scales.** Evaluating both proprietary (Claude Sonnet 3.5/4, Haiku 3.5) and open-weight (Llama 3.1 70B, Deepseek-v2, Qwen 3 Coder 480B) models provides a useful landscape view of current capabilities. The inclusion of Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder as comparably-capable models allows for an interesting comparison of interaction strategies.

**S5 — Nuanced analysis of interaction modes.** The distinction between informational and navigational information requests (Section 3.3) and the exploration-first vs. ask-immediately strategic analysis (Section 5.3) provide practical insights for agent design. The finding that some models rigidly follow protocols even when user-provided information contradicts their workflow is a valuable behavioral observation.

**S6 — Honest limitation discussion.** The paper explicitly acknowledges several constraints: the user proxy may be more cooperative than real users, the detection metric relies on early-turn behavior, and the question quality metrics are approximations. This transparency strengthens the paper's credibility.

## Weaknesses
### W1 — Validity of synthetic underspecification (major)

The underspecified issues in Ambig-SWE are generated by GPT-4o, which creates a validity threat: the synthetic underspecification patterns may not match natural human underspecification. The authors conduct a distributional difference analysis showing that their generated issues are *more aggressively stripped* than natural ones, but this analysis is purely descriptive. There is no validation that agent behavior on synthetic issues generalizes to real-world underspecified reports. The paper explicitly avoids testing on naturally underspecified SWE-Bench examples because they lack paired ground truth—but this design choice means the core empirical findings are conditional on the specific GPT-4o underspecification template. Without human validation or a sensitivity analysis using a different generation method, readers cannot assess how the results might differ with more realistic underspecification patterns. (See annotation on Page 1 — Dataset: synthetic underspecification validity.)

### W2 — Optimistic user proxy bias (major)

The user proxy (GPT-4o) is likely more cooperative, more articulate, and more knowledge-complete than real-world users. The proxy responds helpfully within the full issue scope and says "I don't have that information" for missing details—a clean but unrealistic behavior pattern. Real users may be unavailable, impatient, forgetful, or provide incorrect information. The paper acknowledges this in the limitations but does not quantify the potential overestimation. If the proxy's cooperativity inflates interaction benefits by even a modest margin, the headline recovery rates (74% improvement, 89% relative recovery) represent upper bounds rather than realistic expectations. A sensitivity analysis with a less capable or less cooperative proxy would significantly strengthen the claims. (See annotation on Page 1 — User proxy section.)

### W3 — Unequal interaction budgets confound cross-model comparisons (major)

Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder receive up to 100 interaction turns, while other models are limited to 30 turns. The authors justify this as accounting for "greater reasoning and planning capacity," but this asymmetry means the more capable models also have >3x the opportunity to explore, ask questions, and refine their approach. The headline result—that Claude Sonnet 4 achieves the highest relative recovery (89%)—could partly reflect the extra budget rather than superior interaction ability. Without a matched-budget sensitivity analysis (all models at 30 turns), readers cannot determine whether the interaction improvement stems from better interaction strategies or simply longer trajectories. (See annotation on Page 1 — Experimental Setup.)

### W4 — Detection accuracy conflates underspecification awareness with prompt-following (major)

The RQ2 detection experiment measures accuracy based on whether models interact selectively with underspecified vs. full inputs, using three different prompt framings (Neutral, Moderate, Strong). However, accuracy varies dramatically with prompt framing (e.g., Claude Sonnet 3.5: 0.60 → 0.84 → 0.76), suggesting the metric captures compliance with interaction prompts more than genuine detection ability. A model that never interacts (Qwen 3 Coder) scores 0.50 (chance) simply because it never differentiates—not because it cannot detect. The conclusion that "LLMs struggle to detect missing information" conflates detection ability with prompted interaction behavior. A cleaner experiment would ask models to explicitly rate underspecification without any interaction framing. (See annotation on Page 1 — RQ2: Detection of Incomplete Task Specifications.)

### W5 — Cosine distance metric may measure verbosity rather than information relevance (major)

The RQ3 analysis uses cosine distance between embedding vectors before and after interaction to measure information gain. However, models that ask more verbose questions or engage in longer interactions will naturally produce larger embedding shifts, regardless of whether the additional information is task-relevant. Qwen 3 Coder achieves the highest cosine distance (0.179) with 50% more questions than Claude Sonnet 4, yet both achieve similar resolve rates. This supports the paper's "integration matters more" interpretation, but it also suggests the cosine distance metric confounds volume with relevance. An alternative metric—proportion of user responses containing directly actionable information (file paths, function names, error types)—would be more closely tied to task outcomes. (See annotation on Page 1 — RQ3: Question Quality.)

### W6 — Underpowered claim about model scale and interaction ability (moderate)

Section 3.2 claims "there does not seem to be a direct correlation between the number of parameters or coding ability and a model's ability to leverage interaction." This is based on only 6 models from different families, architectures, and training paradigms—not a controlled scale comparison. Without varying model size within the same architecture (e.g., Llama 3.1 8B vs 70B, Claude Haiku vs Sonnet within the same training run), the data cannot support a conclusion about the relationship between parameter count and interaction ability. The claim should be weakened to a qualitative observation warranting further study. (See annotation on Page 1 — Section 3.2.)

### W7 — Missing statistical rigor in key comparisons (moderate)

Several analytical claims lack statistical backing: (a) Qwen 3 Coder's performance "worsens" with navigational info (52.38% vs 55.43%, difference of 3 percentage points on ~93 issues) without confidence intervals or significance testing. (b) The "up to 74%" improvement figure in the abstract is not explicitly traceable to a specific model × condition combination. (c) The improvement magnitudes across models in Figure 3 are reported without variance estimates (though Wilcoxon tests are mentioned in Table 4, which is in Appendix). Adding standard errors or confidence intervals to the resolve rate comparisons would substantially improve reliability.

### W8 — Question specificity claim lacks causal evidence (minor)

The conclusion states "the most effective questions are specific, actionable, and task-level" but this claim is supported only by qualitative examples (Figure 4), not by a controlled comparison. Within models, question specificity is correlated with overall model capability, making it impossible to attribute success to question quality vs. general competence. A stratified analysis controlling for model identity is needed. (See annotation on Page 1 — Conclusion.)

### W9 — Limited novelty assessment (moderate, deferred)

Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode (external paper search unavailable for this run), the novelty of Ambig-SWE relative to existing interactive evaluation frameworks cannot be fully verified. The paper's positioning against prior work (Chen et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2024; Niwa & Iso, 2024) appears reasonable, but a comprehensive related-work assessment is deferred. The taxonomy of related work (Section 6) is descriptive rather than critically comparative—it describes what prior work does but does not systematically compare assumptions, protocols, and findings.

### W10 — Related work lacks critical comparison (moderate)

Section 6 (Related Work) is organized as a literature survey rather than a critical positioning. It describes prior work on ambiguity, clarification, and interactive systems but does not explicitly state how Ambig-SWE's evaluation framework differs from or improves upon existing benchmarks (e.g., the specific dimensions of underspecificity that prior benchmarks miss). The novelty claim—that prior work studies single missing details while Ambig-SWE studies "multiple, interdependent gaps"—would be stronger with concrete examples of prior benchmarks' limitations using the Ambig-SWE framework.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** This paper makes a useful practical contribution through the Ambig-SWE benchmark and the three-step decomposition framework for evaluating interactive agents under underspecification. The problem is well-motivated and the experimental scope (6 models, 500 issues, three settings) is reasonably comprehensive. However, the score is constrained by several validity concerns that collectively reduce confidence in the headline claims:

- The synthetic underspecification generation (W1), optimistic user proxy (W2), and unequal interaction budgets (W3) introduce systematic biases that may overstate the measured interaction benefits.
- The detection accuracy metric (W4) conflates underspecification awareness with prompt-following, undermining a core analytical contribution.
- The question quality analysis (W5) uses a metric that likely confounds verbosity with relevance.
- Novelty assessment is deferred due to unavailable external retrieval, but the paper's positioning against prior work appears reasonable.

These issues are addressable through additional validation experiments (matched-budget sensitivity, explicit detection task, human validation of synthetic issues, alternative information metrics). The core benchmarking contribution and the diagnostic decomposition are solid and should persist after these fixes. The paper would benefit from revising its stronger claims ("no correlation with model size," "specific questions are most effective") to match the available evidence strength.
