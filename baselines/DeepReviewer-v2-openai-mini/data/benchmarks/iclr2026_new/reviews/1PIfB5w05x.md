## Summary
This paper studies the sparse signal recovery problem in a novel heterogeneous-noise setting where observations come from two quality sources: a small number of high-quality (low-variance) measurements and a larger number of low-quality (high-variance) measurements. The authors establish sufficient sample-size conditions for both information-theoretic and algorithmic (LASSO-based) recovery under two decoder settings—agnostic (noise variances unknown) and informed (per-sample variances known).

**Information-theoretic contribution:** The paper derives sufficient conditions of the form α₁n₁ + α₂n₂ > n* for support recovery in both settings. The key conceptual contribution is the *Price of Quality* γ, defined as the number of low-quality samples needed to replace one high-quality sample while preserving the sufficient condition. In the agnostic setting, γ is uniformly bounded (never exceeds 2), meaning one high-quality sample is worth at most two low-quality samples. In the informed setting, γ can grow arbitrarily large, revealing the value of variance information.

**Algorithmic contribution:** For the agnostic setting, Theorem 3 extends Wainwright's LASSO phase-transition result to heterogeneous noise, showing that the algorithmic threshold depends only on the total sample size n = n₁ + n₂ and the average noise level σ²_avg, not on individual noise variances. This demonstrates that high- and low-quality data contribute equally to LASSO recovery—a striking contrast with the information-theoretic threshold.

The paper is theoretical in nature, with proofs deferred to appendices. The results are relevant to high-dimensional statistics, compressed sensing, and data-quality-aware inference. Novelty verification is deferred due to external literature retrieval being unavailable in this run.

## Strengths
**1. Timely and well-motivated problem formulation.** The paper formalizes a practically relevant extension of sparse recovery—heterogeneous noise from mixed-quality data sources—that addresses real-world scenarios such as combining expert-labeled and LLM-labeled data, multi-site clinical trials, and citizen science campaigns. The distinction between agnostic and informed decoder settings captures two fundamentally different operational regimes, and the motivation is clearly articulated with concrete examples.

**2. Clean conceptual framework (Price of Quality).** The introduction of γ (Price of Quality) as the exchange rate between high- and low-quality samples is elegant and interpretable. This concept transcends the specific theorem statements and provides a generalizable way to think about data quality trade-offs in high-dimensional inference. The asymptotic analysis across different SNR regimes (high, low, mixed) is thorough and yields intuitive conclusions—e.g., that in the high-SNR regime, quality differences become irrelevant.

**3. Non-trivial theoretical extension of LASSO analysis.** Extending Wainwright's (2009) LASSO phase-transition result to the heterogeneous-noise setting is a genuine technical contribution. The proof overcomes a significant obstacle—the loss of the Wishart structure X_S^T X_S due to the non-scalar noise covariance Σ—by employing a QR decomposition and Haar measure arguments. This demonstrates careful and creative technical work.

**4. Interesting contrast between information-theoretic and algorithmic thresholds.** The finding that the information-theoretic threshold is sensitive to quality differences (via different γ values in agnostic vs. informed settings) while the algorithmic (LASSO) threshold is completely insensitive (depending only on total n and average noise) is scientifically valuable and somewhat surprising. This "robustness" of computational recovery to data heterogeneity is a meaningful insight.

**5. Transparent about limitations.** The authors explicitly state that Theorem 1's sufficient condition is not information-theoretically sharp and acknowledge the looseness from Chernoff-bound relaxation. They also candidly discuss omitted analyses (informed LASSO, correlated designs) as future work rather than over-claiming. This scientific honesty strengthens credibility.

## Weaknesses
**1. All information-theoretic results are sufficient, not necessary—limiting the strength of claims.** Theorem 1 (agnostic setting) provides a sufficient condition that the authors themselves acknowledge is not sharp, due to relaxation of the Chernoff exponent. While this is disclosed, it fundamentally limits what the paper can claim about the information-theoretic threshold. The "Price of Quality" γ defined in (12) is only guaranteed to characterize the trade-off *for this sufficient condition*, not for the actual information-theoretic limit. The paper would be stronger if it either (a) provided a matching necessary condition, (b) bounded the gap between sufficient and necessary thresholds, or (c) more explicitly scoped the claims to avoid implying that γ captures the fundamental sample-complexity trade-off (see Page 1 - Abstract: "it is sufficient for (n₁, n₂) to satisfy a linear trade-off defining the Price of Quality").

**2. Price of Quality expression contains a likely typographical error.** The denominator of the first term in Eq. (9) shows $\frac{\delta(2\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_1^2)s}{2\sigma_2^2}$ inside the log, but Eq. (12) (the derived γ expression) uses $\frac{\delta(2\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_1^2)s}{2\sigma_1^4}$—the denominator changes from $\sigma_2^2$ to $\sigma_1^4$ without justification in the proof sketch. This inconsistency needs careful verification. If the $\sigma_1^4$ denominator in (12) is correct, then the sufficient condition (9) appears inconsistent with its own derived γ. If (9) is correct, then (12) is wrong. This mathematical inconsistency undermines trust in the derivations and should be addressed (Page 5 - Interpretation paragraph, Eq. 12 vs Eq. 9).

**3. No experimental validation.** The paper is purely theoretical with no simulations or experiments to validate the claimed thresholds. While theoretical papers need not have experiments, the absence of even simple Monte Carlo verification of the phase transitions (e.g., plotting recovery probability vs. (n₁, n₂) for a fixed (p, s, σ₁², σ₂²) configuration) makes it impossible to assess whether the sufficient conditions are tight or overly conservative. This is especially critical given the known looseness of Theorem 1. The authors could easily run numerical experiments for moderate p to demonstrate the qualitative behavior of γ across SNR regimes.

**4. Unexamined assumption: n₂ > n₁ is never used.** The paper assumes n₂ > n₁ (low-quality samples are more numerous) but this inequality does not appear in any theorem condition or proof. This creates a gap between the motivating narrative (where low-quality data is abundant) and the mathematical results (which hold for any n₁, n₂). Either the assumption should be removed if unnecessary, or the authors should explain where it is needed. If unused, the paper should clarify which results would hold without this ordering.

**5. Limited discussion of practical implications for the agnostic setting's small γ.** The key finding—that under the sufficient condition, one high-quality sample is worth at most two low-quality samples in the agnostic setting—is interesting but under-analyzed. The paper does not discuss whether this bound is fundamentally due to the decoder's ignorance (which would motivate reweighting procedures) or is a genuine limitation of the sufficient-condition analysis (since the bound may be looser than the true information-theoretic trade-off). The authors mention alternative reweighting estimators in Remark 3.2 but do not analyze whether such approaches could improve γ—yet this is precisely the practical question the paper's framing motivates.

**6. LASSO results require n₁, n₂ = ω(s), which may conflict with the mixed-quality narrative.** Theorem 3 requires both n₁ = ω(s) and n₂ = ω(s). But the motivating scenario has "a small collection of high-quality measurements"—if n₁ is small (e.g., constant or logarithmic), this condition fails. The paper should clarify which results apply when n₁ is not asymptotically larger than s, or provide separate analysis for the small-n₁ regime.

**7. The informed LASSO is deferred without sufficient justification.** Remark 4.2 states that extending Theorem 3 to the informed setting is nontrivial because $X_S^T \Sigma^{-2} X_S$ loses the Wishart structure. However, the paper's main conceptual claim (Section 1.3, contribution 2) is about the LASSO in the agnostic setting. The omission is consistent with the paper's scope, but it leaves a natural question unresolved: would an informed LASSO also exhibit robustness to quality differences, or would it yield a different γ? The discussion would benefit from at least a heuristic argument about expected behavior.

**8. Writing and presentation issues.** The paper would benefit from:
- Abstract is a single dense paragraph; breaking it into 4-5 sentences with clear S1-S5 roles would improve readability.
- The transition between §1.1.1 (sparse recovery) and §1.1.2 (mixed-quality data) is abrupt—a bridging paragraph connecting the two literatures would help.
- The notation $\mathbb{B}_{p,s}$ appears in Eq. (15) but was defined as $\mathcal{B}_{p,s}$ earlier; this minor inconsistency should be fixed.
- The SNR definition (3) expresses $\mathbb{E}\|X\beta^*\|_2^2$ in the numerator, but Eq. (7) uses $\mathbb{E}\|X\beta\|_2^2$ without specifying which β—this needs alignment.
- Several sentences are quite long and could be split for clarity.

**9. Novelty verification is deferred.** Due to external literature retrieval being unavailable in this run, the claim "this paper is the first to" (Page 4 - Section 1.3) cannot be independently verified against the existing literature. This is especially important for claims about extending LASSO to heterogeneous noise, as heteroscedastic sparse regression has been studied in econometrics and statistics literature. A thorough literature comparison is needed to establish the precise boundary of novelty.

**10. The "three SNR regimes" classification (Page 4 - Section 2) may be incomplete.** The paper identifies High SNR, Low SNR₂/High SNR₁, and Low SNR regimes, but does not discuss the Low SNR₁/High SNR₂ regime (σ₁² = ω(s), σ₂² = o(s)). While this is excluded by the assumption σ₂² > σ₁², the relative ordering of SNR₁ and SNR₂ could still produce additional regimes not covered. More importantly, the analysis assumes σ₁² and σ₂² can scale arbitrarily with s and p, but the most practically relevant regime—where both noise levels are constant (σ₁², σ₂² = Θ(1)) and s grows—falls between the defined regimes and is not explicitly analyzed.

## Score
Final Score: 6/10

**Rationale.** The paper tackles a well-motivated and timely problem—sparse recovery with mixed-quality data—and introduces an elegant conceptual framework (Price of Quality) to analyze the trade-off between high- and low-quality samples. The technical extension of LASSO threshold analysis to heterogeneous noise is non-trivial and represents genuine scholarly contribution. However, the score is moderated by the following factors:

- **Insufficient claim strength:** The information-theoretic results are only sufficient, not necessary, so the core conceptual quantity γ characterizes only the sufficient condition, not the fundamental sample-complexity trade-off. This limits the paper's ambition relative to its framing.
- **Mathematical concern:** A likely typographical inconsistency between Eq. (9) and Eq. (12) (denominator changes from σ₂² to σ₁⁴ without explanation) requires correction before the derivations can be fully trusted.
- **Absence of validation:** No simulations or experiments support the theoretical predictions, leaving the practical tightness of the bounds unverified—especially problematic for the acknowledged loose bound in Theorem 1.
- **Novelty not independently verifiable:** Without external literature retrieval available in this run, the "first to" claims cannot be confirmed against related work in heteroscedastic sparse regression, robust statistics, and weak supervision.
- **Several framing gaps:** The n₂ > n₁ assumption is not used; the LASSO analysis requires n₁ = ω(s) which may not hold in the motivating small-n₁ scenario; key SNR regimes of practical interest are omitted.

The paper presents solid theoretical work with a clear conceptual contribution, but the combination of unverified derivations, missing necessary conditions, and lack of empirical validation places it at a moderate score. With corrections to the mathematical inconsistency, addition of numerical experiments, and tighter scoping of claims, this work could reach a higher impact level.
