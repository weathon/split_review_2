## Summary
This paper investigates the phenomenon of *benign relearning* in LLM unlearning — where models recover forgotten information after fine-tuning on seemingly benign data. The authors challenge the prevailing explanation that topical relevance drives this phenomenon. Through controlled experiments on the TOFU benchmark and re-analysis of the BLUR benchmark, they demonstrate that **syntactic similarity** (surface-level structural overlap) is a more consistent driver of benign relearning than topical relevance. They show that syntactically similar data lies closer to the target set in representation and gradient space, and that unlearning disproportionately suppresses template tokens while leaving keyword tokens under-suppressed. Motivated by this analysis, they propose **syntactic diversification**: paraphrasing forget queries into diverse syntactic structures before unlearning, which reduces benign relearning and improves certain utility metrics.

The paper's core strengths are a clear causal hypothesis (syntactic similarity > topical relevance), a controlled experimental design that isolates the two factors, and a practical mitigation strategy. Key weaknesses include: (1) all experimental results lack variance reporting and statistical significance testing, making effect sizes unverifiable; (2) the claimed utility-efficiency trade-off alleviation is only partially supported, with mixed results on World Facts metrics; (3) the syntactic similarity measure (Levenshtein distance) is a surface-level proxy, not a true syntactic metric; (4) the GPT-4o-based diversification procedure raises reproducibility concerns; and (5) the template-keyword loss ratio analysis requires clearer methodological specification. Novelty and external literature comparisons cannot be assessed in this run due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode, and should be verified manually.

## Strengths
**S1. Clear causal hypothesis with controlled experimental design.** The paper formulates a well-defined research question — whether syntactic similarity or topical relevance is the primary driver of benign relearning — and designs a clean experiment that isolates these two factors. In the TOFU benchmark, the authors construct topically relevant sets (same entities, different syntax) and syntactically similar sets (different entities, same syntax) that are mutually exclusive by design. This controlled comparison is a methodological strength that allows clear attribution of effects.

**S2. Important identification of confounds in prior work.** Section 4 provides a valuable re-analysis of the BLUR benchmark, identifying two previously overlooked confounds: (1) different dataset sizes leading to unequal gradient update counts, and (2) non-monotonic recovery trajectories making endpoint-only evaluation misleading. The proposed fix (standardizing step budget, evaluating at every step, reporting the maximum) is methodologically sound and changes the interpretation of BLUR's results.

**S3. Mechanistic analysis beyond surface correlation.** The paper does not stop at showing that syntactic similarity correlates with relearning. Section 6 provides two complementary mechanistic analyses: representation and gradient alignment (showing that syntactically similar sets lie closer in representation space), and the template-vs-keyword loss ratio (showing that unlearning disproportionately targets template tokens). This dual analysis strengthens the causal interpretation.

**S4. Practical and simple mitigation strategy.** Syntactic diversification is a conceptually straightforward and easy-to-implement intervention: paraphrasing forget queries before unlearning. Despite the simplicity, it shows meaningful reductions in benign relearning across multiple unlearning methods (GA, NPO, SCRUB). The approach is grounded in the paper's mechanistic analysis, making it an example of theory-driven method design.

**S5. Comprehensive evaluation across multiple unlearning methods.** The paper evaluates the main finding across three distinct unlearning methods (GA, NPO, SCRUB) and their variants (GA+KL, NPO+KL). This provides evidence that the syntactic similarity effect is not specific to one unlearning algorithm, increasing the generality of the findings.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Missing statistical reliability for all quantitative results (Major, Severity: highest risk).** No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests are reported for any experimental result — Table 2, Figure 2 (bar charts without error bars), Figure 4 (heatmaps), and Figure 8 (line plots) all lack variance information. This is the single most important weakness because it undermines the verifiability of the paper's central claims. For instance, in Table 2, World Facts Probability is 0.4187 (baseline) vs 0.4169 (proposed method) — the baseline is actually higher on this metric, yet this is not discussed. Without variance estimates, the reader cannot determine whether the claimed utility improvements are statistically meaningful or within noise range. *Fix: Report mean±std over ≥3 random seeds for all main results; include significance tests for key comparisons (e.g., paired t-test for D_forget vs D'_forget in Table 2).*

**W2. Overclaimed trade-off alleviation (Major, Severity: high).** The conclusion states that syntactic diversification "substantially alleviates the trade-off between unlearning efficacy and model utility," but the evidence in Table 2 is mixed. On World Facts, the proposed method underperforms the baseline on Probability (0.4169 vs 0.4187) and Truth Ratio (0.5568 vs 0.5627). On the Retain set, while ROUGE improves (0.4052 vs 0.1036), the absolute value of 0.4052 is still quite low. The claim of "substantially alleviating" the trade-off is not proportionally supported by these numbers. *Fix: Qualify the claim to acknowledge mixed results, and report effect sizes with confidence intervals to clarify the practical significance.*

**W3. Insufficient specification of the template-keyword token classification (Major, Severity: medium).** The loss ratio analysis in Section 6 is a key mechanistic contribution, but the paper does not specify how template vs. keyword tokens are identified. The token boundary is fuzzy (e.g., is "fictitious" in "The full name of the fictitious author" a template token or part of the descriptive context?). Without a precise classification protocol, the analysis cannot be reproduced or assessed for potential circularity. Additionally, the loss ratio $ \mathcal{L}_{\text{template}} / \mathcal{L}_{\text{keyword}} $ can diverge as $ \mathcal{L}_{\text{keyword}} \to 0 $, and it is unclear whether the observed increase in Figure 6 is driven by rising $ \mathcal{L}_{\text{template}} $ or falling $ \mathcal{L}_{\text{keyword}} $. *Fix: Provide explicit token classification rules (stop-word lists, matching patterns), report absolute NLL values for both token types separately, and consider using a difference-based metric as a complementary measure.*

**W4. Levenshtein similarity is a surface-level proxy, not a true syntactic metric (Moderate, Severity: medium).** The syntactic similarity measure based on normalized character-level Levenshtein distance captures surface-form overlap rather than genuine syntactic structure (parse trees, dependency relations). Two strings with identical syntax but different vocabulary (e.g., "The cat sat" vs "A dog ran") could have lower Levenshtein similarity than two strings with different syntax but high character overlap (e.g., "born in" vs "born on"). The authors acknowledge this in Appendix I, but the main text presents the metric as measuring "syntactic similarity" without sufficient caveats. *Fix: Add an explicit limitation sentence in Section 5.1, and report correlation with parse-tree-based or template-mining similarity measures from Appendix I in the main text.*

**W5. GPT-4o-based diversification raises reproducibility concerns (Moderate, Severity: medium).** The core mitigation strategy relies on GPT-4o, a proprietary model with changing API behavior and cost barriers. The main text does not specify the prompt, temperature, number of paraphrases generated per query, filtering acceptance rate, or similarity threshold for retaining paraphrases. Without these details, the method cannot be reliably reproduced or compared against. *Fix: Provide the exact prompt in the appendix, report acceptance rates, and validate with an open-source alternative (e.g., Llama-3-8B-Instruct).*

**W6. Limited benchmark and task diversity (Moderate, Severity: medium).** The main experimental analysis is conducted almost entirely on the TOFU dataset, which consists of synthetic QA pairs with highly regular, template-based structures. While TOFU is a standard unlearning benchmark, its synthetic nature and rigid formatting may not reflect the diversity of real-world text. The BLUR re-analysis (Section 4) covers WMDP, WHP, and RWKU but uses only the topical-relevance tier partition rather than the controlled syntactic-vs-topic comparison that is the paper's main contribution. *Fix: Add at least one additional benchmark with natural language (non-template) data (e.g., WMDP or RWKU with constructed syntactically similar sets) to demonstrate the effect beyond synthetic templates.*

**W7. Speculative broader implications without main-text support (Minor, Severity: low).** Section 8 raises three important points (LoRA vulnerability, safety training limitations, deployment risks), but two of them lack main-text quantitative evidence. The LoRA vulnerability claim is especially interesting but is relegated to a brief mention with a reference to Appendix B.3.1. *Fix: Move at least one supporting figure for the LoRA claim to the main text, or provide a concrete numerical anchor (e.g., "LoRA achieved X% recovery within Y steps vs Z% for full-parameter").*

**W8. Introduction narrative could be more efficient (Minor, Severity: low).** The first three introduction paragraphs describe the same phenomenon (benign relearning exists and is fragile) at different levels of abstraction, without establishing a clear research question until the fourth paragraph. This delays narrative momentum. *Fix: Merge paragraphs 2-3 into a single paragraph that transitions from examples to the paper's core question: what drives benign relearning?*

## Score
**Final Score: 6.5/10**

**Rationale for score:** The paper makes a meaningful contribution by identifying syntactic similarity as a key driver of benign relearning, challenging the prevailing topical-relevance explanation. The controlled experimental design, confound analysis of BLUR, and mechanistic template-keyword analysis are strong methodological contributions. The proposed syntactic diversification strategy is practical and grounded in the analysis.

However, the score is constrained by the following factors (in decreasing order of impact):

1. **Missing statistical rigor (W1):** No variance or significance testing is reported for any result, making the quantitative claims unverifiable. This is a critical gap that must be addressed before the paper can be accepted.
2. **Overclaimed trade-off alleviation (W2):** The central claim about utility preservation is only partially supported by the evidence, with mixed results on World Facts metrics.
3. **Reproducibility concerns (W5):** The core mitigation depends on a proprietary model (GPT-4o) without sufficient specification.
4. **Limited benchmark diversity (W6):** The main finding is demonstrated primarily on synthetic template-based data (TOFU), and the generality to natural language is not established.
5. **Novelty assessment deferred** due to external literature verification being unavailable in this run. Manual verification of novelty against prior work (especially BLUR-related methods, representation-scrubbing approaches, and data-augmentation for unlearning) is needed before a final novelty judgment can be made.

The paper has clear strengths (S1-S5) and with the recommended revisions — particularly adding statistical rigor, qualifying the trade-off claim, and improving reproducibility documentation — it could become a solid contribution to the LLM unlearning literature. The core insight (syntactic similarity as a driver) is novel and important; the main execution gaps are in evidence presentation and completeness rather than in the underlying research question.
