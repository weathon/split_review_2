## Summary

This paper investigates whether LLM-generated reference lists can be distinguished from human-authored ones by analyzing their induced citation graphs. The authors construct paired citation graphs from ground truth and GPT-4o-generated references for 10,000 focal papers (~275k references) from SciSciNet, alongside field-matched random baselines. Using a progressive modeling strategy — from structural graph features (centrality, clustering) through semantic embeddings (OpenAI text-embedding-3-large, SPECTER2) to Graph Neural Networks — they systematically decompose the discriminative signal.

The core finding is clear and well-supported: structure-only models (Random Forest on graph properties, GNNs with structural features) perform near chance (accuracy ~0.60-0.61) when distinguishing LLM from human citation graphs, while content-aware models using title/abstract embeddings achieve high accuracy (RF ~0.83, GNNs ~0.93). Robustness checks with Claude Sonnet 4.5, alternative embedding models, and cross-generator generalization strengthen the central claim that LLM-generated bibliographies mimic human citation topology but carry a detectable semantic fingerprint.

The study tackles an important and timely problem, the experimental design is rigorous (paired graphs, multiple random baselines, systematic model comparison), and the results have practical implications for automated auditing of LLM-generated scholarly content. However, several methodological limitations and open questions remain: the reliance on sum-pooling of embeddings discards distributional information, the undirected graph conversion may discard directionality-based signals, and the generalizability to retrieval-augmented generation or future LLMs with better semantic calibration is not established. Novelty and literature-positioning conclusions require external literature verification (deferred due to retrieval-disabled mode in this run).

## Strengths
1. **Timely and important problem.** The paper addresses a pressing question in scholarly communication: as LLMs are increasingly used to curate bibliographies and draft literature reviews, can their reference lists be reliably distinguished from human-created ones? This has direct implications for automated auditing tools, citation recommendation systems, and the integrity of scientific workflows.

2. **Well-designed experimental framework.** The paired citation graph design (ground truth vs. LLM-generated for the same focal papers) with multiple random baselines (field-level, subfield-level, temporally constrained) provides a rigorous foundation for comparing graph characteristics. The field-matched random baseline is particularly thoughtful, as it controls for topical confounds that could otherwise explain structural differences.

3. **Progressive and decomposable methodology.** The three-stage strategy (structural RF → embedding RF → GNNs) effectively decomposes the discriminative signal into topological and semantic components. The consistent finding that structure is insufficient while content is highly informative is clearly demonstrated through multiple model families and feature types.

4. **Comprehensive robustness analysis.** The paper extends beyond a single LLM (GPT-4o) to include Claude Sonnet 4.5, tests two embedding backbones (OpenAI and SPECTER2), validates cross-generator generalization, conducts ablation with i.i.d. noise features, and reports results across multiple random seeds and hyperparameter sweeps. This thoroughness substantially increases confidence in the core findings.

5. **Transparent and reproducible reporting.** The experimental setup is described in sufficient detail (data source, filtering criteria, graph construction, random baseline generation, model architectures, hyperparameter search grids) to enable replication. The use of multiple random seeds and distributional reporting (rather than cherry-picking best single runs) reflects good scientific practice.

6. **Clear practical implications.** The paper's main actionable finding — that detection pipelines should prioritize content signals over structural features — is stated directly and supported by evidence. The methodological protocol (paired graphs, domain-matched randomization, stepwise analysis) is presented as a general recipe that could be adopted by other researchers.

## Weaknesses
### Major Weaknesses

**W1. Undirected graph conversion discards directionality signal without justification (Page 3 - Constructing Citation Networks, paragraph 6).** The paper replaces all directed citation edges with undirected ones, arguing this avoids "directionality artifacts or trivial in/out-degree differences." However, citation directionality encodes meaningful information (who cites whom, temporal knowledge flow) that could differ between LLM and human graphs. Since prior work (including the authors' own) shows LLMs differ in self-citation rates and recency preferences — both directionality-dependent phenomena — discarding this information may mask a discriminative signal. An ablation preserving directed variants (PageRank, HITS, directed clustering) should be performed to validate that directionality is indeed non-informative.

**W2. Sum-pooling of embeddings discards distributional information (Page 6 - Section 5, paragraph 1).** The graph-level embedding representation is computed by summing reference embedding vectors, which loses information about variance, skew, and higher-order moments of the embedding distribution. Two reference lists with different semantic structures could produce identical sums. Since the paper's central claim about "semantic fingerprints" rests on this embedding analysis, it should also report per-reference distributional statistics (e.g., mean pairwise cosine similarity, spectral properties of the embedding covariance matrix) to identify which aspects of the embedding distribution carry the discriminative signal.

**W3. Fuzzy-matching thresholds for verifying LLM references are not specified (Page 2 - Section 3, paragraph 1).** The existence of LLM-generated references in SciSciNet is determined through fuzzy matching with "conservative similarity thresholds" that are never defined. This is a critical reproducibility issue: different thresholds would produce different generated graphs (different numbers of matched nodes), which could affect all downstream classification results. The exact thresholds, their calibration process, and a sensitivity analysis showing how results vary with threshold choice should be provided.

**W4. Discussion overclaims generalizability without analyzing failure modes (Page 9 - Discussion).** The paper states that "detection pipelines built on text embeddings or text+graph hybrids are therefore the right tool; structure-only diagnostics will under-detect" as a universal prescription. However, the experiments only cover GPT-4o and Claude Sonnet 4.5 under parametric-knowledge-only settings. The 30% accuracy drop in cross-generator experiments (~0.72 vs. ~0.93) suggests that the semantic fingerprint is partially generator-specific. The Discussion does not address: (a) whether results generalize to retrieval-augmented LLMs, (b) whether adversarial prompting could evade semantic detection, or (c) what boundary conditions exist for the claimed detectability.

**W5. Cross-generator generalization results are under-analyzed (Page 8 - Section 6 Results).** The paper reports that training on GPT-4o and testing on Claude Sonnet 4.5 yields ~0.72 RF accuracy, but provides no analysis of why performance drops ~20% relative to within-generator results. Understanding which aspects of the semantic fingerprint are generator-specific vs. universal is essential for deploying detection systems in practice, yet the paper does not characterize the failure modes or the dimensions along which the two LLMs' citation embeddings differ.

### Minor Weaknesses

**W6. Graph-level edge count used as a per-node feature in GNNs (Page 7 - Experimental setup).** Including the graph's total number of edges as a node-level feature is questionable: it is a graph-level constant that adds no node-level discriminative signal. While this does not affect the paper's conclusions (since structural GNNs already perform at chance), it is a methodological detail that should be corrected in revision.

**W7. Introduction lacks explicit contribution enumeration (Page 1 - Introduction).** The paper would benefit from a clearly stated list of contributions at the end of the introduction. Currently, the contribution is implied but never enumerated, making it harder for readers to quickly grasp the paper's specific advances over prior work.

**W8. Related Work reads as a citation list rather than a structured comparison (Page 1-2 - Related Work).** The related work section catalogs studies without grouping them by thematic axes or explicitly differentiating this paper's approach from the strongest prior work. A reorganized section organized around comparison dimensions (bibliometric analysis, citation network analysis, recommendation bias) with explicit "gap vs. our approach" statements would substantially improve readability and positioning.

**W9. Semantic fingerprint concept is introduced without operational definition (Page 1 - Introduction).** The term "semantic fingerprint" is central to the paper's narrative (appears in title, abstract, and discussion) but is never formally defined. An operational definition — e.g., "systematic differences in the distribution of textual embeddings of suggested references" — would strengthen the paper's conceptual clarity.

**W10. Field assignment heuristic for random baseline may introduce bias (Page 2-3 - Section 3).** Using "the field with the highest normalized citation count" for multi-field papers may systematically assign papers to more popular fields rather than their primary research area. A sensitivity analysis comparing alternative field assignment strategies would increase confidence in the random baseline validity.

## Score
**Final Score: 7/10**

This score reflects the paper's strong experimental design, timely research question, and clear core findings, tempered by several methodological limitations (undirected graph conversion without justification, sum-pooling discarding distributional information, unspecified fuzzy-matching thresholds), overclaiming in the Discussion without adequate boundary analysis, and the inability to fully assess novelty due to retrieval-disabled mode in this run. The paper addresses an important problem with thorough robustness checks, and the main result — that LLM citation graphs are structurally indistinguishable but semantically detectable — is convincingly demonstrated. However, the methodological concerns and limited analysis of failure modes prevent a higher score. Revision addressing W1-W5 would substantially strengthen the paper.
