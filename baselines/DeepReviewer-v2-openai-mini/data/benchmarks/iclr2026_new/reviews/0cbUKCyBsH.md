# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper introduces Influence-Aware Time Series Forecasting (IATSF), a paradigm that incorporates external textual signals—termed "influences"—into time series forecasting to address the performance plateau observed in purely historical-data-driven models. The authors make three primary contributions: (C1) a control-theoretic analysis showing that ignoring external influences imposes an irreducible error floor (Proposition 2.1); (C2) the IATSF paradigm that reframes forecasting as dynamic system modeling with explicit influence conditioning; and (C3) a practical instantiation consisting of the Temporal-Synced IATSF benchmark (with leak-free textual influences) and the FIATS model (featuring Channel-Aware Adaptive Sensitivity Modeling (CASM) and Channel-Aware Parameter Sharing (CAPS)). Experiments across synthetic (FM Toy, Electricity Utility), physics-based (Atmospheric Physics, NYC Traffic Speed), and market (GAUD) datasets show that FIATS consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines including transformer models, foundation models, and LLM-based multimodal approaches. The paper is clearly motivated, well-structured, and addresses an important limitation of current forecasting practice—the neglect of external qualitative information. However, several aspects require significant strengthening: (1) the theoretical contributions, while valid, are relatively standard variance decomposition results framed with overstated novelty; (2) several experimental claims are not precisely supported by the reported numbers; (3) key architectural details in the CASM block contain symbolic inconsistencies; and (4) the paper's promotional language often exceeds what the evidence supports. With substantial revision of claims, numerical verification, and architectural clarifications, the work has potential to make a meaningful contribution to the multimodal forecasting literature.

## Strengths
**S1. Well-motivated problem framing.** The paper correctly identifies a genuine limitation of standard time series forecasting—the neglect of external qualitative information that drives real-world systems. The "self-stimulation" concept, while not technically novel (it is essentially the open-loop vs. closed-loop distinction in system identification), is a useful rhetorical framing that helps readers understand the limitation intuitively. The connection to everyday examples (weather affecting traffic, news affecting markets) makes the motivation accessible.

**S2. Clean theoretical foundation for influence modeling.** Propositions 2.1 and 3.1 provide a formal error-bound analysis that connects unobserved influences to irreducible prediction variance. While the mathematics is essentially a covariance decomposition (law of total variance in system form), its application to motivate textual influence modeling in TSF is a reasonable conceptual contribution. The partial influence efficacy result (Proposition 3.1) provides a useful justification for incorporating any measurable signal, even incomplete.

**S3. Comprehensive experimental validation.** The paper evaluates across a diverse set of tasks: fully controlled synthetic systems, physics-based real-world data, and human-driven market dynamics. The FM Toy experiment is particularly effective as a ground-truth validation of the theoretical claims, showing that when influences are perfectly known, the error bound can be approached. The ablation studies (Table 3) cleanly separate the contributions of influence inputs and channel descriptions.

**S4. Practical operationalization.** The LLM-free FIATS model is a pragmatic choice that avoids the computational overhead and reproducibility concerns of generative LLMs. The CASM and CAPS mechanisms are architecturally plausible designs that directly reflect the theoretical motivation (channel-specific sensitivity to influences). The benchmark contribution has potential value for standardizing evaluation in this emerging area.

**S5. Thorough ablation and robustness analysis.** The ablation studies target the key architectural choices (influence input removal, channel description removal, embedding model variation) and provide clear evidence that both the influence signal and the channel-conditioning mechanism contribute to performance. The noise sensitivity analysis (Fig. 6) directly validates Proposition 3.1's prediction that influence quality affects error reduction.

## Weaknesses
**W1. Overclaimed theoretical novelty.** The paper presents the control-theoretic analysis as a major theoretical breakthrough ("formally prove that this assumption imposes a hard, mathematical barrier"), but the mathematical content is substantially a standard result from estimation theory—when an input variable (influence) is unobserved, MSE-optimal prediction converges to the conditional expectation, and the irreducible error is given by the variance of the conditional expectation function. Proposition 2.1 is essentially a first-order delta-method approximation around the mean influence. This is a useful application framing but not a novel theoretical result. The paper should clearly scope the theoretical contribution as "application of known estimation bounds to motivate influence-aware TSF" rather than claiming new theoretical discovery. The proof is also deferred entirely to Appendix B (unavailable), making the "formal proof" claim unverifiable from the main text. *Reference: Annotation ID a13cc898 (Page 1 - Introduction para 2), Annotation ID f8c5a863 (Page 2 - Proposition 2.1)*

**W2. Numerical discrepancies in reported results.** The paper claims "average MSE reduction of 36.0% on Atmospheric Physics and 44.3% on NYC Traffic Speed compared to PatchTST." My back-of-envelope calculation using the reported Table 1 values gives approximately 32.9% for Atmospheric Physics and 48.7% for NYC Traffic Speed—both deviating from the claimed numbers. The source of this discrepancy (whether the paper uses horizon-weighted averaging, per-channel averaging from Table 2, or some other formula) is not disclosed. Additionally, the paper claims FIATS "approaches the theoretical lower bound" on FM Toy but never numerically computes the bound from Proposition 2.1 to compare against the achieved MSE of 0.003. Without this direct comparison, the claim that the bound is being approached is subjective. *Reference: Annotation ID d95c7229 (Page 6 - RQ2 Statistical Results), Annotation ID a84cc91c (Page 6 - RQ1 Statistical Results)*

**W3. Overselling of causal attribution.** The paper repeatedly asserts that the performance bottleneck in TSF is "the flawed self-stimulation assumption, not model scale" (Introduction, RQ1, Summary of Findings). However, the experiments only demonstrate that *adding influence information helps*—they do not prove that the self-stimulation assumption is *the* primary bottleneck. Alternative explanations for the performance plateau (insufficient data, poor inductive biases, evaluation saturation, benchmark limitations) are not controlled for or discussed. The FM Toy experiment is the most direct test, but it is a fully synthetic system where influences perfectly determine the signal—this proves that influence information *can* be decisive in ideal settings, not that it is the limiting factor in real-world scenarios. *Reference: Annotation ID a13cc898 (Page 1 - Introduction para 1), Annotation ID a84cc91c (Page 6 - RQ1 Statistical Results)*

**W4. Method description lacks clarity and contains symbolic inconsistencies.** The CASM block description has several issues that affect reproducibility: (a) the "channel descriptions" input is never defined—are these hand-crafted text descriptions, learned embeddings, or one-hot identifiers? (b) the attention computation formula includes an unusual Argmax operation ($\hat{c} = \text{Argmax}_i(kW_K)_i$) that is not standard attention and may be a PDF extraction artifact; (c) the tensor contraction paths between Query ($\mathbb{R}^{CN \times D}$), Key ($\mathbb{R}^{M \times D}$) and Value are not explicitly stated. The CAPS description includes the concerning phrase "We will omit the analysis" regarding the causal attention mask. These ambiguities make the method difficult to reproduce from the main text alone. *Reference: Annotation ID 8204ecdd (Page 5 - CASM description), Annotation ID 6725b275 (Page 5 - CAPS description)*

**W5. Core assumption of instantaneous influence effects is unjustified.** The benchmark design assumes "influences take effect instantaneously" (Section 4.1), which is justified only by a single example of photovoltaic panels. Many real-world influence-effect relationships have significant temporal delays (policy changes affecting markets, weather fronts affecting locations hours later, marketing campaigns affecting user acquisition over days). The paper does not discuss the conditions under which this assumption is valid, the error introduced when it is violated, or potential mitigation strategies (lagged influence embeddings). This severely limits the practical applicability of the proposed benchmark. *Reference: Annotation ID 84c1f29d (Page 4 - Leak-Free Dataset Design)*

**W6. Inadequate statistical rigor.** The paper reports only point estimates (MSE) without variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests. Given that the improvements on several datasets are modest (e.g., Electricity Utility: FIATS 0.124 vs PatchTST 0.130 at horizon 96—a 4.6% difference), readers cannot assess whether the gains are statistically significant or within noise. The GAUD results ("ranking first on 59.6% of games") with 3 competitors is only modestly above random chance (33%) and lacks any measure of uncertainty. Multi-seed runs and statistical tests are standard practice in TSF and should be included. *Reference: Annotation ID 4cc3fa85 (Page 7 - GAUD results)*

**W7. Ablation studies missing critical control.** The "Zero News" ablation removes both the influence content and the entire influence encoder path, confounding information loss with capacity reduction. A cleaner control would replace influence embeddings with random vectors of the same dimension while keeping the model architecture intact, directly testing whether the model extracts meaningful signal from text or simply uses added parameters. The "Zero Desc." ablation is cleaner in this regard but still leaves the question partially open. *Reference: Annotation ID d2878857 (Page 8 - Ablation Studies)*

**W8. Promotional and imprecise language throughout.** The paper uses numerous phrases that overstate the findings: "primary path forward for meaningful progress" (Abstract), "fail spectacularly" (RQ1), "decisively validate" (Summary of Findings), "critical resource for the community" (Abstract). These expressions create an expectation of transformative impact that the evidence does not fully support. The "first LLM-free numerical-based forecaster for this task" claim (Section 5) is unverifiable without literature retrieval (deferred due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode) and would require a more systematic comparison with non-LLM multimodal approaches. *Reference: Annotation ID 0a988c53 (Page 1 - Abstract), Annotation ID 60a999ff (Page 1 - FIATS introduction)*

**W9. Conclusion introduces unsupported claims.** The conclusion mentions "complex chaotic systems" and "pretraining techniques" as future directions, neither of which is discussed in the paper's experiments or theoretical framework. The suggestion that pretraining might help contradicts the paper's own central claim that model scale/complexity is not the solution. This inconsistency weakens the paper's message. *Reference: Annotation ID 145b37c3 (Page 8 - Conclusion)*

**W10. Broader impact analysis is dismissive.** The broader impact statement dismisses potential negative consequences without substantive discussion. Given the paper's application domains (market forecasting, traffic prediction, weather-dependent systems), there are clear ethical considerations around reliance on textual influence signals, data privacy when using third-party embedding APIs, and potential misuse of forecasting systems for business decisions. These should be acknowledged and addressed. *Reference: Annotation ID 061dbcaf (Page 9 - Broader Impact)*

**W11. Novelty assessment deferred.** Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode (external paper search unavailable), novelty claims (C1: control-theoretic analysis, C2: IATSF paradigm, C3: benchmark + FIATS) cannot be independently verified against related literature. The paper's relationship to existing work on (a) exogenous variable modeling, (b) multimodal TSF, and (c) text-informed forecasting requires manual literature verification before acceptance decisions can be made. This is especially important for the "first LLM-free" claim and for positioning against concurrent work.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a genuinely important limitation in time series forecasting and provides a coherent framework, benchmark, and model to operationalize influence-aware forecasting. The experiments are comprehensive and the ablation studies are informative. However, the work is significantly weakened by: (a) overclaimed theoretical novelty—the core propositions are standard estimation bounds framed as new discoveries; (b) numerical inconsistencies in reported performance gains that undermine trust in the quantitative claims; (c) insufficient statistical rigor (no variance or significance reporting); (d) promotional language that exceeds what the evidence supports; and (e) architectural ambiguities that hinder reproducibility. The novelty assessment is also deferred due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode, which introduces uncertainty around positioning claims, particularly the "first LLM-free" statement. With major revision—including claim scope reduction, numerical verification, statistical rigor addition, architectural clarification, and toned-down language—the score could potentially reach 6-7/10. The core idea has merit, but the current execution has too many unresolved weaknesses to be accepted in its present form.
