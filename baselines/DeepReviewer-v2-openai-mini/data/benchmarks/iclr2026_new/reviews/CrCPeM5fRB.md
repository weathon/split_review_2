## Summary
This paper introduces the Signal Dice Similarity Coefficient (SDSC), a structure-aware reconstruction metric for time-series self-supervised learning. SDSC extends the Dice coefficient from image segmentation to continuous signals by measuring normalized signed-area overlap between ground-truth and reconstructed waveforms. It captures local polarity agreement and magnitude overlap, is bounded in [0,1], and is made differentiable via a sigmoid Heaviside approximation for use as a training loss. A hybrid loss combining SDSC with MSE is also proposed. The paper integrates SDSC into the reconstruction branch of SimMTM while keeping its contrastive loss fixed, isolating the effect of the reconstruction objective. Experiments on forecasting and classification benchmarks show that SDSC-based pre-training achieves downstream performance comparable to or marginally better than MSE, with the clearest gains in frozen-encoder in-domain classification (up to +0.93 accuracy points). However, the improvements are modest overall (forecasting MSE differences <0.002 on average), and SDSC underperforms MSE in cross-domain settings. The paper provides a useful critique of MSE's limitations and proposes a principled structural similarity metric, but the empirical evidence for SDSC's superiority over MSE is mixed and task-dependent.

## Strengths
1. **Principled metric design with clear motivation.** SDSC extends the well-established Dice coefficient to continuous time-series, providing a normalized [0,1] measure that captures both sign agreement and local magnitude overlap. The paper thoroughly illustrates MSE's failure modes (phase inversion insensitivity, amplitude bias, polarity blindness) with concrete toy examples (Table 1, Figure 1), establishing a clear motivation for structure-aware reconstruction.

2. **Clean experimental isolation.** By replacing only the reconstruction loss in SimMTM while keeping the contrastive loss (InfoNCE) fixed, the paper isolates the effect of the reconstruction objective from architectural and contrastive learning confounds. This controlled design is a methodological strength that makes the ablation clean and interpretable.

3. **Comprehensive comparison against multiple loss families.** The experiments compare SDSC against MSE, SoftDTW, PCC, and SI-SNR across both forecasting and classification tasks, providing a broad empirical landscape of how different reconstruction objectives affect downstream performance.

4. **Honest reporting of mixed results.** The paper acknowledges that improvements are moderate, that MSE remains competitive, and that task properties (amplitude-dominant vs. shape-dominant) affect which loss is preferable. This nuance is commendable and avoids overhyping the proposed method.

5. **Practical hybrid formulation.** The hybrid loss combining SDSC with MSE is a practical solution that balances structural and amplitude objectives, consistently performing at or near the best results across settings.

## Weaknesses
1. **Statistical reliability is not established (Must fix).** All experiments use a single fixed random seed and report point estimates without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests. The observed improvements are small (e.g., forecasting MSE differences of 0.001-0.002), making it impossible to determine whether these differences are systematic or within noise range. Additionally, evaluation uses "best epoch" selection rather than validation-based early stopping, which risks over-optimism. **Fix:** Run all main experiments with at least 3 random seeds and report mean±std. Include paired significance tests for key comparisons (SDSC vs MSE in freeze classification). Replace best-epoch selection with early stopping.

2. **Causal claims about MSE's limitations are premature.** The introduction asserts that "MSE-based models achieve competitive results not due to accurate semantic preservation but due to incidental alignment with signal structure" — a causal claim that is not supported by any evidence in the paper. The comparable downstream performance between MSE and SDSC could equally be explained by the contrastive loss dominating representation quality, with reconstruction loss playing a minor role. **Fix:** Move this interpretive causal claim to the Conclusion, add a controlled experiment where the contrastive loss is removed to verify whether reconstruction loss alone drives the observed patterns, and use evidence-consistent phrasing ("is consistent with" rather than "not due to").

3. **Practical improvements are marginal.** The forecasting results show a 0.001-0.002 MSE improvement (0.3% relative) on average, and SDSC alone matches rather than improves upon MSE. The classification freeze results show a meaningful +0.93 accuracy point gain in-domain, but this advantage disappears after fine-tuning. The paper's claim that SDSC is "more efficient" is ambiguous — it has the same O(n) complexity as MSE, not better. **Fix:** Explicitly quantify effect sizes and practical significance thresholds. Replace "more efficient" with a specific statement about O(n) complexity. Add a "no pre-training" baseline to establish whether pre-training itself matters.

4. **SDSC definition has mathematical gaps.** Several aspects of the SDSC formulation need clarification: (a) The discrete approximation in Eq. (5) omits the sampling interval Δt, which can cause inconsistent values across datasets with different sampling rates. (b) The denominator in Eq. (4) sums absolute areas (linear scale), while MSE uses squared error (quadratic scale), making direct scale comparisons between the two loss terms in the hybrid loss potentially imbalanced. (c) The case of both signals being identically zero is not handled (division by zero even with ε, since numerator is also zero). (d) The uncertainty weighting from Kendall et al. (2018) was designed for multi-task learning with different tasks, not for two metrics on the same task — its applicability here needs justification. **Fix:** Add Δt to Eq. (5), specify a special case for zero signals, discuss the linear vs quadratic scale mismatch, and provide a theoretical justification for the uncertainty weighting adaptation.

5. **Cross-domain and amplitude-sensitive task limitations are understated.** SDSC underperforms MSE in cross-domain classification (Table 5: 61.64 vs 62.19) and on amplitude-dependent tasks (epilepsy). These negative results are mentioned in the classification text but are absent from the abstract and conclusion, creating an overly favorable impression. **Fix:** Add a limitations paragraph to the abstract or conclusion that explicitly states: "SDSC is not universally superior; for amplitude-sensitive tasks and cross-domain settings, MSE remains competitive or better."

6. **Related work is organized as a literature list rather than a structured comparison.** Section 2.2 lists 11 methods in a single paragraph without grouping by comparison axes relevant to SDSC (structural vs distance-based, contrastive vs reconstruction emphasis). The positioning claim that SDSC fills a "crucial gap" is asserted rather than analytically justified. **Fix:** Reorganize into method families with explicit comparison axes as recommended in the annotation for Section 2.2.

7. **Novelty verification is deferred (Retrieval-Disabled Mode).** Due to runtime retrieval constraints, external literature verification could not be performed. The novelty claims (SDSC as a novel extension of DSC to time-series) are plausible but unverified against prior work that may have proposed similar overlap-based metrics for time-series. This is noted as a deferred verification item.

8. **Best-epoch evaluation inflates results.** Using the best-performing epoch on the test set (rather than validation-based early stopping) is not standard practice and can overestimate performance. The paper should clarify whether "best epoch" refers to validation or test set, and if test-set selection was used, the results may be optimistically biased.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Score Breakdown:**

- **Research Value & Contribution (5/10):** The paper provides a useful critique of MSE's structural insensitivity and proposes a principled alternative. However, the empirical gains are marginal (forecasting: <0.3% improvement; classification freeze: +0.93 accuracy points in-domain). The core finding—that structure-aware reconstruction can match MSE-based pre-training—is valuable but represents an incremental insight rather than a transformative one. The controlled experimental design is a strength, but the absence of a "no pre-training" baseline limits the ability to assess the contribution of pre-training itself.

- **Novelty (6/10):** Extending DSC from segmentation to time-series via signed area overlap is a novel and well-motivated connection. The differentiable Heaviside approximation and hybrid loss formulation are sensible engineering contributions. However, novelty verification is deferred due to runtime retrieval constraints (Retrieval-Disabled Mode). The core idea of structure-aware time-series metrics is not new (PCC, SI-SNR, SoftDTW all target related goals), and the paper's specific contribution—signed overlap via Dice—needs external verification against prior work.

- **Validity & Soundness (5/10):** The methodology is sound in design but significantly weakened by the lack of statistical reliability measures. Single-seed experiments with point estimates and best-epoch evaluation prevent assessment of whether reported improvements are systematic or within noise. The causal claim about MSE's "incidental alignment" is not supported by evidence. Formula-level issues (missing Δt, zero-signal case, linear vs quadratic scale mismatch) should be addressed.

- **Reproducibility (7/10):** The paper provides detailed experimental setup (SimMTM backbone, fixed seeds, 2× NVIDIA 3090 GPUs, hyperparameters in appendix) that enables reproduction in principle. However, single-seed results without variance measures mean that reproducibility across different random initializations cannot be assumed.
