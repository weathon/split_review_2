# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper addresses **language confusion** in multilingual LLMs—the unintended mixing of languages during text generation—by proposing the **Language Confusion Gate (LCG)**, a lightweight 2-layer MLP that dynamically masks inappropriate tokens at decoding time without modifying the base model. LCG is trained via norm-adjusted self-distillation: it uses the model's own debiased top-k/p predictions (with token embedding norm removed) as pseudo-labels for language-family prediction (CJ, Latin, Symbols, Low-Res). The gate intervenes sparsely (0.33–0.38% of tokens) and only when the candidate token set contains disallowed language families.

The paper makes three contributions: (1) the LCG mechanism itself, (2) the norm-adjusted self-distillation training method, and (3) curated training/evaluation datasets (to be open-sourced) plus evaluation across Qwen3, Llama3.1, Gemma3, and GPT-OSS in both standard and reasoning modes. Results show confusion reduction by an order of magnitude (e.g., CJ confusion 4.5%→0.1% on Qwen3-8B) without degrading task performance (BLEU, accuracy, Pass@K). A two-part evaluation on the FLORES-WITH-LATIN subset indicates LCG preserves 86.7% of legitimate code-switching opportunities.

The paper is well-motivated, the method is clean and practical, and the core idea—using norm-adjusted self-distillation to train a lightweight gate—is interesting. However, several issues weaken the present form: (1) no variance/statistical significance reported across any metric, (2) the ORPO baseline comparison lacks training hyperparameter transparency, (3) the code-switch preservation evaluation is missing key annotator reliability details, (4) the token classification heuristic may systematically misclassify low-resource script tokens, and (5) the thinking-model evaluation is limited to a single code-generation task. Novelty assessment is deferred because external paper search was unavailable in this run; claims should be manually verified against prior work on logit-based intervention and language-family gating.

## Strengths
**S1 — Well-motivated and clearly framed problem.** Language confusion in multilingual LLMs is a practically important problem that affects user trust and deployment reliability. The paper establishes the problem convincingly with quantitative evidence from commercial systems (Table 2) and connects it to the broader challenge of reasoning-language trade-offs in Large Reasoning Models. The motivation that existing methods either require retraining or cannot distinguish confusion from code-switching is well-articulated.

**S2 — Clean, practical method design.** LCG is remarkably simple: a 2-layer MLP that takes the LLM's hidden state and predicts one of four language families. The norm-adjusted self-distillation training strategy is elegant—it leverages the model's own knowledge while removing a known bias (embedding norm imbalance). The sparse intervention rate (0.33–0.38%) and low overhead (0.4% latency increase) make the method practically deployable in production systems.

**S3 — Comprehensive model coverage.** The evaluation spans multiple architectures (Qwen3 dense/MoE, Llama3.1, Gemma3, GPT-OSS) and both standard and reasoning modes. This breadth strengthens the claim that LCG is model-agnostic and not accidentally tuned to a particular architecture.

**S4 — Thoughtful code-switch preservation analysis.** The two-experiment design (human-validated examples + corpus-level comparison against ground-truth and Claude Sonnet 4) is a principled approach to measuring the critical trade-off between suppressing confusion and maintaining legitimate multilingual behavior. The 86.7% token-level preservation rate is a strong empirical result.

**S5 — Practical efficiency reporting.** The paper includes concrete latency benchmarks (15.95→15.99ms per step, +0.4%) from a production deployment, which is rare and valuable for practitioners evaluating the method's deployability.

## Weaknesses
**W1 — Missing statistical significance and variance reporting (major).** All experimental results (Tables 3, 4, 5) are reported as point estimates without standard deviations, confidence intervals, or multi-seed runs. Confusion rates after LCG intervention are very small (often <0.5%), where a single generation run's results can be highly sensitive to random variation. The BLEU score changes (e.g., Qwen3-8B: 13.2→12.1, a 0.0 change for LCG-adjusted; Llama3.1-8B: 11.3→12.3) are reported as precise values with no error bars, making it impossible to judge whether observed improvements or degradations are within measurement noise. The claim "without negatively impacting task performance" cannot be rigorously evaluated without statistical testing. **Must action:** Report mean ± std over ≥3 random seeds for all metrics and add paired significance tests (e.g., bootstrap or Wilcoxon) comparing No LCG vs. LCG-adjusted.

**W2 — ORPO baseline comparison lacks training transparency (major).** The comparison against ORPO is central to demonstrating LCG's advantage over training-based methods. However, the ORPO implementation details are insufficient: hyperparameters (beta, learning rate), training dataset size and composition, number of training steps, and whether LoRA or full fine-tuning was used are all omitted. The paper states a "multilingual dataset" was prepared "similar as Lee et al. (2025)" but doesn't say how it compares to LCG's 78K training set. Without this information, the observed accuracy degradation (61.4→57.3 on Qwen3-8B INCLUDE) could reflect a suboptimal ORPO configuration rather than a fundamental limitation of ORPO for this task. **Must action:** Provide full ORPO training configuration, dataset statistics, and check whether accuracy degradation is statistically significant.

**W3 — Token classification fallback may systematically harm low-resource language handling (major).** The prioritized heuristic classifies undetermined BPE tokens (incomplete Unicode sequences) as "Symbols," which are never masked during inference (Rule 1). For low-resource languages using non-CJK, non-Latin scripts (Arabic, Hebrew, Thai, Devanagari, etc.), partial BPE byte sequences are common. If these tokens are misclassified as Symbols, they bypass the gate entirely, silently reducing protection for the languages that need it most. The paper reports 10,355 Symbol tokens (6.8% of vocabulary) but does not analyze what fraction of these are actually partial low-resource script tokens. **Must action:** Add an analysis of misclassification rates for each language family, particularly low-resource scripts, and report how this affects confusion rates per language rather than aggregated across all languages.

**W4 — Thinking model evaluation is narrow and does not assess reasoning quality (major).** The claim that LCG works for "thinking models" (Contribution 3) is supported only by Humaneval-XL (code generation in Arabic/Hebrew). This tests CJ confusion in code outputs, not confusion during the reasoning process itself. Key missing evidence: (a) no evaluation on multilingual reasoning benchmarks (e.g., MGSM in non-Latin scripts), (b) no analysis of what language families appear in the reasoning trace (chain-of-thought tokens), (c) no measure of reasoning quality beyond Pass@K (e.g., does LCG make reasoning more English-centric or truncate multilingual reasoning?). **Must action:** At minimum, add a multilingual reasoning benchmark evaluation and analyze the language distribution of thinking tokens. The length analysis should include confidence intervals.

**W5 — Code-switch preservation evaluation lacks annotator reliability (major).** Experiment 1 (86.7% preservation) relies on human annotators judging "natural, appropriate code-switch" in model outputs. The paper provides no inter-annotator agreement metric (Cohen's kappa), no annotator background details, and no annotation guideline summary. The number of judged examples is also omitted. The experiment design introduces selection bias by starting from the unmodified model's outputs. **Must action:** Report number of examples, inter-annotator agreement, annotator qualifications, and ideally add a direct evaluation where human annotators judge LCG-adjusted vs. No-LCG outputs side-by-side on a random sample.

**W6 — Training-evaluation data overlap not analyzed (major).** FLORES+ is used both for generating training data (translation pairs) and for evaluation (FLORES-NO-LATIN and FLORES-WITH-LATIN subsets). The paper does not confirm that training and evaluation sets are disjoint at the sentence level. If overlap exists, the gate may appear more effective than it actually is due to memorization rather than generalization. **Must action:** Add an explicit statement that training and evaluation FLORES+ subsets are disjoint, or report any overlap fraction.

**W7 — Related Work is organized as a sequential list rather than a thematic comparison (minor).** The section reads as a paper-by-paper summary (Marchisio → Nie → Ji → Li → Lee) rather than a structured comparison along shared axes (retraining requirement, code-switch handling, scalability, overhead). A thematic organization with a comparison table would better highlight LCG's positioning. See annotation on Page 2.

**W8 — Norm-adjusted training may have a train-inference gap (minor).** The gate is trained on norm-adjusted logits but applied to unadjusted logits during inference (since the gate predicts language families from the hidden state, not from the logits directly—though the training signal comes from norm-adjusted logits). The paper does not discuss whether this gap affects gate accuracy. See annotation on Page 3.

**W9 — Missing introductory narrative clarity (minor).** The introduction delays the problem statement to the third sentence and contains grammatical issues ("an Hebrew," "seem to reintroduce," "several language"). The three-observation paragraph presents the embedding norm finding as a definitive causal claim in the intro, while later acknowledging it cannot explain all confusion types. See annotations on Pages 1-2.

**W10 — Novelty cannot be fully assessed in this run (deferred).** External paper search was unavailable; thus, novelty claims regarding the LCG mechanism, norm-adjusted self-distillation, and comparison to prior gating or logit-masking approaches could not be independently verified. The authors should manually verify that no prior work (e.g., Li et al. 2025's gating mechanism, other logit-adjustment methods) covers the same core idea under comparable settings.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper addresses a practically important problem with a clean, efficient, and well-motivated method. The core contributions (LCG, norm-adjusted self-distillation, open-source data) are solid engineering contributions with clear practical value. The evaluations span multiple model families and demonstrate consistent confusion reduction.

However, the score is constrained by several validity concerns that prevent a higher rating in the current form:
- The absence of statistical significance testing and variance reporting (W1) undermines confidence in all quantitative claims. Given that confusion rates are very small after intervention, single-run point estimates are insufficient to rule out random variation.
- The ORPO baseline comparison (W2) lacks training transparency, making the claimed advantage over training-based methods unverifiable.
- The code-switch preservation evaluation (W5) and thinking-model evaluation (W4) both need stronger methodology to support the paper's key differentiating claims.
- The token classification heuristic may systematically misclassify low-resource tokens (W3), which could silently undermine the gate's effectiveness for the languages that need it most.
- Novelty verification is deferred due to external search unavailability; the authors should manually confirm no prior work covers the same gating mechanism under comparable settings.

These concerns are addressable through additional experiments (multi-seed runs, significance tests, ORPO configuration details, annotator agreement metrics) and clarifications (training-evaluation overlap, misclassification analysis). The method itself is promising, and the core engineering contributions are likely to be reproducible and practically useful. With the required revisions, the paper could merit a higher score.

**Score Scale Rubric:** 6/10 = Marginally above acceptance threshold. The paper has a solid core contribution and mostly sound methodology, but several validity concerns need to be resolved before the claims can be fully trusted. The paper would benefit from a focused revision addressing statistical rigor, baseline transparency, and evaluation completeness.
