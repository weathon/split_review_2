## Summary

This paper proposes Dimension Domain Co-Decomposition (3D), a PINNs-based framework that combines dimension decomposition with MoE-driven domain decomposition for solving high-dimensional PDEs. The method introduces three main contributions: (C1) a unified architecture integrating per-dimension factorization with adaptive domain decomposition, (C2) a shared-MLP design that processes coordinate-index pairs to reduce model size, and (C3) Variable Interpretability (VI), a subspace-alignment metric that quantifies how well learned per-dimension components match ground-truth separable factors. Experiments on Poisson, Wave, Viscous Burgers, and Linear Transport equations demonstrate parameter efficiency gains and competitive accuracy, with visualizations showing automatic domain decomposition around solution features such as shock fronts. The paper presents promising ideas at the intersection of separable PDE solvers and mixture-of-experts, but several issues in baseline fairness, evidence completeness, and metric interpretation need to be addressed before the contributions can be fully evaluated.

## Strengths
1. **Unified decomposition framework.** The core idea of jointly optimizing dimension decomposition and domain decomposition within a single PINNs-based framework is technically sound and addresses a genuine gap in the literature. Prior work treats these two decomposition strategies separately; integrating them via an MoE router is a thoughtful architectural contribution.

2. **Shared-MLP parameter efficiency.** The shared-MLP design (processing coordinate-index pairs) demonstrably reduces the parameter count compared to independent per-dimension MLPs, with the advantage scaling with input dimension. For 10d Poisson, the parameter reduction from 53,280 to 5,392 parameters is substantial, making high-dimensional PDE solving more accessible.

3. **VI metric novelty.** The proposed Variable Interpretability metric provides a principled, scale-invariant way to assess whether learned per-dimension components align with ground-truth factors. The use of subspace alignment (via QR decomposition and singular values of $Q_F^\top Q_G$) is technically elegant and avoids the pitfalls of simple correlation-based measures.

4. **Automatic domain decomposition.** The MoE router's ability to learn soft domain partitions without predefined subdomains or explicit interface conditions is convincingly demonstrated on the Burgers equation, where the shock at $x=0$ is automatically identified as a splitting boundary. This removes a key practical burden of traditional domain decomposition methods.

5. **Interpretability analysis.** The component-wise convergence analysis for the Wave equation (Figure 3) provides intuitive insight into how the model learns different frequency components over training steps, and the VI results across varying rank $r$ show a clear monotonic improvement trend that supports the metric's usefulness.

## Weaknesses
1. **Unfair baseline comparison (capacity mismatch).** The main accuracy comparison pits a 2-layer shared MLP (5,392 parameters) against a 10-layer vanilla PINNs (~1.8M parameters) with 300× more parameters. The shared MLP's faster convergence and lower error could partly reflect optimization differences between shallow and deep networks rather than the claimed superiority of separable architectures. A capacity-matched baseline (PINNs with comparable parameter count and layer depth) is essential for a fair evaluation. *Severity: Major.*

2. **RTX 5090 hardware reporting error.** The experiments report training on a "NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU," which has not been released as of the time of this review. This appears to be a typographical or factual error (likely RTX 4090), but it undermines reproducibility since hardware specifications cannot be verified. *Severity: Major.*

3. **SPINNs comparison truncated mid-sentence.** The methodological discussion at the end of Section 3.1 cuts off mid-sentence: "While SPINNs rely on forward-mode automatic differentiation (AD), this is not directly compatible with MoE because the router breaks the..." The reasoning is never completed, leaving readers unable to assess whether the claimed incompatibility is genuine or can be circumvented. *Severity: Major.*

4. **Dense MoE justification lacks evidence.** The claim that "dense MoE avoids expert collapse and provides more stable training" is stated without citation or supporting experiment. Moreover, the Burgers K=3 result directly contradicts this: the third expert "tends to receive small weights," which is effectively expert collapse. The paper does not analyze whether sparse MoE with load-balancing loss could achieve similar or better results with lower computational cost. *Severity: Major.*

5. **VI metric normalization is non-standard and unexplained.** Equation (5) normalizes by the unnormalized Euclidean norm (making each column a unit vector) rather than the standard deviation, but the paper does not explain this design choice. The geometric interpretation of VI (Section 3.2) could be clarified: when $s < r$, the average squared singular value $\frac{1}{m}\sum\sigma_i^2$ does not purely measure subspace containment, because rotation within the larger subspace can produce singular values less than 1 even when the exact subspace is fully contained. *Severity: Major.*

6. **K=1 baseline failure not analyzed.** For Burgers, the single-expert case (K=1) achieves only 0.2108 ± 0.1252 ℓ2 error — very high with large variance. The paper does not investigate whether this failure is due to insufficient capacity (width 32 being too small for the full domain) or optimizer instability. Without a matched-capacity single-expert control, the improvement at K=2 (0.0011) cannot be cleanly attributed to domain decomposition versus increased total parameters. *Severity: Major.*

7. **Memory comparison lacks context.** The claimed "77.8% memory" advantage does not specify whether it refers to static parameter storage, activation memory, or total GPU allocation during training. For PINNs, activation memory for the automatic differentiation graph often dominates total memory usage. Without this clarification, the practical significance of the memory savings is unclear. *Severity: Major.*

8. **Consistency evaluation is purely qualitative.** The seed-consistency analysis across 5 random seeds relies entirely on visual inspection of gating-weight heatmaps reported in the appendix. No quantitative overlap metric (e.g., region IoU, shock-location variance, or ℓ2 error variance across seeds for the MoE models) is provided. This weakens the claim that domain decomposition is "driven by intrinsic geometric features." *Severity: Major.*

9. **Conclusion lacks quantitative summary and sufficient limitations.** The conclusion states "good accuracy" without citing any numerical results. It acknowledges only one limitation (VI requires separable reference solutions), omitting important constraints: the dimension decomposition assumes separability, the MoE's soft partition may fail with hard discontinuities, and computational cost scales linearly with K. *Severity: Moderate.*

10. **Minor issues.** (a) Abstract typo: "$VT$" should be "$VI$". (b) Contribution bullet points read as a feature list without explaining the scientific value of each component. (c) The fine-tuning (dimension expansion) claim is fully deferred to the appendix with no main-text summary. (d) The related-work paragraph on APINNs oversimplifies by claiming "all existing approaches require predefined partitions," which neglects that APINNs already uses learned soft gating. (e) No quantitative spectral bias analysis is provided for the Wave equation convergence study. *Severity: Minor.*

**External literature verification:** External paper search was unavailable during this run (API token missing). All novelty and comparison conclusions are manuscript-grounded and should be complemented with manual literature verification by the authors before final submission. In particular, the relationship to SPINNs, APINNs, and recent separable PDE solvers should be thoroughly checked.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Rationale:** The paper presents a technically interesting integration of dimension decomposition and MoE-based domain decomposition for PDE solving, with a novel interpretability metric (VI) that addresses a genuine gap. The shared-MLP architecture demonstrates meaningful parameter efficiency. However, the current evidence is weakened by several unresolved concerns: the main accuracy comparison uses a capacity-mismatched baseline (10-layer vs 2-layer networks), the hardware specification appears factually incorrect (RTX 5090), a key methodological comparison is truncated mid-sentence, the MoE design choice lacks empirical justification, and the VI metric's normalization and geometric interpretation need clarification. The consistency and robustness evaluations rely on qualitative visual inspection rather than quantitative metrics. External literature verification was not available in this run, so novelty claims should be treated as provisional pending manual verification. With revisions addressing the baseline fairness, hardware correction, completion of the truncated comparison, and stronger quantitative evidence, the paper could become a solid contribution. In its current form, the contributions are promising but not yet fully substantiated.
