# Final Review Report

## Summary

This paper introduces the problem of **data reliability scoring** — assessing how much a reported dataset deviates from an unobserved ground truth, using auxiliary observations generated by an unknown statistical experiment. The authors propose the **Gram determinant score**, which computes the determinant of the Gram matrix of class-conditional observation distributions. They show that this score preserves several ground-truth-based reliability orderings (exact-match, Blackwell, and approximated Hamming/dist) under linearly independent experiments, and that it is uniquely experiment-agnostic up to scaling. The paper also establishes impossibility results that delineate the feasible conditions for reliability scoring. Experiments on synthetic categorical data (d=5), CIFAR-10 embeddings (d=10), and CES employment data (d=4) validate that the Gram determinant score correlates with label corruption levels.

**Overall assessment:** The paper addresses a well-motivated and original problem — evaluating dataset reliability without ground truth — with a clean mathematical framework and an elegant score based on the Gram determinant. The theoretical contributions (preservation of orderings, experiment agnosticism, and impossibility results) are technically sound and represent a solid conceptual contribution. However, the paper has several significant weaknesses that limit its impact: (1) the experimental validation lacks baseline comparisons and statistical rigor; (2) the plug-in estimator has an unaddressed self-pair bias; (3) the experiment setup has a critical inconsistency (row vs. column normalization of P) that may invalidate the synthetic experiments' connection to the theory; (4) the approximated Hamming ordering guarantee requires an extremely restrictive corruption regime (δ = O(1/d²)); and (5) the related-work positioning does not adequately differentiate from the most relevant prior work (Kong 2024). The paper would benefit from stronger empirical validation, corrected estimators, and more careful positioning.

## Strengths
1. **Original problem formulation.** The paper formalizes a novel and practically important problem — assessing data reliability when ground truth is inaccessible — that sits at the intersection of information elicitation, statistical inference, and data quality. This problem framing is clear, well-motivated with concrete examples, and likely to spur follow-up work.

2. **Elegant theoretical core.** The Gram determinant score is mathematically clean: $\Gamma(PQ) = \det(P^\top P) \det(Q)^2$ decouples the experiment quality from the misreport matrix. This multiplicative property enables the experiment-agnosticism result — the ranking is independent of the unknown observation experiment. Uniqueness up to scaling (Proposition 4.3) adds further theoretical depth.

3. **Well-structured impossibility results.** Section 3 systematically characterizes when reliability scoring is impossible (exact-match under non-permutation misreports, Blackwell under linearly dependent experiments, Hamming under diagonally dominant misreports). These results provide a principled foundation for the positive results in Section 4 and honestly delineate the method's boundaries.

4. **Experiment-agnosticism is a distinctive property.** The fact that the Gram determinant score produces the same dataset ranking regardless of the unknown observation experiment (Proposition 4.3) is a genuinely novel theoretical insight, and the uniqueness proof (up to scaling) strengthens this contribution considerably.

5. **Kernel extension extends applicability.** The kernelized variant (Definition 4.6) enables application to continuous observation spaces (e.g., image embeddings), which is essential for practical relevance beyond categorical data.

## Weaknesses
### W1. Experimental setup inconsistency: row vs. column normalization of P (Major)
**Location:** Page 7-8 — Section 5, Experiment 1
**Evidence:** The paper defines P as a *column-stochastic* matrix in Section 2.1 ("each column P_x is a distribution over 𝒴"). However, Experiment 1 constructs P by sampling Uniform(0,1) entries and "normalizing rows to be stochastic." Row-stochastic and column-stochastic matrices are fundamentally different — a row-stochastic matrix has rows summing to 1, meaning each observation outcome has a distribution over labels, which contradicts the model where each label has a distribution over observations.
**Impact:** This inconsistency means the synthetic data generation does not match the theoretical framework. If the paper used row normalization, the observation generation process $y_n \sim P_{x_n}$ would not correspond to drawing from a valid probability distribution (columns of a row-stochastic matrix need not sum to 1). This potentially invalidates the connection between the experimental results and the theoretical guarantees.
**Required action:** Clarify which normalization was used. If row normalization was actually employed, either (a) correct to column-stochastic normalization and re-run experiments, or (b) provide a rigorous justification for why row normalization yields equivalent results. If column normalization was intended but misdescribed, correct the text.

### W2. Self-pair bias in the plug-in estimator (Major)
**Location:** Page 7 — Section 4.2, Definition 4.4
**Evidence:** The plug-in estimator $\tilde{G}(x, x') = (1/N^2) \sum_{n,n': \hat{x}_n=x, \hat{x}_{n'}=x'} \mathbb{1}[y_n = y_{n'}]$ includes pairs where $n = n'$. For $n = n'$, the indicator $\mathbb{1}[y_n = y_n] = 1$ deterministically, biasing the estimate upward. The paper acknowledges that "the probability of $y_n = y_{n'}$ is simply the inner product of $P_{x_n}$ and $P_{x_{n'}}$ **if $n \neq n'$**" (emphasis added), but does not correct for or even discuss the self-pair bias in the estimator definition.
**Impact:** The bias is most severe when class sizes are small, where the $N^2$ denominator is dominated by the $N$ self-pair terms. Since the determinant is sensitive to diagonal entries, this bias can systematically affect the score ranking, undermining reliability for small-sample applications.
**Required action:** Either (a) exclude self-pairs and renormalize by $1/(N^2 - N)$, (b) provide a bias-correction term, or (c) prove that the bias vanishes asymptotically. Report the corrected estimator as the primary definition.

### W3. Restrictive conditions for approximated dist ordering (Major)
**Location:** Page 6 — Theorem 4.2, item 3
**Evidence:** The $\frac{1}{4L\Delta}$-dist ordering is preserved only under $\mathcal{Q}_{L, 1/64L^2 d^2}$, meaning the Hamming distance must be bounded by $N/(64L^2 d^2)$. For $d = 10$ classes (as in CIFAR-10) with $L = 2$ (balanced classes), this allows at most $N/(64 \cdot 4 \cdot 100) = N/25600$ corrupted labels — essentially zero corruption for practical sample sizes. This makes the "approximated" Hamming guarantee nearly vacuous for any dataset with more than a handful of classes.
**Impact:** The theoretical guarantee for dist-ordering preservation is so restrictive that it provides little practical assurance. The paper would benefit from explicitly quantifying this limitation and discussing what happens empirically when corruption exceeds the bound (which all of the paper's own experiments do).
**Required action:** Add a discussion paragraph acknowledging that the dist-ordering condition is extremely restrictive (especially as d grows), and provide empirical evidence (e.g., from the existing experiments, which operate at corruption levels far exceeding the bound) that the score still works reasonably in practice despite the theory being silent.

### W4. Missing baseline comparisons in all experiments (Major)
**Location:** Pages 7-9 — Section 5
**Evidence:** All three experiments only show the Gram determinant score's behavior across corruption levels. None compare against alternative reliability scores such as: (a) entropy of the confusion between $\hat{x}$ and $y$, (b) mutual information or pointwise mutual information [Zheng et al. 2025], (c) determinant mutual information [Kong 2024], (d) simple classifier accuracy (predicting $\hat{x}$ from $y$), or (e) principal component analysis-based scores [Amiri et al. 2022].
**Impact:** Without baselines, the reader cannot determine whether the Gram determinant score provides any advantage over simpler or existing methods. The key claim — that the score "effectively captures data quality" — is only supported by demonstrating monotonicity, not by showing superiority or complementarity to existing approaches.
**Required action:** Add at least 2-3 baseline comparisons to the synthetic experiment (Experiment 1). The most natural baselines are: (1) trace of the empirical confusion matrix $\text{Tr}(\tilde{Q})$ where $\tilde{Q}(i,j) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n \mathbb{1}[\hat{x}_n = i, y_n = j]$, which estimates classification accuracy; (2) entropy of the conditional distribution $p(y | \hat{x})$; (3) determinant mutual information as defined in Kong (2024) adapted to this setting.

### W5. Missing novelty differentiation from Kong (2024) in main text (Major)
**Location:** Page 1 — Section 1.1 (Related Work)
**Evidence:** The paper states "The most relevant work is Kong (2024), which introduces determinant mutual information and inspires our Gram Determinant Score. We provide a more detailed comparison with Kong (2024) in the Appendix." However, the main text does not explain what the **difference** is. The reader is referred to the Appendix for this critical positioning, which is insufficient — a reader should not need to consult appendices to understand the paper's primary novelty.
**Impact:** Without explicit differentiation, the novelty contribution relative to the closest prior work is unclear. The Gram determinant score may be essentially equivalent to determinant mutual information applied to $\hat{x}$ and $y$ rather than $x$ and $y$. If so, the experiment-agnosticism result is genuinely novel, but the scoring function itself may not be.
**Required action:** Add 2-3 sentences in Related Work that explicitly state: (1) how the Gram determinant score differs from deterministic mutual information (DMI), (2) what new properties this paper establishes that Kong (2024) did not, and (3) why experiment-agnosticism is consequential for reliability scoring. Keep the detailed technical comparison in the Appendix as supplementary.

### W6. Employment data experiment lacks statistical rigor (Minor)
**Location:** Page 9 — Section 5, Experiment 3
**Evidence:** The employment dataset has N=209 and d=4 categories. The results are reported as a bar chart (Fig. 3d) without error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests. With only 209 samples and 4 categories, the variance of the determinant estimate is potentially large. No comparison baselines are reported.
**Impact:** The real-world experiment — the paper's only non-synthetic validation — lacks the statistical rigor needed to support strong conclusions. The observed monotonic trend (Initial < 1-Month < Final) could be within noise.
**Required action:** Add bootstrap confidence intervals (at least 1000 resamples), permutation tests for pairwise comparisons, and at least one simple baseline (e.g., classification accuracy of predicting $\hat{x}$ from $y$).

### W7. Kernel extension definition is not implementable as stated (Minor)
**Location:** Page 7 — Definition 4.6
**Evidence:** Definition 4.6 defines $\hat{\mathbf{G}}_K(x, x')$ in terms of $\langle \mathbf{P}_{x_n}, \mathbf{P}_{x_{n'}} \rangle_K = \mathbb{E}_{y \sim P_{x_n}, y' \sim P_{x_{n'}}}[K(y, y')]$, which requires knowledge of the conditional distributions $P_x$ — precisely what is unknown in the detail-free setting. The definition should specify an estimator based on empirical evaluations $K(y_n, y_{n'})$ rather than the population expectation.
**Impact:** As written, the definition creates a circular dependency: the kernelized score requires computing expectations over the unknown experiment P.
**Required action:** Revise Definition 4.6 to specify the empirical estimator directly: $\hat{\mathbf{G}}_K(x, x') = \frac{1}{N^2 - N} \sum_{n \neq n': \hat{x}_n = x, \hat{x}_{n'} = x'} K(y_n, y_{n'})$, with appropriate diagonal handling. Then note that this consistently estimates the population quantity because $\mathbb{E}[K(y_n, y_{n'})] = \langle P_{x_n}, P_{x_{n'}} \rangle_K$ for $n \neq n'$.

### W8. Informal language and overclaiming in conclusion (Minor)
**Location:** Page 9 — Section 6
**Evidence:** The conclusion states "finite-sample guarantees" for the plug-in estimator, but Proposition 4.5 only establishes *asymptotic* preservation. The future work section opens with "Looking ahead, it's interesting to design..." — an informal tone unusual for academic writing. The Amazon/Yelp application example is speculative and unsupported.
**Required action:** (1) Distinguish asymptotic vs. finite-sample guarantees for the two estimators. (2) Replace "it's interesting to" with a more direct statement. (3) Remove or qualify the Amazon/Yelp example.

### W9. Notation inconsistencies in Blackwell ordering definition (Minor)
**Location:** Page 3 — Section 2.3, Blackwell dominant ordering
**Evidence:** The definition uses $\succ_{\text{Blackwell}}^\pi$ (superscript $\pi$) while exact-match ordering uses $\succ_{\text{EXACT}}^{\mathbf{x}}$ (superscript $\mathbf{x}$). The undefined variable $\alpha$ appears in $Q_{\hat{x}|\alpha}$ and $Q'_{\hat{x}'|\alpha}$.
**Required action:** Use consistent superscripts throughout Section 2.3. Replace $\pi$ with $\mathbf{x}$ and $\alpha$ with $\mathbf{x}$.

### W10. Missing implicit restriction |𝒴| ≥ |𝒳| for experiment independence (Minor)
**Location:** Page 4 — Section 3, definition of 𝒫_indep
**Evidence:** The paper defines $\mathcal{P}_{\text{indep}}$ as experiments where all columns of P are linearly independent. This requires $\text{rank}(P) = |\mathcal{X}|$, which is only possible if $|\mathcal{Y}| \geq |\mathcal{X}|$ (the observation space must be at least as large as the label space). This restriction is never stated explicitly.
**Required action:** Add a remark that linearly independent experiments require $|\mathcal{Y}| \geq |\mathcal{X}|$, and discuss the implications for practical applications where this may not hold.

## Score
**Final Score: 5/10**

**Scoring rationale:** The paper has a well-motivated problem, an elegant theoretical core, and clean impossibility results. However, the experimental validation has significant weaknesses (no baselines, setup inconsistency, missing statistical rigor) that prevent confirming the practical value of the proposed score. The theoretical guarantees for the approximated Hamming ordering are extremely restrictive (δ = O(1/d²)), limiting practical applicability. The plug-in estimator has an unaddressed self-pair bias. These issues are fixable with careful revision, but in the current form, the paper's contributions are primarily conceptual rather than empirically validated.

**Decision recommendation:** Revise and resubmit after addressing the major weaknesses, particularly: (1) correcting the row/column normalization inconsistency, (2) fixing the plug-in estimator bias, (3) adding baseline comparisons in experiments, and (4) providing clearer differentiation from Kong (2024). With these revisions, the paper could become a solid contribution to the emerging area of data reliability assessment.
