## Summary

This paper proposes Constraint-aware Reward (Re)Labeling (CARL), a wrapper-based approach for offline safe reinforcement learning (OSRL). The core idea is to reformulate the constrained CMDP problem into an unconstrained optimization by enforcing state-action-wise safety constraints ($Q_c^\pi(s,\pi(s)) \leq \kappa, \forall s$), which avoids tuning Lagrangian multipliers. CARL alternates between two steps at each mini-batch: (1) updating the cost Q-function via off-policy evaluation, and (2) relabeling rewards with $-V_{\max}$ for state-action pairs predicted to exceed the cost budget $\kappa$, then running standard offline policy optimization on the relabeled data. The method is designed as a lightweight wrapper around existing batch-update offline RL algorithms (e.g., TD3-BC, IQL). Empirical evaluation on the DSRL benchmark suite (19 tasks spanning Bullet and SafetyGym environments) shows that CARL consistently satisfies cost constraints under tight budgets ($\kappa=5,10$) while maintaining competitive rewards, outperforming prior OSRL methods including FISOR, CAPS, CCAC, CPQ, and CoptiDICE in terms of safety-reward balance. Additional ablations demonstrate backbone-agnostic behavior, effectiveness under varying cost budgets, and the ability to learn safe policies even when trained exclusively on unsafe trajectories.

## Strengths
1. **Clean theoretical formulation.** The reformulation of the constrained CMDP as an unconstrained optimization problem with state-action-wise safety constraints (Problem 2) is elegant and provides a principled way to avoid explicit Lagrangian tuning. Theorem 1 provides a clear equivalence between the two formulations under the assumption that a safe policy exists. This theoretical grounding distinguishes CARL from purely heuristic penalty-shaping approaches.

2. **Minimalist, plug-and-play design.** CARL's wrapper design — alternating between one-step cost Q-function update and one-step reward relabeling plus policy update — is genuinely simple and can be applied to any batch-update offline RL algorithm (TD3-BC, IQL) without modifying their internal loss functions or architectures. This generality is empirically demonstrated in Table 2, where CARL achieves similar safety-reward performance under both backbones.

3. **Strong empirical safety under tight budgets.** On the DSRL benchmark suite, CARL is the only method among seven baselines that satisfies cost constraints on all 8 Bullet Gym tasks at $\kappa=5$, and achieves safe policies on 8 of 11 SafetyGym tasks at $\kappa=10$. This consistent safety under small cost budgets is the paper's most practically significant result, as tight budgets are the most challenging and safety-critical regime.

4. **Informative ablations.** The paper includes several valuable ablations: (a) varying cost budgets showing that CARL exploits larger budgets safely (Figure 2), (b) training on only unsafe trajectories demonstrating recovery ability (Figure 3), and (c) comparison across backbone algorithms (Table 2). The hard-filtering ablation (Table 8, Appendix) showing that simply removing unsafe transitions fails further validates the reward relabeling mechanism.

5. **Honest discussion of limitations.** The paper explicitly acknowledges that theoretical convergence guarantees for $M=K=1$ are an "open problem" (Page 5, line 89), which is appropriately candid. This transparency helps readers calibrate their trust in the empirical results.

## Weaknesses
### (W1) Unsubstantiated "no hyperparameters" claim (Severity: Major)
The paper repeatedly claims CARL introduces "no additional task-specific hyperparameters" (Abstract, Introduction, Section 5). However, the CARL relabeling rule (Equation 5) requires choosing a penalty magnitude. The theory uses $-V_{\max}$, but experiments (Page 7, line 103) switch to $-R_{\max}$ (the maximum observed reward in the dataset), with an ablation reported only in the Appendix (Table 5). This choice affects the trade-off between safety and reward: a penalty that is too small may fail to deter unsafe actions, while an excessively large penalty suppresses exploration. The claim of "no hyperparameters" is technically true only if one accepts the fixed choice $-R_{\max}$, but the existence of an alternative design ($-V_{\max}$) and non-trivial ablation implies that the penalty magnitude is a sensitivity-relevant design decision. **Required fix:** Replace "no hyperparameters" with language acknowledging the penalty design choice, provide clear default recommendation ($R_{\max}$), and move the ablation discussion to the main text.

### (W2) Theorem 1 proof contains a logical subtlety (Severity: Major)
The proof assumes $V_{r_{\pi^*}}^{\tilde{\pi}^*}(s) > 0$ because $\tilde{\pi}^*$ is safe, and concludes $V_{r_{\pi^*}}^{\pi^*}(s) < 0 < V_{r_{\pi^*}}^{\tilde{\pi}^*}(s)$ based on the $-V_{\max}$ penalty. However, the derivation that $-V_{\max} + \mathbb{E}[\sum \gamma^t r_{\pi^*}] < 0$ implicitly relies on $V_{\max}$ being large enough to dominate all future discounted positive rewards. While this holds by definition ($V_{\max} = R_{\max}/(1-\gamma)$ is the maximum possible discounted return), the proof does not explicitly invoke this property, leaving a gap for careful readers. **Required fix:** Add one sentence clarifying the bounding argument: $ -V_{\max} + \mathbb{E}[\sum \gamma^t r_{\pi^*}] \leq -V_{\max} + V_{\max} = 0$, with strict inequality unless future rewards are maximal at each step.

### (W3) Cost value functions are incompletely defined (Severity: Major)
Section 2 defines reward value functions $V_r^\pi, Q_r^\pi$ explicitly, but mentions cost equivalents $V_c^\pi, Q_c^\pi$ only by analogy without formal definitions. Additionally, the constraint in Equation (1) writes $V_c^\pi \leq \kappa$ without specifying whether this is the expected cost over the initial state distribution ($\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_0}[V_c^\pi(s)] \leq \kappa$) or a pointwise bound. The paper later argues that pointwise constraints (Equation 2) are stronger, but the original constraint in (1) should be unambiguous. **Required fix:** Add explicit definitions for $V_c^\pi$ and $Q_c^\pi$, and rewrite (1) as $\max_\pi \mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_0}[V_r^\pi(s)]$ s.t. $\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mu_0}[V_c^\pi(s)] \leq \kappa$.

### (W4) Limited statistical rigor in evaluation (Severity: Major)
The evaluation protocol (Page 6, line 99) uses only 3 random seeds and 20 evaluation episodes per seed. For safety-critical OSRL, this provides insufficient statistical power. Several cost values in Table 1 have standard deviations exceeding the mean (e.g., CarCircle1: cost $4.15\pm 8.93$), indicating high variability. Without confidence intervals or significance tests, claims about "safety satisfaction" are unreliable for high-variance tasks. **Required fix:** Increase to at least 5 seeds, report 95% confidence intervals for cost values, and include a statistical test (e.g., bootstrap) for whether $C_{\text{norm}} \leq 1$ can be asserted with confidence.

### (W5) Results discussion glosses over failure cases (Severity: Major)
The main results paragraph (Page 7, lines 103-104) states "CARL reliably enforces safety constraints" and "CARL's safety does not come at the expense of reward performance," but does not discuss the 3 SafetyGym tasks where CARL is unsafe (CarCircle1: $C_{\text{norm}}=4.15$, CarGoal2: $C_{\text{norm}}=1.77$, CarCircle2: $C_{\text{norm}}=1.57$) and the 3 tasks where it has the lowest reward among safe methods (e.g., PointGoal1: $R_{\text{norm}}=0.06$). Selective reporting reduces scientific transparency. **Required fix:** Add a dedicated failure analysis paragraph discussing when and why CARL fails, e.g., "On CarCircle1 and CarCircle2, the high cost suggests the cost Q-function struggles in environments with sparse cost signals. On PointGoal1, the near-zero reward indicates the penalty is overly conservative."

### (W6) Related work is a flat list; lacks structured comparison (Severity: Minor)
Section 3 lists methods sequentially (CPQ, CoptiDICE, CDT, FISOR, Trac, LSPC, CAPS, CCAC, TREBI) without organizing them along decision-relevant axes (e.g., constrained vs. unconstrained optimization, diffusion-based vs. Q-function-based, architectural overhead). This makes it hard for readers to understand CARL's positioning. **Required fix:** Reorganize into comparison table or paragraph groups: (a) Lagrangian/constrained methods, (b) diffusion-based methods, (c) trajectory-classification methods, (d) our method — highlighting concrete assumption and complexity differences in each group.

### (W7) Conclusion lacks limitation discussion and uses informal language (Severity: Minor)
The Summary section (line 173) uses "embarrassingly simple," which is informal for a scientific paper. More importantly, it does not list limitations. A reader evaluating whether to adopt CARL needs to know its failure modes. **Required fix:** Add a limitation paragraph covering: (a) dependence on accurate cost Q-function estimation, (b) lack of convergence guarantees for $M=K=1$, (c) tasks where safety or reward fails, (d) penalty selection sensitivity.

### (W8) Unsafe-trajectory ablation has a confound (Severity: Minor)
The ablation training on "only unsafe trajectories" (Page 8, lines 162-171) defines unsafety at the trajectory level, but individual transitions within those trajectories may be safe. CARL's state-action-wise relabeling can exploit these safe subsequences, so the experiment does not demonstrate recovery from purely adversarial data. **Required fix:** Add a clarifying sentence distinguishing trajectory-level unsafety (cumulative cost exceeds $\kappa$) from per-transition unsafety (individual cost violations), and note that CARL's performance relies on the former definition.

### (W9) Comparative novelty cannot be assessed without external retrieval (Severity: Verification — deferred)
Due to Retrieval-Disabled Mode in this review run (paper_search API unavailable), claims about CARL being "state-of-the-art" or "outperforming prior methods" could not be independently verified against the external literature. The paper references FISOR, CAPS, CCAC, CPQ, CoptiDICE, CDT as baselines, but the comparison relies entirely on reported numbers in Table 1 without external replication or analysis of whether baselines were optimally tuned. **Required action:** Authors should ensure that all baseline results are verifiable (e.g., via publicly available leaderboards or reproducible configurations). A manual literature check before publication is strongly recommended.

## Score
**Final Score: 6/10**

**Scoring rationale (research value + novelty + validity + reproducibility):**

The paper presents a conceptually clean and practically simple wrapper approach (CARL) for offline safe RL. The core idea — reformulating the constrained CMDP as an unconstrained problem via state-action-wise safety constraints with reward relabeling — is well-motivated, and the empirical results demonstrate consistent safety satisfaction under tight cost budgets across a wide benchmark suite. These constitute genuine research value.

However, several weaknesses reduce confidence:

- **Novelty uncertainty (deferred):** External literature verification was unavailable in this review run, so claims about outperforming state-of-the-art methods cannot be independently confirmed. The core mechanism (reward shaping via cost threshold) has conceptual precursors in online safe RL (penalty-based control, Sauté RL), and the paper does not provide a rigorous differentiation from these families beyond the offline setting and the pointwise constraint formulation.

- **Validity concerns:** The "no hyperparameters" claim is misleading; the penalty magnitude ($R_{\max}$ vs $V_{\max}$) is a non-trivial design choice with documented sensitivity (W1). The Theorem 1 proof has a subtle gap (W2). Cost value functions are incompletely defined (W3). Statistical power is limited (W4).

- **Writing quality:** The paper is generally well-written but the conclusion is too informal and lacks limitation discussion (W7). The contribution list includes a circular entry (item 3: demonstrating effectiveness).

The paper's strengths (clean formulation, strong empirical safety, backbone agnosticism) are significant, but the methodological weaknesses and unverifiable novelty claims prevent a higher score. The work is suitable for a conference publication after addressing the major weaknesses, particularly W1 (penalty claim), W2 (proof gap), W3 (definitions), and W4 (statistical rigor).
