## Summary

This paper introduces LR0.FM, a benchmark designed to evaluate the robustness of visual-language foundation models (VLMs) when processing low-resolution images. The authors investigate how model performance degrades as image resolution decreases, a critical consideration given the prevalence of low-resolution images in real-world scenarios. They find that model size and pre-training dataset quality are more impactful on robustness than image resolution itself. Specifically, larger models and those trained on more diverse datasets exhibit greater resilience to resolution changes. The authors also propose a new metric, Weighted Aggregated Robustness (WAR), to better assess model performance across different resolutions and datasets, addressing limitations of existing metrics like SAR and WAR. Furthermore, they introduce LR-TK0, a method that enhances model robustness against low-resolution inputs without altering pre-trained weights. LR-TK0 achieves this by adding trainable low-resolution tokens to the frozen transformer, trained using self-supervised distillation on synthetically generated high-resolution images. The authors demonstrate that LR-TK0 improves the performance of various models on different datasets, particularly for models like MetaCLIP. The paper's findings highlight the importance of considering low-resolution images in VLM evaluation and offer a practical method for improving model robustness in such scenarios. The work contributes a valuable benchmark and metric to the field, along with a simple yet effective technique for enhancing robustness, which could be particularly useful in real-world applications where high-resolution images are not always available.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper addresses a crucial and often overlooked aspect of visual-language models: their performance on low-resolution images. This is a significant contribution because, as the authors rightly point out, low-resolution images are common in real-world applications, and the robustness of VLMs to such variations is essential for their practical utility. The comprehensive benchmark, LR0.FM, covers a wide range of foundation models and datasets, providing a thorough evaluation of model performance across different conditions. This extensive evaluation allows for a more robust analysis of the factors influencing model robustness. The introduction of the Weighted Aggregated Robustness (WAR) metric is another notable strength. The authors correctly identify the limitations of existing metrics, such as SAR and WAR, and propose a new metric that addresses these shortcomings. WAR provides a more balanced evaluation of model performance across different resolutions and datasets, offering a more reliable measure of robustness. Finally, the proposed LR-TK0 method is both simple and effective. By adding trainable low-resolution tokens to the frozen transformer, the authors demonstrate a practical way to enhance model robustness without requiring extensive retraining of the pre-trained model. This makes LR-TK0 a valuable contribution, as it offers a computationally efficient solution for improving the performance of VLMs on low-resolution images. The experimental results, particularly the improvements achieved by LR-TK0 on various models, further validate the effectiveness of the proposed method.

## Weaknesses

Despite its strengths, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant attention. A primary concern is the lack of a clear motivation for why low-resolution images are important for evaluating the robustness of VLMs. While the authors mention that low-resolution images are common in real-world scenarios, they do not provide specific examples where low-resolution images are crucial for accurate classification and how these images are different from typical low-resolution images encountered in practical applications. For instance, the paper does not discuss scenarios where high-resolution information is not essential for accurate classification, such as in medical imaging where overall scene understanding might be more important than fine-grained details. This lack of specific examples weakens the motivation for studying robustness to low-resolution inputs. Furthermore, the paper does not justify the choice of specific low-resolution resolutions used in the benchmark. The authors do not explain why these resolutions are representative of typical low-resolution images encountered in practical applications or if they are chosen arbitrarily. This lack of justification makes it difficult to assess the practical relevance of the chosen resolutions. The paper also lacks a clear explanation of how the proposed metric, WAR, is calculated. While the authors mention that WAR is based on the Spearman correlation between the rankings of models across different resolutions, they do not provide the specific formulas and parameters used in the calculation. This lack of detail makes it difficult to understand the metric's behavior and how it addresses the limitations of existing metrics. The paper also does not provide a detailed comparison of WAR with other metrics, including a correlation with human performance, which would have been helpful in establishing its value. Additionally, the paper does not provide a detailed explanation of the proposed method, LR-TK0. While the authors describe the high-level concept of adding trainable low-resolution tokens to the frozen transformer, they do not provide a step-by-step explanation of the implementation, the specific architecture of the trainable tokens, or a comparison of computational cost and ease of implementation compared to other methods. This lack of detail makes it difficult to fully understand and evaluate the practical aspects of LR-TK0. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the results obtained using LR-TK0, and how these results compare to those obtained using other methods. While the authors show improvements with LR-TK0, they do not provide a detailed analysis of the specific scenarios where LR-TK0 is particularly effective or less effective. The paper also does not discuss the limitations of the proposed method, and how it might be improved in future work. These omissions weaken the overall impact of the paper and make it difficult to fully assess the value of the proposed method.
