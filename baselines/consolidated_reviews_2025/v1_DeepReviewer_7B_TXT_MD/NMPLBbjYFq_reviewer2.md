### Summary

This paper proposes a new benchmark for evaluating LLMs in competitive economic games. The authors evaluate LLMs in beauty contest and second price auction games, and find that LLMs are not as rational as humans are in these games. The authors also find that some LLMs are able to adapt to dynamic environments and that some LLMs are able to learn from history.

### Suggestions

The authors should consider expanding the scope of their evaluation to include a more diverse set of economic games. While beauty contest and second-price auctions are useful starting points, they do not fully capture the complexities of real-world economic interactions. For example, incorporating games with incomplete information, such as auctions with reserve prices or signaling games, would provide a more comprehensive assessment of LLMs' strategic reasoning capabilities. Furthermore, games with multiple equilibria or those involving asymmetric information could reveal different aspects of LLM behavior. The current selection of games is somewhat limited in its ability to test the full range of economic reasoning skills that humans possess. By including a wider variety of games, the authors could provide a more robust and generalizable evaluation of LLM performance in economic contexts. This would also allow for a more nuanced comparison of different LLMs, as certain models might perform better in specific types of games.

Additionally, the authors should explore the behavior of LLMs in multi-round games. The current evaluation of one-shot games provides a limited view of how LLMs adapt to repeated interactions and learn from past experiences. Multi-round games introduce the possibility of strategic adaptation and the development of more sophisticated reasoning patterns. For instance, in repeated beauty contest games, LLMs might learn to adjust their strategies based on observed patterns of play. Similarly, in repeated second-price auctions, LLMs could potentially develop strategies that take into account the history of bids and the behavior of other agents. Analyzing LLM behavior in multi-round settings would provide valuable insights into their ability to learn and adapt in dynamic environments. This would also allow for a more thorough investigation of the strategic reasoning abilities of LLMs, as they would be forced to consider the potential consequences of their actions over time.

Finally, the authors should consider incorporating more complex economic scenarios that involve multiple agents and more intricate strategic interactions. The current games, while useful for initial evaluation, are relatively simple and may not fully capture the complexities of real-world economic systems. For example, incorporating scenarios with multiple rounds of negotiation, or games with more complex payoff structures, could provide a more challenging test of LLMs' strategic reasoning abilities. Furthermore, the authors could explore the use of more sophisticated game-theoretic models, such as those involving signaling or mechanism design, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of LLMs' economic reasoning capabilities. By incorporating more complex and realistic economic scenarios, the authors could provide a more robust and generalizable assessment of LLM performance in economic contexts.
