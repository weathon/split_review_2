## Summary

This paper explores the potential of pre-training on geometric tasks, specifically monocular depth estimation, for enhancing semantic segmentation performance. The authors investigate whether pre-training on geometric data can reduce the need for extensive pixel-level annotations, a significant bottleneck in semantic segmentation. They compare monocular depth pre-training with other common pre-training methods, such as ImageNet pre-training and no pre-training, across various datasets including KITTI, Cityscapes, and NYUv2. The core methodology involves training monocular depth estimation models using different supervision sources, such as multi-view stereo, binocular stereo, and LiDAR data, and then fine-tuning these models for semantic segmentation. The experiments are conducted using ResNet and DeepLabv3 architectures. The main empirical finding is that monocular depth pre-training consistently improves semantic segmentation performance, often outperforming ImageNet pre-training. The authors also explore the impact of training set size, network architecture, and other factors on the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The overall significance of this work lies in its demonstration that pre-training on geometric tasks can be a viable alternative to human-annotated data for learning semantic concepts, potentially reducing the reliance on expensive manual labeling. This could have significant implications for the field of computer vision, particularly in scenarios where labeled data is scarce or expensive to obtain. However, the paper also acknowledges that the pre-training data for depth estimation comes from sources that may not be entirely free of human annotation, which challenges the claim of a “human-free” approach. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a clear definition of what constitutes “semantic understanding” in the context of this study, and the connection between the learned geometric features and semantic features is not rigorously established. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a compelling case for the potential of geometric pre-training for semantic segmentation, and it opens up avenues for future research in this direction.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the paper's claim of a “human-free” approach to learning semantic concepts through monocular depth pre-training. While the authors argue that depth estimation itself is a “viable” pre-training option, the depth estimation models are trained on data sources that are often acquired using human-annotated data, such as multi-view stereo, binocular stereo, and LiDAR datasets. This reliance on human-annotated data for pre-training contradicts the claim of a “human-free” approach, as the depth estimation itself is not entirely free of human influence. This is evident in the method section, where the authors describe training depth estimation models using multi-view stereo, binocular stereo, and LiDAR data, all of which are often acquired through human-annotated data. Furthermore, the paper does not adequately explain the connection between depth estimation and the emergence of semantic understanding. The authors use the term “semantic understanding” to refer to the emergence of “object-centric bias” in the feature space, but this definition is not sufficiently rigorous. The paper lacks a clear explanation of how the geometric cues from depth estimation translate into the semantic features required for segmentation. The paper does not provide a formal definition of “semantic understanding” or rigorously prove that the learned features are capturing semantic information beyond low-level geometric cues. The Grad-CAM visualizations provide some insight into the features related to semantic boundaries, but it's not a definitive proof of semantic understanding. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of the method's robustness to occlusions and lighting variations. While the datasets used contain some variability, there is no specific analysis of how the method performs under these challenging conditions. The paper does not discuss the robustness of the method to these challenges or provide any analysis of its performance under such conditions. This is a significant limitation, as real-world scenarios often involve occlusions and varying lighting conditions. The paper also does not explore the computational cost of the proposed approach, which could be a limiting factor for practical applications. The authors mention that depth pre-training yields a mapping, but they do not provide specific details on the training time, memory requirements, or hardware used. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method for real-world applications. Finally, the paper does not adequately address the potential limitations of the proposed approach. For example, it is not clear how the method would perform in scenarios with significant occlusions or lighting variations. The paper should discuss the robustness of the method to these challenges and provide some analysis of its performance under such conditions. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the computational cost of the proposed approach, which could be a limiting factor for practical applications. The paper also lacks a direct comparison with object detection-based pre-training methods, which are also common approaches. This omission makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of the proposed method compared to other pre-training techniques. In summary, while the paper presents a compelling case for the potential of geometric pre-training for semantic segmentation, the lack of a rigorous definition of “semantic understanding,” the absence of a thorough robustness analysis, and the lack of a detailed computational cost analysis are significant limitations that need to be addressed.
