## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to medical image segmentation by utilizing synthetic tumors as a validation set, challenging the traditional reliance on static, often small and biased, real-world datasets. The authors propose a continual learning framework where a model is initially trained on a large, diverse set of healthy CT volumes. Subsequently, the model is trained on synthetically generated tumors, which are designed to mimic the variability of real tumors, including their heterogeneity and small sizes. The core idea is that this synthetic validation set can provide a more robust and unbiased evaluation of the model's performance, particularly for challenging cases like tiny tumors. The synthetic tumors are generated through a multi-step process involving the extraction of lesion characteristics from healthy volumes, followed by the creation of synthetic tumors with similar properties. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through experiments on liver tumor segmentation, using the LiTS dataset, and also evaluate the method's performance on the FLARE'23 dataset. The results indicate that the proposed method improves model performance, especially in detecting tiny tumors, and enhances continual learning frameworks by dynamically training on out-of-domain data. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative use of synthetic data as a validation set, which addresses the limitations of traditional validation methods in medical imaging, where data scarcity and heterogeneity pose significant challenges. By leveraging the large volume of healthy data available, the authors propose a method to generate a diverse and unbiased validation set, which can lead to more robust and reliable models. The paper also highlights the potential of continual learning in medical imaging, where models can be continuously trained on new data, including synthetic data, to adapt to evolving conditions and improve performance over time. The authors' approach offers a promising solution to the challenges of limited and biased validation data in medical image segmentation, and their findings suggest that synthetic data can be a valuable tool for improving the accuracy and reliability of medical image analysis. The paper's findings are significant because they offer a practical solution to the problem of limited validation data in medical image segmentation, which is a critical issue in the field. By using synthetic data as a validation set, the authors demonstrate that it is possible to train models that are more robust and reliable, particularly for challenging cases like tiny tumors. This approach has the potential to improve the accuracy of medical image analysis, which can have significant implications for diagnosis and treatment planning. The paper's contribution is not only in its specific method but also in its demonstration of the potential of synthetic data in medical imaging, which could lead to further research and development in this area. The authors' work highlights the importance of considering the limitations of real-world datasets and the potential of synthetic data to overcome these limitations, which is a crucial step towards the development of more accurate and reliable medical image analysis tools.
