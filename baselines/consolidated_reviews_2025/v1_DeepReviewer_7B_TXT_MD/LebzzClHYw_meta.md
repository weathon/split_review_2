## Summary

This paper introduces Instructive Decoding (ID), a novel method designed to enhance the instruction-following capabilities of instruction-tuned language models. The core idea behind ID is to adjust the logits for next-token prediction in a contrastive manner, utilizing predictions from a manipulated version of the original instruction. This manipulation is achieved through various types of noisy instructions, including truncation, shuffling, random word replacement, and opposing instructions. The authors draw inspiration from the 'anchoring effect' from cognitive science, suggesting that initial information exerts a disproportionate influence on subsequent judgments, and they propose that ID can guide the model's attention towards the instruction during generation. The method is presented as simple and lightweight, requiring minimal computational overhead and not necessitating additional parameter updates. The authors evaluate ID across multiple datasets, including UnNatInst and SupNatInst, and across various model sizes within the Tk-Instruct model family. Their experiments demonstrate that ID consistently improves performance compared to baseline models, with the opposing variant yielding the most significant gains. The authors also conduct ablation studies and comparisons with other decoding methods to further validate the effectiveness of ID. The results suggest that contrastive decoding, when guided by appropriately designed noisy instructions, can be a powerful tool for improving the instruction-following capabilities of language models. The paper concludes by highlighting the potential of ID as a practical approach for enhancing the reliability and robustness of instruction-tuned models, particularly in scenarios involving unseen tasks. The authors emphasize the simplicity and effectiveness of their method, positioning it as a valuable contribution to the field of natural language processing. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are some limitations to the study that warrant further consideration.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of the paper, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the novelty of the proposed method is limited. While the authors frame Instructive Decoding (ID) as a novel approach, the core idea of adjusting predictions based on manipulated inputs has been explored in other contexts, particularly in the realm of contrastive decoding and adversarial training. The paper does not adequately differentiate ID from existing methods that also leverage perturbed inputs. For instance, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison with contrastive decoding methods that use different types of noise or other methods that employ adversarial examples. This lack of differentiation makes it difficult to ascertain the unique contribution of ID.
