## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to active probabilistic drug discovery (APDD), termed APDD, which iteratively refines the binding probabilities of molecules to a target protein. The method operates in three key stages. First, it employs probabilistic clustering to group molecules into clusters based on substructure similarity. This clustering is achieved using Fast Probabilistic Clustering (FPC) with the Mixture of Probabilistic Clustering (MPC) algorithm and the Faiss library. Second, the method selects representative molecules from each cluster for subsequent molecular docking simulations using VinaGPU+, a GPU-accelerated implementation of the Vina scoring function. Finally, the binding probabilities of the molecules are updated based on the results of the molecular docking simulations and subsequent wet-lab experiments. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach by comparing it against a baseline method, VE, which selects molecules with the highest Vina scores for wet-lab experiments. The results, presented on two benchmark datasets, DUD-E and LIT-PCBA, show that APDD achieves substantial cost savings, reducing computational docking expenses by 80% and wet-lab costs by 70%, while maintaining high accuracy in lead molecule discovery. The core idea behind APDD is to leverage clustering to identify groups of molecules with similar structural features, thereby reducing the number of individual docking simulations required. By iteratively updating the binding probabilities based on experimental results, the method aims to focus on the most promising molecules for subsequent wet-lab screening. The authors argue that this approach is more efficient than traditional methods that rely on exhaustive screening of all available molecules. The paper highlights the potential of combining computational methods with experimental validation to accelerate the drug discovery process. The authors emphasize the importance of probabilistic clustering and active learning in the context of drug discovery, where the goal is to identify a small set of molecules with high binding affinity. The paper's contribution lies in its integration of these techniques into a practical workflow for active probabilistic drug discovery. The authors also provide a detailed description of the experimental setup, including the datasets used, the clustering algorithm, and the docking software. The results are presented in a clear and concise manner, with tables and figures that effectively illustrate the performance of the proposed method compared to the baseline. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and compelling approach to active probabilistic drug discovery, with a clear focus on efficiency and accuracy. The authors have successfully demonstrated the potential of their method to reduce the computational and experimental costs associated with drug discovery, while maintaining high performance. The paper's emphasis on the iterative nature of the method, where experimental results are used to refine the binding probabilities, is a key strength. This approach allows the method to adapt to new information and focus on the most promising molecules, ultimately leading to a more efficient and effective drug discovery process.
