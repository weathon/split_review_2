## Summary

This paper introduces PETRA, a novel method for parallelizing the training of reversible neural networks. The core idea behind PETRA is to decouple the forward and backward passes, enabling efficient model parallelism without the need for storing extensive computational graphs or additional buffers. This is achieved by allowing parameters to evolve in parallel and computing an approximate inversion during the backward pass. The authors argue that this approach allows for linear speedup compared to standard backpropagation when using synchronous parallelization. The method is particularly relevant for reversible architectures, which, while offering memory advantages during training, have traditionally suffered from the sequential nature of the backward pass. PETRA aims to address this limitation by leveraging the inherent parallelism within the network structure. The authors provide a detailed explanation of the proposed method, including its implementation in PyTorch, and present experimental results on CIFAR-10, ImageNet32, and ImageNet datasets. These experiments demonstrate that PETRA achieves competitive accuracy with standard backpropagation while enabling linear speedup with respect to the number of stages in the network. The paper also includes a comparison with other parallelization techniques, highlighting the memory and computational efficiency of PETRA. The authors emphasize that their method achieves this efficiency without requiring additional buffers or complex communication patterns, which is a key advantage over other approaches. While the paper presents a promising approach for parallelizing reversible network training, it also acknowledges certain limitations, such as the potential for increased communication overhead in certain scenarios and the need for further investigation into the method's scalability to larger models and datasets. The authors also note that the method is not entirely novel in the sense that the idea of decoupling forward and backward passes has been explored in other contexts, but they argue that their approach is specifically tailored for reversible architectures and offers a unique combination of memory and computational efficiency. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of parallelized training of reversible neural networks, offering a practical and efficient method for leveraging model parallelism in this context. However, further research is needed to fully explore the potential and limitations of PETRA and to compare it with other state-of-the-art methods for reversible network training.
