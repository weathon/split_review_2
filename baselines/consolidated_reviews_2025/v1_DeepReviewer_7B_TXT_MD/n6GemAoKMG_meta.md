## Summary

This paper introduces Evolutionary Attack (EvA), a novel adversarial attack method for graph neural networks (GNNs) that directly optimizes the objective function without relying on gradients. The core idea behind EvA is to leverage a genetic algorithm to iteratively perturb the graph structure, aiming to minimize the performance of the GNN while adhering to a budget constraint. The authors argue that traditional gradient-based attacks often fail to find optimal solutions due to the relaxation of the original discrete optimization problem. EvA, by contrast, operates in the discrete space of graph perturbations, employing a novel encoding scheme that represents edge flips as indices, thereby reducing memory complexity and enabling efficient optimization. The paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of EvA on several benchmark datasets, including Cora, Citeseer, and PubMed, demonstrating its effectiveness in reducing the performance of both vanilla and adversarially trained GNNs. Furthermore, the authors introduce new attack objectives, such as reducing the certified ratio and decreasing the coverage, and show that EvA is effective in achieving these goals. The experimental results consistently show that EvA outperforms state-of-the-art gradient-based attacks, highlighting the limitations of relying on gradients for adversarial attacks on GNNs. The paper also includes ablation studies that analyze the impact of different components of the genetic algorithm, such as the population size and mutation type. Overall, this work presents a significant departure from traditional gradient-based adversarial attacks on GNNs, offering a new perspective on how to approach the problem of adversarial robustness in graph-based machine learning. The authors' approach of directly optimizing the objective function without relying on gradients is a notable contribution to the field, and the empirical results demonstrate the effectiveness of their method. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several areas where the paper could be strengthened, particularly in terms of computational complexity analysis, comparisons with other non-gradient-based methods, and a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed approach.

The use of a genetic algorithm to navigate the discrete space of graph perturbations is also a notable technical innovation. The authors' encoding scheme, which represents edge flips as indices, is both efficient and effective, allowing for a significant reduction in memory complexity. This is a significant limitation, as it leaves open questions about the practical applicability of the method to real-world scenarios with large graphs. Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements to strengthen this paper. This analysis should include a formal breakdown of the time and space complexity of each step of the genetic algorithm, such as population initialization, fitness evaluation, and selection. The analysis should also consider the number of nodes, edges, and the population size, and how these factors affect the overall runtime. Furthermore, the authors should include empirical results on the runtime of the attack on different graph sizes, to demonstrate its practical scalability. This analysis should also discuss the memory requirements of the attack, which can be a limiting factor for large graphs. Second, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with other non-gradient-based adversarial attack methods. While the authors compare against a gradient-based method, it is important to compare against other methods that do not rely on gradients, if they exist, or to adapt existing methods to the graph domain. This comparison should not only focus on the attack success rate but also on other metrics such as the number of forward passes required, the computational time, and the memory usage. If no other non-gradient-based methods exist for graph attacks, the authors should discuss why and what are the challenges of adapting existing methods to the graph domain. This discussion should also include a comparison of the performance of EvA with other gradient-based methods, to understand the trade-offs between gradient-based and non-gradient-based attacks. Third, the authors should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This discussion should include an analysis of the sensitivity of the attack to hyperparameters, such as the population size, mutation rate, and the number of evolutionary steps. The authors should also discuss the robustness of the attack to different types of graph structures, such as sparse and dense graphs, and graphs with different degree distributions. This analysis should include empirical results on the performance of the attack on different types of graphs, and should discuss the potential reasons for any observed differences. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the limitations of the attack in terms of the types of objectives that can be optimized, and the challenges of optimizing more complex objectives. This discussion should also include a discussion of the potential for the attack to be adapted to other types of graph-based tasks, such as node classification and link prediction. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the computational complexity of the proposed method, including the number of forward passes required for each evaluation, and how this scales with the graph size. Furthermore, it would be valuable to provide empirical results on the runtime of the attack on different graph sizes, to demonstrate its practical scalability. This would allow readers to better understand the trade-offs between attack effectiveness and computational cost, and to assess the method's applicability to real-world scenarios with large graphs. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make it more impactful to the research community.
