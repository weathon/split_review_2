## Summary

This paper introduces SEGNO, a novel approach that integrates equivariant graph neural networks (GNNs) with Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs) to model multi-object physical systems. The core motivation stems from the limitations of existing equivariant GNNs, which primarily model discrete transitions between system states and do not fully capture the continuous nature of physical dynamics, nor do they consider second-order acceleration information. To address these shortcomings, SEGNO employs Neural ODEs to approximate continuous trajectories between observed states, effectively modeling the system's evolution over time. The method uses a Neural ODE to predict the continuous trajectory, approximating the system's evolution between two observed states. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of SEGNO, demonstrating the existence and uniqueness of learned trajectories and bounding the approximation error. Empirically, the authors evaluate SEGNO on simulated N-body systems, MD22, and CMU motion capture datasets, showing that it outperforms state-of-the-art baselines, achieving significant improvements in prediction accuracy and generalization ability. The paper's primary contribution lies in its novel integration of Neural ODEs with equivariant GNNs to model continuous trajectories, coupled with a theoretical analysis that provides guarantees on the learned trajectories. The authors also present a comprehensive set of experiments that demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in the scope of its experimental evaluation and the depth of its theoretical analysis, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach to modeling physical systems, but there are areas where further work is needed to fully realize its potential.

The authors articulate this problem effectively, motivating the need for a method that can capture the continuous nature of physical systems. Second, the proposed method, SEGNO, is both simple and intuitive. The idea of using Neural ODEs to model continuous trajectories between observed states is a natural and effective approach. The authors' approach of incorporating second-order continuity into the framework is straightforward and intuitive, and it is easy to understand how the method works. Third, the theoretical analysis provided in the paper is rigorous and provides valuable insights into the properties of the model. The authors demonstrate that SEGNO can learn a unique trajectory between adjacent states and bound the approximation error, which strengthens the theoretical foundation of the proposed method. This analysis provides a solid basis for understanding the behavior of SEGNO and its potential for generalization. Finally, the empirical results presented in the paper are comprehensive and demonstrate the effectiveness of SEGNO on multiple datasets. The authors show that SEGNO outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in terms of prediction accuracy and generalization ability. The ablation studies and sensitivity analyses further validate the design choices of the model. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The problem formulation and method description are clear and easy to understand, which facilitates the understanding of the proposed approach. In summary, the paper's strengths lie in its clear problem definition, intuitive methodology, rigorous theoretical analysis, and comprehensive empirical evaluation.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the paper acknowledges related work that uses Neural ODEs for learning dynamics, it does not explicitly articulate the differences between its approach and these existing methods, particularly those that incorporate second-order continuity. The authors mention that their method differs by learning instantaneous accelerations rather than average accelerations, but they do not provide a detailed comparison of the mathematical formulations and the implications for the learned trajectories. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to assess the novelty and contribution of the proposed method. For example, the paper does not discuss how its approach differs from methods like [1, 2] (although these are not explicitly cited), which also explore incorporating second-order continuity. This is a significant oversight, as it leaves the reader unsure about the specific advantages of SEGNO over existing methods. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does not provide a detailed comparison. Second, the theoretical analysis, while rigorous, is not sufficiently solid in its practical implications. The authors primarily show that SEGNO can learn a unique trajectory between adjacent states, but they do not extend this analysis to the whole trajectory. The analysis does not address the stability of the learned trajectory over extended time horizons, which is crucial for physical simulations. The analysis also does not consider the impact of numerical integration errors on the uniqueness of the learned trajectory. The paper uses Euler integration, which is known to have error accumulation properties, but the analysis does not delve into how these errors might affect the overall trajectory. This lack of analysis limits the practical applicability of the theoretical results. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's theoretical analysis is clearly limited in scope. Third, the experimental evaluation, while comprehensive, is not as thorough as it could be. The authors compare their method against a reasonable number of baselines, but they do not include more recent state-of-the-art methods in their comparisons. The experimental evaluation could also benefit from more diverse datasets and tasks to demonstrate the generalizability of the proposed method. The current experiments are limited to relatively simple systems, and it is unclear how the method would perform on more complex and realistic physical systems. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does include relevant baselines but could benefit from more recent and diverse comparisons. Fourth, the paper lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. The authors do not discuss under what conditions SEGNO might fail to accurately model physical dynamics, what the potential failure modes of the method are, or the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter choices. The paper also does not address the potential for numerical instability or the applicability of the method to different types of physical systems, such as systems with strong non-linearities or chaotic behavior. This lack of discussion limits the understanding of the method's applicability and robustness. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include a dedicated section on limitations. Finally, the paper lacks a thorough analysis of the computational complexity of SEGNO. While the authors mention the use of Euler integration, they do not provide a formal analysis of how the computational cost scales with the number of objects, the length of the simulation, or the dimensionality of the state space. The paper also does not discuss the practical limitations of the method in terms of computational resources or the maximum simulation time that can be achieved with a given computational budget. This lack of analysis limits the practical applicability of the method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include a formal analysis of computational complexity.

While the idea of using a Neural ODE with a second-order continuity constraint is not entirely novel, the specific implementation and its application within a GNN framework could be a contribution. A more thorough analysis of the advantages and disadvantages of each approach would be beneficial. This would help to clarify the novelty and contribution of the proposed method. Second, to strengthen the theoretical analysis, the authors should extend their analysis beyond the local uniqueness of trajectories. A more comprehensive analysis should address the global stability and convergence properties of the learned trajectories over extended time horizons. This could involve analyzing the spectral properties of the Neural ODE solver and the impact of numerical integration errors on the long-term behavior of the system. The authors should also investigate how the theoretical error bounds derived for adjacent states translate to the overall error over longer time intervals. Furthermore, the analysis should consider the impact of the choice of the Neural ODE solver and its parameters on the stability and accuracy of the learned trajectories. A more rigorous analysis of these aspects would significantly enhance the theoretical foundation of the paper. Third, the experimental evaluation needs to be significantly expanded to include more recent and relevant baselines. The authors should consider including methods such as Hamiltonian Neural Networks or other approaches that explicitly model physical constraints. The experimental evaluation should also include more diverse datasets and tasks to demonstrate the generalizability of the proposed method. For example, the authors could evaluate the method on more complex physical systems, such as fluid dynamics or granular materials. Furthermore, the authors should provide more details about the experimental setup, including the specific hyperparameter settings and the training procedure. This would allow for a more thorough evaluation of the proposed method and facilitate reproducibility. Fourth, the authors should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This discussion should include specific examples of scenarios where the method might fail or perform poorly. For instance, the authors could explore the performance of the model on systems with non-constant acceleration or external forces that are not captured by the GNN backbone. It would also be beneficial to analyze the model's performance on systems with a large number of objects or long simulation times. The authors should also discuss the limitations of the method in terms of the types of physical systems that it can model, such as systems with strong non-linearities or chaotic behavior. Finally, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of SEGNO, including a comparison with the baselines. This analysis should consider the impact of the number of objects, the length of the simulation, and the complexity of the neural network architecture. The authors should also discuss the practical limitations of the method in terms of computational resources, such as the maximum simulation time that can be achieved with a given computational budget. It would be beneficial to include a discussion on the trade-offs between computational cost and accuracy, and to provide guidelines for selecting appropriate model parameters and simulation settings. Furthermore, the authors should consider including a discussion on the potential for optimizing the computational performance of the method, such as through parallelization or other techniques.
