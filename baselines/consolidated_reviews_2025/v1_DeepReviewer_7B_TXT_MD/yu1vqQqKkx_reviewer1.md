### Summary

The paper proposes a new method for black-box optimization (BBO) using LLMs. The main idea is to equip the LLM with two additional embedding layers for the inputs (molecules in this case) and the output values, and a prediction head that outputs the mean and standard deviation. The LLM is then trained on a mixture of intrinsic functions (e.g. molecular weight) and synthetic functions (e.g. functions generated by Gaussian Processes). The method is evaluated on the PMO benchmark, showing that it outperforms several other BBO methods.

### Suggestions

The paper's primary weakness lies in the limited scope of its empirical evaluation. While the PMO benchmark is a challenging one, it is crucial to assess the method's performance across a broader range of BBO tasks to establish its general applicability. The current evaluation focuses solely on molecular optimization, leaving open the question of how well the proposed approach would perform in other domains, such as optimizing the parameters of complex engineering systems or tuning hyperparameters in machine learning models. To address this, the authors should consider evaluating their method on at least one or two additional BBO benchmarks that represent different problem characteristics, such as those involving discrete or mixed-variable optimization. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to explore the performance of the method on problems with different levels of complexity and dimensionality. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and limitations and its potential for broader adoption.

Another significant limitation is the method's dependence on intrinsic functions. The current implementation relies on a set of intrinsic properties of molecules, which may not be readily available or easily computable in other domains. For example, in the domain of hyperparameter optimization for machine learning models, intrinsic functions might correspond to the model's training time or memory footprint, but these are not typically accessible as intrinsic properties of the hyperparameter configuration itself. To address this, the authors should investigate alternative ways of incorporating domain-specific knowledge into the LLM without relying on intrinsic functions. One possible approach could be to use a knowledge graph or a set of rules to guide the LLM's reasoning process. Another approach could be to use a pre-trained model that has been fine-tuned on a large corpus of domain-specific text. This would allow the LLM to learn relevant patterns and relationships without relying on intrinsic functions.

Finally, the paper's conclusions are limited by the fact that the method is only tested on a single LLM, Llama 2 7B. It is unclear whether the method's performance would generalize to other LLMs, especially those with different architectures or sizes. To address this, the authors should evaluate their method on at least one or two additional LLMs, such as a smaller model like Llama 2 7B or a larger model like Llama 2 70B. This would provide a more robust assessment of the method's generalizability and its potential for use with different LLMs. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to investigate the impact of different LLM architectures and training procedures on the method's performance. This would provide valuable insights into the method's sensitivity to the underlying LLM and its potential for further improvement.
