## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to cognitive modeling by leveraging the power of large language models (LLMs). The core idea is to fine-tune pre-trained LLMs on behavioral data from decision-making tasks, aiming to create models that can better predict human behavior than traditional cognitive models. The authors finetune the last layer of the LLMs on specific behavioral datasets, such as those related to choices from descriptions and decisions from experience, and then evaluate the resulting models' ability to predict human choices. The key empirical finding is that these fine-tuned LLMs outperform traditional cognitive models, including a domain-specific model (BEAST), in predicting human behavior on these tasks. Furthermore, the authors demonstrate that the embeddings learned by the LLMs contain information about individual differences and that these models can generalize to unseen tasks. The paper's significance lies in its potential to bridge the gap between artificial intelligence and cognitive science by demonstrating that LLMs, when adapted to behavioral data, can serve as powerful cognitive models. This approach opens up new avenues for research in both fields, offering a data-driven method for modeling complex human behaviors. The authors also highlight the potential for a domain-general model of human cognition by showing that models trained on one task can generalize to others. This work contributes to the growing body of research exploring the applicability of LLMs in cognitive science, suggesting that these models may capture fundamental aspects of human cognition that were previously unaddressed by traditional models.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The core idea of fine-tuning LLMs to become cognitive models is both innovative and intriguing. The authors have successfully demonstrated that this approach can lead to models that outperform traditional cognitive models in predicting human behavior on decision-making tasks. This is a significant achievement, as it suggests that LLMs, when adapted to behavioral data, can capture complex cognitive processes that were previously difficult to model. The empirical results, particularly the superior predictive accuracy of the fine-tuned LLMs compared to the domain-specific model (BEAST) and the random guessing model, are convincing. The authors also provide compelling evidence that the embeddings learned by the LLMs contain information about individual differences, which is a crucial aspect of cognitive modeling. The fact that the models can generalize to unseen tasks further strengthens the claim that they are capturing underlying cognitive mechanisms rather than just memorizing training data. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes the complex ideas accessible to a broader audience. The use of clear examples and well-structured sections facilitates understanding of the methodology and results. Overall, the paper presents a novel and promising approach to cognitive modeling, and the empirical results are compelling. The potential for LLMs to serve as cognitive models is a significant contribution to both the AI and cognitive science communities.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A significant limitation is the lack of a direct comparison with other LLM-based behavioral models, such as the one proposed by Shinn et al. (2023). While the authors compare their models against traditional cognitive models and a domain-specific model, the absence of a comparison with other LLM-based approaches makes it difficult to assess the relative performance and novelty of their method. This omission is particularly concerning because it leaves open the question of whether the observed improvements are due to the specific fine-tuning approach or simply a consequence of using an LLM. My analysis of the 'Experiments' section confirms that no other LLM-based models were included in the comparisons, which is a notable gap in the evaluation. Furthermore, the paper lacks a clear explanation of why fine-tuning the last layer of the LLM is effective for cognitive modeling. The authors do not provide a theoretical justification for this specific approach, nor do they explore the representational changes that occur in the LLM's embedding space after fine-tuning. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the underlying mechanisms that enable the LLM to capture human cognitive processes. The 'Method' section describes the process of fine-tuning but does not delve into the reasons for its effectiveness, and the 'Experiments' section focuses solely on predictive accuracy without any analysis of the learned representations. This absence of theoretical grounding is a significant weakness, as it leaves the reader wondering whether the improved performance is due to overfitting to the specific training data or whether the fine-tuning process is truly capturing meaningful cognitive representations. Another major weakness is the lack of a thorough discussion on the limitations of the proposed approach. The authors do not address the potential for overfitting, the generalizability of the model to different tasks and datasets, or the computational cost of fine-tuning large language models. The 'Method' section describes the fine-tuning process but does not explicitly discuss these limitations, and the 'Discussion' section briefly mentions generalization but does not delve into the limitations or computational cost. The absence of a discussion on potential biases in the training data is also a significant oversight. This lack of a comprehensive discussion of limitations makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method and its potential for real-world applications. The paper also does not compare the proposed approach with other existing methods for cognitive modeling, such as Gaussian processes or recurrent neural networks. The 'Experiments' section includes comparisons with a random guessing model, an LLM without fine-tuning, and a domain-specific model (BEAST), but it does not include comparisons with other machine learning models commonly used for cognitive modeling. This omission makes it difficult to establish the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach and to contextualize its performance within the broader landscape of cognitive modeling techniques. Finally, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of why fine-tuning the last layer of the LLM is effective for cognitive modeling. The authors do not explore the feature importance analysis or ablation studies to identify the key components of the embedding that contribute to the model's predictive power. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand which parts of the LLM's representation are most relevant for modeling human cognition. The paper also does not discuss the potential for bias in the training data and how this bias might affect the predictions of the model. This is a crucial aspect of cognitive modeling, as it is essential to ensure that the model is not simply learning spurious correlations or biases from the training data. In summary, the lack of comparison with other LLM-based models, the absence of a theoretical justification for fine-tuning the last layer, the lack of a thorough discussion of limitations, the omission of comparisons with other cognitive modeling methods, and the lack of analysis of feature importance and bias are significant weaknesses that need to be addressed.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should include a more thorough comparison with existing LLM-based behavioral models, such as the model proposed by Shinn et al. (2023). This comparison should not only focus on overall performance but also on the interpretability of the models. It would be beneficial to analyze the internal representations of the LLMs to understand how they encode decision-making strategies. For example, the authors could examine the activation patterns of different layers in the LLM to see which parts of the network are most relevant for decision-making. This analysis could provide insights into the cognitive mechanisms that the LLM is learning, which would strengthen the paper's claims about the model's cognitive relevance. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different fine-tuning strategies, such as using different learning rates or regularization techniques. This would help to identify the most effective fine-tuning approach and provide a better understanding of the model's sensitivity to different training parameters. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the theoretical underpinnings of their approach. This should include a discussion of how the architecture of LLMs relates to known cognitive processes. For example, they could discuss how the sequential nature of LLMs might be analogous to the processing of information in working memory or how the attention mechanisms in LLMs might relate to attention mechanisms in the brain. A more thorough discussion of these connections would strengthen the paper's claims about the model's cognitive relevance. The authors should also explore the representational changes that occur in the LLM's embedding space after fine-tuning. Techniques such as representational similarity analysis (RSA) could be used to compare the representations learned by the fine-tuned LLM with those of traditional cognitive models. This would provide a more concrete understanding of how the LLM's internal representations align with known cognitive processes. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the specific features or patterns in the LLM's embeddings that are most predictive of human behavior. This could involve techniques like feature importance analysis or ablation studies to identify the key components of the embedding that contribute to the model's predictive power. Understanding these aspects would strengthen the claims of the paper and provide a more solid foundation for future research. Third, the authors should conduct a more rigorous analysis of the model's performance on different subsets of the data. For example, they could examine the model's performance on tasks with varying levels of difficulty or on datasets with different demographic characteristics. This would help to identify potential biases or limitations of the model. Additionally, the authors should explore the model's sensitivity to different hyperparameters and training procedures. This would provide a better understanding of the robustness of the model and its potential for generalization. The authors should also consider the computational cost of fine-tuning large language models and discuss the scalability of their approach to larger datasets and more complex tasks. This would be important for assessing the practical applicability of the proposed method. Fourth, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with existing cognitive modeling approaches. This should not only include traditional cognitive models but also other machine learning-based models that have been applied to similar tasks. The authors should clearly articulate the advantages and disadvantages of their approach compared to these alternatives. For example, they could compare their model with a simple neural network trained on the same data or with a symbolic model of decision-making. This would help to establish the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. Finally, the authors should address the potential for bias in the training data and how this bias might affect the predictions of the model. This could involve analyzing the training data for biases and using techniques to mitigate bias in the training data and improving the fairness of the model. The authors should also discuss the limitations of their approach in terms of scalability and the potential for applying it to other cognitive domains. This would help to contextualize their findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of their approach. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make a more compelling case for the use of LLMs as cognitive models.
