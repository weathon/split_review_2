## Summary

This paper presents a theoretical framework for understanding and utilizing unpaired multimodal data through the lens of contrastive learning. The authors introduce a Bayesian perspective to interpret the learned representations, demonstrating that the probability of unpaired modalities can be inferred by comparing their representations. This theoretical insight is supported by a Monte Carlo approximation method, which provides a practical way to estimate these probabilities. The core of the paper lies in its theoretical analysis, which is supported by experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets, including language-conditioned reinforcement learning tasks. The authors establish a connection between the learned representations and the underlying data distributions, showing that under certain assumptions, particularly when the marginal distribution of the representations follows a uniform distribution on the hypersphere, the probability of unpaired modalities can be accurately estimated. The paper's main contribution is the theoretical foundation for understanding and utilizing unpaired multimodal data, which is a significant step towards bridging the gap between theory and practice in multimodal learning. The proposed Monte Carlo approximation method offers a practical approach to apply these insights, enabling the comparison of representations from unpaired modalities. The experiments, while limited in scope, effectively validate the theoretical claims, demonstrating the practical applicability of the proposed methods. The paper also highlights the limitations of direct comparison approaches and emphasizes the importance of considering the underlying data distributions when dealing with unpaired multimodal data. Overall, this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of multimodal learning by providing a theoretical foundation for understanding and utilizing unpaired data, and by proposing a practical method for estimating the similarity between such data.

## Weaknesses

Despite its strengths, the paper has several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One of the most significant limitations is the lack of a detailed discussion on the practical implications of the assumptions made, particularly Assumption 1, which posits conditional independence given the intermediate modality. While the authors introduce this assumption as a key part of their theoretical framework, they do not thoroughly examine its real-world validity or potential impact on the results.
