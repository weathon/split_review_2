## Summary

This paper introduces a method to increase the energy-latency cost of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) by crafting imperceptible perturbations to input images. The core idea is to generate verbose images that cause VLMs to produce longer sequences, thereby increasing both the computational cost and the latency of the inference process. The authors propose three loss functions to optimize these perturbations: a delayed Early End-of-Sequence (EOS) loss to encourage the model to generate longer sequences before reaching the end-of-sequence token; an uncertainty loss to increase the rank of the hidden states, aiming to enhance the diversity of the generated tokens; and a token diversity loss to further promote the generation of varied tokens. These loss functions are optimized using Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) to find perturbations that maximize the length of the generated sequences while remaining imperceptible to the human eye. The authors evaluate their method on four VLMs—BLIP, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, and MiniGPT-4—across two datasets, MS-COCO and ImageNet, demonstrating that the verbose images can increase the length of generated sequences by 7.87x and 8.56x, respectively, compared to the original images. The paper's primary motivation stems from the need to address the increasing energy and latency costs associated with VLMs, which are often overlooked in existing methods. However, the paper's approach involves a trade-off, as the adversarial examples crafted to increase energy cost also tend to degrade the performance of the VLMs on clean inputs. The authors aim to increase the cost of using VLMs, which is different from making them more robust in a security sense. The paper's contribution lies in its novel approach to manipulating VLMs through adversarial examples to alter their behavior, but the lack of a clear justification for this approach and the limited evaluation raise concerns about its practical relevance and generalizability.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I have identified several significant weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a clear and compelling motivation for using adversarial examples to increase the energy-latency cost of VLMs. The authors aim to induce high energy-latency cost for VLMs, but the proposed method involves crafting adversarial examples, which, by definition, compromises the performance of the model on clean inputs. This raises a fundamental question: if the goal is to increase the cost of using VLMs, why not simply modify the inference engine itself or explore other methods that do not degrade performance on clean inputs? The paper does not adequately address why optimizing for adversarial robustness, which inherently involves degrading performance on clean inputs, is a suitable approach for increasing inference cost, especially when the goal is to make VLMs more expensive to use rather than to make them more robust in a security sense. This lack of a clear justification undermines the practical relevance of the proposed method.
