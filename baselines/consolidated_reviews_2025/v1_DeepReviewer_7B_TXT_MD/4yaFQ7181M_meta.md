## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to data-driven spatio-temporal simulations of physical systems, addressing the challenge of predicting system behavior at arbitrary locations and times using limited data. The core idea revolves around a 'double observation problem' formulation, where the system is modeled as a pair of interconnected dynamical systems. The first system operates in discrete time and aims to compute a sequence of latent anchor states, which serve as a compressed representation of the system's evolution. The second system, a continuous-time dynamical model, then estimates the dense physical state at arbitrary locations and times using these anchor states as anchors. This two-step approach is designed to leverage the strengths of both discrete and continuous modeling paradigms. The authors propose a specific implementation of this framework, utilizing a discrete dynamical model implemented via a Graph Neural Network (GNN) to compute the latent anchor states, and a continuous-time dynamical model implemented using a state observer, which employs a Transformer and a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) to estimate the dense solution. The method is evaluated on three benchmark datasets, demonstrating superior performance compared to three baseline methods, including IN-Domain, MAgNet, and DINo. The experimental results show that the proposed method achieves lower mean squared error (MSE) in both spatial and temporal predictions. The authors also conduct ablation studies to analyze the impact of different components of their method, such as the time continuity loss. The paper's main contribution lies in the novel formulation of the double observation problem and the specific implementation using GNNs and Transformers. The approach offers a way to handle sparse and irregularly sampled data, and the experimental results suggest that it can achieve higher accuracy than existing methods. However, the paper lacks some crucial details regarding the implementation and the analysis of the results, which limits the reproducibility and the overall impact of the work. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, but it could benefit from a more detailed explanation of the proposed method, a more comprehensive comparison with existing methods, and a more in-depth analysis of the experimental results. The authors also need to address some of the identified weaknesses to strengthen the paper's claims and contributions.
