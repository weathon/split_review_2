## Summary

This paper introduces MaskTAS, a novel self-supervised neural architecture search (NAS) method that leverages masked image modeling (MIM) to explore the design space of vision transformers. The core idea behind MaskTAS is to utilize a teacher-student architecture, where a pre-trained teacher model, trained using MIM, guides the training of a student network through knowledge distillation. This approach aims to circumvent the need for extensive labeled data typically required in supervised NAS. The method begins with a supernet, which is a large network containing multiple sub-nets with varying architectures. These sub-nets are trained in a one-shot manner, where the teacher model provides supervision through feature matching and pixel reconstruction. The student network is then trained to mimic the teacher's behavior, effectively learning a good architecture without explicit labels. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of MaskTAS on the ImageNet dataset, achieving state-of-the-art performance among self-supervised methods. The method's strength lies in its ability to automatically discover high-performing architectures for vision transformers without relying on human expertise or labeled data. The authors also show that the searched architectures can generalize well to other image classification datasets. The paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method, including comparisons with existing NAS techniques and an analysis of the impact of different masking ratios. The results highlight the potential of MaskTAS as a powerful tool for automating the design of neural networks for computer vision tasks. The authors also explore the transferability of the searched architectures to other image classification datasets, demonstrating the robustness of the method. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution to the field of NAS by introducing a self-supervised approach that leverages the power of masked image modeling, paving the way for more efficient and automated design of vision models.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of the proposed method, I have identified two primary weaknesses that limit the scope of the current evaluation. First, the paper's evaluation is narrowly focused on image classification tasks. While the authors demonstrate state-of-the-art performance on the ImageNet dataset, the method's applicability to other vision tasks remains unclear. The paper lacks any evaluation on tasks such as object detection or semantic segmentation, which are crucial for many real-world applications. The experimental setup consistently uses datasets and metrics relevant to image classification, such as top-1 and top-5 accuracy on ImageNet. There is no mention of object detection datasets like COCO or semantic segmentation datasets like ADE20K, nor are there any results reported for these tasks. This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings and raises questions about the method's performance on tasks that require different architectural considerations, such as bounding box regression for object detection or pixel-level predictions for semantic segmentation. This is a significant limitation, as it is unclear whether the architectures discovered by MaskTAS would be suitable for tasks beyond image classification. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the experimental section clearly lacks any evaluation on these other tasks. Second, the paper's evaluation is also limited to vision transformer architectures. The authors explicitly state that they experiment with the standard ViT supernet architecture in Tiny, Small, and Base settings. There is no mention of evaluating the method on other architectures, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or MLP-Mixers. This is a critical oversight, as these architectures have different inductive biases and are widely used in various applications. The lack of evaluation on CNNs, which are known for their ability to capture local features, and MLP-Mixers, which are departure from the traditional transformer architecture, leaves a significant gap in understanding the method's versatility. It is unclear whether the proposed method can discover architectures that are effective for tasks where local feature extraction or different inductive biases are important. This limitation restricts the applicability of the method to a specific subset of vision architectures. My confidence in this assessment is also high, as the experimental details clearly indicate the exclusive use of ViT architectures. These two weaknesses, the limited task scope and the limited architecture scope, significantly restrict the impact of the paper and raise questions about the generalizability of the proposed method.
