## Summary

This paper introduces Context Influence Decoding (CID), a novel method designed to amplify the influence of context on the outputs of large language models (LLMs) while simultaneously mitigating the generation of hallucinations. The authors begin by defining context influence, a measure quantifying how much a given context affects the probability distribution of the model's output. They then propose CID, which leverages a tunable parameter, λ, to adjust the model's output probabilities by incorporating the defined context influence. This approach is motivated by the observation that amplifying the context can reduce hallucination, but it also increases the model's reliance on the provided context, potentially diminishing its ability to generate novel or accurate information. To address this trade-off, the authors provide a theoretical analysis, demonstrating that amplifying the context is linked to hallucination and that CID can effectively reduce context influence while lowering privacy leakage. The core idea is that by manipulating the output probabilities based on context influence, CID can control the extent to which the model relies on the context, thereby balancing the need for accurate information with the risk of generating hallucinations. The empirical evaluation of CID is conducted on summarization tasks using the Llama-3 and GPT-Neo models. The results indicate that CID can improve the ROUGE-L scores, which measure the quality of the generated summaries, while also reducing hallucination, as measured by factuality metrics. The authors also explore the impact of various factors, such as model capacity, context size, response length, and token n-gram influence, on the effectiveness of CID. The findings suggest that CID can be a promising approach for controlling context influence in LLMs, offering a way to mitigate hallucinations without sacrificing the quality of the generated text. The paper's significance lies in its principled approach to controlling context influence, its theoretical grounding, and its empirical validation on summarization tasks. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in the scope of its empirical evaluation and the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of CID. Despite these limitations, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field of LLM interpretability and provides a promising direction for future research.
