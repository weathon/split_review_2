## Summary

This paper introduces a novel active learning framework for image segmentation, specifically designed to reduce the annotation burden by employing binary user queries. Instead of requiring users to annotate entire image regions or provide pixel-level labels, the proposed method asks users to indicate the presence or absence of specific semantic classes within given image regions. This approach aims to streamline the annotation process, making it more efficient and less cognitively demanding for human annotators. The core of the method lies in formulating the image and class selection as a constrained optimization problem, solved using linear programming relaxation. The authors argue that this formulation identifies a batch of image-class pairs that are both informative and diverse, maximizing the information gained from each human query. The method leverages pre-trained deep neural networks, specifically ResNet101, for feature extraction and uncertainty estimation. The optimization process considers both the confidence of the deep model in predicting the presence of a class in an image and the redundancy between images. The authors evaluate their method on three benchmark datasets—Pixel-Level, Cityscapes, and PASCAL VOC—and demonstrate that it achieves comparable or better performance in terms of mean Intersection over Union (mIoU) while requiring less annotation time. The experimental results show that the proposed binary query approach can effectively reduce the amount of human annotation effort needed for image segmentation tasks. The authors compare their method against several baselines, including pixel-level, region-level, and binary-level annotation methods, as well as coreset-based active learning techniques. The results indicate that the proposed method outperforms these baselines in terms of both mIoU and annotation time, suggesting its potential for practical applications in image segmentation. The paper's main contribution is the introduction of a binary query framework for active learning in image segmentation, which is a practical and efficient approach to reducing human annotation effort. The authors provide a clear motivation for their approach and present a well-defined methodology. The experimental results support the effectiveness of the proposed method, demonstrating its potential for real-world applications. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in the areas of scalability, hyperparameter sensitivity, and comparison with state-of-the-art methods, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.
