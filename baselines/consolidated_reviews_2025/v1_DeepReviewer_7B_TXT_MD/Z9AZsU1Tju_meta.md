## Summary

This paper introduces the Information-Theoretic Hierarchical Perception (ITHP) model, a novel approach to multimodal learning that leverages the concept of information bottleneck to construct compact latent states for different modalities. The model is designed to distill relevant information from multiple modalities in a sequential manner, mimicking a hierarchical information processing approach observed in human cognition and neural systems. The ITHP model is evaluated on three multimodal datasets: MUStARD for sarcasm detection, MOSI for sentiment analysis, and MOSEI for emotion recognition. The results demonstrate that ITHP achieves competitive performance compared to existing multimodal fusion methods, and in some cases, even surpasses human-level benchmarks. The paper's core contributions include a novel application of the information bottleneck principle to multimodal fusion, a hierarchical architecture that processes modalities sequentially, and a comprehensive empirical evaluation on multiple benchmark datasets. However, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with existing multimodal fusion techniques, a thorough analysis of the model's computational complexity and scalability, and a comprehensive ablation study to understand the contribution of each component. Additionally, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the ITHP model or potential failure cases, which are crucial for a balanced assessment of its capabilities and robustness. Despite these limitations, the paper provides a promising direction for multimodal learning by integrating information-theoretic principles with a hierarchical perception framework.

## Strengths

The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with a clear presentation of the theoretical foundations and experimental results. The authors provide a thorough explanation of the Information-Theoretic Hierarchical Perception (ITHP) model, including the formulation of optimization problems and the derivation of loss functions. This clarity is commendable and makes the paper accessible to a broad audience. The use of the information bottleneck principle to construct compact latent states for different modalities is a novel and innovative approach that aligns well with the goal of multimodal fusion. The hierarchical structure of the model, where a primary modality is used to guide the fusion of other modalities in a sequential manner, is inspired by neural information processing and mimics the hierarchical information processing approach observed in human cognition. This architectural design is a significant contribution to the field of multimodal learning. The empirical evaluation of the ITHP model is conducted on multiple benchmark datasets, including MUStARD, MOSI, and MOSEI, covering a range of multimodal tasks such as sarcasm detection, sentiment analysis, and emotion recognition. The model demonstrates competitive performance compared to existing multimodal fusion methods and, in some cases, even surpasses human-level benchmarks. The results are well-presented, and the paper includes a detailed analysis of the performance metrics, which provides a solid foundation for the claims made. The authors also explore the impact of Lagrange multipliers on the model's performance, which is a valuable addition to the empirical analysis. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel approach to multimodal fusion, the clarity of its presentation, and the comprehensive empirical evaluation that demonstrates the model's effectiveness across multiple tasks and datasets.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and areas for improvement are evident. One of the most significant concerns is the lack of a detailed comparison with existing multimodal fusion techniques. While the paper mentions several related works and includes a list of baselines in the experimental setup, it does not provide a comprehensive qualitative analysis of how the ITHP model differs from and improves upon these approaches. For instance, the paper could benefit from a discussion of the specific mechanisms used by attention-based fusion methods, graph neural networks for multimodal fusion, and other information-theoretic measures, and how these mechanisms contrast with the hierarchical latent state construction and variational information bottleneck approach proposed in ITHP. This lack of depth in the comparison makes it difficult to fully appreciate the unique contributions of the ITHP model. Another critical weakness is the absence of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of the ITHP model. Given the hierarchical structure and multiple latent states, the model's computational cost and potential bottlenecks are not addressed. The paper does not provide information on training time, inference time, or resource usage, which are essential for practical applications. A systematic exploration of how the model's performance and computational cost scale with the number of modalities and the size of the input data would greatly enhance the paper's practical value. The interpretability of the learned latent states is another area that requires further investigation. While the authors claim that these states capture meaningful information, the paper lacks concrete examples or methods to validate this claim. Visualizations or other techniques to demonstrate the interpretability of the latent states would provide a more rigorous assessment of their utility. The paper also lacks a comprehensive ablation study to analyze the contribution of each component of the ITHP model. For example, the impact of the hierarchical structure, the information bottleneck principle, and different loss functions could be investigated separately to better understand their individual contributions to the overall performance. The current ablation study only explores the impact of Lagrange multipliers, which is insufficient to fully understand the model's behavior. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the ITHP model or potential failure cases. This is a crucial aspect that should be addressed to provide a balanced and realistic assessment of the model's capabilities. The authors should analyze scenarios where the model might struggle, such as with noisy or ambiguous data, or with modalities that are significantly different from the training data. The paper's claim that the ITHP model outperforms human-level benchmarks in sarcasm detection on the MUStARD dataset is not well-supported. The comparison is made against other machine learning models, not actual human performance data, and the paper does not provide any justification or evidence for the existence of such benchmarks in the context of the MUStARD dataset. This claim is misleading and requires further clarification or removal. The paper also lacks a clear explanation of the experimental setup and the choice of hyperparameters. The authors should provide more details on how the hyperparameters were selected and how they affect the performance of the model. For example, the Lagrange multipliers used in the information bottleneck are crucial for the model's performance, and a systematic exploration of how varying these parameters affects the model's performance would be valuable. The sensitivity of the model to different hyperparameter settings should also be investigated to understand the robustness of the proposed method. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and potential avenues for future research. This discussion should include the computational complexity of the ITHP model, its sensitivity to hyperparameter settings, and the potential for the model to overfit to the training data. The authors should also suggest potential directions for future research, such as exploring different architectures for the latent state construction or investigating the use of other information-theoretic measures. These weaknesses, while not undermining the paper's overall contribution, significantly impact the paper's clarity, depth of analysis, and practical applicability.

## Suggestions

To enhance the paper, I recommend a more detailed comparison with existing multimodal fusion techniques. The authors should not only compare the performance metrics but also delve into the architectural differences and the underlying mechanisms of how each method processes multimodal data. For example, a discussion of how attention mechanisms in transformer-based models focus on dynamically weighting the importance of different modalities, while ITHP's hierarchical latent state construction and variational information bottleneck approach differ in its focus on information retention and compression, would be highly beneficial. This analysis should go beyond simple performance comparisons and explore the specific strengths and weaknesses of each method in different scenarios, such as varying levels of modality correlation or different types of multimodal data. A quantitative comparison, using metrics relevant to each task (e.g., accuracy for classification, F1-score for sentiment analysis, MAE for regression), would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach. To address the computational complexity and scalability of the ITHP model, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the model's computational cost and how it scales with the number of modalities and the size of the input data. This analysis should consider the number of parameters, the computational cost of each layer, and the overall training and inference time. It would be beneficial to provide a comparison of the computational complexity of the ITHP model with other state-of-the-art multimodal fusion methods, including those that do not rely on information bottleneck principles. The authors should also discuss potential bottlenecks in the model that could limit its scalability and explore possible solutions, such as model parallelism or distributed training. To improve the interpretability of the learned latent states, the authors should provide concrete examples of how these states relate to the input modalities. This could involve visualizations or other methods to validate the claim that the latent states capture meaningful information. For instance, the authors could use dimensionality reduction techniques like t-SNE or PCA to visualize the latent states and correlate these visualizations with the input data. The authors should also explore methods to quantify the information content of each latent state, such as mutual information or other information-theoretic measures. This would provide a more rigorous assessment of the interpretability of the model and help to validate the claim that the latent states capture meaningful information. A comprehensive ablation study is necessary to understand the contribution of each component of the ITHP model. This study should systematically remove or modify components, such as the hierarchical structure, the information bottleneck principle, and specific loss terms, and evaluate the impact on the model's performance. For example, the authors could compare the performance of the full ITHP model with a version that uses a single latent state, or a version that does not use the information bottleneck principle. This would help to isolate the contribution of each component and provide a more detailed understanding of the model's behavior. The ablation study should also include an analysis of the sensitivity of the model to different hyperparameter settings, such as the Lagrange multipliers, and provide guidelines for selecting optimal values. To address the limitations of the ITHP model and potential failure cases, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of scenarios where the model might struggle. This could involve evaluating the model's performance on datasets with high levels of noise or with modalities that have different characteristics than those used in the training data. The authors should also discuss the potential for the model to overfit to the training data and propose strategies to mitigate this risk, such as using model regularization or cross-validation. Finally, the paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method and potential avenues for future research. This discussion should analyze the computational complexity of the ITHP model and how it scales with the number of modalities and the size of the input data. The authors should also discuss the potential challenges of applying the proposed method to real-world scenarios, such as noisy or incomplete data. Furthermore, the authors should suggest potential directions for future research, such as exploring different architectures for the latent state construction or investigating the use of other information-theoretic measures. This would help to position the proposed method within the broader context of multimodal learning and highlight its potential for future development.
