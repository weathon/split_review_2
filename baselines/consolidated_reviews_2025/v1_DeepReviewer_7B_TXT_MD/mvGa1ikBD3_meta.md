## Summary

This paper introduces a novel Graph Neural Network (GNN) architecture designed to model the behavior of nonlinear and anisotropic materials, a significant advancement over existing methods that primarily focus on isotropic materials. The core innovation lies in a direction encoding scheme that preserves directional information during message passing, a critical aspect for accurately capturing anisotropic material responses. This is achieved by decomposing edge features into components along three orthogonal axes, effectively encoding directional information. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through a series of experiments, including simulations of deformable object deformation under various loading conditions. These experiments show that the proposed method outperforms the state-of-the-art MeshGraphNet in capturing material anisotropy, exhibiting superior convergence speed and lower computational costs. The paper presents a clear methodology, detailing the GNN architecture, the direction encoding scheme, and the training process. The empirical findings, while promising, are primarily limited to relatively simple geometries and boundary conditions, raising questions about the method's generalizability to more complex scenarios. The authors validate their approach through a series of experiments, including convergence analysis, anisotropic elasticity, volume preservation, tip displacement, and generalization to different geometries. The results consistently show that the proposed method achieves better accuracy and efficiency compared to MeshGraphNet, particularly in capturing the complex deformation patterns of anisotropic materials. The paper's significance lies in its ability to address a key limitation in the field of material simulation, offering a more accurate and efficient way to model complex material behaviors. However, the paper also has several limitations, including a lack of detailed computational cost analysis, limited exploration of complex geometries and boundary conditions, and a narrow comparison with only one baseline method. These limitations, which I will discuss in detail, need to be addressed to fully realize the potential of the proposed method. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of material simulation, but further research is needed to address the identified weaknesses and enhance the method's robustness and applicability.

## Strengths

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative direction encoding scheme, which effectively addresses the challenge of modeling anisotropic materials within a GNN framework. The authors have successfully identified a critical limitation in existing GNN architectures, which rely on spatial averaging of edge features and thus discard directional information during message passing. This discard of directional information, as the authors demonstrate, prevents these architectures from accurately modeling anisotropic materials. The proposed direction encoding scheme, which decomposes edge features into components along three orthogonal axes, provides a simple yet effective way to preserve directional information. This approach is not only intuitive but also computationally efficient, making it a practical solution for real-world applications. The paper's methodology is clearly presented, making it easy to follow the proposed approach and understand the technical contributions. The authors provide a detailed description of the GNN architecture, the direction encoding scheme, and the training process, which enhances the reproducibility of their work. Furthermore, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors conduct a series of experiments, including convergence analysis, anisotropic elasticity, volume preservation, tip displacement, and generalization to different geometries, which demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. The results consistently show that the proposed method outperforms MeshGraphNet in capturing material anisotropy, exhibiting superior convergence speed and lower computational costs. The paper's focus on anisotropic materials is also a significant strength, as this is an area that has been relatively unexplored in the field of GNN-based material simulation. By addressing this gap, the authors have made a valuable contribution to the field, offering a more accurate and efficient way to model complex material behaviors. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methodology and experimental setup. The authors have successfully combined theoretical insights with practical implementation, resulting in a method that is both innovative and effective. The overall presentation of the paper is clear and concise, making it accessible to a broad audience of researchers and practitioners.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a novel and promising approach for modeling anisotropic materials using GNNs, several weaknesses limit its overall impact and generalizability. One significant limitation is the restricted scope of the experimental evaluation. While the authors demonstrate the method's effectiveness on a few simple geometries, such as beams and bars, the systematic exploration of more complex scenarios is lacking. Specifically, the paper does not thoroughly investigate the performance of the method on structures with varying topologies or boundary conditions, which are common in real-world applications. The 'Sample Generation' section describes a uniform sampling process for generating training data, which might not fully represent the diversity of real-world scenarios. While the 'Generalization' section shows some complex geometries (T-shape, Y-shape), the systematic exploration of varying topologies and boundary conditions is not explicitly detailed or quantified. This lack of systematic evaluation raises concerns about the method's robustness and applicability to more complex and realistic scenarios. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed GNN architecture. While the 'Training' section mentions that training takes around 5 days and inference takes 9ms for a mesh of 100 elements, there is no further breakdown of the time and memory requirements for different stages of the training or inference process. A comparison of the computational cost with existing methods, such as finite element methods or other GNN-based approaches, is also missing. This absence of a detailed computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical suitability of the method for large-scale simulations. The paper's experimental evaluation is also limited by the narrow comparison with only one baseline method, MeshGraphNet. While MeshGraphNet is a relevant baseline, there are other state-of-the-art GNN architectures and simulation techniques that could have been used for comparison. The lack of comparison with other relevant baselines makes it difficult to fully demonstrate the advantages of the proposed method over existing approaches. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the 'Conclusion' section mentions future work, it does not explicitly discuss the constraints of the method or the scenarios where it might not be suitable. The absence of a dedicated limitations section and a detailed discussion of the method's shortcomings is a significant oversight. Finally, the paper does not explore the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter choices, such as the number of message-passing steps or the architecture of the neural networks used for encoding and decoding. A detailed analysis of these aspects would provide a more complete understanding of the method's capabilities and limitations. The paper also does not discuss the potential for optimizing the method to reduce its computational cost, such as by using more efficient message-passing algorithms or by parallelizing the computation. These weaknesses, which are based on my direct examination of the paper, significantly limit the overall impact and generalizability of the proposed method. The lack of systematic evaluation, limited computational analysis, narrow comparison, and absence of a detailed discussion of limitations are all substantial concerns that need to be addressed to fully realize the potential of this work.

By providing a more comprehensive analysis of the method's strengths and limitations, the authors can significantly enhance the paper's impact and contribute to the advancement of the field.
