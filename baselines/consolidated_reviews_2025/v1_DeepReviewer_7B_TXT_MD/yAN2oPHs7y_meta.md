## Summary

This paper introduces a novel method for learning interpretable rule lists from data in a differentiable manner. The core contribution lies in its ability to learn both the discretization of continuous features and the conjunction of rules without relying on pre-discretization or restrictive feature representations. This is achieved through a combination of techniques, including the use of soft predicates based on sigmoid functions, a differentiable logical conjunction function, and temperature annealing to ensure the convergence of the rule list structure. The method, termed NeuroRuleList, employs a Gumbel-Softmax reparameterization to enable end-to-end training via gradient descent. The authors evaluate their approach on a variety of real-world and synthetic datasets, demonstrating superior performance compared to existing methods in terms of accuracy, rule list length, and sample complexity. Specifically, the experiments show that NeuroRuleList achieves competitive or superior performance on several benchmark datasets, including those from healthcare, finance, and social science domains. The method's ability to handle both continuous and categorical features is a notable advantage, making it applicable to a wide range of datasets. The authors also provide an ablation study to analyze the impact of different temperature annealing schedules on the final performance. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the proposed approach accessible to a broad audience. The differentiability of the method is presented as a key strength, enabling end-to-end training and optimization of the entire rule list structure. The authors also emphasize the interpretability of the learned rule lists, which is a critical aspect for many real-world applications. The experimental results, while promising, are primarily focused on overall performance metrics, and a more detailed analysis of the method's performance on different dataset characteristics is needed. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of the computational complexity and scalability of the proposed method, which is an important consideration for practical applications. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of interpretable machine learning, offering a novel and effective approach for learning rule lists from data.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison against other state-of-the-art methods, particularly those that are also differentiable or use similar techniques. While the authors compare their method against several baselines, including optimal rule lists (CORELS, SBRL), scalable Bayesian rule lists (MDLRL), and other neuro-symbolic methods (RRN, DRNet, xGBoost), they do not include comparisons against methods that specifically learn rule lists using differentiable techniques or those that use neural rule vector representations. For example, the paper does not compare against methods like "Learning Optimal Interpretable Decision Trees under Fairness Constraints" (AAAI 2023), "Learning Interpretable Classifiers using Combinatorial Bayesian Optimization" (AAAI 2019), "Learning Interpretable and Generalizable Rule Lists for Multiclass and Regression Problems" (AAAI 2022), "Learning Rule-Based Models with Neural Rule Vector Representation" (NeurIPS 2021), and "Learning Interpretable Explanations from Neural Networks by Searching for Shallow DNFs" (ICLR 2023). This omission is significant because it limits the ability to contextualize the contribution of the proposed method and highlight its specific advantages. The absence of these comparisons makes it difficult to assess the true novelty and effectiveness of the proposed method compared to other approaches that also leverage differentiable techniques for rule learning. This is a high-confidence issue, as the 'Experiments' section clearly lists the baselines used, and the suggested papers are not among them. Second, while the paper describes the use of the reciprocal sigmoid function for approximating thresholds, it does not provide a detailed explanation of the algorithm used to determine these exact thresholds. The paper states that the method uses a soft binning function as introduced by Yang et al. (2018) and that the reciprocal sigmoid function is used to approximate thresholds. However, it does not explain how the exact thresholds are determined or how the temperature parameter influences this process. This lack of detail makes it difficult to fully understand the mechanism for learning the discretization of continuous features. This is a medium-confidence issue, as the paper describes the mechanism for approximating thresholds using the sigmoid function and temperature, but doesn't detail an explicit algorithm for determining exact thresholds. Third, the paper does not discuss the potential limitations of using a differentiable approach, such as the possibility of converging to local optima or the sensitivity to initialization. While the paper focuses on the benefits of differentiability for interpretability and optimization, it does not address the potential drawbacks. These are valid concerns for differentiable methods, and their absence is a significant oversight. This is a high-confidence issue, as the paper highlights the advantages of differentiability but lacks a discussion of potential limitations. Fourth, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the method's performance on different types of datasets, such as those with high dimensionality, noisy data, or complex relationships between features. While the paper uses a variety of datasets, the analysis is primarily focused on overall performance metrics like F1 score and rule list length. There isn't a detailed breakdown based on specific dataset characteristics like dimensionality or complexity. This is a high-confidence issue, as the experimental analysis focuses on overall metrics rather than a detailed breakdown based on dataset characteristics. Fifth, the paper does not provide a rigorous theoretical analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. While the paper includes an empirical runtime measurement for sample complexity, it doesn't offer a formal analysis of how the algorithm scales with the number of features, rules, or dataset size. This is a crucial aspect for assessing the practical applicability of the method to large-scale datasets. This is a high-confidence issue, as the paper lacks a theoretical analysis of computational complexity in terms of the number of features, rules, and dataset size. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed qualitative analysis of the interpretability of the learned rules, especially in complex scenarios. While the paper includes examples of rule lists, a more in-depth discussion of how to interpret complex rule lists and how they provide insights into the data would be beneficial. This is a medium-confidence issue, as the paper includes examples of rule lists, but a deeper discussion of interpretability in complex scenarios is lacking.
