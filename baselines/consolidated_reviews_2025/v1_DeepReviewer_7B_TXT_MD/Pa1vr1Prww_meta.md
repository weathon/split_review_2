## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to understanding in-context learning (ICL) in large language models (LLMs) by adapting sparse autoencoders (SAEs) and the Sparse Feature Circuits (SFC) methodology. The authors' core contribution lies in their ability to decompose task vectors into sparse representations, revealing interpretable task-specific features within the model's activations. This is achieved through the introduction of the Task Vector Cleaning (TVC) algorithm, which enhances the decomposition process by disentangling task vectors into sparse features. The authors then apply the SFC methodology, modified to analyze ICL, to identify task-detecting and task-execution features. These features are crucial for enabling zero-shot task performance, demonstrating the model's ability to generalize to unseen tasks without explicit fine-tuning. The study focuses on the Gemma-1 2B model, a 2-billion parameter transformer, and applies their methods to this model, demonstrating the effectiveness of SAEs in uncovering interpretable features within the model's activations. The findings suggest that SAEs can be a valuable tool for enhancing interpretability research in LLMs, and the identified task-detecting features are particularly noteworthy as they enable zero-shot task performance. The paper's methodology involves training SAEs on task vectors derived from ICL prompts and then using the SFC methodology to identify the features that are most relevant to task detection and execution. The authors validate their approach through steering experiments, which show that manipulating the identified task-execution features has a significant impact on task performance. Overall, this work represents a significant step towards demystifying the inner workings of ICL in LLMs, providing a new perspective on how models generalize from in-context examples. The paper's focus on the Gemma-1 2B model, while allowing for detailed analysis, also raises questions about the generalizability of the findings to larger and more complex models. The authors acknowledge this limitation and suggest that future work should explore the scalability of their approach to larger models and different architectures. Despite this limitation, the paper's findings are significant and provide a valuable contribution to the field of mechanistic interpretability.

This adaptation allows for a more nuanced analysis of the feature interactions within ICL, providing insights into the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon.
