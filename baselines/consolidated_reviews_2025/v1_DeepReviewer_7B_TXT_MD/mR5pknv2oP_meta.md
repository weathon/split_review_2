## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach for enabling large language models (LLMs) to acquire new skills through self-learning, specifically focusing on enhancing their ability to perform addition. The core idea revolves around iteratively training the model using a two-stage process: supervised fine-tuning followed by self-training. The self-training phase is particularly interesting, as it leverages the model's own generated data to improve its arithmetic reasoning capabilities. A key innovation is the use of chain-of-thought reasoning as a policy improvement operator, drawing inspiration from the Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm. This approach allows the model to simulate different reasoning paths and select the most effective ones. To mitigate error accumulation that often plagues self-training, the authors employ self-consistency checks and commutativity checks. Self-consistency checks ensure that the model generates consistent answers for equivalent problems, while commutativity checks leverage the property that the order of operands in addition does not affect the result. These checks help filter out erroneous data generated during the self-training process. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach by training a 582M parameter model on smaller addition problems and showing its ability to perform addition with significantly larger numbers (up to 30 digits) without chain-of-thought reasoning. The model achieves this by learning to perform addition directly, bypassing the need for complex reasoning steps. The paper presents empirical results showing that the model's accuracy decreases as the number of digits increases after self-training begins, highlighting the limitations of the approach. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to self-improvement in LLMs, demonstrating the potential of using chain-of-thought reasoning and self-consistency checks to enable models to learn new skills. However, the paper also reveals some limitations, particularly in terms of computational cost, scalability, and generalizability, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.
