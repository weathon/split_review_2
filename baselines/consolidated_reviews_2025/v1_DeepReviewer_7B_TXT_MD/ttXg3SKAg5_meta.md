## Summary

This paper introduces a novel three-step method, termed C³ (Connect, Collapse, Corrupt), designed to enhance the interchangeability of embeddings from different modalities, particularly in scenarios where paired multi-modal data is unavailable. The central premise of the work is that multi-modal contrastive learning often results in modality gaps and alignment noise, which hinder the effective use of embeddings across modalities. To address this, the authors propose a theoretical analysis of the geometry of multi-modal embedding spaces, arguing that embeddings from different modalities exhibit a modality gap orthogonal to the image and text embedding spaces. The C³ method is then presented as a practical approach to mitigate these issues. The method consists of three steps: first, the 'Connect' step aims to reduce the modality gap by establishing connections between similar concepts across different modalities; second, the 'Collapse' step removes the modality gap by deleting the pre-computed mean; and third, the 'Corrupt' step introduces Gaussian noise to the embeddings to mitigate alignment noise. The authors validate their approach through experiments across various tasks, including image captioning, audio captioning, video captioning, and text-to-image generation. They demonstrate that the C³ method achieves state-of-the-art results in zero-shot evaluation settings, even when trained solely on uni-modal data. The core contribution of this work lies in its theoretical analysis of the multi-modal embedding space and the practical C³ method, which offers a simple yet effective way to enhance the interchangeability of embeddings across modalities. The paper's findings suggest that by addressing the modality gap and alignment noise, the C³ method enables the learning of more robust and generalizable cross-modal representations. This has significant implications for various applications, including cross-modal retrieval, transfer learning, and the development of more versatile multi-modal models. The authors' approach is particularly relevant in the context of limited paired multi-modal data, which is a common challenge in many real-world applications. By demonstrating the effectiveness of the C³ method in zero-shot settings, the paper opens up new possibilities for leveraging multi-modal data in scenarios where paired data is scarce or unavailable. The paper's overall significance lies in its ability to bridge the gap between theory and practice, providing both a theoretical understanding of the underlying geometry and a practical method for improving cross-modal learning.
