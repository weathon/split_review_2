## Summary

This paper introduces ReMasker, a novel approach to missing value imputation in tabular data, which extends the masked autoencoding (MAE) framework. The core idea behind ReMasker is to randomly re-mask another set of values within the observed data and then train the model to predict the re-masked values. This is achieved by employing a transformer-based encoder-decoder architecture, which is trained to reconstruct the re-masked values. The authors argue that this method encourages the model to learn missingness-invariant representations, which are intended to be robust to missing data patterns. The paper presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of ReMasker on 12 benchmark datasets, comparing its performance against 13 state-of-the-art imputation methods. The results demonstrate that ReMasker achieves competitive performance in terms of both imputation accuracy and utility. The authors also conduct extensive ablation studies to analyze the impact of various components of ReMasker, such as the masking ratio and embedding width. The paper also includes a theoretical justification for the method, arguing that the re-masking approach naturally accounts for missing values and encourages learning high-level representations beyond low-level statistics. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to missing value imputation, leveraging the power of masked autoencoding in a novel way. The authors successfully demonstrate the effectiveness of their method across a range of datasets and missingness scenarios, making a valuable contribution to the field of tabular data analysis.

This extensive evaluation provides strong evidence for the robustness and generalizability of the method. Third, the ablation studies are well-designed and provide valuable insights into the impact of different components of ReMasker. The analysis of the masking ratio and embedding width helps to understand the sensitivity of the method to these parameters and provides guidance for practical applications. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors provide a clear explanation of the method, the experimental setup, and the results, which facilitates the understanding and reproducibility of their work. ## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the authors claim novelty in their approach, the core idea of extending masked autoencoding to missing value imputation is not entirely new. As Reviewer 1 pointed out, the paper acknowledges prior work on MAE for tabular data imputation, and the novelty lies primarily in the specific combination with Transformers and the re-masking strategy. While the paper argues that this combination is novel, it does not adequately address why this straightforward application is a significant contribution beyond a straightforward application of existing techniques. The paper needs to more clearly articulate the specific challenges of applying MAE to tabular data and how their method addresses these challenges in a unique way. Second, the paper lacks a rigorous theoretical analysis of why the proposed method learns missingness-invariant representations. While the authors provide a theoretical justification section, it is based on intuition and reformulation of the loss function rather than a formal mathematical proof. As Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 3 noted, the claim that ReMasker learns missingness-invariant representations remains unsubstantiated without empirical evidence. The paper does not provide any visualization or analysis of the learned representations to show that they are indeed invariant to missingness patterns. This lack of empirical support weakens the claim and makes it unsubstantiated. Third, the paper does not compare the proposed method with other state-of-the-art methods for imputing missing values in mixed-type data. While the paper includes MissForest as a baseline, it does not compare against other relevant methods such as MissForest++ or other advanced MI methods. As Reviewer 1 pointed out, the absence of a comparison with methods specifically designed for mixed-type data is a significant oversight. This limits the scope of the evaluation and makes it difficult to assess the method's performance relative to existing techniques. Fourth, the paper does not adequately compare the proposed method with other state-of-the-art methods for missing value imputation under MNAR settings. While the paper considers MNAR as one of the missingness mechanisms used in the experiments, it does not explicitly compare against methods specifically designed for MNAR. As Reviewer 1 noted, the lack of comparison with methods specifically designed for MNAR settings is a major weakness. This limits the paper's ability to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in handling MNAR data. Fifth, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the proposed method. As Reviewer 2 pointed out, the paper does not discuss how the method performs when the missingness is not random. This lack of discussion limits the paper's scope and makes it difficult to understand the method's applicability in different scenarios. Finally, the paper's evaluation is limited to tabular data. As Reviewer 3 and Reviewer 4 noted, the paper does not discuss the potential challenges and limitations of applying ReMasker to other data types, such as time series or graph data. This limits the paper's scope and makes it difficult to assess the method's generalizability to other data types. In summary, while the paper presents a promising approach to missing value imputation, these weaknesses limit the paper's overall impact and require further investigation.
