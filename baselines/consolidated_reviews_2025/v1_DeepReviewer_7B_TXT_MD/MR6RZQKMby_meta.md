## Summary

This paper introduces the concept of 'model kinship' as an indicator of potential performance gains in model merging. Inspired by biological evolution, the authors propose that models with similar weight changes during training are more likely to complement each other when merged. They define model kinship as the similarity of weight differences between models relative to a base model. The paper presents a Top-k Greedy Merging with Model Kinship strategy, which leverages kinship to guide the selection of candidate models for merging and to determine early stopping points during the merging process. Empirical evaluations on benchmark datasets such as Winogrande, GSM8k, and TruthfulQA MC2 demonstrate that the proposed method can achieve better performance than a vanilla greedy merging strategy. However, the paper lacks a rigorous theoretical justification for the assumption that model kinship is a reliable indicator of merging potential, and the experimental evaluation is limited to a specific set of models and datasets, raising concerns about the generalizability and robustness of the proposed approach.

## Strengths

The paper makes several valuable contributions to the field of model merging. Firstly, it introduces a novel concept of 'model kinship,' which is inspired by biological evolution and provides a new perspective on understanding and predicting the effectiveness of model merging. This concept is well-articulated and offers a creative approach to the problem of model merging, which is a critical task in large language model (LLM) research. Secondly, the paper provides a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between model kinship and performance gains, which is crucial for understanding the effectiveness of the proposed merging strategy. The authors present empirical evidence showing that models with higher kinship scores tend to exhibit greater performance improvements after merging, which is a significant finding. Additionally, the paper demonstrates the practical application of model kinship in guiding the selection of candidate models for merging and in determining early stopping points during the merging process. The experimental results on benchmark datasets such as Winogrande, GSM8k, and TruthfulQA MC2 are promising and show that the proposed method can achieve better performance than a vanilla greedy merging strategy. The clarity and organization of the paper are also commendable, making it easy to follow the proposed method and its evaluation. The authors provide a detailed explanation of the Top-k Greedy Merging with Model Kinship strategy, which is a valuable contribution to the field. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel concept, empirical evidence, and practical application of model kinship in model merging.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's novel contributions and promising empirical results, several key limitations and weaknesses are evident. One of the most significant concerns is the lack of a theoretical justification for the assumption that model kinship is a reliable indicator of merging potential. The paper defines model kinship as the similarity of weight differences between models relative to a base model, but it does not provide a rigorous analysis of why this metric should directly correlate with the potential for performance improvement after merging. This assumption is crucial to the paper's claims, and the absence of a theoretical foundation leaves the reader questioning the validity of the proposed approach. For instance, the paper does not explore how shared and unique components of weight changes contribute to the overall performance of the merged model, nor does it investigate potential scenarios where high kinship might lead to negative outcomes, such as catastrophic forgetting or interference. The lack of a formal proof or a detailed discussion of the underlying mechanisms that link weight change similarity to merging effectiveness is a critical gap that needs to be addressed. Another limitation is the reliance on a specific metric (similarity of weight changes) to define model kinship. While this approach is practical, it may not capture all aspects of model similarity that are relevant to merging. Other factors, such as the functional similarity of the models or the diversity of their learned representations, could also play a crucial role in determining the success of merging. The paper does not explore alternative definitions of model kinship or discuss the limitations of the chosen metric, which raises questions about the generalizability and robustness of the proposed approach. The experimental evaluation is another area of concern. The paper is limited to a specific set of models and datasets, which makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method. The experiments focus on merging three fine-tuned models based on the Mistral-7B architecture and evaluating on three benchmarks: Winogrande, GSM8k, and TruthfulQA MC2. While the results are promising, the lack of a comprehensive analysis of the sensitivity of the results to different experimental settings, such as the choice of merging strategy, the number of models being merged, and the specific datasets being used, limits the confidence in the method's robustness. The paper does not provide a detailed comparison of the proposed method with existing state-of-the-art model merging techniques, such as those based on gradient-based optimization or Bayesian methods. This omission makes it challenging to position the proposed method within the broader context of model merging and to understand its advantages and disadvantages. Furthermore, the paper does not address the computational complexity of the proposed method, which is an important consideration for large-scale model merging tasks. The lack of a detailed analysis of the computational cost and scalability of the method to larger models and datasets is a notable gap. In summary, the paper's theoretical foundation, the scope of its experimental evaluation, and the lack of a detailed comparison with existing methods are significant limitations that need to be addressed to fully validate the proposed approach.

A more comprehensive analysis of different metrics would provide a more robust and generalizable approach to model kinship. The authors should also discuss potential optimizations to reduce the computational burden, such as using more efficient algorithms or parallelizing the merging process.
