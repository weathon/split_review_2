## Summary

This paper introduces Fat-to-Thin Policy Optimization (FtTPO), a novel two-stage actor-critic framework designed to address the challenges of offline reinforcement learning with sparse policies. The core idea behind FtTPO is to first learn a fat policy, which is dense and aims to capture the general action distribution from the offline dataset, and then to extract a sparse policy from this fat policy. The authors instantiate this framework using a two-stage policy parameterization with the general $q$-Gaussian family, which includes both heavy-tailed and sparse distributions. The fat policy is trained using a standard actor-critic approach, while the thin policy is trained using a KL divergence minimization objective, aiming to learn from the actions generated by the fat policy. The paper motivates the approach by highlighting the out-of-support action issue, where sparse policies trained directly on offline data can generate actions that are not present in the dataset, leading to poor performance. The authors argue that the two-stage approach helps mitigate this issue by first learning a dense policy and then extracting a sparse one. Empirically, the paper evaluates FtTPO on a safety-critical treatment simulation task and the standard D4RL MuJoCo benchmark. The results show that FtTPO achieves competitive performance compared to several baselines, including XQL, SQL, SPOT, and InAC, and even outperforms the best environment-specific algorithms in some cases. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different components of the method. While the paper presents a seemingly novel approach to offline RL with sparse policies, my analysis reveals several key limitations. The core components of the method, namely the two-stage learning framework and the use of $q$-Gaussian policies, are not entirely new, and the paper does not adequately differentiate its approach from existing methods. Furthermore, the paper lacks a clear explanation of how the proposed method addresses the out-of-support action issue, despite identifying it as a key motivation. The experimental evaluation, while demonstrating competitive performance, could be strengthened by including a broader range of baselines and a more detailed analysis of the results. Despite these limitations, the paper does present a potentially valuable contribution to the field of offline reinforcement learning, and with further refinement, it could become a significant reference.
