### Summary

This paper addresses the vulnerability of in-context learning (ICL) to demonstration permutation order in large language models (LLMs). The authors introduce a novel Permutation-resilient learning (PEARL) framework, which leverages distributional robust optimization (DRO) to enhance model robustness against demonstration permutations. PEARL consists of a hard permutation mining network (P-Net) and the LLM, where P-Net identifies the most challenging permutations using an entropy-constrained Sinkhorn algorithm. Through adversarial training, the LLM is optimized to handle these permutations effectively. Experiments on LLaMA-3 demonstrate that PEARL significantly improves both average and worst-case performance, outperforming baseline methods in ICL tasks.

### Strengths

- The paper introduces a novel approach to address the instability of in-context learning (ICL) by leveraging distributional robust optimization (DRO). This framework is innovative and addresses a significant vulnerability in LLMs.
- The paper is well-organized and clearly explains the methodology, including the hard permutation mining network (P-Net) and the adversarial training process. The use of visual aids, such as diagrams and tables, enhances understanding.
- The experimental results are robust and demonstrate significant improvements in both average and worst-case performance compared to baseline methods. The evaluation includes multiple tasks and models, providing strong evidence for the effectiveness of PEARL.

For instance, are there specific patterns or structures in the permutations that are more difficult for standard ICL to handle, and how does PEARL specifically target these? - The paper primarily focuses on empirical results, and a more theoretical analysis of the proposed method would be beneficial. For example, the authors could provide a formal analysis of the convergence properties of the adversarial training process and the robustness guarantees of the PEARL framework. It would also be useful to explore the theoretical limitations of the approach, such as the conditions under which it might fail or be less effective than other methods.

### Suggestions

To address the computational cost concerns, the authors should provide a more detailed breakdown of the training time and memory usage for both the P-Net and the LLM. This should include a discussion of how these costs scale with the number of demonstrations, the size of the input sequences, and the model size. For example, they could present a table showing the training time per epoch for different model sizes and numbers of demonstrations, along with the corresponding memory consumption. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to compare the computational cost of PEARL with other methods for improving ICL robustness, such as data augmentation or adversarial training, to provide a clearer understanding of the trade-offs involved. The authors should also discuss potential strategies for reducing the computational overhead, such as using more efficient optimization algorithms or model compression techniques.

To enhance the analysis of challenging permutations, the authors should conduct a more in-depth investigation into the characteristics of permutations that PEARL is most effective against. This could involve analyzing the structure of the permutations generated by P-Net and comparing them to random permutations. For example, they could examine the distribution of permutation lengths, the frequency of specific patterns, or the degree of shuffling. They could also explore whether certain types of demonstrations are more sensitive to specific permutation patterns. Furthermore, it would be valuable to visualize the permutations generated by P-Net and analyze how they differ from the original demonstration order. This analysis could provide insights into how PEARL is able to identify and mitigate the impact of challenging permutations. The authors could also consider using techniques from permutation analysis to quantify the complexity of the permutations and relate it to the performance of the LLM.

Finally, to strengthen the theoretical foundation of the paper, the authors should provide a more formal analysis of the convergence properties of the adversarial training process. This could involve proving that the proposed training algorithm converges to a stable solution and providing bounds on the convergence rate. They should also explore the robustness guarantees of the PEARL framework, such as the minimum level of performance degradation that can be expected under different types of permutations. Furthermore, it would be useful to investigate the theoretical limitations of the approach, such as the conditions under which it might fail or be less effective than other methods. For example, they could analyze the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters or the size of the training data. A more rigorous theoretical analysis would provide a deeper understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses and help to identify potential areas for improvement.
