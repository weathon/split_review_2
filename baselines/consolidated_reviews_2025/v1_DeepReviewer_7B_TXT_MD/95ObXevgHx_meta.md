## Summary

This paper presents an intriguing investigation into the relationship between deep language models (DLMs) and human brain activity during language comprehension. The authors employ electrocorticography (ECoG) data from nine epilepsy patients listening to a podcast while simultaneously extracting contextual embeddings from a pre-trained GPT-2 XL model. Their core aim is to explore whether the layer-wise accumulation of contextual information observed in DLMs correlates with sequential temporal processing in the brain. To achieve this, they extract contextual embeddings from different layers of the GPT-2 XL model and use linear encoding models to predict neural activity. The key finding is that the layer-wise accumulation of contextual information in DLMs mirrors a sequential processing pattern in the brain, particularly within high-order language areas such as the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) and the temporal pole (TP). This suggests a correspondence between the internal sequence of computations in the DLM and the temporal dynamics of neural processing in the brain. The authors further explore the relationship between the GPT-2 XL model's predictions of word probabilities and the neural response, finding that the model's predictions are correlated with neural activity. They also investigate the impact of predictable and unpredictable words on neural responses, finding differences in the temporal dynamics of neural activity for these two conditions. The study's overall significance lies in its attempt to bridge the gap between artificial and biological language processing systems, offering a novel perspective on how DLMs and the brain process language. While the study provides compelling evidence for a shared computational principle, it also reveals several limitations that warrant further investigation. The study's methodology is sound, but the conclusions are somewhat limited by the specific choices made in the study, such as the use of a single language, a single model, and a single type of neural recording. The study's findings suggest that the hierarchical structure of DLMs may be reflected in the temporal processing of neural activity, but further research is needed to fully understand the implications of these findings for cognitive neuroscience and the development of more biologically plausible language models. The study's findings contribute to the growing body of research on the intersection of AI and neuroscience, providing a valuable perspective on how artificial and biological systems process information. The study's methodology and results are a valuable contribution to the field, but the limitations identified by the reviewers highlight the need for further research to fully understand the implications of these findings.

## Strengths

The core strength of this study lies in its interdisciplinary approach, successfully bridging the gap between artificial and biological language processing systems. The authors' decision to use ECoG, a non-invasive technique, allows for the study of neural activity in language processing while the participants are engaged in a natural task, such as listening to a podcast. This approach provides a unique perspective on how the brain processes language and how it relates to the internal computations of DLMs. The use of contextual embeddings from a pre-trained GPT-2 XL model is another strength of the study. By extracting embeddings from different layers of the model, the authors are able to track the progressive integration of contextual information and observe how it affects neural activity. The finding that the layer-wise accumulation of contextual information in DLMs corresponds to sequential temporal processing in high-level language areas is a significant contribution to the field. This suggests that the internal computational sequences in DLMs may be related to the temporal processing of neural activity in the brain, which is a novel and interesting finding. The study also provides a clear and detailed explanation of the methodology, including the selection of electrodes, embedding extraction, and linear encoding models. This clarity enhances the reproducibility and understanding of the study. The authors' exploration of the relationship between the GPT-2 XL model's predictions of word probabilities and the neural response is another strength of the study. The finding that the model's predictions are correlated with neural activity provides further evidence for the shared computational principles between DLMs and the brain. The study's focus on high-level language areas, such as the IFG and TP, is also a strength, as it allows for a more detailed investigation of the neural mechanisms underlying language processing. The authors' use of a single podcast as the stimulus is a reasonable starting point for this type of research, and the results provide a solid foundation for future investigations. The paper's discussion section also provides a thoughtful reflection on the implications of the findings, including the potential for a paradigm shift from a symbolic representation of language to a new family of contextual embeddings and language statistics-based models. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its innovative methodology, clear presentation, and significant findings that contribute to our understanding of the relationship between artificial and biological language processing systems.

## Weaknesses

While this paper presents a compelling study, several weaknesses limit the generalizability and impact of its findings. A primary concern is the study's reliance on a single language, English, and a specific type of neural recording, electrocorticography (ECoG). As noted by multiple reviewers, the use of English limits the generalizability of the findings to other languages, which have different linguistic structures and processing strategies. The study does not explore whether the observed layer-wise accumulation of contextual information is consistent across different languages, such as Spanish or French. This is a significant limitation, as the hierarchical structure of DLMs may be influenced by the specific linguistic features of the language being processed. The choice of ECoG, while providing a non-invasive approach, raises questions about the applicability of the results to other modalities like fMRI, which measures blood oxygenation level dependent signals. The study does not explore whether the findings are specific to ECoG or whether similar temporal dynamics could be observed in other neural representations. This is a critical limitation, as it is possible that similar temporal patterns could be observed in other modalities, and the study would benefit from a comparative analysis. The use of a single deep language model (GPT-2 XL) is another significant weakness. The study does not compare the results with other DLMs with different architectures and training data. It is possible that the findings are specific to GPT-2 XL or whether they are more general to the class of DLMs. The choice of GPT-2 XL, while a common benchmark, does not explore the full spectrum of DLM architectures, and it is possible that other models with different architectures or pre-training objectives might exhibit different temporal dynamics. Furthermore, the use of a single podcast dataset limits the generalizability of the findings to more diverse linguistic contexts and speaking styles. The study does not explore whether the observed layer-wise accumulation of contextual information is consistent across different types of language processing tasks. The paper also suffers from a lack of clarity in its presentation, particularly in the methodology and results sections. The use of technical jargon specific to ECoG and DLMs makes it difficult for readers unfamiliar with these areas to follow the study. The paper assumes a significant amount of prior knowledge in the field, which hinders its accessibility to a broader audience. The interpretation of the results, particularly the connection between DLM layer depth and neural response timing, requires further clarification. The paper would be strengthened by a more thorough discussion of the implications of these findings for our understanding of language processing in the brain, including the role of different brain regions in language comprehension and the mechanisms underlying the temporal dynamics of neural activity. The paper does not explicitly compare its findings with other methods for analyzing neural data, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) or electroencephalography (EEG). This makes it difficult to assess whether the findings are specific to ECoG or whether they are consistent with other methods for analyzing neural data. It is possible that similar temporal patterns could be observed in other modalities, and the study would benefit from a comparative analysis. Finally, while the discussion section touches upon the implications for cognitive neuroscience and the development of language models, a more detailed and explicit discussion of these implications is needed. The paper should clarify how the findings could inform our understanding of language processing in the brain and whether the insights gained from this study could be used to improve the development of more biologically plausible language models.
