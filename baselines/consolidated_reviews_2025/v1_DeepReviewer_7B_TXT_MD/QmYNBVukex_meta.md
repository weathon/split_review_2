## Summary

This paper introduces a novel data selection method, termed Gradient of Optimal Transport D (GOT-D), designed to enhance the performance of pre-fine-tuning large language models (LLMs). The core idea revolves around leveraging optimal transport theory to identify and select data points that are most similar to the target data distribution, thereby guiding the pre-training process more effectively. The authors argue that traditional data selection methods, which primarily focus on aligning the selected data with the target distribution, often fall short. Instead, GOT-D aims to shift the pre-training distribution towards the target distribution, leading to more efficient and effective fine-tuning. The method is grounded in the concept of optimal transport, where the goal is to find the optimal mapping between two probability distributions. In this context, the pre-training and target distributions are used to calculate an optimal transport plan. The key innovation lies in selecting data points that contribute most to aligning the pre-training distribution with the target distribution, as quantified by the gradient of the optimal transport plan. This approach is theoretically motivated and aims to provide a more principled way of selecting pre-fine-tuning data. The authors provide a theoretical justification for their method, demonstrating that it can be seen as a principled approach to data selection. Empirically, the method is evaluated on BERT-base, a widely used model, across various natural language understanding (NLU) tasks and the GLUE benchmark. The results show that GOT-D outperforms existing data selection methods, particularly in scenarios where the pre-training data is limited. The authors demonstrate that their method can achieve better performance with fewer training examples, suggesting its potential for resource-constrained settings. The paper also includes experiments on general NLU tasks and GLUE tasks, showing the effectiveness of GOT-D across different tasks. The authors claim that their method is computationally efficient, leveraging state-of-the-art optimal transport solvers. However, they do not provide empirical evidence to support this claim, such as running time comparisons with other methods. Overall, the paper presents a theoretically sound and empirically validated method for data selection in pre-fine-tuning LLMs. The use of optimal transport theory to guide data selection is a significant contribution, and the experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method. However, the paper has some limitations, particularly in its reliance on the availability of the target data and the lack of empirical evidence to support its efficiency claims. These limitations will be discussed in detail in the following sections.

## Weaknesses

Despite its strengths, the paper has several notable weaknesses that I have identified through careful analysis. The most significant limitation is the method's reliance on the availability of the target data during the pre-fine-tuning stage. As the authors themselves acknowledge, the method requires the target distribution to calculate the optimal transport plan. However, in practice, the target data is often not available, and the pre-training data is also used for evaluation. This creates a significant challenge for the proposed method, as it cannot be applied in such scenarios. The paper attempts to address this by using the pre-training data as a proxy for the target data, but this approach is not a true solution and has limitations. The core method still depends on the target distribution, and the use of pre-training data as a proxy may not always be accurate, especially if the pre-training data is not representative of the target data. This limitation significantly restricts the applicability of the method in real-world scenarios.
