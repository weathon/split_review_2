## Summary

This paper introduces Permutation-resilient learning (PEARL), a novel framework designed to enhance the robustness of large language models (LLMs) against demonstration permutation order in in-context learning (ICL). The core motivation stems from the observation that LLMs exhibit instability in ICL due to the order in which demonstrations are provided. To address this, the authors propose a distributional robust optimization (DRO) approach, which aims to optimize the model's performance against the worst-case permutations of demonstrations. The PEARL framework comprises two key components: a hard permutation mining network (P-Net) and an adversarial training process. P-Net is responsible for identifying challenging permutations of demonstrations, using an entropy-constrained Sinkhorn algorithm to generate permutations that are likely to be detrimental to the model's performance. The adversarial training process then focuses on optimizing the LLM to handle these worst-case permutations effectively. The authors evaluate PEARL on LLaMA-3 models, demonstrating significant improvements in both average and worst-case performance across various tasks. Specifically, the experiments show that PEARL can improve performance by up to 40% in the worst-case scenario compared to baseline methods. The paper's primary contribution lies in its innovative application of DRO to the problem of demonstration permutation order in ICL, offering a new perspective on how to enhance the reliability of LLMs in real-world scenarios where the order of information may vary. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling, suggesting that PEARL is a promising approach for improving the robustness of LLMs in ICL tasks. However, the paper also has several limitations, particularly in terms of computational cost analysis, theoretical depth, and the scope of experimental validation, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths

The primary strength of this paper lies in its introduction of a novel and well-motivated approach to address the instability of in-context learning (ICL) in large language models (LLMs) due to demonstration permutation order. The authors' decision to leverage distributional robust optimization (DRO) is a significant contribution, offering a principled way to tackle the worst-case scenarios that are often overlooked in standard ICL practices. The proposed Permutation-resilient learning (PEARL) framework is technically sound, with the hard permutation mining network (P-Net) effectively identifying challenging permutations using an entropy-constrained Sinkhorn algorithm. This approach is innovative and efficient, avoiding the need for exhaustive search of all possible permutations. The adversarial training process, where the LLM is trained to handle the permutations generated by P-Net, further enhances the model's robustness. The experimental results presented in the paper are robust and compelling. The authors demonstrate significant improvements in both average and worst-case performance across various tasks and models, providing strong empirical evidence for the effectiveness of PEARL. The evaluation includes multiple tasks and models, which provides a good overview of the method's performance. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methodology and experimental setup. The use of visual aids, such as diagrams and tables, enhances the understanding of the proposed framework and the experimental results. The authors have successfully identified a critical vulnerability in LLMs and have proposed a novel and effective solution. The paper's focus on worst-case performance is particularly relevant, as it addresses a significant limitation of current ICL methods. The combination of a well-motivated problem, a technically sound solution, and strong empirical results makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field of LLM research.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of the proposed PEARL framework, several weaknesses need to be addressed. First, and perhaps most significantly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost and scalability of the proposed method. While the authors mention that P-Net is a "small" model and that they train the LLM from scratch, they do not provide any quantitative analysis of the training time or memory requirements for either P-Net or the LLM. This is a critical omission, as the computational overhead of P-Net and the adversarial training process could be substantial, especially when dealing with larger models and more complex tasks. The paper does not discuss how the computational cost scales with the number of demonstrations, the size of the input sequences, or the model size. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of PEARL, as the computational cost could be a limiting factor in real-world deployments. The absence of a comparison of the computational cost with other methods for improving ICL robustness further compounds this issue. This is a high-confidence concern, as the lack of computational analysis is a clear gap in the paper's evaluation. While the paper demonstrates that PEARL improves performance under worst-case permutations, it does not delve into the specific characteristics of these permutations that PEARL targets. The analysis remains at the task level, rather than the permutation level. For example, the paper does not investigate whether certain patterns or structures in the permutations are more difficult for standard ICL to handle, and how PEARL specifically addresses these. This lack of analysis limits our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of PEARL and its strengths and weaknesses. This lack of theoretical analysis makes it difficult to understand the fundamental properties of PEARL and its limitations. For example, the paper does not explore the conditions under which PEARL might fail to improve performance or be less effective than other methods. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper does not include any theoretical analysis. Fourth, the paper does not explore the limitations of PEARL in scenarios where the number of demonstrations is very large or when the demonstrations are highly diverse. The experiments are conducted with a relatively small number of demonstrations, and the paper does not thoroughly investigate how PEARL performs with significantly larger numbers or more diverse demonstrations. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper does not provide any analysis of these scenarios. Finally, while the paper demonstrates improvements in both average and worst-case performance, it does not provide a detailed comparison with other methods for improving ICL robustness. The paper does not discuss how PEARL compares to methods that use data augmentation or adversarial training, nor does it provide any empirical evidence to support its claims. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper does not provide any comparison with other methods.
