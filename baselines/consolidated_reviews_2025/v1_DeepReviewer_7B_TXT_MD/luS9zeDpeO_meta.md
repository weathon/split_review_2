## Summary

This paper introduces a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithm designed for safe multi-agent systems, specifically focusing on homogeneous agents within a constrained Markov game. The core contribution lies in the development of a primal-dual actor-critic algorithm that aims to maximize the team-average reward while adhering to safety constraints. The authors leverage the multi-time-scale stochastic approximation framework to prove the convergence of their algorithm. The algorithm's design incorporates entropy regularization to promote exploration and ensure a balance between exploration and exploitation. The authors also present a practical implementation of their algorithm based on deep reinforcement learning techniques, using deep neural networks for both policy and value function approximation. Empirically, the algorithm is evaluated on three safety-aware continuous multi-robot task scenarios, demonstrating its ability to achieve a Nash equilibrium under certain cost constraints. The paper's significance lies in its attempt to address the challenge of safety in multi-agent systems, a critical issue in real-world applications. However, the paper's focus on homogeneous agents and its reliance on theoretical analysis, while valuable, limit its practical applicability and generalizability to more complex and realistic scenarios. The paper's theoretical contributions, while sound, are built upon well-established techniques, and the empirical evaluation is limited in scope, raising questions about the algorithm's robustness and scalability. Despite these limitations, the paper provides a valuable starting point for further research in safe multi-agent reinforcement learning.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths, I have identified several significant weaknesses that limit the paper's impact and generalizability. The most prominent limitation is the assumption of homogeneous agents. As I verified, the paper explicitly defines the problem within the context of homogeneous constrained Markov games (Section 2.2), where agents share the same state, action, and transition functions. This assumption, while simplifying the analysis, severely restricts the practical applicability of the proposed algorithm. Real-world multi-agent systems often involve heterogeneous agents with different capabilities, goals, and objectives, and the paper does not adequately address this challenge. This limitation is further compounded by the fact that the paper does not discuss the challenges of extending the proposed approach to heterogeneous settings, such as the need for communication between agents or the potential for conflicts in objectives. My analysis confirms that the paper does not provide any theoretical or practical insights into how the algorithm could be adapted to handle heterogeneous agents, which significantly undermines its practical relevance.
