## Summary

This paper tackles the critical issue of modality gap in Contrastive Language-Image Pretraining (CLIP) models, specifically focusing on its impact on intra-modal tasks such as image-to-image and text-to-text retrieval. The authors demonstrate that the performance of these tasks is significantly hindered by the misalignment between image and text features in the shared embedding space. To address this, they propose a novel approach that leverages modality inversion techniques to transform native modality inputs into the complementary modality, effectively improving the performance of intra-modal tasks. The core idea is that by inverting the modality, the features become more aligned within their own space, thus enhancing the performance of tasks like image-to-image retrieval. The authors introduce two modality inversion strategies: one based on optimizing a text feature to represent an image (OTI) and another based on optimizing an image feature to represent a text (OVI). They conduct extensive experiments across multiple datasets and models, showing that the inter-modal representations obtained through modality inversion significantly outperform the intra-modal baselines. Furthermore, the paper explores the impact of the modality gap by showing that adding intra-modal loss or reducing the modality gap can further mitigate the misalignment issue. The findings suggest that modality inversion provides a more effective solution to intra-modal tasks than traditional intra-modal learning approaches. The paper's significance lies in its ability to bridge the gap between inter-modal and intra-modal tasks, offering a new perspective on how to improve the performance of models like CLIP. By demonstrating the effectiveness of modality inversion, the authors open up new avenues for research in multimodal learning and representation alignment. The paper's empirical results are compelling, showing consistent improvements across various datasets and models, which underscores the robustness of the proposed approach. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the modality gap, which is crucial for understanding the underlying limitations of current CLIP models. Overall, this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field by highlighting the importance of modality alignment and proposing a practical solution to address the challenges posed by intra-modal tasks in multimodal models.

## Weaknesses

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the limited scope of the experimental evaluation, which primarily focuses on image-to-image and text-to-text retrieval tasks. While these tasks are relevant, they do not fully capture the complexities of other intra-modal tasks, such as image captioning or visual question answering. As I've verified, the experimental sections explicitly focus on these two retrieval tasks, and there is no evaluation on tasks that require more complex reasoning or multimodal understanding. This narrow focus raises questions about the generalizability of the proposed modality inversion techniques to other types of intra-modal tasks. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead associated with the modality inversion techniques. The iterative optimization process used for modality inversion can be computationally expensive, especially for large-scale datasets or complex models. As I've confirmed, the paper mentions the hardware used (NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs) and the number of optimization steps but does not provide any quantitative analysis of the time or memory requirements. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Furthermore, the paper does not adequately address the potential limitations of the proposed approach. Specifically, it lacks a thorough investigation of the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter settings. This lack of sensitivity analysis raises concerns about the robustness of the method and its ability to generalize to different datasets and tasks. Additionally, the paper does not discuss the potential for generating artifacts or distortions in the transformed features. This is a critical oversight, as the optimization process could potentially introduce noise or alter the features in unintended ways. This lack of theoretical analysis weakens the overall impact of the paper. The paper also does not explore potential optimizations or approximations to reduce the computational burden of the proposed approach. This is a significant limitation, as the computational cost of the optimization process could hinder the practical applicability of the method.
