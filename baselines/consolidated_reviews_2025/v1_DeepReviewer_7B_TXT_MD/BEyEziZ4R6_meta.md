## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to differentially private (DP) deep learning by leveraging Lipschitz constrained networks. The core idea is to train neural networks where the parameter-wise gradients are bounded by a constant, ideally 1, thereby avoiding the need for per-sample gradient clipping, which is a common but computationally expensive component of traditional DP-SGD. The authors propose a method to compute the Lipschitz constant of each layer with respect to its parameters, using backpropagation to calculate the spectral norm of the Jacobian. This computed Lipschitz constant is then used to normalize the gradients and add Gaussian noise, ensuring differential privacy. The paper provides a theoretical analysis of the proposed method, including bounds on the sensitivity of the gradient computation queries. Empirically, the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach on several image classification datasets, including MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet, as well as the Adbench dataset for tabular data. They compare their method against vanilla DP-SGD and Opacus, showing comparable or better performance in some cases. The authors also provide a Python package, `lip-dp`, to facilitate the implementation of their method. The paper's main contribution lies in its ability to train differentially private models without per-sample gradient clipping, which has the potential to significantly reduce computational overhead. However, the paper also raises several important questions regarding the practical implications of the proposed method, such as the accuracy of the Lipschitz constant estimation, the impact of the Lipschitz constraint on model expressiveness, and the scalability of the method to larger and more complex datasets. Overall, the paper presents a promising direction for efficient DP training, but it also highlights several areas that require further investigation and refinement. The authors' work is a valuable contribution to the field, offering a new perspective on how to achieve differential privacy in deep learning, but it also underscores the need for more comprehensive evaluation and analysis.
