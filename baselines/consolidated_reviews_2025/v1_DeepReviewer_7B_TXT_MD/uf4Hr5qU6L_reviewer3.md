### Summary

This paper proposes a novel prompting framework called PreCoT, which enhances the reasoning capabilities of LLMs by incorporating problem representation, inspired by human problem-solving strategies. The authors evaluate PreCoT on 15 benchmarks across arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness in both few-shot and zero-shot settings. The results show that PreCoT outperforms CoT baselines on most tasks, and the authors provide an analysis of the types of errors made by PreCoT compared to standard CoT, highlighting its strengths and weaknesses.

1. The paper lacks a detailed analysis of the types of errors made by PreCoT compared to standard CoT, which would provide insights into the method's strengths and weaknesses.
2. The improvements over standard CoT are modest, especially on more complex tasks, raising questions about the practical significance of the approach.
3. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of PreCoT to different problem representations or the impact of the quality of the initial and goal states on the final reasoning performance.

### Suggestions

The paper would benefit significantly from a more in-depth error analysis. Specifically, the authors should categorize the errors made by PreCoT and standard CoT, such as arithmetic errors, logical fallacies, or misinterpretations of the problem context. This analysis should not only identify the types of errors but also quantify their frequency and severity. For instance, are there specific types of arithmetic operations or logical structures where PreCoT consistently fails compared to CoT? Furthermore, the analysis should consider the complexity of the problem. Does PreCoT's performance degrade more rapidly than CoT as the problem size or complexity increases? This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses and help to identify specific areas for improvement. A detailed error analysis would also help to understand the limitations of the proposed approach and guide future research.

To address the concern about the modest improvements, the authors should investigate the reasons behind the limited gains on complex tasks. It is possible that the problem representation extracted by PreCoT is not sufficiently informative for more complex reasoning tasks, or that the CoT baseline is already performing well on these tasks, leaving little room for improvement. The authors should explore different strategies for extracting problem representations, such as using more sophisticated natural language processing techniques or incorporating external knowledge sources. Additionally, the authors should analyze the cases where PreCoT fails and identify the specific factors that contribute to these failures. This could involve examining the intermediate reasoning steps generated by PreCoT and comparing them to the reasoning steps of CoT. Such an analysis could reveal the limitations of the current approach and suggest avenues for future research. It would also be beneficial to compare the performance of PreCoT with other state-of-the-art methods for reasoning in LLMs, to better contextualize the results.

Finally, the paper should include a more thorough investigation of the sensitivity of PreCoT to different problem representations and the impact of the quality of the initial and goal states. The authors should experiment with various ways of encoding the initial and goal states, such as using different levels of abstraction or incorporating additional contextual information. For example, instead of just extracting the numerical values, the representation could include units or the context of the problem. This would help to understand which aspects of the problem representation are most important for the success of PreCoT. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of the quality of the extracted initial and goal states. How does the method perform when the extracted states are incomplete or inaccurate? This analysis would help to understand the robustness of the method and its sensitivity to the quality of the problem representation. It would also be beneficial to analyze the computational cost of extracting the initial and goal states and compare it to the cost of the reasoning process.

### Questions

1. How does the quality of the extracted initial and goal states affect the performance of PreCoT? Could the authors provide more details on the process of extracting these states and any potential biases or limitations?
2. What are the computational costs associated with PreCoT, especially in comparison to standard CoT prompting? How does the method scale to more complex problems or larger models?
3. Could the authors elaborate on the potential for extending PreCoT to other reasoning tasks or domains, such as natural language inference or planning problems?
