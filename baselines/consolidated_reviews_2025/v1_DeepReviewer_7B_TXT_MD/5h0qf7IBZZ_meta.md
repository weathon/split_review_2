## Summary

This paper introduces MiniLLM, a novel knowledge distillation method designed to transfer knowledge from large language models (LLMs) to smaller, more efficient language models. The core idea behind MiniLLM is to minimize the reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the teacher and student distributions, a departure from the more common forward KL divergence approach. The authors argue that reverse KL is more suitable for language generation because it focuses on accurately learning the major modes of the target distribution, which is critical for high-quality text generation. This approach is motivated by the observation that forward KL can lead to overestimation of low-quality regions of the teacher distribution, resulting in suboptimal student models. To make the reverse KL approach practical for LLMs, the authors propose three key techniques: single-step decomposition, teacher-mixed sampling, and length normalization. The single-step decomposition simplifies the objective by decomposing it into single-step generation quality and lower probabilities, making it easier to optimize. Teacher-mixed sampling involves mixing the teacher and student distributions at each time step, which helps to stabilize the training process. Length normalization normalizes the probabilities based on the length of the generated sequence, which is important for language generation tasks. The authors evaluate MiniLLM on a range of LLMs, including models with 120M to 13B parameters, and demonstrate that it consistently outperforms standard knowledge distillation baselines across various evaluation metrics, including Rouge-L and GPT-4 scores. The experiments also show that MiniLLM exhibits lower exposure bias, better calibration, and higher long-text generation performance. The paper's main contribution lies in the application of reverse KL divergence to the context of LLM knowledge distillation and the development of techniques to make this approach feasible. While the core idea of reverse KL is not entirely novel, its application to LLMs and the specific techniques developed to address the challenges of this application represent a significant contribution to the field. The paper provides a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method, demonstrating its effectiveness across different model sizes and evaluation metrics. The authors also provide an ablation study to analyze the impact of each of the proposed techniques. Overall, this paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to knowledge distillation for LLMs, offering a promising alternative to standard forward KL divergence methods.

While the concept of using reverse KL is not entirely new, its adaptation to the complexities of LLMs and the introduction of specific techniques to address these complexities represent a significant contribution. The authors have successfully demonstrated that minimizing reverse KL divergence leads to improved performance compared to standard forward KL methods. This is a notable achievement, as it challenges the conventional wisdom of using forward KL for all language generation tasks. The proposed techniques, namely single-step decomposition, teacher-mixed sampling, and length normalization, are crucial for making the reverse KL approach practical and effective. These techniques address key challenges associated with applying reverse KL to LLMs, such as the difficulty of optimizing the objective and the potential for overestimation of low-quality regions. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors have conducted extensive experiments on a range of LLMs, including models with 120M to 13B parameters, and have shown that MiniLLM consistently outperforms standard knowledge distillation baselines across various evaluation metrics. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors have clearly explained the motivation behind their approach, the technical details of their method, and the results of their experiments. The paper's comprehensive evaluation and clear presentation of results make it a valuable contribution to the field of knowledge distillation for language models. ## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach to knowledge distillation using reverse KL divergence, there are some limitations that warrant further discussion. One key weakness is the incremental nature of the method. While the application of reverse KL to LLMs is novel, the core idea of using reverse KL is not entirely new, as the paper itself acknowledges its use in computer vision. The paper introduces three specific techniques—single-step decomposition, teacher-mixed sampling, and length normalization—to stabilize and accelerate training. However, the paper lacks a detailed ablation study to isolate the impact of each of these techniques. While the authors do include an ablation study in Section 4.3, it only evaluates the impact of removing each of the three stabilization techniques, not the impact of different weighting schemes for the single-step decomposition or the effect of this decomposition on the training dynamics. This makes it difficult to fully understand the contribution of each component of the proposed method. For instance, the paper does not explore the sensitivity of MiniLLM to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and batch size, and how these parameters interact with the reverse KL objective. A more granular analysis of the training process, perhaps visualizing the loss curves for both forward and reverse KL, could also provide insights into the method's behavior. Without such analysis, it remains unclear whether the observed improvements are primarily due to the reverse KL objective, the proposed training stabilization techniques, or a combination of both. This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to fully understand the mechanisms behind the performance gains and limits the generalizability of the findings. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide sufficient evidence to isolate the impact of each component of the proposed method. Another significant weakness is the lack of concrete examples to support the claim that MiniLLM produces more precise responses. While the paper provides quantitative evidence (Rouge-L and GPT-4 scores) and qualitative descriptions of the benefits of reverse KL, it does not include specific examples of generated text comparing MiniLLM with baseline methods. The paper argues that reverse KL is more suitable for language generation because it "concentrates on $p$'s major modes, which is crucial to ensure the correctness and faithfulness of text generation" (Section 3.1). However, without concrete examples, it is difficult to assess whether the generated responses are indeed more precise and less prone to factual inaccuracies or verbose outputs. The paper mentions that forward KL can lead to "overestimation of low-quality regions of the teacher distribution" (Section 3.1), but it does not provide any examples to illustrate this point. The paper also mentions that reverse KL "focuses on accurately learning the major modes of the target distribution, which is critical for high-quality text generation" (Section 3.1), but again, this is not supported by concrete examples. The lack of such examples makes it difficult to fully validate the claim of more precise responses and limits the practical impact of the paper. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide any empirical evidence to support the claim of more precise responses. In summary, while the paper presents a promising approach to knowledge distillation, the lack of detailed analysis of the individual components of the method and the absence of concrete examples to support the claim of more precise responses are significant limitations that need to be addressed.

While the use of reverse Kullback-Leibler divergence is presented as a key differentiator, the paper lacks a detailed ablation study to isolate its impact. Specifically, the authors could experiment with different weighting schemes for the single-step decomposition of the reverse KLD, or explore the effect of this decomposition on the training dynamics. For example, they could compare the performance of their method with a baseline that uses forward KLD without the proposed stabilization techniques. Furthermore, it would be valuable to analyze the sensitivity of MiniLLM to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and batch size, and how these parameters interact with the reverse KLD objective. A more granular analysis of the training process, perhaps visualizing the loss curves for both forward and reverse KLD, could also provide insights into the method's behavior. These examples should focus on scenarios where MiniLLM demonstrates a clear advantage in terms of response quality. For example, the authors could present cases where the baseline models generate responses that are either too verbose, contain factual inaccuracies, or fail to capture the nuances of the input prompt. In contrast, the authors should highlight instances where MiniLLM generates more concise, accurate, and contextually relevant responses. These examples should be carefully selected to highlight the specific strengths of MiniLLM, and should include both quantitative metrics and qualitative analysis. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to analyze the types of errors made by each model, and to discuss the potential reasons for these errors. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of MiniLLM. Finally, the paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the experimental results are promising, it is important to acknowledge the potential shortcomings of MiniLLM. For example, the authors could discuss the computational cost of training MiniLLM, or the potential for overfitting to the training data. It would also be valuable to explore the generalizability of MiniLLM to different datasets and tasks. A thorough discussion of these limitations would provide a more balanced and nuanced view of the proposed method, and would help guide future research in this area. By addressing these weaknesses, the paper would be significantly strengthened and would provide a more comprehensive and reliable contribution to the field of knowledge distillation for language models.

## Questions

Several key questions arise from my analysis of the paper. First, what is the precise mechanism by which the single-step decomposition of the reverse KLD contributes to the overall performance of MiniLLM? The paper introduces this technique as a way to simplify the objective, but it does not fully explain how this simplification affects the training dynamics and the quality of the generated text. A more detailed analysis of the impact of this technique, perhaps through ablation studies or visualizations of the loss landscape, would be beneficial. Second, how does the choice of hyperparameters, such as the learning rate and batch size, interact with the reverse KLD objective? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of MiniLLM to these hyperparameters, and it is unclear whether the reported results are robust across different settings. A more thorough investigation of this aspect would be valuable. Third, what are the specific types of errors that MiniLLM is able to avoid compared to the baseline methods? The paper argues that reverse KL is better at focusing on the major modes of the target distribution, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the specific types of errors that this leads to. A more granular analysis of the error patterns, perhaps through error analysis techniques, would be helpful. Fourth, how does the performance of MiniLLM scale with the size of the student model? The paper presents results for a range of student model sizes, but it does not explore the limits of this approach. It would be interesting to see how MiniLLM performs on even smaller models, and whether there are any trade-offs between model size and performance. Finally, what are the computational costs associated with training MiniLLM, and how do these costs compare to those of other knowledge distillation methods? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for training, and it is important to understand the practical implications of this for real-world applications. Addressing these questions would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method and its limitations, and would help guide future research in this area.
