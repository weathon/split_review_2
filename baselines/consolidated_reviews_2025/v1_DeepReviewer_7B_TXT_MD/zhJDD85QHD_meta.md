## Summary

This paper introduces CEIR, a Concept-based Explainable Image Representation learning method, designed to generate human-interpretable concepts from image features without relying on labeled data. The core idea involves projecting image features into a concept vector space using a pretrained CLIP model, followed by a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to learn a latent representation. The authors claim that this approach enhances interpretability and preserves the robustness of the learned representations. The method is evaluated on several datasets, including CIFAR10, CIFAR100-20, CIFAR100, STL10, and ImageNet, demonstrating competitive performance in unsupervised clustering tasks. The paper emphasizes the interpretability of the learned representations, showing that both training and open-world images can be associated with meaningful concepts. While the paper presents a novel integration of CLIP and VAE for unsupervised representation learning, it faces several limitations, particularly in terms of novelty, evaluation metrics, and discussion of limitations. The overall significance of the paper lies in its attempt to bridge the gap between representation learning and interpretability, but the lack of comprehensive validation and detailed analysis of the method's limitations hinders its broader impact.

## Strengths

The paper is well-organized and easy to follow, which is a significant strength. The authors provide a clear explanation of the proposed method, CEIR, and its components, including the use of a pretrained CLIP model for concept generation and a VAE for learning latent representations. The motivation for the method is well-articulated, highlighting the need for interpretable and robust representations in unsupervised settings. The use of a concept bottleneck to extract meaningful features from image representations is a valuable contribution, as it allows for the generation of human-understandable concepts, which is a step towards more transparent and interpretable machine learning models. The experimental results on multiple datasets, including CIFAR10, CIFAR100-20, CIFAR100, STL10, and ImageNet, demonstrate the effectiveness of CEIR in unsupervised clustering tasks. The paper also emphasizes the interpretability of the learned representations, showing that both training and open-world images can be associated with meaningful concepts. This is a significant achievement, as it addresses the need for explainable AI in scenarios where labeled data is scarce or unavailable. The authors also provide a clear description of the experimental setup and the metrics used for evaluation, which is essential for reproducibility. The paper's focus on unsupervised learning is also a strength, as it addresses a critical challenge in the field of representation learning. The integration of CLIP and VAE is a technical innovation that contributes to the overall effectiveness of the method. The authors' efforts to make the learned representations more interpretable are commendable, and the results presented in the paper are competitive with state-of-the-art methods in unsupervised clustering.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and weaknesses are evident. First, the novelty of the proposed method is limited. The overall framework of CEIR, which involves projecting image features into a concept vector space and using a VAE for representation learning, is similar to LF-CBM. The primary difference lies in the use of a pretrained CLIP model to generate concept labels instead of manually defined labels. However, the use of pretrained models to generate concept labels has been explored in other works, such as T-CAV and LF-ImageNet. The core idea of using a concept bottleneck to extract interpretable features from a representation is not new, and the specific implementation using CLIP and VAE, while potentially useful, does not represent a significant conceptual leap. This lack of substantial novelty is a critical weakness, as it questions the originality of the proposed approach. Second, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. For instance, the authors do not address how the method would perform on datasets with complex or abstract concepts, which are not easily captured by the concept pool generated by CLIP. The impact of the choice of concept labels on the quality of the learned representations is also not discussed. This is a significant oversight, as the quality of the concept labels can significantly affect the performance of the method. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss the computational cost of the method, particularly the use of a large language model for concept generation and the training of the VAE, which could be a significant barrier for some users. The absence of a detailed analysis of the computational cost makes it difficult to assess the practicality of the method. Third, the evaluation metrics used in the paper are not comprehensive. While the authors report NMI, ACC, and ARI for clustering tasks, they do not provide a detailed analysis of the learned representations using other metrics, such as the ability to preserve semantic relationships between concepts or the robustness to adversarial attacks. The lack of these metrics makes it difficult to fully assess the quality and utility of the learned representations. The paper also lacks a comparison with other state-of-the-art representation learning methods, which makes it difficult to assess the true performance of the proposed approach. The absence of a comprehensive comparison with other methods, including both unsupervised and supervised approaches, limits the paper's ability to demonstrate the superiority of CEIR. Finally, the paper does not include an ablation study on the impact of concept labels, which is crucial for understanding the sensitivity of the method to the quality of the concept labels. The authors should have explored how different concept labels affect the quality of the learned representations. These weaknesses collectively undermine the paper's claims and limit its overall impact. I have a high confidence level in these identified issues, as they are consistently supported by the paper's content and omissions.
