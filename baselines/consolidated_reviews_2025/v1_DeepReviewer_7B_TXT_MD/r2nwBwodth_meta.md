## Summary

This paper introduces Prediction of Functionals from Masked Latents (PFML), a novel self-supervised learning (SSL) method tailored for time-series data. The core idea behind PFML is to predict statistical functionals of the input signal corresponding to masked embeddings, thereby circumventing the issue of representation collapse often observed in other SSL methods. Unlike approaches that focus on reconstructing the input signal or predicting its future values, PFML operates by predicting statistical properties of the masked embeddings, which are derived from unmasked portions of the time series. This approach, according to the authors, encourages the model to learn more robust and informative representations. The method is conceptually simple and can be readily applied to various time-series domains. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of PFML through experiments on three different data modalities: infant posture and movement classification from multi-sensor IMU data, emotion recognition from speech data, and sleep stage classification from EEG data. The results show that PFML is superior to a conceptually similar method, MAE, and is competitive with the current state-of-the-art data modality agnostic SSL method, data2vec. The paper also includes an analysis of representation collapse, showing that PFML exhibits a lower probability of collapse compared to the baseline methods. The authors argue that by predicting functionals, PFML avoids the collapse of the learned representations, which they hypothesize is due to the less complex masking strategy compared to methods like MAE. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to SSL for time series, offering a promising alternative to existing methods. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several areas where the paper could be strengthened with more detailed analysis and experimentation.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. A significant limitation is the lack of a detailed comparison of PFML with other SSL methods, particularly in terms of computational efficiency and scalability. While the paper qualitatively compares PFML with MAE and data2vec in terms of performance, it does not provide a quantitative analysis of their computational cost. Specifically, the paper lacks specific training times, inference times, and memory usage for PFML and the baseline methods. This is a critical omission, as it is essential to understand the practical implications of using PFML compared to other methods, especially when dealing with large datasets or high-dimensional time series. The paper mentions the hardware used for pre-training, stating that they pre-trained their models using relatively small minibatches so that the pre-training processes could be run on a single GPU with 16 GB of VRAM. However, this does not provide a clear picture of the computational cost of PFML compared to other methods. This lack of computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of PFML in real-world scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the absence of these metrics is clearly evident in the experimental section. 

Another significant weakness is the lack of a rigorous theoretical analysis or empirical evidence to support the claim that PFML avoids representation collapse. While the paper argues that PFML avoids collapse by alleviating the complexity of MAE's masking strategy, it does not provide a formal theoretical analysis of why this is the case. The paper states that they hypothesize that for SSL pre-training with time-series data, a model would learn more useful features for downstream tasks if the complex setting of MAE would be alleviated slightly. They also state that if a representation collapse occurred during data2vec pre-training, they restarted the pre-training process. However, this does not constitute a rigorous theoretical analysis. Furthermore, the empirical evidence for avoiding collapse is limited to a single experiment, where the probability of collapse is measured. The paper does not explore the properties of the learned representations or test the robustness of the learned representations to different types of noise and perturbations. The paper includes an experiment on "Representation Collapse Experiments" (Section 4.3, Table 4) where PFML shows a lower probability of representation collapse compared to MAE and data2vec. However, this is not a comprehensive analysis of the properties of the learned representations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a theoretical analysis and a more diverse set of experiments. 

Furthermore, the paper does not adequately discuss the limitations of PFML or potential failure cases. It is important to understand the scenarios where PFML might not perform well, such as time series with very high variability or those with complex, non-stationary patterns. The paper does not explore these scenarios or discuss the sensitivity of PFML to hyperparameter choices. The paper also does not provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for different types of time series data. The paper focuses on demonstrating the effectiveness of PFML but does not explore its weaknesses or the impact of its design choices in detail. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the absence of such a discussion is clearly evident in the paper. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the impact of the choice of functionals on the performance of the model. While the paper mentions the functionals used (mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, autocorrelation, zero-crossing rate), it does not provide a detailed analysis of their impact or discuss the sensitivity to these choices. This is a limitation, as the choice of functionals could significantly affect the performance of the model. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide any analysis of the impact of different functionals.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational aspects of PFML. This should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for both training and inference, comparing PFML against established methods like MAE and data2vec. This analysis should not only consider the theoretical complexity but also include empirical measurements on the datasets used in the paper. For instance, reporting the training time per epoch and the memory footprint during both training and inference would provide a clearer picture of the practical implications of using PFML. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to explore the scalability of PFML by evaluating its performance on larger datasets or with longer time series. This could involve experiments with synthetic datasets that vary in size and complexity, allowing for a more systematic assessment of the method's scalability. Such an analysis would help readers understand the trade-offs between performance and computational cost when choosing between different SSL methods for time series data. 

Second, to strengthen the claim that PFML avoids representation collapse, the authors should provide a more rigorous theoretical analysis of the method's behavior. This could involve examining the properties of the learned representations and demonstrating how they differ from those obtained by methods prone to collapse. For example, the authors could analyze the variance of the learned embeddings during pre-training and show that it remains non-zero, unlike what might be observed in methods that suffer from collapse. Additionally, the empirical evidence should be expanded to include a more diverse set of experiments that specifically test the robustness of the learned representations to different types of noise and perturbations. This could involve adding Gaussian noise to the input data or introducing adversarial perturbations to the embeddings. Such experiments would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's ability to learn meaningful representations that are not trivial or invariant to input variations. The authors should also consider visualizing the learned representations to gain insights into their structure and properties. 

Third, the paper should include a more detailed discussion of the limitations of PFML and potential failure cases. This should include an analysis of the scenarios where PFML might not perform well, such as time series with very high variability or those with complex, non-stationary patterns. For example, the authors could investigate the performance of PFML on time series with abrupt changes or sudden spikes, which might challenge the model's ability to learn stable representations. Furthermore, the sensitivity of PFML to hyperparameter choices should be explored, and guidelines for selecting appropriate values for different types of time series data should be provided. This discussion should include an analysis of how the choice of functionals affects the performance of the model. For instance, the authors could investigate the impact of using different statistical functionals or combinations of functionals on the quality of the learned representations. This would provide a more complete understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses and help guide its application to new datasets. 

Finally, the authors should explore the performance of PFML on a wider range of time series datasets, including those with different characteristics, to demonstrate the generalizability of the method. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of PFML and help guide its application to new datasets. This would also help to validate the claim that PFML avoids representation collapse, as it would be tested on a wider range of data. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen the paper and provide a more complete and robust analysis of the PFML method.
