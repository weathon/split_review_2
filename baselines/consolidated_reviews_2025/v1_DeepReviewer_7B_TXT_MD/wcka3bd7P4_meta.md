## Summary

In this paper, I explore a novel framework called FROND, which extends existing integer-order continuous Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) by incorporating non-local fractional derivatives. The authors propose a method to generalize integer-order GNNs, providing a comprehensive interpretation of the node feature updating process from a non-Markovian random walk perspective. This approach is theoretically analyzed for oversmoothing, and the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of FROND through extensive experiments on node classification tasks. The core contributions of the paper include the introduction of Caputo fractional derivatives, the formulation of a generalized continuous GNN framework, and the empirical validation of the proposed method. However, the paper's practical implementation and analysis of computational complexity, sensitivity to the fractional order parameter, and potential for overfitting are areas that require further attention and clarification. Despite these limitations, the paper offers a promising direction for enhancing GNNs by leveraging fractional calculus, which could have significant implications for graph representation learning and node classification tasks.

## Strengths

The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with a clear motivation for the proposed framework. The authors provide a comprehensive analysis of the proposed framework, including the interpretation of the node feature updating process from a non-Markovian random walk perspective. This perspective is particularly valuable as it offers a novel way to understand the dynamics of feature propagation in GNNs. The theoretical analysis of the oversmoothing issue is thorough and provides a solid foundation for the proposed method. The experimental results are extensive and demonstrate the effectiveness of FROND in node classification tasks, outperforming existing integer-order continuous GNNs on several benchmark datasets. The authors also provide a detailed ablation study on the impact of the fractional order parameter, which is a valuable contribution to understanding the behavior of the proposed framework. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field of graph representation learning by introducing a new framework that leverages fractional calculus to generalize integer-order GNNs.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and areas for improvement are evident. One of the primary concerns is the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed framework. The paper introduces the Caputo fractional derivative and the Adams-Moulton method for solving the fractional ODE, but it does not provide a formal time or space complexity analysis. This is a significant oversight, as the computational cost of fractional derivatives can be substantial, especially for large graphs. The authors should include a theoretical analysis of the time and space complexity, along with empirical measurements of training time and memory usage for different datasets and fractional orders. This would help readers understand the practical implications of using the proposed framework and its trade-offs between performance gains and computational costs. For instance, the number of operations required for the Caputo derivative calculation and how this scales with the graph size and the fractional order are not discussed. The memory requirements for storing intermediate results, particularly for large graphs, are also not addressed.
