## Summary

This paper introduces a meta-learning framework for fine-tuning foundation models (FMs), aiming to improve their adaptability to unseen tasks. The authors identify a critical limitation in the conventional two-phase approach: retraining followed by parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) with methods like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA). They argue that this approach can lead to suboptimal adaptation, as the retrained model may not be universally adaptable to future tasks. To address this, the authors propose a meta-learning objective, termed meta-LoRA, which explicitly considers the fine-tuning process and the low-rank adapter parameters. Their core idea is that by meta-learning the low-rank adapters, the model can achieve better generalization to unseen tasks. The theoretical analysis, conducted on linear models, demonstrates that the meta-LoRA objective can provably recover the ground-truth parameters under certain conditions. This is a significant contribution, as it provides a theoretical foundation for the proposed approach. Empirically, the authors validate their method on both synthetic data and the ConvAI2 dataset, a collection of large language models. The results show that meta-LoRA outperforms standard retraining, providing strong evidence for its practical value. The paper's main contribution lies in its novel meta-learning framework for fine-tuning FMs, which addresses a critical gap in the conventional two-phase approach. The theoretical analysis, while limited to linear models, provides a strong foundation for the method, and the empirical results on both synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate its effectiveness. The authors' focus on meta-learning the low-rank adapters, rather than simply retraining, is a key innovation that leads to improved generalization. The paper's findings suggest that meta-learning can be a powerful tool for fine-tuning FMs, and that the choice of fine-tuning method is crucial for achieving optimal performance. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field of meta-learning and fine-tuning, and their findings have the potential to significantly impact how FMs are adapted to new tasks. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with a clear motivation and a detailed explanation of the proposed method. The theoretical analysis is rigorous, and the empirical results are compelling. Overall, this paper presents a significant advance in the field of meta-learning and fine-tuning, and it is a valuable contribution to the literature.

## Weaknesses

While this paper presents a compelling approach to fine-tuning foundation models, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's theoretical analysis is limited by its reliance on linear models. As explicitly stated in Section 3.1, the authors focus on multi-output linear regression problems, which simplifies the analysis but may not accurately reflect the complexities of deep neural networks. This assumption, while simplifying the theoretical derivations, may limit the applicability of the theoretical results to more complex, real-world scenarios. The paper does not explore the implications of this assumption or discuss how the theoretical guarantees might change with non-linear models. This is a significant limitation, as the non-linear interactions present in deep learning models could potentially lead to a gap between the theoretical guarantees and practical performance. The authors do not provide any analysis of the convergence properties of their meta-learning objective when applied to non-linear models, which further exacerbates this concern. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is explicitly stated in the paper and directly impacts the generalizability of the theoretical results. Second, the paper's focus on LoRA as the sole parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) method is a significant limitation. As stated in Section 3.1, the authors use LoRA as an example of a low-rank adaptation method. The meta-LoRA objective and theoretical analysis are specifically defined in terms of LoRA adapters. The paper does not discuss the applicability of the meta-learning framework to other PEFT methods, such as adapter-based fine-tuning or prompt-based fine-tuning. This limits the generality of the proposed approach, as it is unclear whether the same performance gains can be achieved with other parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques that operate on different parts of the model or use different parameterizations. The authors do not provide any analysis of how the meta-learning approach would perform with other PEFT methods, which is a significant oversight. My confidence in this limitation is high, as it is explicitly stated in the paper and directly impacts the generalizability of the proposed approach. Third, the paper's experimental evaluation is limited in scope. The experiments are conducted on a limited set of tasks and datasets. While the ConvAI2 dataset is relevant, it would be helpful to see results on a broader range of tasks and datasets to assess the generalizability of the proposed method. The current evaluation does not fully demonstrate the robustness of the approach across diverse problem settings.
