## Summary

This paper introduces a novel algorithm, termed AE-AGS, designed to address the challenge of learning in matching markets where agents possess indistinguishable preferences. This problem is particularly relevant in real-world scenarios such as labor markets, school choice, and organ transplantation, where individuals may have similar, though not identical, preferences for available options. The authors' core contribution lies in the development of a polynomial regret bound algorithm that operates under this more realistic preference structure, a significant advancement over existing methods that often assume strict preference ordering. The AE-AGS algorithm is built upon an adaptive exploration mechanism, guided by a comparison matrix, which allows agents to learn and explore different matchings while still adhering to the principles of the Gale-Shapley stable matching algorithm. The algorithm proceeds in two stages: a centralized exploration phase and a decentralized exploitation phase. The centralized phase allows for a more structured exploration of the matching space, while the decentralized phase enables agents to make decisions based on their individual preferences and the information gathered during exploration. The paper provides a theoretical analysis of the algorithm's performance, demonstrating a regret bound of O(N), where N is the number of agents, which is a notable improvement over the state-of-the-art results for strict preferences, which are often exponential in N. The authors also present a lower bound for the problem, further contextualizing their result. The empirical evaluation of the algorithm is conducted through simulations in a simplified market setting, where the performance of AE-AGS is compared against explore-then-Gale-Shapley (C-ETC and P-ETC) baselines. The results indicate that AE-AGS outperforms these baselines in terms of stable matching regret and market stability. The paper's overall significance lies in its contribution to the theoretical understanding of learning in matching markets with indistinguishable preferences, providing a practical algorithm with strong theoretical guarantees and promising empirical results. The work opens up new avenues for research in this area, particularly in exploring the algorithm's performance in more complex and realistic settings. The authors' approach of combining the principles of stable matching with the adaptive exploration of bandit learning is a novel and promising direction for addressing the challenges of matching market design in the presence of uncertain preferences.
