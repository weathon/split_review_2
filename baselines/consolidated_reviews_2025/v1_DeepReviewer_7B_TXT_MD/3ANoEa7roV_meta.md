## Summary

This paper introduces a systematic evaluation framework for assessing tabular data synthesis algorithms, addressing a critical need in the field of privacy-preserving data analysis. The authors propose three key metrics: a Wasserstein-based fidelity metric, a membership disclosure score (MDS)-based privacy metric, and a machine learning affinity (MLA)-based utility metric. The Wasserstein distance is used to quantify the statistical similarity between the original and synthetic datasets, aiming to capture the structural information lost during the synthesis process. The membership disclosure score, on the other hand, measures the impact of individual training samples on the performance of machine learning models trained on the synthetic data, serving as a proxy for privacy leakage. Finally, the machine learning affinity score assesses the utility of the synthetic data by evaluating the performance of classifiers trained on it. The authors conduct extensive experiments across 12 real-world datasets and eight different synthesizers, providing valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of various synthesis techniques. The results highlight the trade-offs between fidelity, privacy, and utility, offering a comprehensive view of the performance landscape. The paper also includes an ablation study that demonstrates the importance of tuning the fidelity metric, which is a novel contribution to the field. The framework is designed to be modular and extensible, allowing for the evaluation of other synthesizers beyond the ones tested in the paper. The authors also explore the impact of different training strategies on the performance of the proposed metrics. Overall, this paper makes a significant contribution by providing a unified framework for evaluating tabular data synthesis algorithms, which is crucial for advancing the field of privacy-preserving data analysis. The proposed metrics are intuitive and reasonable, and the experimental results provide valuable insights into the performance of different synthesizers. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors also provide a clear explanation of the proposed metrics and their computation, which is essential for reproducibility. The paper's focus on the practical aspects of evaluating tabular data synthesis algorithms is particularly valuable, as it addresses the challenges faced by researchers and practitioners in the field. The paper's findings have important implications for the development of more effective and privacy-preserving data synthesis techniques. The authors' work provides a solid foundation for future research in this area, and their framework will likely be widely adopted by the research community.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a valuable framework for evaluating tabular data synthesis algorithms, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the lack of novelty in the proposed metrics themselves. As the paper itself acknowledges, the Wasserstein distance and membership disclosure score are established metrics, widely used in the literature. While their application to tabular data synthesis is a contribution, the paper does not adequately justify why these specific metrics are superior to other existing metrics in the context of tabular data. For instance, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison with other commonly used metrics for fidelity, such as total variation distance or KL divergence, nor does it explain why Wasserstein distance is a better choice than these alternatives in the context of tabular data synthesis. Similarly, the paper does not provide a clear justification for why membership disclosure score is a better privacy metric than other existing metrics, such as differential privacy or Rényi differential privacy. The paper acknowledges that MDS is not designed to replace differential privacy metrics but lacks a direct comparison. This lack of justification weakens the paper's claims and raises questions about the necessity of introducing these specific metrics. The paper also lacks a detailed explanation of how the proposed metrics are computed in practice. While the paper provides a high-level description of the algorithms used, such as optimal transport for Wasserstein distance and training of neural networks for MDS, it does not provide sufficient details on the implementation. For example, the paper does not specify how the optimal transport plan is computed for the Wasserstein distance, how the membership disclosure score is calculated for different types of synthesizers, or how the machine learning affinity score is computed for different types of machine learning models. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results and limits the practical utility of the framework. The paper also does not address the computational complexity of each metric and discuss the practical implications of these complexities for large-scale datasets. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the proposed metrics. While the paper presents results for 8 different synthesizers across 12 datasets, it would be beneficial to include a more detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the proposed metrics to different parameters and settings. For example, the authors should investigate how the Wasserstein distance is affected by the choice of the transport plan and how the membership disclosure score is affected by the choice of the training data. The authors should also compare the performance of the proposed metrics with other existing metrics on a wider range of datasets and synthesizers. This would provide a more robust evaluation of the proposed metrics and demonstrate their practical utility. Finally, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of how the proposed metrics are computed in practice. The authors should provide more details on the implementation of the proposed metrics and how they are used in the evaluation framework. This should include a step-by-step description of the algorithms used to calculate the Wasserstein distance, membership disclosure score, and machine learning affinity score. For example, the authors should specify how the optimal transport plan is computed for the Wasserstein distance, how the membership disclosure score is calculated for different types of synthesizers, and how the machine learning affinity score is computed for different types of machine learning models. The authors should also discuss the practical challenges of implementing these metrics, such as the computational cost of calculating the Wasserstein distance for high-dimensional data and the sensitivity of the membership disclosure score to the choice of the training data. Providing concrete examples of how these metrics are applied to different types of tabular data would also enhance the paper's clarity and practical value. The paper would also benefit from a more thorough justification of the chosen metrics. While the paper provides some rationale for using Wasserstein distance, membership disclosure score, and machine learning affinity score, it lacks a detailed comparison with other common metrics in the respective domains of fidelity, privacy, and utility. For instance, the authors should discuss the specific properties of these metrics that make them appropriate for capturing fidelity, privacy, and utility in the context of tabular data.
