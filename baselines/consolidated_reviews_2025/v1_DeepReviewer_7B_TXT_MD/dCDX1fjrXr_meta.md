## Summary

This paper introduces a novel framework, ELI (Estimating Label Information), designed to enhance the performance of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) in the Semi-Supervised Node Classification (SLNC) task, particularly in scenarios with extremely limited labeled data, termed Sparse Labels Node Classification (SLNC). The core idea revolves around estimating label information and leveraging reformulations of well-known semi-supervised learning frameworks. The proposed method is structured into four key steps: label distribution estimation, key node selection, label distribution incorporation, and optimization. The label distribution estimation step employs an unsupervised convolution-based clustering framework, specifically adapting the approach from Kamhoua et al. (2022), to infer pseudo-labels for unlabeled nodes based on graph structure and node features. This step aims to capture the underlying label distribution without relying on per-class basis label selection, which is a common practice in semi-supervised learning. Following the estimation of the pseudo-label distribution, the method selects a subset of nodes, termed 'key nodes,' using a clustering-based approach. The rationale behind this is to select nodes that are representative of the different clusters in the graph, which are assumed to correspond to different classes. The label distribution incorporation step then integrates the estimated label information into the GNN training process, using a modified loss function that takes into account the estimated pseudo-labels. Finally, the method optimizes the GNN parameters using a standard optimization procedure. The authors evaluate their framework on several benchmark attributed graphs, including Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, Wiki, CS, and Photo, demonstrating significant improvements over baseline GNN models, particularly when the number of labeled nodes is extremely small. The experimental results suggest that the proposed framework can effectively enhance the performance of GNNs in the SLNC task, which is a practical and challenging problem in real-world applications. However, the paper's contributions are somewhat limited by the reliance on existing techniques and the lack of a comprehensive comparison with state-of-the-art methods. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and memory requirements of the proposed method, which are crucial for assessing its scalability and practical deployability. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a well-motivated problem and a reasonable approach to address it, contributing to the ongoing research in semi-supervised learning on graphs.
