## Summary

This paper introduces a computational framework for investigating the role of spontaneous muscle activation (SMAs), often referred to as 'intermittent behavioral variability' (IBV), in shaping dynamic body representations. The authors propose that IBV, mimicking the neural activity observed in the development of motor control, plays a crucial role in facilitating adaptive motor representations. To explore this hypothesis, they developed a simulation environment where a simple four-jointed agent, resembling a human finger, learns to reach targets. The core methodology involves training this agent using a neural network, with the input being the agent's joint angles and the output being the desired joint velocities. The key innovation lies in the introduction of an 'IBV model,' which is trained intermittently alongside the standard reaching task. This IBV model is inspired by the spontaneous muscle activations observed during rest and is designed to mimic the effects of intrinsic behavioral variability. The authors then compare the performance of agents trained with no IBV, pre-training with IBV, and intermittent IBV. Performance is measured in terms of the time taken to reach a target and the neural representational variability, quantified using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on the weight matrices. The main empirical finding is that intermittent IBV leads to better performance and higher neural representational variability compared to the other two conditions. This suggests that the intermittent exposure to behavioral variability can enhance the adaptability and flexibility of motor representations. The authors interpret their results as evidence that IBV facilitates the formation of more robust and flexible motor control strategies. The significance of this work lies in its novel computational approach to understanding the role of intrinsic behavioral variability in motor learning and adaptation. By using a simulation environment and a neural network model, the authors provide a concrete framework for investigating the effects of IBV, which could have implications for understanding motor adaptation in both biological and artificial systems. The paper also highlights the potential of using computational models to study the effects of intrinsic variability on motor control, which could be a valuable avenue for future research.
