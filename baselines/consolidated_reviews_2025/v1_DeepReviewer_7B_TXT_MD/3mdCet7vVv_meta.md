## Summary

This paper introduces Maestro, a novel method for training low-rank neural networks that dynamically adjusts the rank of each layer during training. The core idea revolves around a progressive shrinking technique, where the rank of each layer is gradually reduced according to a hierarchical group-lasso penalty. This penalty encourages a nested structure in the ranks across different layers, which the authors theoretically demonstrate can recover Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) under certain conditions. The method is presented as an alternative to traditional low-rank training techniques, aiming to improve efficiency and effectiveness. Empirically, Maestro is evaluated on a variety of datasets, including CIFAR-10, ImageNet, and various Transformer models, and compared against other low-rank training methods. The results suggest that Maestro achieves competitive or superior performance in terms of accuracy and computational efficiency compared to these baselines. The authors emphasize the method's computational efficiency, claiming it requires only a fraction of the resources needed for full-rank training. The paper also includes a theoretical analysis, demonstrating that Maestro can recover SVD and PCA under specific conditions. The method is presented as a training technique, where the low-rank structure is learned during training, rather than as a post-training compression method. The authors introduce a hierarchical group-lasso penalty to encourage a nested structure in the ranks across layers, which is a key aspect of their approach. The experimental results show that Maestro achieves competitive or superior performance compared to other low-rank training methods, while also being more computationally efficient. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different components of the method, such as the hierarchical group-lasso penalty and the progressive shrinking technique. Overall, the paper presents a novel and interesting approach to low-rank training, with promising empirical results and a solid theoretical foundation. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are several areas where the paper could be strengthened, particularly in terms of comparisons with pruning methods, computational cost analysis, and a more thorough discussion of limitations and robustness.
