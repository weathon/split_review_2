## Summary

This paper introduces Adaptive Prompt Prototype Learning (APPLe), a novel approach for adapting vision-language models (VLMs) to downstream tasks by learning multiple prompts per class, which are referred to as 'prototypes.' The core idea is to address the limitations of single-prompt approaches, which often fail to capture the inherent visual variance within a class. APPLe leverages a large language model (LLM), specifically GPT-3, to generate diverse prompts for each class, effectively creating a 'class prototype' that represents the various visual manifestations of that class. To further refine these prototypes, the authors introduce an adaptive attention mechanism. This mechanism assigns higher weights to 'representative' prototypes, which are identified by averaging the cosine similarities of all prototypes within a class and selecting those that are most similar to this average. This attention mechanism aims to focus on the most informative visual representations when computing the final class embedding. Additionally, a prototype decorrelation loss is incorporated to reduce the influence of co-occurring multiple prototypes, which is particularly beneficial in scenarios where visual samples are biased or limited. The method is evaluated on several tasks, including generalization to unseen classes, new target datasets, and domain generalization. The experimental results demonstrate that APPLe consistently outperforms existing methods, including CoOp, ProDA, PLOT, MaPLe, and CoCoOp, on various benchmarks. The authors also conduct an ablation study to analyze the impact of the number of prototypes on performance. Overall, the paper presents a simple yet effective method for adapting VLMs to downstream tasks by leveraging multiple prompts and an adaptive attention mechanism, achieving state-of-the-art performance on several benchmarks. However, as I will discuss in the weaknesses section, the paper could benefit from a more in-depth analysis of the proposed method and a more thorough comparison with existing approaches.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be commendable. First, the core idea of learning multiple prompts per class to capture visual variance is intuitive and well-motivated. The authors clearly articulate the limitations of single-prompt approaches and provide compelling examples of how a single prompt can fail to represent the full range of visual variations within a class. The use of GPT-3 to generate diverse prompts is a practical and effective way to create these class prototypes. The adaptive attention mechanism is another strength of the paper. By assigning higher weights to representative prototypes, the method effectively focuses on the most informative visual representations when computing the final class embedding. This mechanism is simple yet effective, and the authors provide a clear explanation of its implementation. The prototype decorrelation loss is also a valuable addition, particularly in scenarios with biased or limited visual samples. The experimental results are another strong point of the paper. The authors conduct a comprehensive evaluation of APPLe on several tasks, including generalization to unseen classes, new target datasets, and domain generalization. The results consistently demonstrate that APPLe outperforms existing methods, including CoOp, ProDA, PLOT, MaPLe, and CoCoOp, on various benchmarks. This provides strong empirical evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors clearly explain the proposed method and the experimental setup, making it accessible to a broad audience. The inclusion of an ablation study on the number of prototypes further strengthens the paper by providing insights into the sensitivity of the method to this hyperparameter. In summary, the paper presents a simple yet effective method for adapting VLMs to downstream tasks, supported by strong empirical results and a clear presentation.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, while the paper introduces the concept of multiple prompts and an adaptive attention mechanism, the novelty of the proposed method is somewhat limited. As pointed out by multiple reviewers, the idea of learning multiple prompts for each class has been explored in previous works such as MaPLe and CoCoOp. Similarly, the adaptive attention mechanism is similar to the attention mechanism used in CoCoOp, and the prototype decorrelation loss is similar to the decorrelation loss used in MaPLe. While the paper combines these ideas, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the unique aspects of its implementation or its advantages over existing methods. The paper could benefit from a more explicit discussion of how the adaptive attention mechanism and prototype decorrelation loss contribute to improved performance compared to existing methods. This lack of clear differentiation from prior work is a significant concern, as it makes it difficult to assess the true contribution of the proposed method.
