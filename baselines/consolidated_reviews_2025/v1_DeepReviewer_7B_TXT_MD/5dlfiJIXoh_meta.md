## Summary

This paper introduces a novel video-language pre-training framework aimed at improving the understanding of fine-grained details in both videos and text. The core contribution lies in the integration of two key design elements: inter-clip spatial grounding and intra-clip temporal grouping. The inter-clip spatial grounding mechanism is designed to align specific regions within video clips with corresponding words in text, thereby establishing fine-grained correspondences between visual and textual elements. This is achieved through a self-supervised approach that leverages a cut-and-paste operation to introduce temporal scene changes during pre-training, followed by the use of learnable group tokens and a clustering-based module to group semantically similar regions. The intra-clip temporal grouping, on the other hand, focuses on capturing temporal dynamics within individual video clips by distinguishing between foreground and background representations. This is accomplished through a clustering-based module that identifies and groups semantically similar regions, effectively learning temporal-aware features. The authors evaluate their proposed method on a range of downstream tasks, including text-video retrieval, video question answering, video action recognition, and temporal action localization. The results demonstrate that the proposed method outperforms existing approaches on these tasks, suggesting the effectiveness of the proposed pre-training strategy. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different pre-training datasets, training objectives, and model architectures. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and well-executed study that advances the field of video-language pre-training by introducing novel mechanisms for capturing fine-grained spatio-temporal relationships. The empirical results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed approach, and the ablation studies offer valuable insights into the design choices. However, as I will discuss in the following sections, there are some limitations that warrant further consideration.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, a significant limitation of the proposed method is its reliance on the assumption that input video clips are well-separated and do not contain significant temporal dynamics. As I verified, the method uses a fixed temporal window for grouping, as described in Section 3.1, where the start and end indices of the video clip are sampled and then a fixed window is used for temporal grouping. This approach, while computationally efficient, may not be suitable for videos with complex temporal structures and transitions. Specifically, the fixed window may not be able to capture long-range temporal dependencies, potentially leading to a loss of crucial information about the temporal evolution of events within the video. This is especially concerning when dealing with videos that have rapid scene changes or gradual transitions, where the fixed window might not be able to capture the relevant temporal context. This limitation is further supported by the fact that the method does not incorporate any mechanism for dynamically adjusting the temporal window size based on the video content. This is a significant oversight, as it restricts the method's ability to handle real-world videos with complex temporal dynamics. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the method description in Section 3.1. Second, the proposed method requires a large amount of computational resources and time for pre-training. The use of self-supervised learning with a large dataset like VideoCC, which contains 3.3M video-caption pairs, along with the additional computational cost of the proposed spatial grounding and temporal grouping modules, makes the method less practical for researchers with limited computational resources. As I verified, the model architecture in Section 3.2 includes a large pre-trained ViT-base model and additional modules like learnable group tokens and grouping blocks, which inherently increase the computational cost. The ablation study in Section 4.3.3 also highlights the importance of the pre-training dataset size, suggesting that the method is sensitive to the amount of pre-training data. Furthermore, the inference process, which includes the use of multiple attention mechanisms, could also be computationally expensive, making it difficult to deploy the model in real-time applications. This is a significant practical limitation, as it restricts the accessibility of the method to a smaller community. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the model architecture in Section 3.2 and the ablation study results in Section 4.3.3. Third, while the paper introduces novel mechanisms for spatial grounding and temporal grouping, the core pre-training objectives are based on existing methods. The paper uses a cut-and-paste operation and an intra-clip temporal grouping module, which are built upon existing pre-training objectives. While the specific design choices within these modules are novel, the underlying pre-training objectives are not. This limits the novelty of the proposed method, as it primarily combines existing techniques rather than introducing entirely new pre-training paradigms. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does introduce novel components, but the core objectives are not. Fourth, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with the latest works in the field. Specifically, the paper does not compare against Video-MME, EVA-02, or InternVideo-2, which are recent state-of-the-art models. This omission makes it difficult to fully assess the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method compared to the current state of the art. The absence of these comparisons limits the paper's ability to demonstrate the superiority of the proposed method over existing approaches. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the absence of these comparisons in the experimental section. Finally, the paper does not investigate the impact of varying the temporal window size for grouping on the performance of the model. As I verified, the method description in Section 3.1 uses a fixed window size `s` and `e` for temporal grouping, and the paper does not include experiments that vary this window size. This is a significant oversight, as it is possible that a smaller window might be more suitable for capturing rapid changes, while a larger window might be better for capturing long-range dependencies. An adaptive windowing approach, where the window size is adjusted based on the temporal characteristics of the video, could be a potential solution. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the method description in Section 3.1 and the lack of experiments on varying window size. In summary, these weaknesses highlight areas where the paper could be improved to enhance its robustness, practicality, and overall impact.
