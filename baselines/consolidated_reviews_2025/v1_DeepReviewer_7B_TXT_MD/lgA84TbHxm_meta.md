## Summary

This paper introduces a novel temperature scaling method, termed DySTreSS, designed to enhance contrastive learning by addressing the issue of overestimating the tolerance of hard negative samples in the InfoNCE loss function. The authors begin by observing that the standard InfoNCE loss, which treats all negative samples as positives, can lead to suboptimal feature representations. They propose that the temperature parameter, a crucial component of the InfoNCE loss, should be dynamically adjusted based on the cosine similarity between positive and negative samples. Specifically, they introduce a cosine-based temperature scaling function that increases the temperature for samples with low cosine similarity and decreases it for samples with high cosine similarity. This approach aims to control the learning dynamics of hard negatives, which are often treated as positives, and to improve the overall alignment of the feature space. The authors motivate their approach by theoretically analyzing the conditions under which the temperature function should have specific properties, leading to a cosine-based function. They then empirically validate their method on several benchmark vision datasets, demonstrating that DySTreSS outperforms existing contrastive learning methods. The core idea is that by dynamically scaling the temperature, the model can better distinguish between hard negatives and true negatives, leading to more robust and discriminative feature representations. The paper's main contribution lies in the proposed cosine-based temperature scaling method and its empirical validation. While the method is presented as a solution to the overestimation of hard negative tolerance, the paper lacks a direct theoretical link between the proposed temperature scaling and the mitigation of this specific issue. The empirical results show that the method improves performance, but the underlying mechanism remains somewhat opaque. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of the feature space, such as visualizations or quantitative metrics, to support the claim of a more uniform distribution of samples. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a simple and effective approach to temperature scaling in contrastive learning, which has the potential to improve the performance of self-supervised learning methods.
