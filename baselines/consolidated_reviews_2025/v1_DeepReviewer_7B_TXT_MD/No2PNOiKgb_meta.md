## Summary

This paper introduces a method for indoor scene decomposition that builds upon the work of Vavilala et al. (2023), primarily by incorporating negative primitives and an ensemble of networks with varying numbers of primitives. The core idea is to improve upon existing methods by explicitly including negative primitives, which represent the background, and by using an ensemble of networks, each trained with a different number of convex primitives, to refine the initial predictions. The method employs a standard convolutional neural network to predict initial parameters for a set of primitives, and then refines these predictions using a combination of training losses. The final decomposition is obtained by selecting the best prediction from the ensemble based on a refinement process. The authors evaluate their method on the NYUv2 dataset, demonstrating state-of-the-art results compared to the baseline method. The paper's main contribution lies in the specific combination of negative primitives and ensembling, rather than in introducing entirely novel techniques. While the empirical results are promising, the paper lacks a strong theoretical justification for the refine-then-select strategy, and the method's reliance on a fixed set of primitives limits its ability to adapt to novel scene structures. Furthermore, the experiments are primarily conducted on the NYUv2 dataset, which may not fully demonstrate the method's generalizability to more diverse and complex scenes. The paper also does not address the computational cost associated with ensembling multiple models, which could be a significant limitation for real-time applications. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of indoor scene decomposition, but it could be further strengthened by addressing the identified limitations.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a promising approach to indoor scene decomposition, several key weaknesses limit its overall impact. A primary concern is the lack of a strong theoretical justification for the refine-then-select strategy. The authors employ this strategy, where they first refine each predicted start point and then choose the one with the best error metrics. While the empirical results demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of why this specific order is optimal. There is no theoretical analysis to support the claim that refining before selection is superior to other possible selection strategies. This lack of theoretical grounding weakens the paper's contribution, as it leaves the reader wondering if the observed performance is due to the specific order of operations or if there is a more principled approach. Furthermore, the paper does not explore alternative selection strategies, such as weighted averaging or more sophisticated voting schemes, which could potentially lead to better performance or robustness. The current approach of selecting the best prediction based on a single metric, such as minimizing training loss, may be too simplistic and could be improved by considering multiple factors, such as depth accuracy, surface normal consistency, and semantic coherence. The paper's reliance on a fixed set of primitives is another significant limitation. The method uses a predefined number of primitives (K) in the initial regressors, and the ensemble combines predictions from different numbers of primitives. This approach limits the method's ability to adapt to novel scene structures, as it cannot dynamically adjust the set of primitives based on the scene content. This is a significant limitation, as real-world scenes can vary greatly in their complexity and structure. The experiments are primarily conducted on the NYUv2 dataset, which may not fully demonstrate the generalizability of the proposed approach to more diverse and complex scenes. The paper does not address the computational cost associated with ensembling multiple models, which could be a significant limitation for real-time applications. The authors provide the training time but lack a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the ensembling process itself, such as the time to combine predictions. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method, especially in resource-constrained environments. The paper also lacks a discussion of the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost, and it does not provide guidance on how to choose the number of models to ensemble based on the available resources. Finally, the paper does not address the memory footprint of the method, which could be a limiting factor for deployment on resource-constrained devices. These limitations, taken together, highlight the need for further research to improve the robustness, generalizability, and practical applicability of the proposed method.
