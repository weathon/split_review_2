## Summary

This paper introduces a novel framework for quantifying the generalizability of machine learning studies, a critical aspect often overlooked in the field. The authors propose a formal definition of generalizability, conceptualized as the probability that any two empirical studies, approximating the same ideal study, yield similar results. This definition is operationalized through a mathematical framework that quantifies the similarity between the results of different experimental conditions using the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) and evaluates the distance between the results using the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence. The core idea is that a generalizable study is one where the results are robust to variations in the experimental conditions. The paper also presents an algorithm to estimate the number of experiments required to achieve a desired level of generalizability. This algorithm leverages the similarity between the results of different experimental conditions to determine the minimum number of experiments needed to achieve a specified level of generalizability. The authors apply their framework to two case studies: a benchmark of categorical encoders and a benchmark of large language models (LLMs). These case studies demonstrate how the proposed approach can be used to assess the generalizability of existing benchmarks and to determine the minimum number of experiments needed to achieve a desired level of generalizability. The paper's main contribution lies in its formalization of generalizability and the development of a practical method for estimating the number of experiments needed to achieve a desired level of generalizability. This framework has the potential to improve the rigor and reliability of machine learning research by providing a principled way to assess and control the generalizability of experimental results. The authors' approach moves beyond the traditional focus on a single ideal study and instead considers the distribution of results across different experimental conditions, offering a more nuanced and realistic perspective on generalizability. The paper's methodology is grounded in sound statistical principles, making it a valuable contribution to the field of machine learning research. The authors also provide a practical algorithm for estimating the size of an empirical study based on a desired generalizability threshold, which can be used by researchers to design more efficient and effective experiments. Overall, this paper presents a significant step towards a more rigorous and reliable approach to machine learning research, with the potential to improve the quality and impact of future studies.
