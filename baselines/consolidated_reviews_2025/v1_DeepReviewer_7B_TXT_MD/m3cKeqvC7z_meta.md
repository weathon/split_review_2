## Summary

This paper introduces UlikeMamba, a novel approach to 3D medical image segmentation that leverages the state space model, Mamba, as an alternative to traditional Transformer architectures. The authors explore the applicability of Mamba for long-range dependency modeling in 3D medical images, addressing three key questions: Can Mamba replace Transformers for long-range dependency modeling? Can it enhance multi-scale representation learning? Are complex scanning strategies necessary? To investigate these questions, they propose a U-shaped network architecture incorporating 3D depthwise convolutions, multi-scale modeling, and a Tri-scan scanning strategy. The core methodological contribution lies in the specific integration of these components within the Mamba framework. The authors replace the 1D depthwise convolutions of Mamba with 3D depthwise convolutions to better preserve volumetric data's spatial coherence. They also introduce four multi-scale modeling schemes (MSv1 to MSv4), each combining features from different receptive fields using various strategies, including concatenation and parallel Mamba layers. Furthermore, they propose a Tri-scan scanning strategy, which involves scanning the input in three directions: left-right, up-down, and front-back, to capture a broader range of spatial relationships. The empirical findings, based on experiments conducted on three public 3D medical imaging datasets (AMOS, TotalSegmentator, and BraTS), demonstrate that UlikeMamba achieves competitive accuracy and computational efficiency compared to advanced models like nnUNet and CoTr. Specifically, the authors report Dice scores and FLOPs to evaluate the performance and efficiency of their proposed model and its variants. The results suggest that the proposed modifications, particularly the 3D depthwise convolutions and multi-scale modeling schemes, contribute to improved segmentation accuracy and efficiency. The Tri-scan strategy, while showing only marginal improvements in some cases, is presented as a way to capture a broader range of spatial relationships. Overall, the paper aims to establish Mamba as a viable and efficient alternative to Transformers for 3D medical image segmentation, highlighting the potential of Mamba's state space model for long-range dependency modeling in this domain. The authors position their work as a comprehensive exploration of Mamba's capabilities, addressing key questions and providing valuable insights for future research in 3D medical image segmentation.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach to 3D medical image segmentation using Mamba, several weaknesses limit its overall impact. A primary concern is the incremental nature of the proposed modifications. The authors introduce 3D depthwise convolutions, multi-scale modeling schemes, and the Tri-scan scanning strategy, but these are, in themselves, not novel techniques. As I've verified, the paper states that they replace the original 1D depthwise convolutions with 3D depthwise convolutions (Section 4.2). Similarly, the multi-scale modeling and Tri-scan scanning, while empirically shown to improve performance, are specific implementations within the Mamba architecture rather than fundamental innovations. This lack of substantial novelty is a significant limitation, as it reduces the paper's contribution to the field. The paper does not adequately explore the theoretical underpinnings of why these specific combinations are optimal for 3D medical image segmentation. While the authors provide empirical results, they do not delve into the theoretical reasons behind the observed performance differences. For instance, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the spectral properties of the 3D depthwise convolutions or the receptive fields of the multi-scale modeling layers. This lack of theoretical justification weakens the paper's argument and limits its potential impact. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational trade-offs associated with each modification. While the authors present FLOPs and parameter counts, they do not provide a nuanced discussion of the computational overhead of each component. For example, the paper does not analyze whether the individual components of the multi-scale modeling strategy are redundant or if they contribute uniquely to the overall performance. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the efficiency gains and losses associated with each modification. Furthermore, the experimental evaluation lacks diversity in terms of data types and modalities. The paper primarily focuses on three public datasets: AMOS, TotalSegmentator, and BraTS. While these datasets are relevant, they do not represent the full spectrum of medical imaging modalities and segmentation challenges. As I've verified, the paper explicitly states the use of these three datasets (Section 5). Testing on a wider range of datasets, including those with different image characteristics and segmentation challenges, would strengthen the generalizability claims. The paper also does not provide a detailed analysis of the performance on different classes within the segmentation masks. The authors report overall Dice scores, but they do not analyze performance on different types of structures or classes within the segmentation masks. This lack of class-wise analysis could reveal potential biases or limitations of the proposed method. For example, the model might perform better on simpler or more complex structures, and this would not be captured by overall Dice scores alone. This limitation makes it difficult to assess the model's performance on different types of anatomical structures. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of their approach and potential avenues for future research. While the conclusion briefly mentions future research, it does not address the computational cost of the proposed method, explore alternative architectures for the Mamba network, or investigate the robustness of the method to different types of noise and artifacts. This lack of discussion limits the paper's potential impact and leaves several open questions for future research. The paper also does not address how the proposed method could be adapted or extended to other volumetric data modalities, such as CT or MRI scans. This lack of discussion limits the paper's applicability and potential impact. In summary, the paper's limitations stem from the incremental nature of its modifications, the lack of theoretical justification, the limited diversity in the experimental evaluation, and the lack of a detailed discussion of the limitations and future research directions. These weaknesses significantly impact the paper's overall contribution and highlight the need for further investigation.

A discussion of potential biases and strategies for mitigating them would be valuable. Fourth, what are the limitations of the proposed approach, and what are the potential avenues for future research?
