## Summary

This paper introduces 3DTrajMaster, a novel approach for controllable video generation that focuses on manipulating multi-entity 3D motions. The core idea is to enable precise control over the movement of entities in 3D space, which is achieved through a plug-and-play 3D-motion grounded object injector. This injector fuses entity prompts and 3D trajectories, allowing for fine-grained control over video generation. The authors address the challenge of limited training data by constructing a synthetic dataset called 360-Motion, which features diverse entities and various 3D motion templates generated using Unreal Engine and advanced rendering techniques. The method employs a gated self-attention mechanism to fuse entity prompts and 3D trajectories, enhancing the model's ability to handle multiple entity trajectories. The model is trained using a combination of synthetic data and a domain adaptor to improve its generalization ability. The experimental results demonstrate that 3DTrajMaster achieves state-of-the-art accuracy and generalization in controlling 3D entity motions, showcasing its effectiveness in generating high-fidelity videos with precise entity movements. The authors present a well-structured paper, providing a detailed explanation of the proposed method, including the dataset construction process, model architecture, and experimental setup. The figures and diagrams are informative and effectively illustrate the key concepts and technical details. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in controllable video generation by enabling precise manipulation of multi-entity 3D motions, which is a notable departure from traditional 2D control signals. The use of a synthetic dataset and the gated self-attention mechanism are key contributions that enhance the model's ability to handle complex 3D motion control. The paper's focus on 3D motion control is a significant step forward in the field of video generation, offering new possibilities for applications in areas such as animation, robotics, and virtual reality.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant limitation is the reliance on a synthetic dataset for training. The authors explicitly state that they construct the 360-Motion dataset using Unreal Engine and GPT, which generates 3D trajectory templates for animated 3D assets (Section 3.3). This approach, while offering diversity, may limit the model's ability to generalize to real-world scenarios. The synthetic data, while diverse, might not capture the full range of complexities and variations present in real-world videos, particularly in terms of lighting, occlusions, and dynamic backgrounds. This could lead to a domain shift issue, where the model performs well on the training data but struggles to generalize to unseen real-world videos. The paper does not include any experiments on real-world video datasets, which further exacerbates this concern. The lack of real-world data makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper explicitly states the use of synthetic data and does not provide any evidence of real-world performance. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method, especially for real-time applications or deployment on resource-constrained devices. The computational cost of the method is a significant barrier to its widespread adoption. ## Suggestions

To address the limitations identified, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to mitigate the domain gap between the synthetic and real-world data, future work should focus on expanding the training dataset to include more diverse real-world scenarios. This could involve incorporating videos with varying lighting conditions, occlusions, and dynamic backgrounds. Furthermore, data augmentation techniques could be explored to artificially increase the diversity of the training data and improve the model's robustness. For example, techniques such as random rotations, scaling, and color jittering could be applied to the synthetic data to simulate different real-world conditions. Additionally, the model could be trained on a combination of synthetic and real-world data to leverage the strengths of both. This would require careful consideration of the domain gap between the two datasets and the development of techniques to bridge this gap effectively. The authors should also investigate the use of domain adaptation techniques to improve the model's generalization ability to real-world scenarios. This would involve techniques such as adversarial training or domain-invariant feature learning. Second, to address the lack of computational cost analysis, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the computational resources required for training and inference. This should include information on training time, memory usage, and inference speed. The authors should also explore techniques to reduce the computational cost of the method, such as model compression, quantization, and efficient implementation of the attention mechanisms. For example, the authors could investigate the use of sparse attention or low-rank approximations to reduce the computational complexity of the self-attention layers. Furthermore, the authors should consider optimizing the implementation of the method for different hardware platforms, such as GPUs and TPUs, to improve its efficiency. A thorough analysis of the computational cost would provide a more complete understanding of the method's practical feasibility and guide future research efforts. Finally, the authors should consider exploring the use of more advanced evaluation metrics that can capture the quality of the generated videos more comprehensively. While the current metrics provide some insight into the performance of the method, they may not fully capture the nuances of video quality and motion accuracy. For example, metrics such as perceptual quality scores, temporal consistency scores, and motion smoothness scores could be used to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method. Additionally, the authors should consider conducting user studies to assess the subjective quality of the generated videos and to identify any potential issues that may not be captured by the objective metrics. This would provide a more holistic evaluation of the method and guide future improvements.
