## Summary

This paper introduces a local-global algorithm for approximating shortest path distances on random graphs, specifically focusing on Erdős–Rényi (ER) graphs. The core idea is to leverage Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to compute local path distances, which are then combined with a global computation step based on the triangle inequality to obtain approximate shortest paths. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of the algorithm's performance on ER graphs, demonstrating a $(1 	ext{±} 	ext{ε})$-approximation with high probability for graphs with bounded average degree. The paper also presents empirical results on both synthetic ER graphs and benchmark datasets, showing that the proposed GNN-based approach outperforms a vanilla local-global algorithm. The authors claim that their work is the first to provide a theoretical analysis of local-global algorithms for shortest path problems on ER random graphs. The paper's main contribution lies in the integration of GNNs into the local computation phase of the algorithm, which is intended to improve the quality of the local path distance estimates. The authors argue that this approach offers a more efficient and accurate way to compute shortest paths in large-scale networks, particularly in the context of ER graphs. However, the paper's analysis and experimental validation are limited in scope, and several key aspects of the method and its performance require further investigation. The paper's focus on ER graphs, while providing a theoretical framework, raises questions about the generalizability of the results to other types of graphs. The lack of comparison with other state-of-the-art shortest path algorithms and the limited discussion of the experimental results also weaken the paper's overall impact. Despite these limitations, the paper presents an interesting approach to integrating GNNs into local-global algorithms for shortest path computation, and the theoretical analysis on ER graphs provides a valuable contribution to the field. However, the paper requires significant improvements in its literature review, theoretical analysis, and experimental evaluation to fully realize its potential.
