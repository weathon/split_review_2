## Summary

This paper presents a theoretical analysis of diffusion models, positioning them within a framework that connects them to subspace clustering. The authors propose that diffusion models, when trained on real-world image datasets, learn the underlying data distribution by effectively solving a subspace clustering problem. They introduce the concept of a 'mixture of low-rank Gaussians' (MoLRG) as a model for real-world image data, demonstrating empirically that denoising autoencoders (DAEs) trained on real-world datasets exhibit low-dimensional structures, supporting this assumption. The core theoretical contribution lies in establishing an equivalence between the training loss of diffusion models and the subspace clustering problem. This equivalence is derived under certain assumptions, including the MoLRG data model and specific approximations made in the parameterization of the diffusion model. The authors then leverage this theoretical connection to derive a scaling law for the number of training samples required to learn the underlying distribution. This scaling law suggests that the number of samples should be proportional to the intrinsic dimensionality of the data, which is estimated from the data itself. The paper presents empirical evidence to support the theoretical findings, demonstrating the phase transition in the generalization ability of diffusion models with increasing training samples and the alignment of the intrinsic dimension with the number of subspaces. Furthermore, the authors show that the subspaces learned by diffusion models correspond to semantic representations of image data, which can be used to control image generation. The overall significance of this work lies in providing a theoretical framework for understanding how diffusion models learn image distributions and how the number of training samples affects this learning process. This work offers a new perspective on diffusion models, moving beyond empirical observations to provide a more fundamental understanding of their behavior. The paper also highlights the potential of diffusion models for controlling image generation by manipulating the learned subspaces, which could have implications for various applications. However, the paper's theoretical analysis relies on several simplifying assumptions, and the practical implications of the derived scaling law are not fully explored. The empirical validation, while supportive, is limited in scope and does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art methods. Despite these limitations, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the theoretical understanding of diffusion models and opens up new avenues for future research.
