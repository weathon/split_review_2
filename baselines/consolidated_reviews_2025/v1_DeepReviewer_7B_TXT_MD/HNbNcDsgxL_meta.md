## Summary

This paper introduces Delta, a novel inference-time decoding method designed to mitigate hallucinations in large language models (LLMs). The core idea behind Delta is to refine the output of an LLM by contrasting the results of processing the original input with the results of processing a masked version of the input. The masking process randomly obscures portions of the input sequence, forcing the model to rely more heavily on contextual cues and less on potentially hallucinated elements. The outputs are then compared, and the logits (log-odds) associated with hallucinated tokens are reduced by subtracting a value derived from this contrastive decoding process. This method aims to filter out less reliable predictions without requiring any additional fine-tuning or changes to the model's parameters. The authors evaluate Delta on a range of question-answering datasets, including SQuAD v1.1, SQuAD v2, TriviaQA, and Natural Questions, demonstrating that it can improve performance, particularly in context-rich scenarios. The experimental results show that Delta achieves notable gains in accuracy and F1 score on these datasets, suggesting that it is effective at reducing hallucinations when the input context is well-defined. However, the paper also reveals that Delta's performance is limited on tasks that do not rely heavily on context, such as CommonsenseQA and MMLU, where it often shows only marginal or no improvement. This suggests that Delta's effectiveness is tied to the presence of contextual information within the input. The authors also provide an ablation study that explores the sensitivity of Delta to different masking ratios and logit ratios, demonstrating that the method is relatively robust to these hyperparameters. Overall, the paper presents a simple yet effective method for reducing hallucinations in LLMs at inference time, with strong empirical results on context-rich question-answering tasks. However, the paper also highlights the limitations of the method, particularly on tasks that lack strong contextual dependencies, and the need for further research to explore its applicability to a broader range of tasks and models. The paper's contribution lies in its practical approach to hallucination mitigation, offering a computationally efficient method that can be easily applied to existing LLMs without requiring additional training.
