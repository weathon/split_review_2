## Summary

This paper introduces ODEFormer, a novel transformer-based model designed for the symbolic regression of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) from time series data. The core contribution lies in its ability to infer the underlying equations governing a system's dynamics directly from observed trajectories, a task that is traditionally challenging due to the complexity of ODEs and the often noisy nature of real-world data. The authors leverage a transformer architecture, which is well-suited for capturing long-range dependencies in sequential data, and adapt it to the specific challenges of ODE inference. A key aspect of their approach is the use of a numerical integration scheme to generate training data, which allows them to create a large dataset of ODEs with known solutions. This dataset, ODEBench, is presented as a valuable resource for the community. The model is trained to predict the symbolic representation of the ODE, such as a polynomial or trigonometric expression, given a time series of observations. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of ODEFormer through experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets, showing that it outperforms existing methods in terms of robustness to noise and irregular sampling, while also maintaining computational efficiency. The paper also includes a discussion of the limitations of the current approach, such as the restriction to first-order ODEs, and suggests directions for future research. Overall, this work presents a significant step towards automating the discovery of physical laws from data, which has the potential to accelerate scientific discovery in various fields. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors have also made their code and benchmark dataset publicly available, which will facilitate further research in this area. The use of a transformer architecture for symbolic regression of ODEs is a novel approach, and the results presented in the paper are promising. The creation of the ODEBench dataset is also a valuable contribution to the community, as it provides a standardized benchmark for evaluating methods for symbolic regression of ODEs. The authors have clearly demonstrated the potential of their method to handle noisy and irregularly sampled data, which is a common challenge in real-world applications. The paper is a significant contribution to the field of symbolic regression and has the potential to impact various scientific disciplines.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach to symbolic regression of ODEs, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed method. While the authors acknowledge that they only considered first-order ODEs in their experiments (as stated in both the "Discussion and Future Directions" and "Conclusion" sections), they do not provide a broader discussion on the types of ODEs that ODEFormer can and cannot handle. For instance, the paper does not address the challenges associated with systems that exhibit chaotic behavior, high-dimensional dynamics, or complex non-linearities. This lack of a comprehensive analysis makes it difficult to assess the scope and applicability of the method. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the performance of the method on systems with known identifiability issues, which could reveal potential weaknesses in the model's ability to recover the true underlying equations. This is a significant limitation, as real-world systems often exhibit these characteristics. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states the limitation to first-order ODEs but lacks a broader discussion on other limitations. Second, the paper provides insufficient details on the computational cost of ODEFormer. While the authors mention that ODEFormer runs on the order of seconds, compared to minutes for other methods except SINDy (as stated in Section 4, "Benchmarking Dynamical Symbolic Regression Methods", "Reconstruction on \odebench{}"), they do not provide a detailed quantitative analysis of training and inference times for ODEFormer and other methods. The paper mentions the hardware used for training (NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPUs) and the number of training epochs, but it does not provide a breakdown of the time spent on different stages of the process, such as data preprocessing, model training, and equation evaluation. This lack of detail makes it difficult to fully assess the practical efficiency of ODEFormer. Additionally, the paper does not discuss the scalability of the method to larger datasets and more complex models, which is an important consideration for real-world applications. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper provides only a high-level comparison of computational time and lacks a detailed quantitative analysis. Finally, the paper does not discuss the potential biases in the training data or the evaluation metrics. The authors describe the process of generating the training data, which includes filtering out unstable systems and divergent trajectories (as described in Section 3, "Model, Training, and Inference"). However, they do not analyze the impact of these filtering steps on the performance of ODEFormer or discuss potential biases in the training data generation process. Similarly, the paper defines an evaluation metric based on a threshold, but it does not discuss the potential biases in this metric or consider alternative metrics that may provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model's performance. This lack of a bias analysis weakens the robustness and reliability of the conclusions drawn from the experiments. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks any discussion on potential biases in the training data or evaluation metrics.
