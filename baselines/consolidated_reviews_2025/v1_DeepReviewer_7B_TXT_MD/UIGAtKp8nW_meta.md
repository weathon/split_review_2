## Summary

This paper introduces MUBEN, a benchmark designed to evaluate uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods within the context of molecular representation models. The core contribution lies in its comprehensive evaluation of various UQ techniques across a diverse set of molecular property prediction tasks. The authors investigate the performance of these methods when applied to different molecular representation models, including both deterministic and ensemble-based approaches, and across different UQ methods, such as Deep Ensembles, Monte Carlo Dropout, and Bayesian Neural Networks. The study employs a standardized experimental protocol, utilizing scaffold splitting to minimize dataset randomness and consistent hyperparameter grids to ensure fair comparisons. The main empirical findings suggest that Deep Ensembles generally outperform other deterministic baselines, while temperature scaling and Monte Carlo dropout are effective for classification tasks, and Bayesian methods are more suitable for regression tasks. A key observation is that Uni-Mol, which incorporates 3D molecular conformations, exhibits poorer calibration compared to simpler descriptors, suggesting that shared structural features in training and test molecules can lead to overconfidence. The authors also highlight the importance of model architecture and task type in selecting appropriate UQ methods. Overall, the paper provides a valuable resource for researchers in the field by offering a systematic comparison of UQ methods in molecular property prediction, which is crucial for reliable predictions, especially in real-world applications where overconfidence can lead to unreliable predictions. The benchmark's focus on uncertainty quantification is particularly relevant given the increasing use of large-scale models in drug discovery and other areas of molecular property prediction. The authors' detailed analysis of the results, including the performance of different UQ methods across various models and tasks, offers valuable insights for practitioners seeking to select the most appropriate UQ method for their specific needs. The paper's emphasis on the limitations of existing benchmarks and the need for more robust evaluation protocols further underscores its significance in advancing the field of molecular property prediction.

While the authors provide some general insights, such as the tendency of Uni-Mol to be overconfident, they do not offer a systematic approach for practitioners to choose the most appropriate UQ method. For example, the paper does not provide a decision tree or a set of rules of thumb based on task type (classification vs. regression) and model architecture (e.g., Uni-Mol vs. DNN).
