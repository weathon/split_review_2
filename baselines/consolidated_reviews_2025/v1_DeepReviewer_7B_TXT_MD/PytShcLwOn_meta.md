## Summary

This paper introduces a novel task, SIGHT-Fusion, which aims to generate realistic and diverse 3D hand trajectories from single images of objects. The authors propose a pipeline that extracts features from the input image and uses a conditional diffusion model to generate the hand trajectories. The pipeline includes extracting object and part features, which are then used to condition the diffusion model. The authors evaluate their method on two datasets, FPHAB and HOI4D, and demonstrate its effectiveness through quantitative metrics and a physics simulation setting. The results show that the proposed method outperforms several baselines, including adaptations of whole-body motion generation methods and a text-based hand motion generation method. The paper also includes ablation studies to validate the importance of different components of the pipeline. While the task is novel and the results are promising, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of practical applications, a broader comparison with existing hand motion generation methods, and a more comprehensive analysis of the method's limitations. The authors also need to address the potential failure cases of the method, such as when the input image is of poor quality or when the hand is occluded, and provide a more detailed analysis of the dataset's limitations to better understand the scope and limitations of the proposed method.

## Strengths

The paper makes several significant contributions to the field of hand motion generation. Firstly, the introduction of the SIGHT-Fusion task is a novel and challenging problem that has not been extensively explored before. The task of generating 3D hand trajectories from single images is crucial for various applications, including robotics, augmented reality, and computer vision. The authors provide a clear and well-motivated problem statement, which sets the stage for their methodological contributions. Secondly, the proposed pipeline for extracting features and generating hand trajectories is technically sound and well-designed. The use of a conditional diffusion model is appropriate for this task, and the authors provide a detailed explanation of the feature extraction process, including the use of object and part features. The pipeline is well-structured and easy to follow, which enhances the reproducibility of the work. Thirdly, the empirical evaluation is comprehensive and well-executed. The authors compare their method against several strong baselines, including adaptations of whole-body motion generation methods and a text-based hand motion generation method. The results show that the proposed method outperforms these baselines in terms of accuracy and diversity, as measured by metrics such as ACC, DIV, and FID. The ablation studies further validate the importance of the different components of the pipeline, providing a clear understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses. Lastly, the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their method in a physics simulation setting, which is a significant step towards real-world applications. The ability to generate realistic hand trajectories that can successfully complete intended actions in a simulation environment highlights the practical relevance of the proposed method.

## Weaknesses

Despite the novel and promising contributions, the paper has several limitations that need to be addressed. Firstly, the motivation for the SIGHT-Fusion task, while mentioned, could be more compelling. The authors briefly touch upon the potential applications of generating realistic hand trajectories, such as in robotics, augmented reality, and computer vision. However, they do not provide specific examples of how this task would be used in these domains or a detailed discussion of the unique challenges and benefits of the task. For instance, in robotics, the ability to generate hand trajectories that are not only realistic but also efficient and adaptive to different scenarios is crucial. The authors should discuss how this task could be used to enable a robot to interact with objects in a more natural and intuitive way, such as in assembly tasks or in providing virtual assistance. In augmented reality, the ability to generate realistic hand trajectories would be essential for creating immersive experiences, such as guiding a virtual character or interacting with 3D models. The authors should provide concrete examples to illustrate the practical applications of their research and how it could lead to more innovative uses of hand trajectory generation. Secondly, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison with existing methods for hand motion generation. While the authors adapt state-of-the-art baselines from the whole-body motion generation literature, they do not include a broader range of hand motion generation methods, if available and relevant. This makes it difficult to assess the novelty and effectiveness of the proposed approach in the context of the broader field. The authors should compare their method with relevant baselines, such as methods that use different types of input data, such as skeleton data or video data, and methods that use different types of generative models, such as GANs or VAEs. A quantitative and qualitative comparison would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's performance and its advantages and limitations. Thirdly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed method. The authors mention the use of the HOI4D dataset, which contains manipulations with already empty objects, leading to generated trajectories that outperform ground-truth trajectories. However, they do not discuss other potential failure cases, such as when the input image is of poor quality or when the hand is occluded. A more thorough discussion of these limitations would provide a more balanced and realistic assessment of the method's capabilities. Additionally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the dataset's limitations, such as the number of objects and the diversity of hand motions. The authors should discuss how the dataset could be expanded to include more diverse and challenging scenarios, which would help to improve the generalizability of the proposed method. The evaluation of the proposed method's generalization capability is also somewhat limited. The authors claim that the method generalizes well to unseen objects, but the evaluation is based on only four objects from the HOI4D dataset. A more rigorous evaluation would involve testing the method on a larger and more diverse set of objects, including those with varying shapes, sizes, materials, and occlusions. The authors should also consider using a more rigorous evaluation protocol, such as cross-validation, to ensure that the results are not biased by the specific choice of objects. Furthermore, the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the computational cost of the method and how it compares to existing methods. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's practical feasibility and scalability. Lastly, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of the potential applications of this task beyond the examples mentioned in the introduction. The authors should discuss how this task could be used in virtual try-on scenarios, where a user might want to see how a specific clothing item would look on them with a realistic hand pose. They could also explore the potential of this task in robotics, where understanding the hand's motion is essential for manipulation tasks. Providing concrete examples would significantly strengthen the motivation for this research.
