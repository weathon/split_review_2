## Summary

This paper introduces a novel synthetic graph navigation task designed to investigate the stepwise inference capabilities of transformer models, particularly in the context of logical reasoning. The authors conceptualize stepwise inference as a graph traversal problem, where a transformer must navigate a graph by applying a sequence of primitive operations, mimicking a chain-of-thought approach. The core idea is that the ability of a transformer to solve this task effectively reflects its capacity for stepwise inference. The authors generate synthetic datasets using two types of directed acyclic graphs: Bernoulli DAGs, which allow for varying edge densities, and hierarchical DAGs, which follow a feedforward structure. These graphs are constructed with specific edge probabilities and are used to represent input data, with the goal of determining if a given input corresponds to a path between a start and goal node. The model is tasked with predicting whether such a path exists, and the output is a sequence of tokens representing the path. The authors then conduct a series of experiments, primarily using a 2-layer Transformer model, to explore the impact of various factors on the model's performance. These experiments include varying the sampling temperature to observe the diversity-accuracy tradeoff, incorporating exemplars to mimic in-context learning, and analyzing the model's preference for the shortest path. The key findings reveal that stepwise inference does indeed improve the model's performance, there is a diversity-accuracy tradeoff in sampling temperature, the model prefers shorter paths, and the model can generalize to unseen combinations of motifs and longer paths. These results suggest that transformers can benefit from a stepwise approach to reasoning, and that their internal mechanisms align with the proposed graph navigation abstraction. The authors also explore the model's ability to generalize to unseen combinations of motifs and longer paths, which is a crucial aspect of real-world reasoning tasks. The paper's overall significance lies in its attempt to provide a controlled environment for studying stepwise inference in transformers, offering insights into the mechanisms underlying this type of reasoning. By framing the problem as a graph navigation task, the authors provide a novel perspective on how transformers might perform complex reasoning tasks, and their findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the capabilities and limitations of these models in logical inference.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The most compelling is the introduction of a novel synthetic graph navigation task to study stepwise inference in transformers. This approach is innovative and provides a much-needed controlled environment for investigating this type of reasoning. By framing the problem as a graph traversal task, the authors have effectively translated a complex cognitive process into a tractable computational problem. This is a significant contribution, as it allows for a more systematic and rigorous analysis of stepwise inference in transformers. Furthermore, the authors' decision to use a synthetic dataset allows for precise control over the input data and the underlying graph structure. This is crucial for isolating the factors that influence the model's performance and for understanding the specific mechanisms at play. The use of Bernoulli and hierarchical DAGs, with specific edge densities, enables the exploration of different graph structures and their impact on the model's ability to perform stepwise inference. The experiments are well-designed and clearly presented, allowing for a thorough analysis of the results. The authors systematically explore the impact of various factors, including sampling temperature, exemplars, and path length, on the model's performance. The results are presented with appropriate visualizations and statistical analysis, making it easy to interpret the findings. The observation that stepwise inference improves the model's performance is a key finding, and it provides empirical support for the proposed abstraction. The diversity-accuracy tradeoff, which is explored by varying the sampling temperature, is also an important contribution, as it highlights the limitations of relying solely on greedy decoding. The finding that the model prefers shorter paths is also interesting and suggests that transformers may have an implicit bias towards efficiency in their reasoning process. Finally, the authors' analysis of the model's generalization ability, both to unseen combinations of motifs and to longer paths, is a crucial step towards understanding the model's ability to perform real-world reasoning tasks. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its innovative approach, its rigorous methodology, and its insightful findings, which collectively contribute to a deeper understanding of stepwise inference in transformers.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A significant limitation is the lack of a dedicated 'Related Work' section. This absence makes it difficult to contextualize the authors' findings within the existing literature on stepwise inference in transformers. Without a thorough review of prior studies, it is challenging to assess the novelty and significance of the results. For example, the paper does not compare its findings to any existing work on stepwise inference in transformers, which makes it hard to determine how the authors' results align with or differ from previous studies. This lack of context is a major oversight, as it prevents a comprehensive understanding of the current state of research in this area. Furthermore, the paper's experimental setup is limited by the use of a single model architecture. The authors exclusively use a 2-layer Transformer, which, while intended to mimic the GPT architecture, does not explore the full range of transformer capabilities. This limitation raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings to other model sizes and architectures. The authors do not investigate how the model's performance might be affected by different numbers of layers, attention mechanisms, or pre-training objectives. This lack of exploration limits the scope of the conclusions and makes it difficult to assess the universality of the observed phenomena. Similarly, the paper relies solely on a single type of synthetic dataset. The authors do not explore the impact of different graph structures, such as density or connectivity patterns, on the model's performance. This limitation raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings to other graph structures and real-world graph navigation tasks. The authors do not investigate whether the observed effects are specific to the generated dataset or if they generalize to more complex or real-world graph structures. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed approach. While the authors briefly mention future directions, they do not provide a thorough discussion of the potential biases in the synthetic task, the generalizability of the findings, or the computational cost of the approach. For example, the authors do not discuss how the specific graph structures used in the synthetic task might influence the results, or how the findings might differ if applied to more complex or real-world graph navigation tasks. This lack of discussion limits the scope of the conclusions and makes it difficult to assess the practical implications of the findings. Finally, while the paper does explore the diversity-accuracy tradeoff by varying the sampling temperature, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the relationship between the sampling temperature and the model's exploration-exploitation trade-off. The paper also lacks a detailed explanation of the mechanisms through which the proposed graph navigation task relates to stepwise inference in transformers. While the task is presented as a proxy, a more detailed explanation of how the model's internal representations and inference steps correspond to the graph traversal process is needed. For instance, how does the model's attention mechanism facilitate the step-by-step decision-making process, and how does the graph structure influence the model's ability to perform stepwise inference? These limitations, which are supported by my direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the overall conclusions and highlight the need for further investigation.
