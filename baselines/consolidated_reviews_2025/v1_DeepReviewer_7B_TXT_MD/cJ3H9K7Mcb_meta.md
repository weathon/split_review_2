## Summary

This paper delves into the critical issue of out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, specifically examining the brittleness of robustness under varying degrees of distribution shifts. The authors challenge the common assumption that better performance on moderate distribution shifts implies better performance on more extreme shifts. Through a series of experiments on image classification tasks using datasets like NoisyMNIST, RotatedMNIST, NoisyImageNet15, and LR-ImageNet15, they demonstrate that models exhibiting strong performance on datasets with mild shifts can exhibit surprisingly poor performance on datasets with more severe shifts. This observation highlights a significant vulnerability in current OOD generalization methods, suggesting that models may not be as robust as previously assumed when faced with more diverse or challenging data. Furthermore, the study investigates the behavior of pre-trained models, such as CLIP, under novel distribution shifts and finds them to be particularly sensitive, often performing worse than randomly initialized models on datasets with unseen shifts. The core methodology involves training various models, including CNNs, ResNets, and ViTs, on datasets with controlled degrees of distribution shift and then evaluating their performance on test sets with different shift magnitudes. The main empirical findings reveal a consistent pattern of performance degradation as the degree of distribution shift increases, even for models that perform well under less extreme conditions. This brittleness is not merely a matter of model architecture or training strategy, but rather a fundamental property of OOD generalization. The authors also explore the impact of different training strategies, such as linear probing, on the observed robustness. The overall significance of this work lies in its identification of a critical flaw in current OOD generalization practices, emphasizing the need for more robust evaluation protocols and methods that can handle the complexities of real-world distribution shifts. The paper's findings underscore the importance of considering the full spectrum of possible distribution shifts, rather than focusing on limited ranges, and highlight the limitations of current pre-trained models in handling truly novel scenarios. This work serves as a valuable contribution to the field by exposing the fragility of current OOD methods and pointing towards the need for more robust approaches.

## Strengths

This paper presents a compelling and well-executed investigation into the brittleness of OOD generalization under varying degrees of distribution shifts. The authors have successfully identified a critical gap in our understanding of how models perform under different shift magnitudes, a point that is often overlooked in the literature. The core strength of this work lies in its empirical rigor and the clear demonstration of the observed phenomenon. The experiments are comprehensive, covering a range of datasets and model architectures, and the results are consistently presented and analyzed. The use of multiple datasets, including NoisyMNIST, RotatedMNIST, NoisyImageNet15, and LR-ImageNet15, allows for a thorough exploration of the impact of different types of distribution shifts, such as noise and rotation. The authors' focus on evaluating models under different degrees of shift, rather than just within a limited range, is a significant contribution. This approach reveals a more nuanced understanding of model robustness than previous studies. Furthermore, the paper's emphasis on the sensitivity of pre-trained models like CLIP to novel distribution shifts is particularly noteworthy. This finding highlights a critical limitation of current pre-training strategies and suggests that these models may not be as robust as previously assumed. The authors' work also provides a valuable resource for the community by making their code and datasets publicly available, which will facilitate further research in this area. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the findings accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their motivation, methodology, and results, and the conclusions are well-supported by the experimental evidence. The paper's overall contribution is significant, as it challenges some of the underlying assumptions of current OOD generalization methods and points towards the need for more robust approaches. The authors have successfully highlighted a critical issue that needs to be addressed in future research, and their work will serve as a valuable reference for the community.

## Weaknesses

While this paper makes a valuable contribution to the field, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's conclusion, while acknowledging the brittleness of robustness, does not offer concrete, actionable steps for improving model robustness. The authors state that they should be very careful when interpreting evaluation results based on data under a limited range of shift degrees, but they do not provide specific guidance on how to address this issue. This lack of actionable insights limits the practical impact of the work. Second, the paper lacks a theoretical framework to explain the observed brittleness. While the experiments are comprehensive, they do not provide a mechanistic understanding of why models exhibit such inconsistent behavior. The authors do not explore potential theoretical frameworks that could shed light on the underlying mechanisms causing this behavior. This absence of theoretical analysis limits the generalizability of the findings and makes it difficult to predict when and why brittleness might occur. Third, the paper's investigation into the impact of different types of distribution shifts is limited. The authors primarily focus on noise and rotation, but it is unclear whether the observed brittleness is specific to these types of shifts or if it generalizes to other types, such as changes in color, style, or object appearance. The datasets used, such as NoisyMNIST and RotatedMNIST, are designed to systematically vary the degree of distribution shift, but they do not represent the full spectrum of real-world distribution shifts. This narrow focus limits the generalizability of the findings and raises questions about the robustness of the conclusions. Fourth, the paper does not explore the effectiveness of different model architectures in handling distribution shifts. While the authors use different architectures, such as CNNs, ResNets, and ViTs, they do not systematically investigate how different architectural choices *within* a specific architecture family affect robustness to different shifts. The experiments primarily focus on comparing different training strategies across architectures rather than systematically varying architectural properties. This lack of architectural analysis limits the understanding of the factors that contribute to the observed brittleness. Fifth, the paper's experimental scope is limited to image classification tasks. The authors do not investigate whether the observed brittleness generalizes to other types of tasks, such as object detection or semantic segmentation. This limitation raises questions about the applicability of the findings to other areas of machine learning. Finally, the paper's investigation into the impact of different types of distribution shifts is limited to Gaussian noise and rotation. The authors do not explore other types of distribution shifts, such as changes in object pose, viewpoint, or background. This limited scope raises questions about the generalizability of the findings to more complex real-world distribution shifts. These limitations, while not invalidating the core findings, highlight areas where further research is needed to fully understand the brittleness of OOD generalization.

Second, the authors should explore potential theoretical frameworks that could explain the observed brittleness. The authors could also consider using information-theoretic measures to quantify the amount of information about the distribution shift that is encoded in the model's parameters or activations. This could provide a more principled way to understand the relationship between the degree of shift and the model's performance. Third, the authors should expand their investigation to include a wider range of distribution shift types and model architectures. This could involve examining other types of shifts, such as changes in color, style, or object appearance, to understand the generalizability of the findings. For semantic segmentation, they could assess the robustness of models to changes in object appearance, lighting conditions, and viewpoint. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to explore the impact of different model architectures on the observed brittleness. Fifth, the authors should investigate a wider range of distribution shift types. While Gaussian noise is a common type of perturbation, it is not representative of all real-world distribution shifts. The authors should consider other types of shifts, such as changes in object pose, viewpoint, background, and lighting conditions. This would help to determine whether the brittleness of OOD generalization is specific to Gaussian noise or if it is a more general phenomenon. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the underlying mechanisms that cause the observed brittleness. This could involve exploring the internal representations of the models and analyzing how they change under different types of distribution shifts. For example, the authors could visualize the feature maps of the models and to analyze how they are affected by different types of perturbations. This would provide a more mechanistic understanding of the observed brittleness. Furthermore, the authors could investigate the impact of different regularization techniques on the brittleness of OOD generalization. For example, they could explore the use of adversarial training or data augmentation techniques to improve the robustness of models to distribution shifts. This would provide practical guidance for developing more robust models. By addressing these limitations, the authors can significantly strengthen their work and provide more valuable insights for the community.
