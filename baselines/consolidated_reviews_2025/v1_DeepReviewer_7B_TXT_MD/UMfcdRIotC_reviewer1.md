### Summary

The paper addresses the challenge of explaining black-box NLP models by proposing two approaches for counterfactual (CF)-based model explanation. The first approach involves generating CFs using large language models (LLMs) and measuring their causal effect on the model's predictions. The second approach focuses on learning a latent embedding space that aligns with causal graphs, enabling efficient matching of CFs. The authors also introduce a new benchmark dataset, CEBaB, which consists of human-annotated, high-quality CFs for model explanation tasks. The study evaluates the proposed methods across multiple models and demonstrates that both approaches achieve state-of-the-art performance in model explanation.

While the paper compares against several baselines, it does not include a comparison with methods that are specifically designed for model explanation, such as LIME or SHAP. This makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of the proposed methods compared to existing approaches. The absence of such comparisons limits the paper's ability to position its contributions within the broader landscape of model explanation techniques.

### Suggestions

Specifically, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the time complexity for each step of the LLM-based CF generation process, including the number of forward passes, the average response time, and the associated financial cost. This analysis should also consider the impact of different LLM sizes and prompting strategies on the computational cost. Furthermore, the paper should quantify the cost of embedding generation and matching within the latent space, including the time required for training the embedding model and the cost of using the learned embeddings for matching. This would provide a more complete picture of the computational resources required for each approach and allow for a more informed assessment of their practical applicability. The authors should also discuss the trade-offs between computational cost and explanation quality, and provide guidance on how to choose the most appropriate method for a given application.

This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed methods compared to existing approaches. The authors should also discuss the limitations of the proposed methods and the conditions under which they are likely to perform poorly, such as datasets with complex causal relationships or high-dimensional input spaces.
