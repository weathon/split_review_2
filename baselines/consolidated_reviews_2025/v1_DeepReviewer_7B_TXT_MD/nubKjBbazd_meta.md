## Summary

This paper introduces Adversarial Perturbation Dropout (APD), a novel method aimed at enhancing the transferability of adversarial examples across different target models. The core idea behind APD is to disrupt the synergy of perturbations across different attention regions by employing a dropout mechanism on adversarial images. The authors propose that by randomly dropping perturbations in different regions of the image, the adversarial example can maintain its effectiveness even when part of the perturbation is not aligned with the target model's attention. This is achieved by using Grad-CAM++ to identify the most important regions of the image, dropping perturbations in these regions, and then using the modified images to update the adversarial perturbation. The method is motivated by the observation that adversarial perturbations often focus on specific regions of an image, and by disrupting this focus, the adversarial example can be more robust to transfer attacks. The authors conduct experiments to evaluate the effectiveness of APD, comparing it against several baseline methods, including MI-FGSM, DIM, TIM, SIM, and AA-TI-DIM. The results, presented in terms of attack success rates, suggest that APD can achieve better transferability than these baselines. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different parameters, such as the number of centers and scales used in the perturbation dropout. While the paper presents a seemingly novel approach to enhancing adversarial transferability, my analysis reveals significant limitations in its novelty and experimental validation. The core mechanism of applying dropout to perturbations, while presented as a key contribution, lacks a clear distinction from existing input transformation techniques. Furthermore, the experimental evaluation, while including several baselines, does not sufficiently demonstrate the superiority of APD over a broader range of state-of-the-art methods. The absence of a direct comparison with AA-TI-DIM as a standalone method, and the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method, further weaken the experimental validation. Despite these limitations, the paper addresses an important problem in the field of adversarial robustness and provides a starting point for further research in this area.

## Weaknesses

My primary concern with this paper lies in the limited novelty of the proposed method and the insufficient differentiation from existing input transformation techniques. The core idea of using a dropout mechanism on adversarial perturbations, while presented as a key contribution, bears a striking resemblance to other input transformation methods that aim to reduce transferability. Specifically, the paper states, "To achieve this, we employ dropout Srivastava et al. (2014), a commonly used yet simple method to improve the generalization of deep models, to improve the transferability of adversarial examples." (Section 3.4). This statement, coupled with the method description in Section 3.4, which details the dropout mechanism applied to adversarial perturbations, suggests that the method is essentially applying a dropout to the input space, a concept that has been explored in various forms in the literature. The paper does not sufficiently differentiate the proposed method from existing input transformation techniques, particularly in how the perturbation dropout mechanism fundamentally differs from other input space manipulations to reduce transferability. This lack of clear differentiation undermines the novelty claim of the paper. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the specific parameters used in the dropout mechanism and how these parameters affect the transferability of the adversarial examples. While the paper includes an ablation study on the hyperparameter β (Section 4.3), it does not explore the impact of other dropout parameters, such as the probability of dropping a perturbation or the size of the regions being dropped. This lack of a detailed analysis of the method's parameters limits the understanding of the method's behavior and its potential for further optimization. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's method description and related work section do not sufficiently address the similarities and differences with existing input transformation techniques. Another significant weakness is the insufficient experimental validation. While the paper includes comparisons with several baseline methods, including MI-FGSM, DIM, TIM, SIM, and AA-TI-DIM, the experimental setup and analysis are not comprehensive enough to fully demonstrate the effectiveness and novelty of the proposed method. The paper also lacks a comprehensive comparison with other relevant transfer attacks, such as MI-FGSM, DIM, TIM, SIM, and AA-TI-DIM, in the single model attack section. This limited comparison makes it difficult to assess the true effectiveness and novelty of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the experimental section clearly lacks a direct comparison with AA-TI-DIM as a standalone method and a comprehensive comparison with a wider range of state-of-the-art transfer attacks.
