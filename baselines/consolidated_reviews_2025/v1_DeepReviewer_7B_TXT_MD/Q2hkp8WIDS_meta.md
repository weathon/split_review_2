## Summary

This paper introduces OC-STORM, a model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) algorithm that incorporates object-centric representations to enhance sample efficiency in visually complex environments. The core idea is to leverage pre-trained foundation models, specifically Cutie for object detection and a VQ-VAE for feature encoding, to extract object features from raw pixel observations. These object features are then combined with the raw visual input and used to train a world model via a categorical variational autoencoder (CVAE). The learned world model is subsequently used to generate simulated trajectories for policy training using model-free RL. The authors evaluate OC-STORM on two benchmark suites: Atari 100k and the visually complex game Hollow Knight. The empirical results demonstrate that OC-STORM outperforms the baseline STORM on 18 out of 26 Atari games and achieves the best-known sample efficiency on several Hollow Knight bosses. The authors argue that their object-centric approach allows the agent to focus on decision-relevant details, leading to improved performance in visually complex environments. The paper's main contribution lies in the integration of object-centric learning with a model-based RL framework, aiming to address the limitations of traditional MBRL methods that rely on auto-encoding with L2 loss. The authors claim that their approach is simple and intuitive, yet effective in improving sample efficiency. However, the paper's claims are not fully supported by the analysis, and several limitations exist, particularly in the motivation, experimental design, and clarity of the method. The paper's approach is a step towards addressing the challenges of applying MBRL in visually complex environments, but it requires further refinement and validation to fully realize its potential. The authors' attempt to use object-centric representations is a promising direction, but the lack of a strong theoretical justification and the limited experimental comparisons make it difficult to fully assess the method's true contribution. The paper's findings suggest that incorporating object-centric learning into MBRL can be beneficial, but further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and to explore the method's limitations. The authors' work highlights the potential of using pre-trained foundation models to improve the performance of MBRL agents in complex environments, but the paper's current form needs significant improvements in terms of motivation, experimental design, and clarity.

## Summary:

This paper introduces OC-STORM, a model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) algorithm that incorporates object-centric representations to enhance sample efficiency in visually complex environments. The core idea is to leverage pre-trained foundation models, specifically Cutie for object detection and a VQ-VAE for feature encoding, to extract object features from raw pixel observations. These object features are then combined with the raw visual input and used to train a world model via a categorical variational autoencoder (CVAE). The learned world model is subsequently used to generate simulated trajectories for policy training using model-free RL. The authors evaluate OC-STORM on two benchmark suites: Atari 100k and the visually complex game Hollow Knight. The empirical results demonstrate that OC-STORM outperforms the baseline STORM on 18 out of 26 Atari games and achieves the best-known sample efficiency on several Hollow Knight bosses. The authors argue that their object-centric approach allows the agent to focus on decision-relevant details, leading to improved performance in visually complex environments. The paper's main contribution lies in the integration of object-centric learning with a model-based RL framework, aiming to address the limitations of traditional MBRL methods that rely on auto-encoding with L2 loss. The authors claim that their approach is simple and intuitive, yet effective in improving sample efficiency. However, the paper's claims are not fully supported by the analysis, and several limitations exist, particularly in the motivation, experimental design, and clarity of the method. The paper's approach is a step towards addressing the challenges of applying MBRL in visually complex environments, but it requires further refinement and validation to fully realize its potential. The paper's findings suggest that incorporating object-centric learning into MBRL can be beneficial, but further research is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and to explore the method's limitations. The authors' work highlights the potential of using pre-trained foundation models to improve the performance of MBRL agents in complex environments, but the paper's current form needs significant improvements in terms of motivation, experimental design, and clarity.
