## Summary

This paper introduces a novel framework, the Delayed Observation Markov Decision Process (DOMDP), to address the challenge of signal delay in deep reinforcement learning (DRL). The authors argue that signal delays, common in real-world applications, can significantly degrade the performance of DRL algorithms by misaligning the agent's perception of the environment with its actual state. To tackle this issue, the paper proposes a series of techniques aimed at mitigating the impact of these delays. These include a Delay-Reconciled Training for Critic (DTC), which attempts to reconcile historical information to overcome the effects of delays, State Augmentation for Actor (SA), which augments the actor's input to facilitate training, and Complementary Techniques for DOMDP Resolution, which includes prediction and encoding-based auxiliary loss. The core idea is to provide the agent with a more accurate representation of the environment by incorporating past observations and actions, thereby improving the learning process. The authors evaluate their proposed methods on several continuous robotic control tasks using MuJoCo environments, demonstrating that their approach can achieve performance comparable to non-delayed scenarios. The experimental results show that the proposed methods are effective in reducing the negative impact of signal delays, and the paper includes ablation studies to analyze the contribution of each technique. The paper's significance lies in its attempt to address a critical issue in DRL, namely the impact of signal delay on learning performance. By introducing the DOMDP framework and proposing a set of techniques to mitigate the effects of delay, the authors contribute to a deeper understanding of DRL in the presence of delays and provide a potential solution for improving the performance of DRL agents in real-world applications. However, the paper's contribution is somewhat limited by the fact that the core ideas of incorporating historical information are not entirely novel, and the paper lacks a strong theoretical justification for why the proposed methods are particularly well-suited for the delay problem. Furthermore, the experimental evaluation is limited to continuous robotic control tasks, which may not fully capture the complexity and diversity of real-world applications. Despite these limitations, the paper provides a valuable contribution to the field of DRL and opens up avenues for future research in this area.
