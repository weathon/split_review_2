## Summary

This paper introduces Newton Loss, a novel approach to improve the training of differentiable algorithms by replacing their standard loss functions with a Newton loss based on the empirical Fisher or Hessian. The core idea is to leverage second-order optimization techniques to address the challenges posed by non-convex loss landscapes, particularly those arising from hard-to-optimize algorithms. The authors propose two variants of Newton Loss: one using the empirical Fisher information matrix and another using the empirical Hessian matrix. The method is presented as a way to locally approximate the loss function with a quadratic model, allowing for more efficient optimization compared to traditional gradient descent. The paper evaluates the proposed method on two distinct tasks: ranking and shortest path problems. In the ranking task, the authors compare their approach against differentiable sorting algorithms, demonstrating improved performance in terms of both the percentage of correctly identified rankings and the accuracy of individual element ranks. In the shortest path problem, they apply Newton Loss to differentiable relaxations of the Bellman-Ford algorithm, showing improvements in the percentage of perfect matches. The empirical results suggest that the proposed Newton Loss can effectively enhance the performance of differentiable algorithms, particularly in scenarios where the loss function is highly non-convex. The authors also explore the impact of the hyperparameter λ, which controls the strength of the second-order regularization, and find that it plays a crucial role in the performance of the method. The paper's main contribution lies in the application of Newton loss to differentiable algorithms, providing a new perspective on how to address the challenges of non-convex optimization in this domain. While the core concept of Newton loss is not entirely novel, its specific application and the introduction of the two variants using empirical Fisher and Hessian represent a valuable contribution to the field. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. However, the simplicity of the method and the limited scope of the experimental evaluation raise some concerns about its novelty and generalizability, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths

One of the primary strengths of this paper is the simplicity and clarity of the proposed method. The idea of replacing the standard loss function with a Newton loss based on the empirical Fisher or Hessian is straightforward to implement and understand. This simplicity makes the method accessible and easy to adopt for researchers and practitioners working with differentiable algorithms. The authors clearly articulate the motivation behind using a Newton loss, highlighting the limitations of first-order methods in navigating non-convex loss landscapes. The introduction of two variants of Newton Loss, one using the empirical Fisher and the other using the empirical Hessian, is a valuable contribution. This allows for flexibility in the choice of regularization and provides insights into the impact of different second-order information on the optimization process. The empirical results presented in the paper are also a significant strength. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of Newton Loss on two distinct tasks: ranking and shortest path problems. In both cases, they show that their method outperforms existing differentiable algorithms, indicating its potential for practical applications. The experiments on ranking, using the MNIST dataset, show improvements in both the percentage of correctly identified rankings and the accuracy of individual element ranks. Similarly, the experiments on shortest path, using the Warcraft dataset, demonstrate improvements in the percentage of perfect matches. These results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method in improving the performance of differentiable algorithms. This analysis provides valuable insights into the behavior of the proposed method and helps to understand the role of second-order information in the optimization process. Overall, the strengths of this paper lie in its simplicity, clarity, empirical effectiveness, and the introduction of novel variants of Newton Loss.
