## Summary

This paper introduces a novel token-level pruning method, termed Fine-Grained Token Pruner (FTP), designed to reduce the computational overhead of large language models (LLMs) during inference. The core idea revolves around identifying and skipping less important tokens dynamically as they propagate through the model's layers. FTP achieves this through a learnable router, a lightweight neural network, which assesses the importance of each token based on four low-dimensional factors. This router is trained using a combination of three loss functions, aiming to optimize the sparsity pattern while preserving the model's performance. The method also incorporates a sparsity scheduler, which is initially searched for using a Genetic Algorithm and then fine-tuned to allocate different sparsity ratios to different layers. The authors conduct extensive experiments on various LLMs, including LLaMA2-7B, LLaMA2-13B, LLaMA3-8B, and Qwen1.5-7B, and evaluate the performance on several benchmarks such as HellaSwag, MMLU, ARC-easy, ARC-challenge, and WinoGrande. The results demonstrate that FTP achieves better accuracy retention compared to other state-of-the-art pruning methods at similar sparsity levels. The paper claims that FTP is a significant step towards deploying LLMs in resource-constrained environments without retraining, offering a practical solution to the computational challenges posed by these models. Overall, the paper presents a technically sound and empirically validated approach to token-level pruning, contributing a novel method that shows promise for improving the efficiency of LLM inference. However, several limitations, particularly regarding the lack of detailed computational analysis and comparisons with other token-level pruning methods, need to be addressed to fully assess the practical implications of the proposed method.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the proposed method, FTP, introduces a fine-grained, token-wise pruning approach that is more nuanced than traditional block-level pruning techniques. The use of a learnable router to assess token importance dynamically is a significant advancement, allowing for adaptive and context-sensitive pruning. This is a novel contribution that addresses the limitations of static pruning methods. Second, the paper provides a comprehensive set of experiments across different LLMs and benchmarks, demonstrating the robustness and effectiveness of FTP. The results consistently show that FTP outperforms other pruning methods in terms of accuracy retention at comparable sparsity levels. This extensive empirical validation strengthens the claims made by the authors. The motivation for the work is also clear: to reduce the computational demands of LLMs during inference without requiring retraining, which is a critical issue for deploying these models in real-world applications. The authors have clearly identified a significant problem and proposed a practical solution. Furthermore, the introduction of a sparsity scheduler, which is initially searched for using a Genetic Algorithm and then fine-tuned, is a valuable addition that allows for more flexible and efficient sparsity allocation across different layers. The combination of the learnable router and the sparsity scheduler makes FTP a powerful and adaptable pruning method. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the technical details accessible to a broad audience. The authors have clearly explained the method, the experiments, and the results, which facilitates a thorough understanding of their work.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with recent token-level pruning methods, specifically AOT-Sparse and Dynamic LLM Pruning. While the paper mentions other token-level pruning methods in the related work section, it fails to discuss or benchmark against these two relevant methods. This omission is significant because these methods also address token-level optimization in LLMs, and a direct comparison is necessary to fully understand the advantages and disadvantages of FTP. The absence of these comparisons makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of FTP in the context of the current state-of-the-art. Second, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational and memory costs associated with FTP, particularly the overhead introduced by the token router. While the authors describe the router as a "lightweight neural network" and a "two-layer MLP", they do not provide a quantitative analysis of the additional FLOPs, latency, or memory footprint introduced by the router during both training and inference. This lack of analysis is a significant limitation because the overhead of the router could potentially negate some of the benefits of token pruning, especially for shorter sequences where the relative cost of the router might be higher. The paper only provides speedup ratios, which do not offer a complete picture of the computational costs. Third, the paper does not provide a clear explanation for why the methods mentioned in the first weakness (AOT-Sparse and Dynamic LLM Pruning) perform worse than FTP in Table 1, especially considering that these methods achieve better performance in their original papers.
