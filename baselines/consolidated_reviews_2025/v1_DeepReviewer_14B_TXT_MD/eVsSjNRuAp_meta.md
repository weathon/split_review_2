## Summary

This paper introduces Predictive Differential Training (PDT), a novel framework for accelerating the training of deep neural networks by leveraging Koopman operator theory. The core idea is to view the training process as a nonlinear dynamical system in the weight space and use Koopman operator theory to predict the network's weights in future epochs, thereby bypassing computationally expensive Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) iterations. PDT incorporates differential learning rates, applying different learning rates to different parts of the network based on the prediction quality. The framework includes a masking strategy to select parameters with good prediction performance and an acceleration scheduler to manage prediction errors. The authors conduct experiments on various benchmark models (e.g., AlexNet, ResNet, and ViT) and datasets (e.g., CIFAR-10 and ImageNet), demonstrating that PDT consistently outperforms baseline methods in terms of faster convergence, lower training and testing loss, and fewer epochs to achieve the best loss. However, the paper has several limitations, including a lack of detailed computational complexity analysis, insufficient theoretical justification for the masking strategy, limited exploration of other neural network architectures, and instability at larger prediction steps. Additionally, the paper does not provide code for reproducibility, which hinders the validation of the experimental findings.

## Strengths

The paper presents a compelling and innovative approach to deep neural network training by framing the process as a nonlinear dynamical system and applying Koopman operator theory. This perspective is refreshing and offers a new way to understand and optimize the training dynamics of neural networks. The authors provide a thorough introduction to the necessary background concepts, such as Koopman operator theory and Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD), making the paper accessible to readers with a background in machine learning and dynamical systems. The methodological approach is well-organized and clearly described, with a focus on the masking strategy and acceleration schedule, which are designed to ensure stable and efficient training. The empirical evaluation is comprehensive, with experiments conducted on various benchmark models and datasets, including AlexNet, ResNet, and ViT on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet. These experiments provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of PDT, showing consistent improvements in training and testing loss, as well as faster convergence compared to baseline methods. The paper also includes a detailed analysis of the impact of different hyperparameters, such as the prediction horizon and the frequency of Koopman operator updates, which helps to understand the practical implementation of the method. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field by introducing a novel training framework that leverages advanced mathematical concepts to improve the efficiency of deep learning models.

## Weaknesses

Despite the innovative contributions, the paper has several notable weaknesses that need to be addressed. One of the primary concerns is the computational overhead associated with the Koopman operator theory and DMD. The authors claim that the computational load is manageable, but the paper lacks a rigorous breakdown of the time and memory complexity, especially for very large models. For instance, the SVD operation, which is a key component of DMD, has a complexity of \\(\bar{O(N \times h^2)}\\), where \\(N\\) is the number of parameters and \\(h\\) is the number of past epochs used for the snapshot matrix. While the authors argue that \\(h\\) is typically small (5 to 10), the quadratic dependence on \\(h\\) can become significant as the model size increases. Moreover, the memory footprint for storing the necessary snapshots for DMD reconstruction is not discussed, which could be a limiting factor for very large models (high confidence, based on Section 3.3 and the absence of a detailed computational analysis). This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of PDT for large-scale models, and a more thorough investigation of the computational cost, including empirical results on models of varying sizes, would be beneficial.

Another significant weakness is the heuristic nature of the proposed masking strategy and acceleration schedule. The authors introduce two criteria—quantity and direction—for selecting parameters with good prediction performance. However, the paper does not provide a theoretical justification for why these specific criteria are optimal or sufficient for ensuring stable and efficient training. The quantity criterion requires the absolute weight change between the predicted and current weights to be larger than the change from one-step optimization, while the direction criterion ensures consistency with the optimization direction. These criteria are presented with intuitive justifications but lack a formal analysis of their impact on the training dynamics, stability, and convergence properties (high confidence, based on Section 3.1 and the absence of theoretical proofs). A more in-depth theoretical analysis would strengthen the paper's contributions and provide a better understanding of the method's behavior.

The paper's experimental evaluation is limited in scope, focusing primarily on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and image classification tasks. While the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of PDT on models like AlexNet, ResNet, and ViT, they do not explore its applicability to other types of neural networks, such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) or Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). The dynamics of these models can be significantly different from CNNs, and it is unclear how PDT would adapt to these different scenarios. For example, RNNs have temporal dependencies that might require a different approach to predicting weight dynamics, and GNNs have structural dependencies that could necessitate a different masking strategy (high confidence, based on the limited experimental scope in Section 4). Additionally, the paper does not discuss the potential challenges and limitations of applying PDT to different domains, such as natural language processing or reinforcement learning. This limitation in the experimental evaluation makes it difficult to assess the generality of the proposed method and its potential impact on the broader machine learning community.

The method exhibits instability when the prediction steps are large, as shown in Figure 9(a). The training loss starts to increase significantly beyond a certain number of prediction steps, indicating that the method is not robust in the long term. This instability is a major concern, as it limits the practical applicability of PDT. The authors do not provide a clear analysis of the trade-off between acceleration and stability, nor do they offer guidelines for choosing the prediction parameters in practice (high confidence, based on Figure 9(a) and the lack of a detailed analysis). A more thorough investigation of the method's sensitivity to different hyperparameter settings, especially the prediction horizon and the frequency of Koopman operator updates, is necessary to understand its limitations and ensure stable training.

The paper does not compare PDT with other existing training acceleration methods, which makes it difficult to assess its relative performance. The authors compare PDT with standard optimizers like SGD, SGD with momentum, and Adam, but they do not include comparisons with techniques such as adaptive learning rate methods (e.g., Adam, RMSprop), second-order optimization methods (e.g., Newton, BFGS), or other methods that exploit training dynamics. This lack of comparison is a significant limitation, as it does not provide a complete picture of PDT's strengths and weaknesses (high confidence, based on the experimental setup in Section 4). A more comprehensive evaluation, including comparisons with state-of-the-art training acceleration techniques, would be essential to demonstrate the practical utility of PDT.

Finally, the paper does not provide the code for reproducibility, which is a major limitation. This should include a formal justification for why the chosen quantity and direction criteria are effective in selecting parameters with good prediction performance. The authors could explore the relationship between these criteria and the stability of the optimization process, as well as the convergence properties of the algorithm. A theoretical analysis of the conditions under which the proposed masking strategy guarantees convergence would be a significant contribution. Furthermore, the authors could investigate alternative masking strategies and compare their performance with the proposed approach, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the design choices.

This should include a discussion of the potential challenges and limitations of applying PDT to these different architectures. For instance, the temporal dependencies in RNNs might require a different approach to predicting the weight dynamics, and the structural dependencies in GNNs might necessitate a different masking strategy. The authors should also discuss the potential applicability of PDT to other domains, such as natural language processing and reinforcement learning, and identify the specific challenges that might arise in these contexts. This would help to establish the generality of the proposed method and its potential impact on the broader machine learning community.

It is crucial to provide clear guidelines on how to choose these parameters to achieve a good balance between acceleration and stability. The authors could explore adaptive prediction strategies that adjust the prediction step based on the training dynamics, which might help to mitigate the instability. Additionally, the authors should investigate the reasons behind the instability at larger prediction steps and propose potential solutions to address this issue.

To provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method's training acceleration capabilities, the authors should compare PDT with a wider range of established acceleration techniques. This should include methods such as various learning rate schedules (e.g., cosine annealing, cyclical learning rates), momentum-based optimizers, and adaptive gradient methods. The comparison should not only focus on the number of epochs to convergence but also on the actual training time, considering the computational overhead of the proposed method. A detailed ablation study would help to understand the contribution of each component of the proposed method and identify potential bottlenecks.

Finally, the authors must provide the code for reproducibility. This is essential for the scientific community to validate the results and build upon the proposed method. The code should be well-documented and easy to use, including clear instructions on how to reproduce the experiments presented in the paper. The authors should also consider releasing the code under an open-source license to facilitate further research and development.
