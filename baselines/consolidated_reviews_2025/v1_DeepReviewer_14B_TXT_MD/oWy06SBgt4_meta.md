## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to fully quantized training (FQT), pushing the boundaries of low-precision neural network training by exploring 1-bit quantization for weights, activations, and gradients. The authors' core contribution lies in their development of a 1-bit FQT framework, which includes two key techniques: Activation Gradient Pruning (AGP) and Sample Channel joint Quantization (SCQ). AGP is designed to mitigate the high variance introduced by 1-bit gradient quantization by selectively pruning activation gradients, thereby allocating more computational resources to gradients with larger ranges. SCQ, on the other hand, addresses the challenge of unaccelerated weight gradient computation by employing a combination of per-channel and per-sample quantization strategies. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence behavior of FQT with both Adam and SGD optimizers, highlighting the impact of gradient variance on convergence and demonstrating that SGD is more sensitive to gradient variance than Adam. Empirically, the proposed method is evaluated on transfer learning tasks across vision and NLP domains, showing that it outperforms per-sample quantization (PSQ) baselines. Furthermore, the authors demonstrate significant training speedups on both Hygon and Raspberry Pi devices, achieving up to 5.13x speedup compared to full-precision training. The paper's significance lies in its attempt to push the limits of FQT to the extreme, exploring the feasibility of 1-bit quantization for both weights, activations, and gradients. This is a challenging area, and the authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field by introducing novel techniques and demonstrating their effectiveness on real hardware. The paper also provides a theoretical analysis of the convergence of FQT with Adam and SGD, which is a valuable contribution to the field. The experimental results show that the proposed method outperforms per-sample quantization on transfer learning tasks in both vision and NLP domains, and can achieve a significant training speedup on Hygon and Raspberry Pi devices. The paper's focus on hardware-friendly techniques and real-world speedups makes it a valuable contribution to the field of low-precision neural network training. However, the paper also has some limitations, which I will discuss in detail in the 'Weakness' section.
