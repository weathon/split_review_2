## Summary

This paper introduces PGODE, a novel approach for modeling interacting dynamical systems by integrating disentangled contextual information with a prototypical graph ODE framework. The core idea revolves around extracting both object-level and system-level contexts from historical trajectories, which are then used to inform a continuous graph ODE model. The object-level contexts capture individual object dynamics, while the system-level contexts encapsulate the overall system parameters and interactions. The authors propose a method to disentangle these two levels of context using mutual information minimization, aiming to improve the model's generalization capabilities, particularly in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. To enhance the model's expressivity, PGODE employs a set of learnable GNN prototypes, which are combined to model the evolution of each object. This prototype decomposition allows the model to capture diverse interaction patterns within the system. The entire model is trained end-to-end using a variational inference framework, maximizing the likelihood of the observed trajectories. The authors evaluate PGODE on both physical and molecular dynamics simulations, demonstrating its superior performance compared to several baselines, including other GNN-based methods and ODE-based approaches. The experimental results show that PGODE achieves better accuracy, especially in OOD settings, suggesting that the disentanglement of object-level and system-level contexts contributes to improved generalization. The paper also includes ablation studies to validate the contribution of each component of the model, such as the disentanglement module and the prototype decomposition. The authors further analyze the sensitivity of the model to the number of prototypes, finding that performance improves with an increasing number of prototypes up to a certain point, after which the computational cost becomes a limiting factor. Overall, the paper presents a technically sound and empirically validated approach for modeling complex interacting dynamical systems, with a focus on improving generalization and expressivity through disentanglement and prototype decomposition. The proposed method offers a promising direction for future research in this area, with potential applications in various fields, including physics, chemistry, and robotics.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the paper claims that the proposed method can alleviate the issue of out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, I found the explanation of *how* the disentanglement module achieves this to be somewhat lacking. The paper states that minimizing the mutual information between object-level and system-level contexts is intended to make object representations invariant to system changes, which is a common strategy for improving OOD generalization. However, the paper does not explicitly link this disentanglement to the model's ability to generalize to unseen system parameters. While the experimental results in OOD settings provide empirical evidence supporting the claim, the theoretical link between disentanglement and OOD generalization could be more clearly articulated. For example, the paper could benefit from a more detailed explanation of how the disentanglement allows the model to learn system-invariant object representations, and how this leads to better OOD performance. This lack of a clear theoretical explanation weakens the claim of OOD generalization. My confidence in this issue is medium, as the empirical results do support the claim, but the theoretical justification is not fully developed. Second, the paper's experimental evaluation of OOD generalization is not sufficiently comprehensive. While the paper includes results for both in-distribution and out-of-distribution settings, the OOD scenarios are limited to variations in system parameters within the same type of dataset. For example, the OOD settings in the physical dynamics simulations involve changes in spring constants and charges, while the molecular dynamics simulations involve changes in solvent parameters. However, the paper does not explore more challenging OOD scenarios, such as varying the system size or the number of interacting particles. This is a significant limitation, as real-world systems often exhibit variations in both system parameters and system size. The absence of experiments with varying system sizes makes it difficult to assess the model's robustness and generalizability to different types of OOD scenarios. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper clearly does not include experiments with varying system sizes. Finally, while the paper includes a theoretical analysis regarding the existence and uniqueness of the proposed graph ODE, it lacks a more in-depth theoretical analysis of the model's behavior. Specifically, the paper does not provide any analysis of the model's convergence properties, stability, or generalization bounds. While the existence and uniqueness result is relevant to the claims about modeling dynamics, a more rigorous theoretical analysis would help to provide a deeper understanding of the model's behavior and its limitations. For example, the authors could analyze the conditions under which the proposed model is guaranteed to converge to a stable solution, or they could derive bounds on the generalization error of the model. The absence of such analysis weakens the theoretical foundation of the proposed approach. My confidence in this issue is medium, as the paper does include some theoretical analysis, but it could be more comprehensive.
