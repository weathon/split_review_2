## Summary

This paper introduces VChangeCodec, a novel neural speech codec designed for real-time communication (RTC) that integrates voice conversion (VC) capabilities directly into the codec architecture. The core idea is to embed a lightweight causal projection network within the encoding module, allowing for seamless switching between the original voice and a customized voice mode. This approach aims to address the limitations of traditional cascaded VC-codec systems, which often suffer from high latency and computational overhead. The proposed codec leverages a fully causal convolutional encoder-decoder network with scalar quantization (SQ) for efficient speech compression. The voice conversion is achieved by adapting the codec tokens using the causal projection network, which is conditioned on the target speaker's timbre information extracted through openSMILE. The model is trained using a combination of reconstruction, adversarial, feature matching, perceptual, and token commitment losses. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of VChangeCodec through comprehensive subjective and objective evaluations, showing superior performance compared to state-of-the-art voice conversion methods in terms of speech quality and timbre adaptation. The model achieves a low latency of 40 ms and requires fewer than 1 million parameters, making it suitable for real-time communication applications. The paper presents a novel approach to integrating voice conversion into a speech codec, offering a promising solution for real-time applications with customized voice features.

## Strengths

I find the integration of voice changing capabilities directly into the codec to be a particularly novel and compelling aspect of this work. This approach effectively addresses the limitations of traditional cascaded VC-codec systems, which often introduce significant latency and computational overhead. By embedding the voice conversion module within the encoding process, the authors have created a more streamlined and efficient system. The proposed method achieves impressive efficiency, with a very low latency of 40 ms and a small number of parameters (<1M), making it highly suitable for real-time communication applications. This low latency is crucial for real-time communication scenarios, and the small model size is beneficial for deployment on resource-constrained devices. Furthermore, the paper provides a thorough evaluation of the proposed method, including both subjective and objective assessments, demonstrating its effectiveness in terms of speech quality and timbre adaptation. The use of metrics such as DNSMOS, MCD, and speaker similarity, along with subjective evaluations, provides a comprehensive view of the model's performance. The authors also compare their method against several state-of-the-art voice conversion models, showing that VChangeCodec achieves competitive results. The use of scalar quantization instead of vector quantization is another strength, as it reduces the complexity of the model while maintaining high performance. The causal convolutional architecture also ensures real-time processing, which is essential for the target application. Overall, the combination of low latency, small model size, and high-quality voice conversion makes this a significant contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the model is only evaluated on a sampling rate of 16kHz, which is not the case for most state-of-the-art (SOTA) models. As stated in the paper, "all utterances are sampled at 16kHz" and "All speech utterances are at a sampling rate of 16kHz." This limitation restricts the applicability of the model to scenarios where higher sampling rates are required, and it makes direct comparisons with other SOTA models difficult. The authors do not provide any justification for this choice or conduct experiments at higher sampling rates, which is a significant oversight. Second, while the paper claims that the proposed method can be applied to any encoder-quantizer-decoder codec, the causal projection network is specifically trained using a codec with scalar quantization. The paper states, "We utilize the encoder described in section 3.1 to extract discrete tokens" and "We also use the SQ on the adapted tokens obtained from the three grouped residual units." The architecture and training objective are tailored to the discrete nature of scalar-quantized tokens, and it is unclear how it would adapt to the continuous-valued codes produced by autoencoders or the discrete codebooks of VQ-VAEs. The projection network might require significant modifications or retraining to be compatible with these different token representations. This limits the generalizability of the proposed method. Third, the paper claims that the proposed method can switch between the original voice mode and the voice change mode seamlessly. However, the causal projection network is trained with paired data generated by an open-source voice conversion toolkit. The paper mentions, "We follow an open-source implementation 2 to acquire attributes of the target speaker." While the test set includes utterances from 42 different speakers, the subjective evaluation focuses on only two target timbres. This raises concerns about the robustness of the voice conversion performance when applied to speakers with significantly different timbral characteristics than those used in training. The paper does not demonstrate the network's ability to generalize to unseen speakers. Finally, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method compared to existing approaches. While the number of parameters is mentioned, a more comprehensive comparison of real-time factors (RTF) or latency would be beneficial. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the computational cost associated with each component of the VChangeCodec, such as the encoder, the causal projection network, and the decoder. This makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method on resource-constrained devices. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss the memory footprint of the model, which is a crucial factor for real-time applications, especially on mobile devices. These limitations, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the overall assessment of the proposed method.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct experiments using a higher sampling rate, such as 48kHz, to demonstrate the model's performance in those conditions. This would make the paper more relevant to a broader range of applications and allow for a more direct comparison with other state-of-the-art models. The authors should also provide a justification for using a 16kHz sampling rate if they choose not to explore higher rates. Second, the authors should investigate the applicability of the causal projection network to different quantization methods, such as vector quantization and autoencoders. This would involve adapting the projection network's architecture and training procedure to accommodate the different characteristics of these tokens. For example, when using autoencoder tokens, the network might need to be modified to handle continuous-valued inputs, potentially by incorporating a regression-based approach rather than a classification-based one. Similarly, for vector quantized tokens, the network would need to be adapted to operate on discrete codebook indices, possibly by using a cross-entropy loss or a similar approach. These experiments would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the network's generalizability and limitations. Third, the authors should evaluate the performance of the voice conversion on a diverse set of speakers not included in the training data. This evaluation should include both objective metrics, such as Mel-Cepstral Distortion (MCD) and speaker identification accuracy, and subjective listening tests to assess the naturalness and similarity of the converted speech. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of the speaker embedding quality on the voice conversion performance. This could involve analyzing the relationship between the speaker embedding similarity and the quality of the converted speech. Such analysis would provide insights into the robustness of the proposed method and its ability to handle a wide range of speaker variations. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method, including a breakdown of the real-time factor (RTF) for each component of the VChangeCodec, such as the encoder, the causal projection network, and the decoder. This should be done on a variety of hardware platforms, including both high-end and resource-constrained devices. Furthermore, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the memory footprint of the model, including the size of the weights, activations, and intermediate representations. This analysis should be accompanied by a discussion of the trade-offs between computational complexity, memory footprint, and performance. The authors should also compare the computational complexity of their method with existing state-of-the-art voice conversion and speech coding techniques, providing a clear understanding of the efficiency gains achieved by their approach. These improvements would significantly strengthen the paper and address the identified limitations.

These questions target the core methodological choices and assumptions of the paper, and I believe that addressing them would significantly enhance the overall understanding and impact of the work.
