## Summary

This paper introduces Graph2Tac (G2T), a novel graph neural network (GNN) approach for tactic prediction within the Coq proof assistant. The core idea is to represent Coq terms and their dependencies as a graph, enabling the model to learn hierarchical representations of mathematical concepts. This graph-based representation allows G2T to reason about the context in which a theorem is being proved, taking into account the definitions and theorems available in the environment. The model is trained on a large dataset of Coq projects, and a key feature is its ability to adapt to new definitions and theorems in real time, without requiring retraining. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of G2T through comprehensive experiments, comparing it against state-of-the-art k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) predictors and other machine learning-based solvers in Coq. The empirical results show that G2T outperforms these baselines in terms of the number of theorems proved and the time taken to prove them. Furthermore, the paper highlights the complementarity of G2T with other online models that learn from proof scripts written by users, suggesting a potential for synergistic integration. The authors also introduce a novel definition task, which is used to learn the embeddings of definitions, and this task is shown to be crucial for the model's performance. Overall, this work makes a significant contribution to the field of automated theorem proving by introducing a new way of representing and reasoning about mathematical concepts in a formal setting, and by demonstrating the effectiveness of a GNN-based approach for tactic prediction in Coq. The ability of the model to adapt to new definitions and theorems in real time is a particularly valuable feature, making it a practical tool for assisting users in interactive theorem proving.

I find the core strength of this paper lies in its innovative application of graph neural networks to the domain of interactive theorem proving. The graph-based representation of Coq terms, which captures the dependencies between definitions, is a significant departure from traditional text-based or symbolic approaches. This allows the model to reason about the context of a theorem, a crucial aspect often overlooked by other methods. The ability of G2T to adapt to new definitions and theorems in real time is another notable strength. This online learning capability is essential for practical applications, as it allows the model to seamlessly integrate new information without requiring retraining. The empirical results presented in the paper provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The authors demonstrate that G2T outperforms state-of-the-art k-nearest neighbor predictors and other machine learning-based solvers in Coq, both in terms of the number of theorems proved and the time taken to prove them. The comprehensive experimental evaluation, which includes comparisons with multiple baselines, further strengthens the validity of these findings. The introduction of a novel definition task, which is used to learn the embeddings of definitions, is another significant contribution. This task is shown to be crucial for the model's performance, highlighting the importance of explicitly modeling the relationships between definitions. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly explains the technical details of the proposed model and its evaluation, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors have successfully demonstrated the potential of GNNs for tactic prediction in Coq, and their work opens up new avenues for research in this area.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I've identified several key weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the types of theorems that the model can and cannot prove. While the authors present overall statistics on the number of theorems proved by different models (Figures 5 and 7), they do not provide a breakdown of performance based on the type of theorem. For instance, it remains unclear how the model performs on theorems that require specific tactics like 'induction' or 'apply', or those that involve complex logical structures. This lack of granular analysis makes it difficult to understand the model's strengths and weaknesses, and to identify areas where it could be improved. This is a significant limitation, as it prevents a nuanced understanding of the model's capabilities. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's 'experiments' section and results focus on overall performance metrics without categorizing theorem types. Secondly, the paper does not provide a detailed discussion of the limitations of the model. While the authors mention that the graph is pruned to 1024 nodes (in the 'Graph Neural Network (GNN) Model' description), they do not discuss the implications of this limitation on the model's ability to handle complex proof states or very long proofs. The paper also lacks an analysis of how the model scales with the size of the proof state graph and the length of the proof sequence. Furthermore, it is unclear how the model handles proof states with a large number of hypotheses or complex dependencies between them. This lack of discussion on the model's limitations makes it difficult to assess its practical applicability. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper mentions the graph pruning step but lacks a comprehensive discussion of the model's limitations regarding complex proof states, long proofs, and scalability. Thirdly, the paper does not provide a detailed comparison of the model to other approaches for tactic prediction in Coq, particularly those based on symbolic methods or other machine learning techniques. While the authors compare G2T to CoqHammer, k-NN, and a Transformer baseline in the 'NON-GRAPH APPROACHES' section, they do not delve into a qualitative comparison of the methodologies and their trade-offs. The paper lacks a discussion of the advantages and disadvantages of each approach, and how they compare to G2T in terms of their ability to handle different types of theorems or proof states. This lack of a comprehensive comparison makes it difficult to position G2T within the broader context of existing research. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper describes the baselines and presents experimental results but lacks a detailed comparative discussion of the different approaches. Fourthly, the empirical evaluation of the proposed approach is not sufficiently extensive and robust. The authors randomly select 2000 theorems from the Coq packages in the test set, without providing a justification for this random selection. It is unclear if this random selection provides a fair and representative evaluation of the approach. The paper also lacks an evaluation on the entire test set, and does not provide arguments for why such an evaluation is not possible. This lack of a comprehensive evaluation makes it difficult to assess the generalizability of the model's performance. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly mentions the random selection of 2000 theorems without further justification. Finally, the paper does not adequately address the potential for data leakage between the training set and the test set. While the authors state that they split the packages into training and test ensuring that no test package depends on a training package, this does not guarantee that definitions and theorems are unique across the two sets. The paper lacks a discussion of the potential impact of such data leakage on the evaluation results. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper describes package-level splitting but lacks a guarantee of unique definitions and theorems.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the types of theorems that the model can and cannot prove. This analysis should go beyond simply reporting overall statistics and delve into specific categories of theorems, such as those involving induction, recursion, or higher-order logic. The authors could analyze the model's performance on theorems that require specific tactics like 'induction' or 'apply', and compare this to its performance on theorems that can be solved with more straightforward tactics. This analysis should also consider the complexity of the theorems, perhaps using metrics like the number of logical connectives or the depth of the proof tree. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to include examples of theorems that the model was able to prove and those it failed on, to give a concrete sense of the model's strengths and weaknesses. Secondly, the authors should provide a more thorough discussion of the limitations of the model. This discussion should address the model's scalability with respect to the size of the proof state graph and the length of the proof sequence. The authors should also discuss how the model handles proof states with a large number of hypotheses or complex dependencies between them. For example, the authors could analyze the model's performance on theorems that require a large number of proof steps, or on theorems that involve a large number of hypotheses. This analysis should also consider the computational cost of the model, and how it scales with the size of the proof state graph. It would be useful to know the maximum size of the proof state graph that the model can handle, and how the model's performance degrades as the graph size increases. Thirdly, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison of the model to other approaches for tactic prediction in Coq. This comparison should not only include other machine learning-based methods, but also symbolic methods and hybrid approaches. For example, the authors could compare their model to decision tree-based methods, or to methods that use a combination of symbolic and neural techniques. This comparison should discuss the advantages and disadvantages of each approach, and should highlight the unique contributions of the proposed model. The authors should also discuss how their model compares to existing tools like CoqHammer, and how it could be integrated with such tools to improve overall performance. Fourthly, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive empirical evaluation of the proposed approach. Instead of relying on a random subset of theorems, they could explore stratified sampling based on theorem complexity or package origin. This would ensure a more balanced representation of the diverse theorem landscape within the Coq libraries. Furthermore, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the characteristics of the randomly selected theorems, such as the average number of dependencies, the depth of the proof tree, and the types of tactics required for proof. If evaluating the entire test set is computationally prohibitive, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the computational costs associated with the evaluation, including the time and memory requirements for each theorem, and justify the use of a subset based on these constraints. Finally, the authors need to address the potential for data leakage more rigorously. While splitting packages based on dependencies is a good start, it does not fully eliminate the possibility of overlapping definitions and theorems between the training and test sets. The authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how they handle such overlaps, including a discussion of the specific measures taken to ensure that no definition or theorem appears in both sets. For example, they could consider using a more fine-grained approach to data splitting, such as splitting at the level of individual definitions or theorems, rather than at the package level. They should also provide a quantitative analysis of the overlap between the training and test sets, such as the number of shared definitions and theorems, to demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach.
