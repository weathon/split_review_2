## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to scene decomposition using convex primitives, focusing on improving the accuracy of fitting these primitives to complex indoor scenes. The authors build upon prior work, specifically Vavilala_2023_ICCV, and propose two main enhancements: the incorporation of negative primitives using Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG) and an ensembling technique. The core idea is that by allowing for the subtraction of volume using negative primitives, the method can represent more complex geometries than with positive primitives alone. Furthermore, the authors demonstrate that ensembling multiple regression models, each trained with a different number of initial primitives, significantly improves the overall fitting accuracy. The method is evaluated on the NYUv2 dataset, a standard benchmark for indoor scene understanding, and the results show substantial improvements over existing state-of-the-art methods in terms of depth and normal prediction, as well as scene segmentation. The authors also provide ablation studies to demonstrate the effectiveness of both the negative primitives and the ensembling strategy. The paper's key contribution lies in its innovative use of negative primitives and the ensembling approach, which together address the limitations of previous methods in handling complex scene geometries. The empirical results, while promising, also highlight areas for further investigation, particularly regarding the computational cost of the ensembling approach and the generalizability of the method to more diverse datasets. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in the field of scene decomposition, offering a more accurate and robust approach to fitting convex primitives to complex indoor scenes. However, the paper also raises several questions regarding the practical implications of the proposed method, especially concerning its computational demands and the lack of detailed analysis of failure cases. The authors' approach, while demonstrating impressive accuracy gains, requires further investigation to fully understand its limitations and potential for real-world applications. The paper's findings suggest that the use of negative primitives and ensembling are promising directions for future research in this area, and I believe this work will stimulate further investigation into more robust and accurate scene decomposition techniques.
