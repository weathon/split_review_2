## Summary

This paper introduces a novel framework for conformal prediction tailored to structured prediction settings, where the output space is complex and requires more interpretable prediction sets. The authors address the challenge of providing coverage guarantees in such settings by adapting existing conformal prediction algorithms to output structured prediction sets that implicitly represent sets of labels. Specifically, they focus on scenarios where the prediction sets can be represented as a set of nodes in a directed acyclic graph (DAG), such as hierarchical labels in image classification. The core idea is to leverage the structure of the DAG to construct prediction sets that are both informative and provide coverage guarantees. The proposed method adapts existing conformal prediction algorithms to output structured prediction sets that implicitly represent sets of labels, enhancing interpretability while maintaining coverage guarantees. The authors provide theoretical guarantees for both marginal and PAC (Probabilistic Approximation Correct) coverage, demonstrating the validity of their approach. The framework is evaluated empirically on three tasks: predicting integers represented by a list of MNIST digits, hierarchical image classification using ImageNet, and question answering on the SQuAD dataset. These tasks were chosen to represent different types of structured output spaces and to demonstrate the versatility of the proposed framework. The authors formulate the problem of finding the optimal structured prediction set as an integer program, which is then solved to obtain the conformal prediction sets. The paper's main contribution lies in extending the applicability of conformal prediction to complex structured output spaces, offering a way to quantify uncertainty in a more interpretable manner. The authors argue that this approach can significantly improve the reliability of predictions in structured prediction settings by providing users with a clear understanding of the uncertainty associated with the predictions, enabling more informed decision-making. The paper is well-organized, presenting a clear problem formulation, a detailed description of the proposed algorithms, and a thorough experimental evaluation. The theoretical guarantees and the empirical results on diverse tasks suggest the potential of the proposed framework to address a significant gap in the field of uncertainty quantification for structured prediction.
