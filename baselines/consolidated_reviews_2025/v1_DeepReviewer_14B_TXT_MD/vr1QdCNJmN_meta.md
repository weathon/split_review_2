## Summary

This paper introduces a novel class of divergences on discrete spaces, termed Difference-of-Submodular Bregman Divergences (DBDs), which extend the existing framework of submodular Bregman divergences. The core idea is to leverage the difference-of-submodular (DS) decomposition, which allows for the construction of more expressive set functions than those achievable with submodular functions alone. The authors demonstrate that DBDs satisfy the properties of a proper divergence, namely non-negativity and identity of indiscernibles. They further show that the expressive power of DBDs increases with the richness of the set function class. A key contribution is the development of a learnable form of DBD using permutation-invariant neural networks, specifically epsilon-PointNet, which enables the practical application of these divergences. The authors present experimental results on the ModelNet40 dataset, demonstrating that the proposed DBD outperforms existing submodular Bregman divergences in clustering and set retrieval tasks. The paper's main contribution lies in the theoretical development of the DBD framework and its practical implementation using neural networks. The authors provide a solid theoretical foundation for the proposed divergence, proving its validity as a divergence measure and demonstrating its increased expressiveness compared to submodular Bregman divergences. The use of permutation-invariant neural networks to learn the submodular functions that form the basis of the DBD is a significant methodological innovation, allowing for the application of the framework to real-world datasets. The empirical findings, while limited to the ModelNet40 dataset, provide initial evidence of the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The paper's significance lies in its introduction of a more flexible and expressive framework for measuring dissimilarity between discrete sets, which has potential applications in various machine learning tasks. However, the paper also has several limitations, including a lack of detailed computational complexity analysis, limited experimental comparisons, and insufficient exploration of the method's sensitivity to hyperparameters. These limitations, which I will discuss in detail, highlight areas where further research is needed to fully realize the potential of the proposed DBD framework. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of discrete optimization and machine learning, but further work is needed to address the identified weaknesses and fully validate the proposed approach.
