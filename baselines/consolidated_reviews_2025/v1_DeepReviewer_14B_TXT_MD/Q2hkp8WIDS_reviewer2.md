### Summary

This paper proposes an object-centric model-based reinforcement learning pipeline that leverages recent advances in computer vision. The pipeline involves four steps: 1) Annotating key objects in a small number of frames using segmentation masks. 2) Extracting object features through a parameter-frozen pre-trained foundation vision model conditioned on these annotations. 3) Utilizing both these object features and the raw observations as inputs for training an object-centric world model that predicts the dynamics of the environment while considering the relationships between different objects and the scene. 4) Training the policy with imagined trajectories generated by the world model. The authors show that their proposed method outperforms baseline STORM on 18 of 26 tasks in the Atari 100k benchmark and achieves the best-known sample efficiency on several Hollow Knight bosses.

### Weaknesses

#### Some Related Works


#### comment

1. The technical novelty is limited. The proposed method is primarily an integration of the STORM model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) framework with the Cutie vision model. While this integration is practically relevant, it does not introduce significant theoretical or algorithmic advancements in the field of reinforcement learning or computer vision. The core idea of using object-centric features within an MBRL framework is not entirely new, and the paper does not sufficiently highlight the unique aspects of this specific combination compared to existing object-centric RL methods.

2. The paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. Specifically, the reliance on annotated segmentation masks for key objects raises concerns about scalability and applicability to more complex environments where such annotations are not readily available or are computationally expensive to obtain. Furthermore, the paper does not address the potential for error propagation when the object features extracted by Cutie are imperfect or when the world model struggles to accurately predict the dynamics of the environment based on these features.

3. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by incorporating the Cutie vision model. While the authors mention that Cutie is used to extract object features, they do not quantify the additional computational cost compared to the baseline STORM model. This is a critical aspect for practical applications, as the increased computational burden might outweigh the performance gains in resource-constrained settings. A comparison of training time, inference time, and memory usage would be essential to fully evaluate the proposed method's efficiency.

### Suggestions

The authors should more clearly articulate the specific technical contributions of their work beyond the simple combination of existing models. A more detailed comparison with existing object-centric reinforcement learning methods is needed to highlight the unique aspects of their approach. This should include a discussion of how their method addresses the limitations of previous approaches and what specific challenges they overcome with their particular integration of Cutie and STORM. For example, the authors could analyze the impact of different object feature representations on the performance of the world model and policy learning, and compare their approach to other object-centric methods that use different feature extraction techniques.

To address the limitations, the authors should investigate the sensitivity of their method to the quality of the segmentation masks and the object features extracted by Cutie. They could perform ablation studies to evaluate the impact of noisy or incomplete segmentation masks on the overall performance. Furthermore, they should explore alternative methods for obtaining object features that do not rely on manual annotations, such as using unsupervised object discovery techniques. This would make their method more applicable to a wider range of environments. The authors should also discuss the potential for error propagation and how their method mitigates the impact of inaccurate object features or world model predictions. This could involve analyzing the robustness of the policy to uncertainties in the predicted dynamics.

Finally, the authors need to provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of their method. This should include a comparison of training time, inference time, and memory usage with the baseline STORM model. They should also investigate the scalability of their method to more complex environments with a larger number of objects. This analysis should be presented with concrete data and visualizations to allow for a thorough evaluation of the method's efficiency. The authors could also explore techniques for optimizing the computational performance of their method, such as using model compression or parallel processing.
