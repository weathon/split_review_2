## Summary

This paper introduces Differentiable Pruning at Initialization (DPaI), a novel method for pruning neural networks at initialization by leveraging a differentiable formulation of the Node-Path Balancing (NPB) principle. The core idea is to optimize pruning masks using gradient-based methods, which contrasts with traditional pruning approaches that rely on iterative pruning and retraining or discrete optimization techniques. The authors argue that existing pruning-at-initialization (PaI) methods often focus on parameter importance while neglecting network topology, leading to suboptimal, hard-to-optimize networks. DPaI addresses this by directly optimizing for effective nodes and paths within the network, which they claim results in more efficient and performant sparse networks. The method involves calculating differentiable scores for each connection, which are then used to determine the pruning mask. These scores are updated using gradient-based optimization, with the goal of maximizing the number of effective nodes and paths while adhering to a target sparsity level. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence of their method and demonstrate its effectiveness through extensive experiments on various architectures, including Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers (ViTs), and datasets. The empirical results show that DPaI outperforms state-of-the-art PaI methods, particularly at high sparsity levels, achieving significant improvements in accuracy. The paper also includes an ablation study to analyze the impact of key hyperparameters on the method's performance. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in the field of pruning at initialization by introducing a differentiable approach that considers network topology, leading to more efficient and effective sparse networks. The authors claim that their method is more efficient than existing methods, particularly in terms of pruning time, and that it can discover superior sparse sub-networks across multiple sparsity levels. The paper's main contribution is the differentiable formulation of the NPB principle, which allows for the use of gradient-based optimization for pruning at initialization. This is a significant departure from existing methods that rely on discrete optimization or heuristics. The authors also provide a comprehensive convergence analysis of their method, which adds to the theoretical foundation of the work. The empirical results are extensive and demonstrate the effectiveness of DPaI across different architectures and datasets. The paper's findings suggest that DPaI is a promising approach for pruning neural networks at initialization, and that it has the potential to significantly improve the efficiency and performance of sparse networks.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach to pruning at initialization, several weaknesses warrant attention. First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of DPaI compared to existing methods. While the authors present pruning time comparisons in Figure 3, they do not provide a comprehensive breakdown of the time spent on different stages of the pruning process, such as the calculation of effective paths, nodes, and kernels, nor do they quantify the memory footprint of the method, including the storage of masks and intermediate variables. This is a significant omission, as it makes it difficult to assess the practical scalability of DPaI, especially for larger networks and higher sparsity levels. The paper also does not analyze how the computational cost scales with network size and sparsity level. This lack of detailed computational analysis limits the practical understanding of the method's efficiency. Second, the paper does not thoroughly investigate the sensitivity of DPaI to different hyperparameter settings. While the authors include an ablation study for hyperparameters α and β, they do not provide a detailed analysis of the impact of other hyperparameters, such as the learning rate (η) and the number of iterations (T). The paper mentions using a grid search to optimize α and β, but it does not provide details on the range of values explored, the impact of each hyperparameter on the final performance, or the computational cost associated with the grid search. This lack of sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to determine the robustness of the method and to provide guidelines for selecting appropriate hyperparameter values. The paper also does not analyze the sensitivity of the method to the choice of the balancing parameter in the Node-Path Balancing Principle, which could significantly impact the final network structure. Third, the paper does not explore the applicability of DPaI to other types of neural networks, such as recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or graph neural networks (GNNs). The current evaluation is limited to CNNs and ViTs, and it is unclear how the differentiable NPB formulation would translate to other architectures with different connectivity patterns and computational flows. This limits the generalizability of the method and its potential impact. The paper also lacks a detailed comparison with other pruning methods in terms of the trade-offs between accuracy and sparsity. While the paper compares accuracy at different sparsity levels, it does not include a Pareto front analysis to visualize the trade-offs between accuracy and sparsity for different methods. The paper also does not discuss the limitations of DPaI in scenarios where high accuracy is more critical than sparsity, and vice versa. This lack of a detailed trade-off analysis limits the practical understanding of the method's strengths and weaknesses. Finally, while the paper mentions that DPaI is efficient, it does not provide a detailed explanation of why the proposed method can make the network thinner and faster. The paper explains that the method prunes weights by setting them to zero based on the mask, but it does not explicitly connect the optimization objective (maximizing effective nodes and paths) to the resulting sparsity that leads to these benefits. This lack of a clear connection between the optimization objective and the resulting network thinness/faster speed is a weakness. The paper also does not provide a detailed explanation of the significance of applying pruning at initialization methods to various tasks. While the introduction briefly mentions the benefits of PaI in resource-constrained environments, it does not provide a detailed discussion of the specific advantages of PaI for different types of tasks or applications. This lack of a detailed discussion of the significance of PaI for various tasks is a weakness. These weaknesses, while not invalidating the core contributions of the paper, highlight areas where further research and analysis are needed to fully understand the potential and limitations of DPaI.
