## Summary

This paper introduces a novel problem setting termed Sparse Label Node Classification (SLNC), which addresses the challenge of node classification in graphs when only a limited number of labeled nodes are available for training, and these labels are not provided on a per-class basis. This contrasts with traditional semi-supervised node classification, where a substantial number of labeled nodes per class are typically assumed. The authors propose a framework called Estimating Label Information (ELI) to tackle this problem. ELI leverages unsupervised learning techniques to estimate a pseudo-label distribution across the graph. This estimated distribution is then used to enhance existing semi-supervised learning methods and guide the selection of key nodes for labeling. Specifically, ELI employs an unsupervised convolution-based clustering method to generate pseudo-labels, which are then incorporated into a modified label propagation framework. The framework also generalizes to graph convolutional networks like SGC by using an average Laplacian matrix derived from the estimated label information. The core idea is to use the unsupervised learning of label distributions to improve the performance of semi-supervised methods in this challenging low-label regime. The authors demonstrate through experiments that their approach outperforms baseline methods by 10-20% in the SLNC setting when the number of labeled nodes is extremely low. The paper's main contribution lies in the introduction of the SLNC problem setting and the proposed ELI framework, which offers a way to leverage unsupervised learning to improve semi-supervised learning in scenarios with sparse and randomly distributed labels. The experimental results, while promising, are limited to a few datasets and baselines, and the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity and sensitivity to hyperparameters. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field by addressing a realistic and challenging problem in graph-based learning.

## Strengths

I find the introduction of the Sparse Label Node Classification (SLNC) problem setting to be a significant strength of this paper. It addresses a realistic and challenging scenario where labeled data is scarce and not distributed evenly across classes, which is a common occurrence in real-world applications. This setting is a departure from traditional semi-supervised learning assumptions and provides a more relevant benchmark for evaluating graph learning methods. The proposed Estimating Label Information (ELI) framework is another notable strength. The framework's core idea of leveraging unsupervised learning to estimate label distributions and then using this information to enhance semi-supervised learning is both intuitive and effective. The use of an unsupervised clustering method to generate pseudo-labels, which are then incorporated into label propagation and graph convolutional networks, demonstrates a creative approach to the problem. The experimental results, while limited in scope, consistently show that ELI outperforms baseline methods by a substantial margin (10-20%) in the SLNC setting when the number of labeled nodes is extremely low. This performance improvement is a strong indication of the framework's potential. The paper also provides a clear description of the ELI framework, outlining the steps involved in label distribution estimation, key node selection, and incorporation of label information into existing SSL frameworks. This clarity makes it easier to understand the proposed method and its underlying mechanisms. The authors also provide a theoretical analysis of their approach, which further strengthens the validity of their work. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel problem setting, the well-motivated and effective ELI framework, and the promising experimental results.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I've identified several key weaknesses that warrant attention. First, the paper's core method, ELI, appears to be largely constructed from existing techniques. While the authors combine these techniques in a novel way, the individual components are not new. Specifically, the paper explicitly states that it uses an "unsupervised convolution-based clustering framework of Kamhoua et al. (2022)" for label distribution estimation, modifies "popular label propagation (LP) (Delalleau et al., 2005)" for label distribution incorporation, and uses an average Laplacian in "graph convolution neural networks (GCNs) like SGC (Wu et al., 2019)" for generalization. This reliance on existing methods, while not inherently negative, diminishes the novelty of the proposed approach. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how these individual components are modified or adapted to fit the sparse label setting. This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to assess the true contribution of ELI beyond a simple combination of existing methods. Second, the paper assumes that the number of classes, denoted as 'c', is known in advance. This assumption, as stated in Section 3, is a significant limitation, as it is not always the case in real-world scenarios. The performance of the method could be significantly impacted if the assumed number of classes deviates from the true number of classes, potentially leading to misclassification errors. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the method to this assumption or propose any mechanisms to handle situations where the number of classes is unknown. Third, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other methods that do not require the number of classes to be known in advance. This omission makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of ELI compared to existing approaches that are more robust to unknown class numbers. Without such comparisons, it is unclear whether ELI offers a significant advantage over existing techniques or if it simply performs well under the specific assumption of known class numbers. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a baseline comparison with other methods that select labeled nodes on a per-class basis. This omission makes it difficult to assess the effectiveness of ELI compared to existing approaches that are specifically designed to handle imbalanced or sparse labeling scenarios. It is important to understand how ELI performs in comparison to methods that explicitly address the challenge of selecting labeled nodes in a class-balanced manner, especially when some classes have very few labeled examples. The paper also suffers from a limited baseline comparison. While the paper includes more than just LP and SGC, the field of graph representation learning is rapidly evolving. Including more recent and highly performing baselines would strengthen the evaluation. The current comparison is limited to a few traditional methods and one more recent method (CGPN). The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed ELI framework. While the authors mention that their approach is efficient, a more rigorous analysis is needed to understand its scalability and practical applicability. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements of each step in the ELI framework, such as the unsupervised clustering, label propagation, and model training. Furthermore, it would be helpful to compare the computational complexity of ELI with that of the baseline methods. Finally, the paper needs a more thorough investigation of the sensitivity of the ELI framework to its hyperparameters. The performance of machine learning models often depends heavily on the choice of hyperparameters, and it is important to understand how the performance of ELI varies with different settings. The authors should conduct a sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of key hyperparameters, such as the number of clusters, the number of neighbors, and the weights of the different terms in the objective function. This analysis should include a systematic exploration of the hyperparameter space and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for these parameters. While the paper mentions a sensitivity study for the number of neighbors, a more comprehensive sensitivity analysis covering other key hyperparameters is missing. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be improved to provide a more robust and comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method.

Fifth, the authors should expand their baseline comparisons to include more recent and state-of-the-art methods in graph representation learning. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of ELI's performance in the context of current research. Finally, the authors should conduct a more thorough sensitivity analysis of the ELI framework to its hyperparameters. This analysis should include a systematic exploration of the hyperparameter space and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for these parameters. Furthermore, it would be helpful to visualize the performance of ELI under different hyperparameter settings, such as by plotting performance curves or heatmaps.
