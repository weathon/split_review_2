## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to training and validating AI models for liver tumor segmentation, addressing the challenge of limited annotated data, particularly for early-stage tumors. The core idea revolves around the use of synthetically generated tumors embedded within healthy liver CT scans to create a large and diverse validation set. This strategy aims to overcome the limitations of traditional validation methods that rely on scarce real-world annotated data, which can lead to biased model selection and overfitting. The authors propose a multi-step process for generating realistic synthetic tumors, starting with the placement of an ellipsoid within a healthy liver, followed by elastic deformation to create more natural shapes, and finally, the addition of Gaussian noise and blurring to simulate realistic textures. The generated synthetic tumors are then used as a validation set to select the best model checkpoint during training on real data. The authors also introduce a continual learning framework that leverages a stream of out-of-domain data with synthetic tumors to further improve model performance. The experimental results demonstrate that using synthetic data for validation leads to better model performance on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution data, especially for detecting tiny liver tumors. The paper's main contribution lies in the innovative use of synthetic data as a validation tool, which not only reduces the reliance on annotated real data but also enhances the generalization capabilities of the trained models. The authors present a compelling case for the potential of synthetic data in medical image analysis, particularly in scenarios where acquiring sufficient annotated data is challenging. The paper's findings suggest that synthetic data can play a crucial role in improving the robustness and accuracy of AI models for medical image segmentation, ultimately contributing to better diagnostic tools and patient care. The authors also highlight the potential of their approach to be extended to other medical imaging tasks and domains, paving the way for broader applications of synthetic data in the field.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly compelling. The most significant strength lies in the innovative use of synthetic data as a validation set. This approach directly addresses the practical challenge of limited annotated data in medical imaging, especially for rare or early-stage conditions like tiny liver tumors. By generating a large and diverse synthetic validation set, the authors effectively mitigate the risk of overfitting to a small, potentially biased real-world validation set. This is a crucial contribution, as it allows for more robust model selection and improved generalization to unseen data. The paper's experimental results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of this approach. The models trained and validated using synthetic data demonstrate superior performance on both in-distribution and out-of-distribution test sets, particularly for detecting tiny liver tumors. This is a significant achievement, as detecting these small tumors is often challenging even for expert radiologists. The authors' continual learning framework, which incorporates a stream of out-of-domain data with synthetic tumors, is another notable strength. This framework further enhances model performance and demonstrates the potential of synthetic data to improve model adaptability to new data distributions. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making the methodology and findings accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly articulate their approach and provide sufficient detail to understand the key steps involved in the synthetic data generation and model training processes. The use of multiple datasets for evaluation, including both in-domain and out-of-domain data, further strengthens the paper's findings and demonstrates the generalizability of the proposed approach. Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for the use of synthetic data as a validation tool in medical image analysis, and its findings have the potential to significantly impact the development of more robust and accurate AI models for clinical applications.
