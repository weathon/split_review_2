## Summary

The paper introduces a novel stochastic variant of deep neural networks (DNNs) called stochastic neural networks (StoNet). The core contribution of this work is the integration of StoNet into the framework of statistical modeling, which allows for the application of sparse learning theory and the Lasso penalty to DNNs. The authors establish that the StoNet is consistent in network structure selection and provide a recursive method to quantify prediction uncertainty. They further demonstrate the asymptotic equivalence between StoNet and DNN, suggesting that consistent sparse deep learning can be achieved by training a DNN with an appropriate Lasso penalty. The paper also proposes a post-StoNet procedure to remodel the last hidden layer output and the target output of a well-trained DNN on a validation dataset, aiming to improve prediction uncertainty assessment. Empirical results show that the proposed method outperforms conformal inference and temperature/matrix scaling in terms of uncertainty quantification. However, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with other existing methods for structure interpretability and uncertainty quantification, and the computational complexity and hyperparameter sensitivity of the proposed method are not thoroughly analyzed. Additionally, the theoretical proofs, particularly for Lemma 1 and Corollary 1, are not sufficiently rigorous, and the prediction error bounds in Section 4 are not valid due to the omission of the convergence rate of the estimator. Despite these limitations, the paper offers a promising approach to addressing fundamental issues in deep learning, such as structure interpretability and uncertainty quantification, by leveraging statistical modeling techniques.

## Strengths

I find the paper to be well-written and easy to follow, which is a significant strength. The introduction of stochastic neural networks (StoNet) as a stochastic variant of DNNs is a novel and valuable contribution. The authors successfully show that StoNet falls into the framework of statistical modeling, which is a crucial step in addressing issues such as structure interpretability and uncertainty quantification in deep learning. By demonstrating the asymptotic equivalence between StoNet and DNN, the paper provides a theoretical foundation for applying sparse learning theory and the Lasso penalty to DNNs. This equivalence is particularly important as it suggests that consistent sparse deep learning can be achieved by training a DNN with an appropriate Lasso penalty. The paper also introduces a recursive method to quantify prediction uncertainty, which is a significant technical innovation. The empirical results, while limited in scope, show that the proposed method outperforms conformal inference and temperature/matrix scaling in terms of uncertainty quantification. The authors provide a clear and concise explanation of the IRO and ASGMCMC algorithms used for training StoNet, which are well-established in the literature. The paper's focus on the practical implications of the theoretical results, such as the post-StoNet procedure for improving prediction uncertainty, is commendable and adds to the paper's overall significance.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and weaknesses are evident. One of the most significant concerns is the lack of a detailed comparison with other existing methods for addressing structure interpretability and uncertainty quantification in deep learning. The paper primarily compares StoNet with conformal inference and temperature/matrix scaling, but it does not discuss or compare with methods that directly regularize the DNN architecture, such as those using group sparsity or other structural constraints. This omission makes it difficult to fully appreciate the advantages and limitations of the proposed approach. For instance, the paper does not mention or compare with Bayesian neural networks, which are widely used for uncertainty quantification. A comprehensive comparison with these methods would provide a more balanced view of the proposed approach's effectiveness and novelty (Section 6, Experiments). Another critical weakness is the absence of a computational complexity analysis. The paper does not discuss the time and memory requirements for training and inference using StoNet, which is essential for understanding the practical feasibility of the method, especially when dealing with large-scale datasets (Section 4, Method). The lack of a thorough sensitivity analysis of the StoNet to hyperparameters, such as the noise variance and regularization parameters, is also a notable limitation. While the paper shows the effect of $	au$ on coverage rates and mentions trying different $eta$ values, it does not provide a systematic exploration of how these parameters impact the model's performance and stability (Section 5, Experiments). This gap in the analysis leaves practitioners without clear guidance on selecting appropriate hyperparameter values. The theoretical proofs, particularly for Lemma 1 and Corollary 1, are not sufficiently rigorous. The proof of Lemma 1 relies on the continuous mapping theorem and the strong law of large numbers but lacks the necessary details for their application in this specific context. For example, the function to which the continuous mapping theorem is applied, and the domain and codomain, are not specified. Additionally, the identically distributed condition required for the strong law of large numbers is not verified (Supplementary Material, Proof of Lemma 1). The proof of Corollary 1, which relates the consistency of the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE) of complete data to the consistency of the MLE of the DNN, is also not convincing. The authors claim that the consistency of the penalized likelihood function of the complete data implies the consistency of the penalized likelihood function of the DNN, but the connection between these two estimators is not clearly established (Section 3.1, Corollary 1). ## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First, the paper should include a more thorough comparison with existing methods for structure interpretability and uncertainty quantification in deep learning. Specifically, the authors should compare their approach with methods that directly regularize the DNN architecture, such as those using group sparsity or other structural constraints. This comparison should not only focus on theoretical aspects but also on practical performance and computational cost. For example, the authors could compare the performance of StoNet with methods that use L1 regularization directly on the weights of a standard DNN or with methods that employ dropout as a Bayesian approximation. This would provide a more comprehensive view of the proposed method's advantages and limitations. Second, the paper should include a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed StoNet method. The authors should provide a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for both the training and inference phases, considering the impact of the network size, the number of layers, and the choice of hyperparameters. This analysis should be compared with that of standard DNNs and other methods for uncertainty quantification to highlight the practical feasibility of the proposed approach. Third, the paper should include a sensitivity analysis of the StoNet to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the noise variance and the regularization parameters. The authors should systematically explore the parameter space and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for these hyperparameters. For instance, they could perform experiments with different values of the noise variance and regularization parameters and analyze their impact on prediction accuracy, sparsity of the learned network, and the quality of the uncertainty estimates. This analysis should also discuss the trade-offs between different parameter choices and provide recommendations for practitioners. Fourth, the theoretical proofs, particularly for Lemma 1 and Corollary 1, need to be more rigorous. The authors should explicitly state all assumptions and provide detailed justifications for the application of the continuous mapping theorem and the strong law of large numbers. For Lemma 1, the function being mapped, the domain, and the codomain should be clearly specified, and the identically distributed condition for the strong law of large numbers should be verified. For Corollary 1, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how the consistency of the MLE of complete data relates to the consistency of the MLE of the DNN, including the specific forms of the penalized likelihood functions. Finally, the prediction error bounds in Section 4 should be revised to account for the convergence rate of the estimator $oldsymbol{	heta}$. The authors should explicitly incorporate the convergence rate into the error bound to ensure that the bound converges to a non-negative value as $n 	o 	ext{large}$. This would make the theoretical results more mathematically sound and practically relevant. Additionally, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of the practical implementation of the proposed method, including specific steps for training the StoNet, selecting the noise variance parameters, and quantifying prediction uncertainty. Code examples or tutorials would also be beneficial to help other researchers reproduce the results and apply the method to their own problems.
