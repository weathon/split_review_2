## Summary

This paper presents a theoretical investigation into the behavior of contrastive learning when applied to multimodal data, specifically focusing on scenarios where certain modalities are unpaired. The authors introduce a novel theoretical framework, which they term the “Law” of the Unconscious Contrastive Learner, that aims to explain why and when the dot product or negative squared distance between representations of unpaired modalities can effectively recover the correct marginal density ratio. This framework is built upon three key assumptions: the conditional independence of modalities given a bridge modality, the ability of contrastive learning with the InfoNCE loss to model the correct density ratio, and specific properties of the representation distribution (either uniform or Gaussian). Under these assumptions, the authors derive closed-form expressions for the marginal probability ratio, demonstrating that the similarity between representations of unpaired modalities can be used to estimate this ratio. The paper further proposes a practical Monte Carlo approximation algorithm, based on this theoretical framework, to estimate the marginal probability ratio between unpaired modalities. This algorithm is presented as a way to combine pre-trained models or handle language ambiguity in reinforcement learning. The authors validate their theoretical findings through experiments on synthetic data and real-world datasets, demonstrating the effectiveness of their approach in multimodal alignment tasks. The paper's core contribution lies in providing a theoretical justification for a commonly used heuristic in contrastive learning, and in proposing a practical algorithm that leverages this theory for multimodal alignment. The authors explore applications of their theory in combining pre-trained models and handling language ambiguity in reinforcement learning, highlighting the potential impact of their work. Overall, the paper offers a valuable theoretical perspective on contrastive learning and provides a practical method for multimodal alignment, with empirical support from both synthetic and real-world experiments. However, the paper also relies on several assumptions that may not always hold in practice, and the experimental evaluation could be expanded to include more diverse datasets and comparisons with existing methods.

## Strengths

This paper makes a significant contribution by providing a theoretical framework for understanding the behavior of contrastive learning in multimodal settings, particularly when dealing with unpaired modalities. The introduction of the “Law” of the Unconscious Contrastive Learner is a novel and insightful concept that helps to explain why the dot product or negative squared distance between representations of unpaired modalities can be used to recover the marginal density ratio. The authors' rigorous mathematical analysis, including proofs for their main results, is a major strength of the paper. The use of Bayesian reasoning and the connection to probabilistic graphical models further enhance the theoretical depth of the work. The paper also introduces a practical algorithm, the Monte Carlo LogSumExp method, which is derived from the theoretical framework and can be applied to real-world scenarios where the assumptions of the theory may not perfectly hold. This algorithm is a valuable contribution, as it provides a way to combine pre-trained models and handle language ambiguity in reinforcement learning. The experimental results, while not without limitations, demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach in multimodal alignment tasks. The applications of the theory to combining pre-trained models and handling language ambiguity in reinforcement learning are interesting and highlight the potential impact of the proposed framework. The paper is generally well-written and clearly explains the theoretical concepts and their practical implications, making it accessible to readers with varying levels of expertise in the field. The authors also provide a clear motivation for their work, highlighting the importance of understanding how contrastive learning generalizes to unpaired modalities. The paper's focus on a fundamental question in multimodal learning, and its attempt to provide a theoretical grounding for a commonly used heuristic, makes it a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling theoretical framework and a practical algorithm, several weaknesses need to be addressed. First, the theoretical framework relies on several assumptions that may not always hold in practice, and the paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of the limitations of these assumptions. Specifically, the conditional independence assumption (Assumption 1), which states that desired modalities A and C are conditionally independent given the intermediate bridge modality B (A ⊥ C | B), is a strong assumption that may not be valid in scenarios where modalities share common underlying factors or have complex interdependencies. As the paper itself notes, without this assumption, it is generally impossible to uniquely determine P(C|A). The paper provides a brief justification for this assumption, but a more in-depth discussion of its limitations and potential violations is needed. For instance, in a scenario where both image and text describe the same object, the shape of the object could be a shared latent factor influencing both modalities, violating the conditional independence assumption. The assumption about the distribution of representations (Assumption 3) is also a potential limitation. The paper assumes that contrastive learning under symmetrized InfoNCE with an inner product and unit-norm latent vectors acquires representations whose marginal distribution is uniform over the hypersphere. While the paper cites prior work to justify this assumption, it does not discuss the implications of deviations from this assumption, such as when the learned representations have a more complex, multimodal distribution. The paper also makes an assumption (Assumption 2) that applying contrastive learning to the symmetrized InfoNCE objective results in representations that model a density ratio. This assumption, while supported by prior work, is difficult to directly test in practice and the paper does not explore alternative analyses that relax this assumption. The lack of discussion about the limitations of these assumptions and their potential impact on the practical applicability of the proposed method is a significant weakness. Second, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other baseline methods, which makes it difficult to assess the relative performance gains of the proposed method. The experimental sections primarily compare against a “direct comparison” baseline, which serves as a negative control to highlight the benefit of the proposed method. However, the paper does not benchmark against existing multimodal alignment techniques, such as those based on canonical correlation analysis or other contrastive learning approaches that directly handle multiple modalities. This lack of comparison makes it unclear whether the theoretical contributions translate into practical improvements over established methods. For example, in the experiments on pre-trained models, the paper shows that the Monte Carlo method outperforms direct comparison, but it does not compare against other methods that might achieve similar results. Similarly, in the reinforcement learning experiments, the paper compares against a direct representation comparison approach, but does not compare against other state-of-the-art methods for language-conditioned RL. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the true effectiveness of the proposed method. Third, the paper does not include an analysis of sensitivity to hyperparameters, which is crucial for understanding the robustness of the proposed method. The authors should investigate how the performance of their method varies with different choices of learning rates, batch sizes, and other relevant hyperparameters. This analysis is crucial for practical application, as it determines the stability and reliability of the method under different experimental conditions. The absence of such an analysis is a significant weakness, as it leaves open the question of how sensitive the results are to the specific hyperparameter settings used in the experiments. Finally, the experimental section could be expanded to include more real-world datasets and a wider range of modalities. The current experiments, while illustrative, do not fully capture the complexity and variability of real-world data. The paper primarily focuses on synthetic data and a limited set of real-world datasets. It would be beneficial to see results on more diverse and challenging datasets, including those with noisy or incomplete data, to better assess the practical utility of the proposed approach. The limited scope of the experimental evaluation is a weakness that limits the generalizability of the findings. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight the need for further investigation and refinement of the proposed method.
