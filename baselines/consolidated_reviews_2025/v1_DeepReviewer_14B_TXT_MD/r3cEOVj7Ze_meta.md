## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to human trajectory prediction by integrating a neuralized Markov Random Field (MRF) with conditional variational autoencoders (CVAEs). The core idea is to explicitly model both individual motion dynamics and crowd interactions within a unified framework. The authors propose a neuralized MRF, where the potential functions are learned through neural networks, allowing for efficient inference. The model employs two CVAEs: one for approximating the initial state distribution and another for modeling the temporal evolution of trajectories. This architecture enables the model to capture the stochastic nature of human motion and generate multiple plausible future trajectories. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach through extensive experiments on several benchmark datasets, including ETH/UCY, SDD, NBA, and JRDB. The results show that the proposed method achieves state-of-the-art performance in terms of prediction accuracy while maintaining real-time inference capabilities. Furthermore, the paper highlights the robustness of the model to noisy observations and its potential for group reasoning tasks. The authors emphasize that their approach addresses a key gap in existing methods by explicitly modeling the dynamic and hierarchical nature of human interactions. The use of CVAEs for tractable learning and inference is a significant technical innovation, allowing the model to handle the complexity of human motion. The paper is well-organized and clearly written, with a logical flow that makes it easy to follow the development of the proposed method. The authors provide detailed explanations of the MRF framework, the use of CVAEs for tractable inference, and the neural sampler for generating multimodal samples. The method's ability to perform real-time stochastic inference is a significant advantage, especially for applications in dynamic environments such as autonomous driving and robotics. The paper provides evidence of the computational efficiency of the approach, which is crucial for practical deployment. Overall, this paper presents a significant contribution to the field of human trajectory prediction by offering a robust, efficient, and accurate method that explicitly models both individual and interactive motion dynamics.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. A primary concern is the reliance on the Markovian assumption, which states that future states depend only on the current state. This assumption, while simplifying the model, may not fully capture the complexities of human motion, particularly in scenarios with long-term dependencies. As the paper states, "It is built on the assumption that human movements are Markovian up to certain frequencies" (Section 1, Paragraph 3). This assumption is further reinforced by the mathematical formulation in Equation 1, where the future trajectory is modeled as dependent only on the current state. This limitation is particularly problematic in situations involving occlusions or interactions that span multiple time steps. For instance, if an agent is occluded for several frames, its future trajectory will likely be influenced by its pre-occlusion path, violating the Markovian assumption. Similarly, complex interactions, such as a group of agents coordinating their movements, cannot be fully captured by a model that only considers the current state. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of these limitations, nor does it explore potential extensions to address them. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly tied to the core assumption of the model and the lack of discussion regarding its limitations. Another significant weakness is the limited analysis of the model's performance under varying levels of noise and in highly dynamic environments. While the paper includes a section on robustness against observation noise (Section 4.2), the evaluation is limited to 'Dropped History' and 'Noisy History' scenarios on the JRDB dataset (Table 6). The paper does not evaluate the model's performance with other types of noise, such as Gaussian noise or salt-and-pepper noise, which are common in real-world sensor data. Furthermore, the paper lacks a comprehensive analysis of the model's behavior in highly dynamic environments, such as those with sudden changes in crowd density or unexpected agent behaviors. The absence of such analysis makes it difficult to assess the model's reliability in real-world scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's evaluation is clearly limited in the types of noise and dynamic scenarios considered. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method, particularly concerning the MRF component. While the authors claim real-time performance and report inference speeds in Tables 1, 3, and 5, they do not provide a breakdown of the computational costs associated with the MRF formulation, such as message passing or belief propagation steps. The scalability of the approach to large-scale scenarios, where the number of interactions grows quadratically, is also not addressed. This raises concerns about the practical applicability of the method in dense crowds. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide the necessary computational analysis to support its claims of real-time performance and scalability. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art methods, particularly in terms of computational efficiency and robustness. While the paper compares its method with several baselines on accuracy metrics (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5), the comparison is limited in terms of computational efficiency and robustness. The inference speed is only reported for some baselines, and the robustness analysis is limited to the JRDB dataset. A more detailed and consistent comparison across all baselines and noise types is missing. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's comparison is clearly not comprehensive in terms of computational efficiency and robustness. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed ablation study to demonstrate the contribution of each component of the proposed method. The ablation study (Section 4.3) only compares different sampler types and strides, but it does not compare against methods without MRF modeling or CVAE. It would be beneficial to see how the performance changes when the MRF component is removed or when different neural architectures are used for the CVAEs.
