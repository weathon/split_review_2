## Summary

This paper introduces a novel framework, State Decoupling with Q-supervised Contrastive representation (SDQC), designed to address the challenges of safe offline reinforcement learning (RL). The core idea of SDQC is to decouple global observations into distinct reward-related and cost-related representations, aiming to improve generalization capabilities, particularly when faced with unfamiliar observations during testing. This decoupling is achieved through a Q-supervised contrastive learning approach, which encourages similar Q-values across actions to have similar representations, resulting in a coarser, yet theoretically sound, representation. The authors provide a theoretical foundation for their method, demonstrating that this coarser representation preserves the optimal policy while enhancing generalization. The method involves training separate representation networks for reward and cost, using a contrastive loss function that leverages the Q-function to identify similar and dissimilar states. These representations are then used to train three separate policies using weighted regressed diffusion models, an approach adapted from prior work. The empirical evaluation of SDQC is conducted on the DSRL benchmark, a standard suite for safe offline RL, and demonstrates that SDQC outperforms several baseline algorithms in terms of both safety and performance. Specifically, the results show that SDQC achieves near-zero constraint violations in many tasks and exhibits superior generalization in unseen environments. The authors also perform generalization tests by varying the number of obstacles in the environment, further supporting their claims of improved generalization. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and theoretically grounded approach to safe offline RL, with empirical results that suggest a significant advancement in the field. The decoupling of state representations and the use of Q-supervised contrastive learning are innovative techniques that address the critical issue of out-of-distribution generalization in safe offline RL. The paper's contributions are significant, offering a promising direction for developing more robust and reliable safe RL algorithms.

## Strengths

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative approach to safe offline reinforcement learning through the decoupling of state representations. The idea of separating reward-related and cost-related information is both intuitive and well-motivated, addressing a critical challenge in offline RL: the out-of-distribution problem. By learning distinct representations for reward and cost, the method aims to improve generalization capabilities, which is crucial for safe decision-making in unseen environments. The theoretical analysis provided in the paper is another significant strength. The authors rigorously prove that their Q-supervised contrastive learning method generates a coarser representation while preserving the optimal policy, providing a solid foundation for their approach. This theoretical backing adds credibility to the method's effectiveness and differentiates it from purely empirical studies. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling and further support the method's efficacy. The experiments on the DSRL benchmark demonstrate that SDQC outperforms other baseline algorithms in terms of both safety and performance. The ability of SDQC to achieve near-zero constraint violations in many tasks is particularly impressive, highlighting its potential for real-world applications where safety is paramount. Furthermore, the generalization tests, which involve varying the number of obstacles in the environment, provide strong evidence that SDQC can generalize to unseen scenarios. The use of a standard benchmark and the inclusion of relevant baselines make the empirical evaluation robust and reliable. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making the complex ideas accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain their methodology and provide sufficient details for reproducibility. The combination of a novel approach, strong theoretical backing, and compelling empirical results makes this paper a significant contribution to the field of safe offline reinforcement learning.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach to safe offline RL, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. One significant limitation is the lack of a detailed mechanistic explanation of how the decoupled representations specifically mitigate the out-of-distribution (OOD) problem. While the paper argues that decoupling and the resulting coarser representation improve generalization, it does not provide a clear explanation of *how* the learned reward and cost representations are more robust to OOD states compared to standard representations. The paper states that the contrastive loss encourages similar Q-values to have similar representations, leading to a coarser representation, but it does not explicitly demonstrate how this coarser representation is less sensitive to changes in the input space. This lack of a clear mechanistic explanation undermines the claim that the method directly addresses the OOD problem. Furthermore, the generalization tests, while demonstrating improved performance in unseen environments, are limited in scope. The tests are conducted within the same environment type, varying only the number of obstacles. This does not fully demonstrate the method's ability to generalize to significantly different scenarios, such as environments with different dynamics or reward structures. The current experiments do not sufficiently demonstrate the method's ability to generalize to significantly different scenarios. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. The method involves training three separate diffusion models, which adds to the computational overhead and complexity. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the time complexity for each step of the algorithm, including the contrastive learning phase, the Q-function update, and the policy extraction. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the practical implications of the method's computational demands and how it compares to other state-of-the-art safe offline RL algorithms. The paper also does not adequately address the potential for the decoupled representations to lose crucial information when the reward and cost functions are not easily separable in the state space. This could lead to suboptimal policies in complex environments where the optimal action depends on a nuanced combination of reward and cost considerations. The paper assumes that reward and cost information can be effectively decoupled, but it does not explore the potential limitations of this assumption. Additionally, the paper lacks a discussion on the sensitivity of the method to the choice of the contrastive loss function and the specific architecture used for representation learning. The paper uses a specific contrastive loss function and neural network architecture, but it does not explore the impact of different choices on the method's performance. This lack of sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the method and its ability to generalize to different types of environments. Finally, the paper does not include experiments that explicitly test the method's performance under different safety thresholds. This is a critical aspect of safe RL, and the lack of such experiments limits the evaluation of the method's robustness and its ability to adapt to different safety requirements. These weaknesses, while not invalidating the paper's contributions, highlight areas where further investigation and analysis are needed to fully understand the practical implications and limitations of the proposed method. I have high confidence in these identified weaknesses, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the lack of specific analyses or experiments.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete improvements can be made. First, the paper should provide a more detailed mechanistic explanation of how the decoupled representations specifically mitigate the out-of-distribution (OOD) problem. This could involve a theoretical analysis or empirical study that demonstrates how the learned reward and cost representations are less sensitive to OOD states compared to standard representations. For example, the authors could analyze the feature space of the learned representations and show that they are more robust to changes in the input space. Furthermore, the authors should consider including experiments that explicitly test the method's performance on OOD data, such as by training on a subset of the environment and testing on a significantly different subset. This would provide more direct evidence for the method's ability to handle OOD scenarios. Second, the generalization tests should be expanded to include more diverse environments. The current experiments are limited to variations of the same environment, which does not fully demonstrate the method's ability to generalize to significantly different scenarios. The authors should test the method in environments with different dynamics, reward structures, or constraint functions. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method's generalization capabilities. Additionally, the authors should consider using metrics that specifically measure generalization performance, such as the performance gap between training and testing environments. This would provide a more quantitative assessment of the method's ability to generalize to unseen scenarios. Third, the paper should include a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. This should include a breakdown of the time complexity for each step of the algorithm, including the contrastive learning phase, the Q-function update, and the policy extraction. The analysis should also consider the impact of different hyperparameters, such as the size of the representation space and the number of negative samples used in the contrastive learning process. A comparison with other state-of-the-art safe offline RL algorithms should be provided, including a discussion of the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. This comparison should not only focus on the overall training time but also on the memory requirements and the number of parameters. Fourth, the paper should investigate the potential for the decoupled representations to lose crucial information when the reward and cost functions are not easily separable in the state space. The authors should investigate the performance of SDQC in environments where these functions are highly intertwined. For example, they could consider scenarios where the optimal policy requires a delicate balance between maximizing reward and minimizing cost, and where the state space contains regions where both reward and cost are high. Analyzing the behavior of SDQC in such environments would provide valuable insights into the robustness of the method. Furthermore, the authors could explore alternative decoupling strategies that are more adaptive to the specific characteristics of the reward and cost functions. This could involve incorporating a mechanism that dynamically adjusts the degree of decoupling based on the observed data or the current state of the agent. Fifth, the paper should include a sensitivity analysis of the method to the choice of the contrastive loss function and the specific architecture used for representation learning. The authors could experiment with different loss functions, such as InfoNCE or triplet loss, and different architectures, such as convolutional or recurrent neural networks. This would provide a better understanding of the robustness of the method and its ability to generalize to different types of environments. Furthermore, the authors could explore techniques for regularizing the learned representations to prevent overfitting and improve generalization. Finally, the paper should include experiments that explicitly test the method's performance under different safety thresholds. This would provide insights into the robustness of the method and its ability to adapt to different safety requirements. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and provide a more complete understanding of the proposed method's capabilities and limitations.

## Questions

Several key questions arise from my analysis of this paper, focusing on the core methodological choices and assumptions. First, how does the proposed method specifically handle the exploration-exploitation trade-off in the offline setting? While the paper focuses on representation learning and policy extraction, it does not explicitly discuss how the method balances the need to exploit known information with the need to explore new strategies, particularly in the context of offline data. Are there any specific strategies used to balance these two aspects, and how do they impact the overall performance and safety of the learned policy? Second, can the proposed method be extended to other types of constraints, such as time-varying constraints or constraints that depend on the history of past actions? The current formulation of SDQC focuses on constraints that are functions of the current state. However, in many real-world scenarios, constraints may be more complex and dependent on past actions or time. How would the proposed method need to be modified to handle such constraints, and what are the potential challenges and limitations of such extensions? Third, how does the performance of the proposed method compare to other state-of-the-art safe offline RL algorithms in terms of computational complexity and scalability? The paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with training three separate diffusion models and the contrastive learning component. A more thorough comparison with other methods, not just in terms of wall-clock time but also in terms of the number of operations required, would be beneficial. How does the method scale with increasing state and action space dimensionality, and what are the potential bottlenecks? Fourth, what is the impact of the choice of the contrastive loss temperature parameter, the number of negative samples, and the learning rate for the Q-function on the performance of the method? The paper does not include a sensitivity analysis of these key hyperparameters. How do these parameters affect the quality of the learned representations and the overall performance of the algorithm? Finally, how does the method handle the potential for instability in the learning process, particularly when training three separate diffusion models? The paper does not discuss the potential for instability and how it might be mitigated. What are the practical considerations for ensuring stable and reliable training of the proposed method? These questions highlight key uncertainties and areas where further investigation is needed to fully understand the practical implications and limitations of the proposed method.
