## Summary

This paper introduces MUBen, a benchmarking tool designed to evaluate the effectiveness of various uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods when applied to molecular representation models. The core contribution of this work lies in its systematic assessment of multiple UQ techniques across a range of state-of-the-art backbone models and molecular property prediction tasks. The authors explore a diverse set of UQ methods, including deterministic prediction, Bayesian neural networks (BBP and SGLD), post-hoc calibration (Temperature Scaling), Monte Carlo Dropout, and deep ensembles. These methods are applied to several backbone models, such as ChemBERTa, GROVER, Uni-Mol, and a DNN, which utilize different molecular descriptors like SMILES strings, 2D graphs, and 3D conformations. The empirical evaluation is conducted on both classification and regression tasks, using datasets commonly employed in molecular property prediction. The main findings suggest that deep ensembles and Monte Carlo Dropout are particularly effective for regression tasks, while temperature scaling shows promise for classification. However, the paper also highlights that no single UQ method consistently outperforms the deterministic baseline across all tasks and datasets. The authors emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate UQ methods for reliable predictions in materials science and drug discovery, aiming to provide a practical guide for researchers in these fields. Overall, the paper presents a valuable benchmark for the community, but it also reveals several limitations in the current state of UQ for molecular representation learning, particularly in the lack of molecular-specific adaptations and the limited analysis of computational costs. The study's significance lies in its attempt to bridge the gap between molecular representation learning and uncertainty quantification, two critical areas for reliable AI applications in science. However, the paper's limitations, as detailed below, suggest that further research is needed to fully realize the potential of UQ in this domain. The authors acknowledge that their work is not exhaustive, given the rapid advancements in both molecular representation learning and UQ methods, but it serves as a solid foundation for future investigations. The paper's findings underscore the need for more nuanced approaches to UQ in molecular modeling, moving beyond the direct application of methods developed in other domains. The authors' focus on practical applications in drug discovery and materials science highlights the real-world implications of this research, where reliable uncertainty estimates are crucial for informed decision-making. The paper's contribution, therefore, is not only in its empirical findings but also in its identification of key challenges and opportunities for future research in this important area.

## Weaknesses

Despite its strengths, this paper exhibits several weaknesses that significantly impact the validity and generalizability of its conclusions. One major concern is the lack of molecular-specific adaptations in the application of UQ methods. As I've verified, the paper applies UQ techniques in a rather straightforward manner, without exploring how the unique characteristics of molecular data, such as graph structure or chemical properties, could influence the choice or adaptation of these methods. For instance, the descriptions of Bayesian Learning and Inference, Post-Hoc Calibration, and Deep Ensembles in the 'Method' section are standard implementations without specific modifications for molecular data. This is a missed opportunity to advance the field beyond standard applications of existing techniques. The absence of such adaptations raises questions about the optimality of the chosen UQ methods for molecular representation models. This is further compounded by the lack of justification for using UQ methods developed in other domains. The paper does not provide a clear rationale for why certain UQ methods, which may have been developed for image processing or natural language processing, are expected to perform well in the context of molecular property prediction. This lack of justification, as I've confirmed, raises concerns about the relevance and interpretability of the benchmark results. The paper does not discuss the underlying assumptions and limitations of these methods when applied to molecular data, which is a critical oversight. Another significant weakness is the superficial analysis of the experimental results. The conclusions drawn from the experiments are not sufficiently informative and lack persuasiveness. For example, the statement that 'none provides a consistent guarantee of performance improvement over the deterministic baseline, except for Deep Ensembles, and MC Dropout for regression' is a generalization that doesn't fully capture the nuances in the results tables. As I've seen in Tables 1 and 2, the performance of UQ methods varies considerably across different datasets and tasks, and the paper does not delve into the underlying reasons for these variations. The analysis does not explore the specific factors that contribute to the success or failure of each UQ method in different scenarios, such as the size and diversity of the datasets, or the complexity of the molecular descriptors used. This lack of in-depth analysis makes it difficult to draw actionable insights from the study. Furthermore, the paper does not adequately address the impact of different molecular descriptors on the performance of UQ methods. While different backbone models using different descriptors are evaluated, the analysis does not isolate the effect of the descriptor itself. This is a significant oversight, as the choice of molecular descriptor can greatly influence the representation of the molecules and, consequently, the effectiveness of the UQ methods. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with each UQ method. This information is crucial for practitioners who need to balance performance with computational resources. The paper does not include any information about the training time, inference time, memory usage, or the number of parameters for each method. This omission is particularly problematic for Bayesian methods and deep ensembles, which are known to be computationally expensive. The absence of this information limits the practical applicability of the benchmark. Additionally, the use of scaffold splitting, while ensuring novelty, does not fully capture the challenges of real-world scenarios where the test data distribution can differ significantly from the training data. As I've verified, the paper acknowledges the OOD nature of scaffold splitting but does not discuss its limitations regarding different types of distribution shifts, such as covariate shift. This limitation raises concerns about the generalizability of the results to more complex real-world scenarios. Finally, while the paper includes strong models like ChemBERTa and Uni-Mol, the benchmark might be missing some of the most recent and advanced backbone models and UQ methods. The field is rapidly evolving, and newer, potentially more advanced methods exist. This limitation, while acknowledged by the authors, suggests that the benchmark may not be fully representative of the state-of-the-art in molecular representation learning and uncertainty quantification. In summary, these weaknesses, including the lack of molecular-specific UQ adaptations, insufficient justification for UQ method choice, superficial analysis of results, missing computational cost analysis, limitations of scaffold splitting, and the potential for more recent methods, significantly impact the paper's conclusions and limit its overall impact.
