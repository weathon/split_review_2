## Summary

This paper introduces a novel federated learning setting, termed Modality-Collaborated Federated Learning (MCFL), which aims to facilitate collaboration among uni-modal clients, each possessing data from different modalities. This contrasts with traditional multi-modal federated learning, which typically assumes clients have access to multi-modal data. The authors propose a framework called Federated Modality Collaboration (FedCola), based on a modality-agnostic transformer, to address the challenges of model heterogeneity and modality gaps inherent in MCFL. FedCola explores optimal strategies for cross-modal parameter sharing, model aggregation, and temporal modality arrangement. Specifically, the framework employs an attention-sharing mechanism, modality compensation to address sample imbalance, and a modality warm-up strategy. The core idea is to enable uni-modal clients to benefit from each other's knowledge without requiring access to multi-modal data. The authors conduct comprehensive evaluations, comparing FedCola against adapted baselines like Uni-FedAVG and CreamFL, across various federated learning scenarios, including different numbers of clients, levels of data heterogeneity, and client availability ratios. The results demonstrate that FedCola outperforms existing solutions in the proposed MCFL setting, establishing it as a robust baseline. The paper's primary contribution lies in the introduction of the MCFL setting and the development of a practical framework to address its challenges. However, the technical novelty of the proposed method and the scope of the experimental evaluation are areas that warrant further scrutiny. The paper's focus on vision and language modalities, while relevant, limits the generalizability of the findings to other types of multi-modal data. Additionally, the paper's analysis of computational and communication costs, while present, lacks the depth required to fully assess the practical feasibility of the proposed framework in resource-constrained environments. Overall, the paper presents an interesting and practical problem setting, but the technical contributions and experimental validation could be strengthened to fully realize its potential impact.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a novel setting and a practical framework, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the distinction between the proposed MCFL setting and existing multi-modal federated learning settings is not as substantial as the paper suggests. The core difference, as I've verified, lies in the evaluation metric, which is derived from the individual performances across each modality, rather than a joint multi-modal task. The paper states, "The final performance will be evaluated as the equal-weighted arithmetic mean of Top-1 Accuracy among all modalities." This is a subtle distinction and does not fundamentally alter the underlying problem of learning a shared representation across modalities, especially when explicit cross-modal supervision is absent during local training. The paper does not adequately address how the proposed method tackles the challenge of aligning cross-modal representations in the context of unimodal clients. The evaluation metric, while considering individual modality performance, does not fundamentally address the core challenge of learning a shared representation across modalities when explicit cross-modal supervision is absent during local training. This is a significant limitation, as the core challenge of multi-modal learning is to learn a shared representation that captures the complementary information from different modalities. The paper's approach seems to treat each modality independently, which is not ideal for multi-modal learning. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's definition of MCFL and the description of the evaluation metric. 

Second, the technical novelty of the proposed FedCola framework is somewhat limited. While the paper introduces a modality-agnostic transformer, the core of FedCola boils down to exploring parameter-sharing strategies and model aggregation schemes within this architecture. The paper describes the components of FedCola as "Attention Sharing", "Modality Compensation", and "Modality Warm-up". These components primarily involve modifications to parameter sharing and aggregation within a transformer architecture. The paper does not introduce a fundamentally new transformer architecture or a novel theoretical framework for handling heterogeneity beyond these adaptations. The exploration of parameter sharing strategies, while practical, does not introduce a fundamentally new technical insight into how to handle the inherent heterogeneity of unimodal clients in a multi-modal world. The paper lacks a deep analysis of the specific challenges posed by the MCFL setting that would necessitate a novel approach beyond standard parameter sharing and aggregation. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is based on my direct examination of the method description in Section 5.

While these baselines are relevant, the evaluation would benefit from comparisons with a broader range of existing multi-modal federated learning methods, even if they are not directly designed for the MCFL setting. This would provide a better understanding of how FedCola stacks up against the state-of-the-art in related areas. The chosen baselines, while relevant, do not fully capture the breadth of existing multi-modal federated learning approaches, particularly those that focus on cross-modal alignment and knowledge transfer. The paper's focus on adapting existing methods to the MCFL setting, rather than benchmarking against a wider array of multi-modal federated learning techniques, limits the scope of the evaluation. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is based on the explicit description of the comparison methods in Section 6.1. 

Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational and communication costs associated with the proposed FedCola framework. While Section 6.3 provides a comparison of computation and communication costs, stating that "CreamFL requires 1.97x computation" and "FedCola maintains the same resource requirements as Uni-FedAVG", this analysis lacks a detailed breakdown of the computational complexity of individual components or a formal analysis of communication overhead. A thorough analysis of the computational complexity and communication overhead of FedCola compared to existing methods would be valuable, especially for practical deployment in resource-constrained environments. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for both the client-side and server-side operations, nor does it quantify the communication overhead in terms of the number of parameters transmitted between clients and the server. This lack of detailed analysis limits the practical insights that can be gained from the paper. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is based on the limited scope of the resource analysis in Section 6.3. 

Fifth, the paper focuses on a specific type of multi-modal data (vision and language) for demonstration. While this is a common and relevant combination, it would be beneficial to explore the applicability of the proposed framework to other types of multi-modal data, such as audio and video, or different combinations of modalities. The paper states, "As vision and language are the most popular modalities in current studies, we focus on these two modalities for demonstration." The experiments are limited to vision and language, and in the medical domain, medical imaging and transcripts. This limited evaluation does not adequately demonstrate the scalability or robustness of the proposed method to more complex scenarios involving a larger number of modalities, larger datasets, or more diverse data distributions. The choice of datasets, while common, does not fully capture the challenges of real-world multi-modal federated learning. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is based on the explicit description of the datasets used in Section 6.1. 

Finally, the paper does not extensively discuss the potential limitations or challenges of the proposed framework. While the paper introduces "Modality Compensation" (Section 5.2) to address imbalances in the number of training samples per modality, it lacks a comprehensive discussion of potential limitations and failure modes. The paper does not explicitly discuss potential failure modes beyond the performance drops observed in the vanilla MAT. Second, the authors should enhance the technical novelty of the FedCola framework. This would require a more rigorous theoretical analysis of the proposed method and its properties. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method compared to existing state-of-the-art approaches. Specifically, it would be beneficial to compare against methods that explicitly address cross-modal alignment and knowledge transfer, even if they are not directly designed for the MCFL setting. This would provide a more complete picture of the effectiveness of the proposed method and its robustness to different experimental conditions. Additionally, the paper should investigate the performance of FedCola with different combinations of modalities, such as vision and audio or language and video. This would help to establish the generalizability of the proposed approach and its potential for real-world applications. Finally, the authors should include a more detailed discussion of the potential limitations and challenges of the FedCola framework. This should include an analysis of how the framework handles scenarios with highly imbalanced data distributions across modalities or clients. For example, the authors could investigate the impact of varying the number of samples per modality on the performance of the global model. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the potential failure modes of the proposed approach, such as the risk of negative transfer between modalities or the sensitivity of the framework to hyperparameter settings. A thorough analysis of these limitations would provide a more balanced perspective on the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed framework and guide future research in this area.
