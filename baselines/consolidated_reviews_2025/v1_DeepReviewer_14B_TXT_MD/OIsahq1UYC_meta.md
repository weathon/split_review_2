## Summary

The paper introduces a novel method for sampling from intractable densities by integrating diffusion models with ideas from Generative Flow Networks (GFlowNets). The core innovation lies in the introduction of a flow function that aggregates information for training intermediate states, enabling the model to update its parameters using partial trajectory segments and receive intermediate learning signals. This approach is designed to reduce gradient variance and improve the efficiency of the sampling process. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their method, termed Diffusion Generative Flow Samplers (DGFS), through a series of benchmark experiments, including Mixture of Gaussians (MoG), Funnel, Manywell, VAE, and Cox distributions. The empirical results show that DGFS outperforms several existing methods, such as Path Integral Sampler (PIS) and Denoising Diffusion Sampler (DDS), in terms of lower gradient variance and better sampling performance. However, the paper's clarity, experimental scope, and theoretical foundation are areas that could be improved to better assess the method's robustness and practical utility.

## Strengths

I found the paper to be well-written and easy to follow, which is a significant strength. The proposed method, DGFS, is a novel and well-motivated extension of the Path Integral Sampler (PIS) framework. By integrating GFlowNet concepts, the authors introduce a flow function that allows for the use of intermediate learning signals, which is a creative and innovative approach. This method is designed to reduce gradient variance, a critical issue in training diffusion models, and the experimental results provide promising evidence of its effectiveness. The authors compare DGFS to several existing methods, including SMC, VI-NF, CRAFT, PIS, and DDS, and show that it outperforms these baselines on a variety of benchmark tasks. The paper also includes a detailed discussion of the method's workflow and the subtrajectory balance loss, which is a key technical innovation. The introduction of the flow function and the ability to update parameters using partial trajectories are significant contributions that address the limitations of standard diffusion models, particularly in terms of gradient noise and convergence stability.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, there are several verified limitations that need to be addressed. Firstly, the method introduces additional complexity compared to standard diffusion models, which may make it harder to implement and train. The introduction of the flow function and the subtrajectory balance loss adds to the model architecture and training process, requiring the training of an additional neural network and the computation of a more complex loss function. This increased complexity is a valid concern, as it could pose practical challenges for researchers and practitioners looking to adopt the method. Secondly, the experimental evaluation is limited to relatively low-dimensional settings. While the paper includes a 1600-dimensional benchmark, this is not extremely high-dimensional compared to the state-of-the-art in modern machine learning, especially in areas like deep generative modeling. The lack of experiments on very high-dimensional problems makes it difficult to assess the method's scalability and practical utility in real-world applications. Thirdly, the paper does not provide sufficient detail on the architecture and training of the flow function. The specific architecture of the neural network used for the flow function is not described, and there is no sensitivity analysis of the method to the choice of this network's architecture or hyperparameters. This omission makes it challenging to reproduce the results and evaluate the practical feasibility of the method. Additionally, the claim that the method leads to lower gradient variance is not sufficiently supported by the experiments. While Figure 2 shows a reduction in gradient variance, the paper does not clearly link this reduction to improved sampling performance or provide a detailed analysis of the relationship between gradient variance and sampling quality. A more comprehensive comparison with other relevant baselines, such as standard diffusion models with different training objectives, would strengthen this claim. Furthermore, the paper lacks theoretical guarantees for the method's convergence and performance. The absence of formal proofs or error bounds makes it difficult to assess the reliability and robustness of the method. The paper also uses notation and terminology that may be confusing for readers. For example, the term 'state' is used to refer to a node in a graph, which is inconsistent with the continuous space in which the method operates. The notation $	ext{p}^{refe}_n$ is not clearly defined, and the use of 'forward process' is opposite to its standard meaning in diffusion model literature. These issues can hinder the readability and accessibility of the paper. Finally, the paper could benefit from a more thorough comparison with other classes of sampling algorithms, such as Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) and its variants, as well as other advanced MCMC techniques. A detailed comparison should not only focus on the final sampling performance but also on the computational cost and convergence properties of each method. This would provide a more informed assessment of the method's practical utility and its potential advantages over existing approaches.

## Suggestions

To address the identified limitations, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. Firstly, the paper should provide a more detailed description of the flow function's architecture and training process. This includes specifying the neural network architecture used, how it is trained in conjunction with the diffusion model, and the computational overhead of training this additional network. A sensitivity analysis of the method to the choice of the flow function's architecture and hyperparameters would also be beneficial. Secondly, the experimental evaluation should be expanded to include higher-dimensional benchmark tasks. This would help to assess the method's scalability and practical utility in real-world applications. The authors should consider including benchmarks with varying levels of multimodality, dimensionality, and complexity, such as those found in the 'Benchmarking and Evaluation of Sampling Algorithms' paper. Thirdly, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the relationship between gradient variance and sampling quality. This could involve showing how the reduced gradient variance affects the convergence rate of the sampling process and comparing the gradient variance of DGFS with other relevant baselines, such as standard diffusion models with different training objectives. A more comprehensive comparison with other classes of sampling algorithms, including HMC and advanced MCMC techniques, would also be valuable. This comparison should focus on both the final sampling performance and the computational cost and convergence properties of each method. Fourthly, the paper should clarify the use of notation and terminology. For example, the term 'state' should be consistently used to refer to a point in the continuous space, and the notation $	ext{p}^{refe}_n$ should be clearly defined. The use of 'forward process' should be reconsidered or explained more carefully to avoid confusion with the standard terminology in diffusion models. A table summarizing all the notations and their definitions would be very helpful. Finally, the paper should include a theoretical analysis of the method's convergence properties and error bounds. This could involve analyzing the properties of the learned flow function and its relationship to the target distribution, as well as exploring the connection between the proposed method and existing theoretical frameworks for sampling, such as those based on Markov chain theory. Such theoretical insights would not only provide a deeper understanding of the method but also guide its further development and application.

## Questions

I have several questions that I believe would help to clarify the core methodological choices and assumptions in the paper. Firstly, how does the performance of DGFS compare to other approaches for sampling from intractable densities, such as normalizing flows and Markov chain Monte Carlo methods? While the paper includes some comparisons, a more detailed analysis of the computational cost and convergence properties of these methods would be beneficial. Secondly, how does the method perform in higher-dimensional settings, and what are the practical limitations of the method as the dimensionality of the problem increases? The current experiments are limited to a maximum of 1600 dimensions, and it would be valuable to see how the method scales to even higher dimensions. Thirdly, how does the choice of the reference process affect the performance of the method? The paper mentions the reference process but does not explore its impact on the sampling performance. Fourthly, what is the exact contribution of the paper, and how does the proposed method improve upon previous work? The paper should clearly articulate the novel aspects of DGFS and how it addresses the limitations of existing methods. Lastly, how does the method estimate the partition function of the unnormalized target distribution, and what is the relationship between the proposed method and the path integral framework? A more detailed explanation of these aspects would help to better understand the theoretical underpinnings of the method.
