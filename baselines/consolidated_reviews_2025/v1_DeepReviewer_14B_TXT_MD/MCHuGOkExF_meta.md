## Summary

This paper introduces Scattered Forest Search (SFS), a novel approach to code generation that reframes the task as a black-box optimization problem within the code space. The core idea is to enhance the diversity of solutions explored by Large Language Models (LLMs) during inference, thereby improving the likelihood of finding correct and efficient code. The authors propose three key techniques: Branch Scatter, which dynamically varies input prompts to encourage diverse solution exploration; Forest Search, which performs tree search from multiple random seed solutions to avoid local optima; and Branch Scout, which shares feedback across search branches to guide the search process. These techniques are motivated by optimization theory and analyzed using Markov chain theory, providing a theoretical foundation for the proposed approach. The authors present a comprehensive experimental evaluation on five code generation benchmarks: HumanEval, MBPP, APPS, CodeContests, and Leetcode. The results demonstrate significant performance improvements over state-of-the-art methods in terms of accuracy, scalability, and solution diversity. Specifically, SFS achieves higher pass rates, converges faster, and generates more diverse solutions compared to existing techniques. The method is designed to be simple to implement, parameter-free, and does not require additional training data, making it practical for large-scale deployments. The paper's main contribution lies in its novel framing of code generation as a black-box optimization problem and the introduction of SFS as an effective method for exploring the code space. The empirical results strongly support the efficacy of SFS, showcasing its potential to advance the field of code generation. The theoretical analysis, while not fully predictive of the empirical results, provides a valuable framework for understanding the behavior of the method. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field, offering a practical and effective approach to scaling LLM inference for code generation.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to address the lack of computational cost analysis, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the time spent on each component of the SFS method. This should include the time spent on branch scatter, forest search, and branch scout, as well as the time spent on evaluation. The authors should also compare the computational cost of SFS to that of baseline methods, such as simple sampling or tree search, on the same hardware. This comparison should include not only the total time but also the memory usage. The authors could also explore techniques to reduce the computational cost of SFS, such as using more efficient data structures or parallelizing the search process. This would make the method more practical for large-scale deployments. Additionally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of how the computational cost scales with the size of the code space and the number of iterations. Second, to address the lack of sensitivity analysis, the authors should conduct a thorough investigation of how variations in the hyperparameters of the underlying LLM (e.g., temperature, maximum token length) and the specific implementation details of the search algorithm affect the performance of SFS. This analysis should include not only the final pass@k rate but also other metrics such as the convergence speed and the diversity of the generated solutions. For example, the authors could present a series of plots showing how the pass@k rate varies with different temperature settings of the LLM, or how the convergence speed is affected by different selection strategies in the SFS algorithm. This would provide a better understanding of the robustness of the proposed method and help to identify the optimal hyperparameter settings for different tasks and datasets. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the potential for adaptive hyperparameter tuning, where the hyperparameters are adjusted during the search process based on the performance of the algorithm. Third, to address the lack of analysis of the limitations, the authors should provide a more in-depth discussion of the specific scenarios where SFS might fail or underperform. This should include a discussion of the potential challenges of applying SFS to tasks that require complex reasoning or planning, or to tasks where the solution space is highly constrained. For example, the authors could discuss how SFS might struggle with code generation tasks that require a deep understanding of the problem context or that involve multiple interacting components. Additionally, the authors should explore the potential for SFS to get stuck in local optima, and discuss strategies for mitigating this issue. This could involve incorporating techniques such as simulated annealing or genetic algorithms into the SFS framework. Fourth, to address the lack of analysis of the generalization ability, the authors should consider applying the method to a few representative problems in other fields. This could include combinatorial optimization problems, such as the traveling salesman problem or the knapsack problem, or natural language processing tasks, such as text summarization or machine translation. The authors should discuss the potential challenges and adaptations required to apply SFS to these domains. For example, how would the branch scatter and scout techniques be adapted to non-code domains? Would the evaluation function need to be modified? The authors should also analyze the performance of SFS on these other domains and compare it to existing methods. This would demonstrate the versatility of the proposed approach and broaden its impact. Finally, to address the limited evaluation across different LLMs, the authors should conduct a more comprehensive analysis of how the performance of SFS varies with different LLMs, including open-source models. This analysis should include not only the final pass@k rate but also other metrics such as the convergence speed and the diversity of the generated solutions. This would provide a better understanding of the generalizability of the proposed method and help to identify the optimal LLM for different tasks and datasets.
