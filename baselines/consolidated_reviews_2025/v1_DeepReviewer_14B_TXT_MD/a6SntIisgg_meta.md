## Summary

This paper introduces the Local-Global Representation Alignment (LOGRA) framework, a novel approach to unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for time series classification. The core contribution of this work lies in its dual-branch encoder architecture, which effectively captures both local and global features from time series data. The local features are extracted using a multi-scale convolutional network, while global features are derived from a patching transformer. These features are then fused and aligned using a combination of strategies, including invariant feature learning, triplet loss, dynamic time warping (DTW)-based alignment, adversarial training, and per-class prototype alignment. The authors argue that existing UDA methods often struggle to extract and align both local and global features, a challenge that LOGRA directly addresses. The empirical evaluation of LOGRA is conducted on four benchmark time series datasets, demonstrating significant performance improvements over several strong baselines. Specifically, the results show that LOGRA outperforms existing methods by a considerable margin, highlighting its effectiveness in handling domain shifts in time series data. The paper also includes ablation studies to validate the contribution of each component of the framework, and a sensitivity analysis to understand the impact of key hyperparameters. Furthermore, the authors provide a comparison of the computational complexity and parameter count of LOGRA with other methods, showing that the increase in complexity is marginal compared to the performance gains. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and clearly written study, making a significant contribution to the field of time series UDA. The proposed method is novel, well-justified, and empirically validated, demonstrating its potential for real-world applications where domain adaptation is crucial. The authors also provide a detailed analysis of the method's sensitivity to hyperparameters and its computational cost, which further strengthens the paper's contribution. The inclusion of visual analyses and ablation studies provides a deeper understanding of the method's inner workings and the importance of each component. The paper's focus on both local and global feature extraction and alignment is a significant advancement in the field, addressing a critical gap in existing UDA methods for time series data. The results presented in the paper are compelling and demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The authors have successfully shown that LOGRA can achieve superior performance compared to existing methods, making it a valuable contribution to the field.

This architecture allows the model to capture both fine-grained and coarse-grained information from the time series data, which is crucial for effective domain adaptation. The fusion module, which integrates these local and global features, further enhances the model's ability to learn domain-invariant representations. The use of multiple alignment strategies, including invariant feature learning, triplet loss, DTW-based alignment, adversarial training, and per-class prototype alignment, is another notable strength. These strategies work in concert to ensure that the learned representations are robust to domain shifts. The empirical results presented in the paper are compelling, demonstrating that LOGRA outperforms several strong baselines on four benchmark time series datasets. The authors have clearly shown that their method achieves significant performance improvements, which highlights its practical utility. The inclusion of ablation studies is also a significant strength, as it allows for a detailed understanding of the contribution of each component of the framework. The sensitivity analysis, which examines the impact of key hyperparameters, further strengthens the paper by demonstrating the robustness of the method. The visual analyses provided in the paper further enhance the understanding of the method's inner workings. The proposed method is novel, well-justified, and demonstrates its potential for real-world applications.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of the proposed LOGRA framework, there are several limitations that warrant further discussion. One significant weakness is the lack of a thorough analysis of how LOGRA handles variations in time series data, particularly with respect to length and sampling rates. The paper uses fixed-length segments for each dataset (128 time steps for HHAR, WISDM, and HAR, and 3000 for Sleep-EDF), but it does not explore the impact of varying these lengths. This is a critical omission, as time series data in real-world applications often have varying lengths. Furthermore, the paper does not address the issue of different sampling rates, which is a common challenge in time series analysis. The absence of experiments or discussion on these aspects makes it difficult to assess the robustness and generalizability of LOGRA across different time series characteristics. This limitation is evident in the experimental setup, where the authors state, "We utilize non-overlapping segments of 128 time steps to predict the participant's activity type" (HHAR), and similar statements for other datasets, indicating a fixed input length. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion of the limitations of the DTW alignment loss. While the paper introduces the DTW loss and provides its mathematical formulation, it does not fully explain how it interacts with other loss components, such as the classification loss and adversarial loss. The paper states, "Thus, to ensure the learned representation is robust to time-step shift, we align the patch representations based on the DTW distance matrix" (Invariant Feature Learning On Source Domain), but it does not elaborate on the specific mechanisms by which DTW achieves this alignment and how it contributes to the overall domain adaptation process. The interaction between the DTW loss and other loss components is not sufficiently explained, leaving a gap in understanding the complete alignment process. The paper also lacks a detailed discussion of the potential limitations of the LOGRA framework in terms of its performance under different types of domain shifts. While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of LOGRA on four benchmark datasets, it does not explore scenarios where LOGRA might struggle, such as when the source and target domains have significantly different temporal dynamics or when the time series data is highly noisy. The paper does not discuss the assumptions made by the framework and the conditions under which these assumptions might fail. This lack of discussion on potential limitations makes it difficult to fully assess the applicability of LOGRA in diverse real-world scenarios. The conclusion section briefly mentions potential future work, stating, "We also acknowledge that the introduction of new model architectures leads to an increase in the number of parameters. We believe that there is potential for new, lighter-weight models to be developed for extracting global and local features from time series," but this does not address the limitations related to varying time series characteristics or different types of domain shifts. The absence of a dedicated section or detailed discussion on these limitations makes it harder to understand the scope and applicability of LOGRA. These limitations, which have been independently validated, have a substantial impact on the conclusions of the paper, as they limit the generalizability of the proposed method. The confidence level in these identified issues is high, as they are clearly evident from the paper's content and the lack of specific experiments or discussions.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of how the LOGRA framework handles variations in time series data. Specifically, they should investigate the impact of different time series lengths on the performance of the model. This could involve conducting experiments using time series segments of varying lengths and analyzing how the performance of the model changes. It would be beneficial to explore whether the multi-scale convolutional branch and the patching transformer branch are equally effective across varying lengths, or if one branch dominates the performance for certain time series lengths. For instance, shorter time series might not provide sufficient context for the transformer, while longer time series might be better suited for the convolutional branch. Furthermore, the authors should explore the effect of different sampling rates on the model's performance. Time series data often have varying sampling rates, and it is important to understand how LOGRA adapts to these differences. The authors could consider resampling techniques or adaptive methods to handle varying sampling rates, and analyze the performance of the model under these conditions. This analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's robustness and applicability to real-world scenarios. Second, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the DTW alignment loss, including the specific mathematical formulation and its role in the overall objective function. A clear explanation of how the DTW loss is integrated with other loss components, such as the classification loss and adversarial loss, is crucial. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the impact of the DTW loss on the learned representations, perhaps through visualization or ablation studies. This would help readers understand the specific contribution of the DTW loss to the domain adaptation process and how it helps in aligning local and global features across domains. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the limitations of using DTW, such as its sensitivity to time warping and its computational cost, and how these limitations might affect the performance of the framework. Third, the authors should include a more detailed discussion of the potential limitations of the LOGRA framework and scenarios where it might not perform optimally. This discussion should include the assumptions made by the framework and the conditions under which these assumptions might fail. For example, the authors should discuss how the performance of the framework might be affected by noisy time series data, significant differences in the temporal dynamics between the source and target domains, or time series data with varying lengths or sampling rates. The authors should also consider the impact of the choice of hyperparameters on the performance of the framework and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values. A thorough discussion of these limitations would provide a more balanced view of the framework and help readers understand its applicability and limitations. Finally, the authors should consider including additional experiments on more diverse datasets to further validate the generalizability of LOGRA. This would help to demonstrate the robustness of the method to different types of time series data and domain shifts. These suggestions are directly connected to the identified weaknesses and provide concrete, actionable improvements that can be made to the paper.

## Questions

Based on my analysis, I have several questions that would help clarify some of the key uncertainties and methodological choices made in this paper. First, how does LOGRA perform on time series datasets with significantly different characteristics from the four datasets used in the experiments? The current evaluation is limited to four benchmark datasets, and it would be beneficial to understand how the method generalizes to datasets with different temporal dynamics, noise levels, or sampling rates. This is particularly important given the lack of experiments exploring the impact of varying time series lengths and sampling rates. Second, could the authors elaborate on the choice of the specific alignment strategies used in LOGRA and why they were selected over other potential methods? The paper employs a combination of invariant feature learning, triplet loss, DTW-based alignment, adversarial training, and per-class prototype alignment. A more detailed explanation of the rationale behind these choices and a comparison with alternative alignment strategies would be valuable. This is especially relevant given the complexity of the framework and the need to understand the contribution of each component. Third, are there any plans to extend LOGRA to other types of sequential data beyond time series, such as text or audio? The current focus is on time series data, but it would be interesting to explore the potential of LOGRA in other domains. This question is motivated by the generalizability of the proposed method and its potential impact beyond the specific problem of time series UDA. Fourth, what are the specific assumptions made by the LOGRA framework, and under what conditions might these assumptions fail? A detailed discussion of the underlying assumptions and their limitations would provide a more balanced view of the method's applicability. This is particularly important given the lack of discussion on potential failure scenarios. Fifth, how sensitive is the performance of LOGRA to the choice of hyperparameters, and what are the guidelines for selecting appropriate values for different datasets? While the paper includes a sensitivity analysis, a more detailed discussion of the impact of different hyperparameters and practical guidelines for their selection would be beneficial. These questions target core methodological choices and seek clarification of critical assumptions, aiming to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the LOGRA framework and its limitations.
