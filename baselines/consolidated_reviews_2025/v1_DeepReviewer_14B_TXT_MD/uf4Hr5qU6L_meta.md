## Summary

This paper introduces Problem Representation Enhanced Chain-of-Thought (PRECOT), a novel prompting framework designed to improve the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). The core idea behind PRECOT is to explicitly guide the LLM to construct a problem representation before attempting to solve it. This representation consists of two key components: the initial state, which encapsulates the given information in the problem, and the goal state, which defines the objective or the question to be answered. The authors argue that this explicit problem representation construction, inspired by human problem-solving processes, can lead to more robust and accurate reasoning. The PRECOT framework is implemented in two variants: a few-shot version, where the LLM is provided with demonstrations of how to extract the initial and goal states, and a zero-shot version, where the LLM is instructed to perform this extraction without explicit examples. After constructing the problem representation, the LLM then proceeds to solve the problem using a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) approach, which is a widely used prompting technique that encourages the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps. The authors evaluated PRECOT on a diverse set of 15 benchmarks spanning arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning tasks. Their results demonstrate that PRECOT outperforms standard CoT in most cases, in both few-shot and zero-shot settings. This suggests that explicitly guiding the LLM to understand the problem structure can significantly enhance its reasoning performance. The paper's contribution lies in its novel approach to prompting LLMs by incorporating problem representation, and the empirical evidence provided supports the effectiveness of this approach across various reasoning domains. The authors also provide code for reproducibility, which is a valuable contribution to the research community. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically supported method for improving LLM reasoning, making it a valuable contribution to the field.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the core idea of incorporating problem representation into the prompting framework is both novel and intuitively appealing. The authors' inspiration from cognitive psychology, specifically the concept of problem representation construction in human problem-solving, provides a solid foundation for their approach. This is not just a random tweak to the prompting strategy; it's grounded in a well-established understanding of how humans approach complex tasks. The explicit separation of the problem into initial and goal states forces the LLM to engage in a more structured reasoning process, which I believe is a key factor in the improved performance. Second, the empirical evaluation is quite comprehensive. The authors tested PRECOT on 15 different benchmarks across three distinct reasoning categories, which demonstrates the generalizability of their approach. The fact that PRECOT consistently outperforms standard CoT in most of these tasks, in both few-shot and zero-shot settings, is a strong indicator of its effectiveness. The inclusion of both few-shot and zero-shot evaluations is also important, as it shows that PRECOT is not solely reliant on task-specific demonstrations. Furthermore, the authors have made their code publicly available, which is crucial for reproducibility and allows other researchers to build upon their work. This commitment to open science is commendable. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors clearly explain their methodology, experimental setup, and results, making it accessible to a broad audience. The use of examples throughout the paper helps to illustrate the key concepts and makes the approach more concrete. The combination of a strong theoretical foundation, comprehensive empirical evaluation, and clear presentation makes this a valuable contribution to the field of LLM research.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the authors position PRECOT as a novel approach, I find that its novelty is somewhat limited. The core mechanism of guiding the LLM through a step-by-step reasoning process is fundamentally similar to zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT), particularly the 'Let's think step by step' approach. The explicit extraction of initial and goal states in PRECOT can be seen as a more detailed version of the implicit process encouraged by the 'Let's think step by step' instruction. The paper itself acknowledges the existence of zero-shot CoT and its 'Let's think step by step' instruction in the introduction. While PRECOT adds a problem representation stage, the underlying mechanism of guiding the LLM through a reasoning process is similar. This is not to say that PRECOT is not effective, but the incremental novelty compared to existing methods is not as substantial as the authors claim. Second, the paper's evaluation is limited by the choice of baselines. The authors only compare PRECOT with few-shot and zero-shot CoT, despite mentioning other relevant methods in the related work section, such as problem decomposition and planning. The related work section explicitly mentions problem decomposition and planning as other approaches, yet these are not included in the experimental comparison. This omission makes it difficult to assess the unique advantages of PRECOT compared to other reasoning strategies. For example, it would be valuable to see how PRECOT performs against methods that explicitly decompose problems into sub-problems or use planning algorithms to guide the reasoning process. This lack of comparative analysis limits the paper's ability to demonstrate the unique contributions of PRECOT. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed approach. While the paper describes the two stages of PRECOT (Problem Representation Construction and Solution Searching), it does not provide any quantitative analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the problem representation construction stage. The experimental setup mentions using public APIs of PaLM 2 and GPT-3 with greedy decoding and zero temperature for reproducibility, but there is no mention of time taken for each stage, API call costs, or resource consumption. This is a significant omission, as the additional step of problem representation construction might introduce a non-negligible computational overhead. Understanding the time and resource implications of PRECOT is crucial for its practical application. Fourth, while the authors mention that their approach is inspired by cognitive psychology, the connection to specific cognitive theories or models is not thoroughly explored. The paper cites Newell (1972), Greeno (1977, 1978), and Gick (1986) in relation to cognitive psychology and problem-solving, but the discussion primarily focuses on the high-level idea of problem representation construction. The paper does not delve into how PRECOT's mechanisms align with or build upon specific cognitive models of problem-solving, such as the problem-space theory or the ACT-R framework. This lack of a deeper connection to cognitive psychology weakens the theoretical foundation of the work. Finally, the paper primarily focuses on performance improvements and does not investigate the interpretability of the generated problem representations. The experimental sections focus on quantitative accuracy comparisons between PRECOT and CoT, and while error analysis is provided, there is no analysis dedicated to examining or interpreting the generated problem representations themselves. Understanding the nature of these representations and how they contribute to improved reasoning would provide valuable insights into the model's reasoning process. The absence of such analysis limits the paper's contribution to a purely performance-based evaluation, neglecting the potential for deeper understanding of the model's behavior.

## Suggestions

Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for this paper. First, the authors should more clearly articulate the distinction between their approach and existing methods, particularly zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT). While the paper positions PRECOT as a more structured approach by explicitly defining initial and goal states, the core mechanism of guiding the LLM through a step-by-step reasoning process is fundamentally similar to zero-shot CoT. To strengthen the paper, the authors need to provide a more rigorous analysis of how the explicit problem representation in PRECOT leads to qualitatively different reasoning behaviors compared to simply prompting with 'Let's think step by step'. This could involve a detailed examination of the intermediate representations generated by the model under both approaches, highlighting the specific ways in which PRECOT's structured representation facilitates more accurate or efficient reasoning. Furthermore, the authors should explore the limitations of PRECOT, such as scenarios where the initial and goal states are difficult to define or where the problem requires a more flexible reasoning approach that doesn't fit into a strict initial-to-goal state framework. Second, the authors should include a more comprehensive evaluation that incorporates other reasoning strategies like decomposition and planning. The current evaluation only compares PRECOT with CoT, which is insufficient to demonstrate the unique advantages of the proposed method. The authors should investigate how PRECOT compares to methods that explicitly decompose problems into sub-problems or use planning algorithms to guide the reasoning process. This would involve not only comparing the final performance metrics but also analyzing the reasoning trajectories generated by each method. For example, the authors could examine whether PRECOT's structured approach leads to more efficient search or better handling of complex problems compared to decomposition or planning methods. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of PRECOT and its place within the broader landscape of LLM reasoning techniques. Third, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with PRECOT. This analysis should include not only the time taken but also the number of API calls and the overall resource consumption, which are crucial for practical applications. The authors should quantify the additional time or resources required for the problem representation construction stage and compare it with other prompting techniques, such as Chain-of-Thought (CoT), to understand the trade-offs between performance gains and computational overhead. This analysis should also explore how the time complexity scales with the size of the input problem or the complexity of the reasoning task. Fourth, the connection to cognitive psychology needs to be more thoroughly explored. The authors should discuss how the concept of problem representation aligns with existing cognitive models of problem-solving, such as the problem-space theory or the ACT-R framework. A more in-depth analysis of these connections would strengthen the theoretical foundation of the work and provide a more solid justification for the proposed method. It would also be beneficial to discuss how the proposed method might relate to human cognitive biases or limitations, and whether it can help mitigate these issues in LLMs. Finally, the authors should delve deeper into the interpretability of the generated problem representations. They should analyze the structure and content of the generated problem representations to understand what kind of information is being captured and how it contributes to the improved reasoning performance. For example, are the representations capturing key entities, relationships, or constraints? Can these representations be visualized or analyzed to gain insights into the reasoning process of LLMs? Furthermore, it would be interesting to investigate whether the quality of the problem representation correlates with the quality of the final solution, and whether poor representations can be identified and corrected. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the PRECOT framework.

## Questions

I have several questions that arise from my analysis of this paper. First, given the similarity between PRECOT and zero-shot CoT, particularly the 'Let's think step by step' approach, could the authors provide a more detailed explanation of the specific mechanisms by which the explicit problem representation in PRECOT leads to qualitatively different reasoning behaviors? I am interested in understanding how the explicit extraction of initial and goal states results in a more robust reasoning process compared to the implicit process encouraged by the 'Let's think step by step' instruction. Second, why did the authors choose to compare PRECOT only with CoT, and not with other reasoning methods such as decomposition and planning, especially given that these methods are mentioned in the related work section? I am curious about the rationale behind this choice and whether the authors have any specific reasons for not including these methods in their experimental comparison. Third, what is the computational cost associated with the problem representation construction stage in PRECOT, and how does this cost compare to other prompting techniques like CoT? I am interested in understanding the practical implications of the additional step introduced by PRECOT, particularly in terms of time, API calls, and resource consumption. Fourth, how does the quality of the extracted problem representation impact the overall reasoning performance of PRECOT? I am curious about whether poor problem representations can be identified and corrected, and whether there is a correlation between the quality of the problem representation and the quality of the final solution. Finally, what are the limitations of PRECOT, and in what types of problems does it struggle? I am interested in understanding the scenarios where the initial and goal states are difficult to define or where the problem requires a more flexible reasoning approach that doesn't fit into a strict initial-to-goal state framework. Addressing these questions would provide a more complete understanding of the PRECOT framework and its potential for future research.
