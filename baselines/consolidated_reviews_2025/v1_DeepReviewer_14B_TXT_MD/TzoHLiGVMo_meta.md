## Summary

This paper introduces ODEFormer, a novel transformer-based model designed for symbolic regression of dynamical systems. The core contribution lies in the application of a sequence-to-sequence transformer architecture to infer multidimensional ordinary differential equation (ODE) systems directly from observed solution trajectories. The authors generate a large synthetic dataset of ODEs, using this data to train ODEFormer. They also introduce a new benchmark dataset, odeBench, which is a curated collection of 63 ODEs ranging from one to four dimensions, intended to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of symbolic regression methods than existing benchmarks. The method is evaluated on both odeBench and the existing Strogatz dataset, with the results demonstrating that ODEFormer outperforms existing methods in terms of accuracy and robustness to noise and irregular sampling. The authors emphasize the model's ability to directly infer symbolic expressions for the ODEs, offering a more interpretable approach compared to black-box models. The paper highlights the potential of transformer models for symbolic regression tasks, particularly in the context of dynamical systems. The authors also acknowledge limitations of their approach, including its restriction to first-order ODEs, its struggle with chaotic systems, and its reliance on single trajectory inference. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a significant step forward in the application of deep learning to symbolic regression of dynamical systems, offering a promising avenue for future research in this area. The work is significant because it provides a new method for discovering the underlying mathematical equations of dynamical systems, which is a fundamental problem in many scientific disciplines. The use of transformers for this task is innovative, and the introduction of the odeBench dataset is a valuable contribution to the field. The paper's findings suggest that transformer models can be effective tools for symbolic regression, and the authors' work opens up new possibilities for the automated discovery of mathematical models from data.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the introduction of ODEFormer as a transformer-based approach for symbolic regression of dynamical systems is a novel contribution. The application of sequence-to-sequence transformers to this task is, as far as I can tell, original, and it demonstrates the potential of these models for discovering mathematical expressions from data. The authors have successfully adapted the transformer architecture to handle the specific challenges of symbolic regression, which is a non-trivial task. Second, the creation of the odeBench dataset is a valuable contribution to the field. The existing Strogatz dataset is limited in size and diversity, and odeBench provides a more comprehensive and challenging benchmark for evaluating symbolic regression methods. The authors have carefully curated this dataset, which includes ODEs of varying dimensions and complexities, making it a useful resource for the community. The fact that the dataset includes chaotic systems is also important, as these systems are known to be challenging for many modeling techniques. Third, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate that ODEFormer outperforms existing methods on both the Strogatz and odeBench datasets, showing that it is more accurate and robust to noise and irregular sampling. These results are supported by clear and well-presented figures and tables. The fact that ODEFormer can infer symbolic expressions directly from data, without requiring prior knowledge of the underlying equations, is a significant advantage. This interpretability is a key strength of the method, as it allows researchers to gain insights into the underlying dynamics of the system. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors clearly explain their methodology, and the figures and tables are well-designed. The paper is also well-organized, making it easy for the reader to understand the main contributions and findings. The authors have also provided a detailed discussion of the limitations of their approach, which is a sign of good scientific practice. Overall, I believe that the paper makes a significant contribution to the field of symbolic regression and that the proposed method has the potential to be widely adopted.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's evaluation is primarily based on two datasets: the Strogatz dataset and the newly introduced odeBench. While odeBench is a valuable contribution, the limited number of datasets raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed method. As noted by multiple reviewers, it remains unclear how ODEFormer would perform on other datasets or in real-world applications. The Strogatz dataset, while commonly used, is relatively small and may not fully capture the diversity of dynamical systems encountered in practice. The authors acknowledge this limitation in the discussion section, stating, 'Lastly, all existing methods for dynamical SR, including ours, perform inference based on a single observed trajectory.' This reliance on a limited set of benchmarks makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the method to different types of ODEs and data characteristics. Second, while the authors do discuss some limitations of their approach in the 'Discussion and Future Directions' section, I find that a more detailed analysis of these limitations is needed. For example, the authors acknowledge that ODEFormer struggles with chaotic systems, stating, 'Fourth, based on the four representatives in odeBench, ODEFormer (as well as all other benchmarked models) struggles with chaotic systems, which have been argued to provide good assessments for data-driven modeling and forecasting.' However, they do not provide a detailed analysis of why this is the case or what specific characteristics of chaotic systems make them challenging for the model. Similarly, the authors mention that their method is limited to first-order ODEs, but they do not explore the challenges of extending the method to higher-order ODEs or systems with more complex interactions. The lack of a deeper analysis of these limitations makes it difficult to fully understand the applicability of the method and its potential for future development. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of ODEFormer. While the authors report inference times in the experimental results, they do not offer a formal analysis of the computational cost of training and evaluating the model. This is an important consideration for practical applications, as the model may be computationally expensive to train and evaluate, especially for large datasets or complex ODEs. As noted by one reviewer, 'The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of ODEFormer. This is an important consideration for practical applications, as the model may be computationally expensive to train and evaluate.' The absence of a computational complexity analysis makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the method and its suitability for different computational environments. Fourth, the paper does not compare ODEFormer with some of the latest work in symbolic regression, such as $AI^{2}$ and DSR. As noted by one reviewer, 'The authors did not compare with some of the latest work in the SR, such as $AI^{2}$ and DSR. The absence of comparisons with these recent methods limits the assessment of the proposed model's relative performance and its position within the current state-of-the-art in symbolic regression.' The lack of comparison with these recent methods makes it difficult to assess the relative performance of ODEFormer and its position within the current state-of-the-art in symbolic regression. Finally, while the authors evaluate on systems up to 4D, the reviewer's concern about the complexity of the ODEs is partially valid. The paper doesn't provide a rigorous definition of 'simple' vs. 'complex' ODEs. The inclusion of chaotic systems in odeBench contradicts the idea that only 'very simple' ODEs are tested, although the model struggles with them. The authors should have provided a more detailed analysis of the complexity of the ODEs in the benchmark and how the model performs on different types of ODEs. These weaknesses, taken together, suggest that while ODEFormer is a promising approach, further work is needed to fully assess its capabilities and limitations.

## Suggestions

Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for future work. First, the authors should expand the evaluation of ODEFormer to include a wider range of datasets. This should include not only more synthetic datasets with varying characteristics but also real-world datasets from different scientific domains. For example, datasets from physics, biology, or chemistry could be used to assess the method's performance in various contexts. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the generalizability of the method and its applicability to different types of problems. Specifically, the authors could consider datasets with varying levels of noise and irregular sampling to better understand its robustness. Second, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the limitations of ODEFormer. This should include a more in-depth investigation of why the model struggles with chaotic systems and what specific characteristics of these systems make them challenging for the model. The authors should also explore the challenges of extending the method to higher-order ODEs and systems with more complex interactions. This could involve reformulating higher-order ODEs as systems of first-order equations or developing new techniques that can directly handle higher-order derivatives. A more thorough analysis of these limitations would provide a better understanding of the method's applicability and its potential for future development. Third, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of ODEFormer. This should include an analysis of how the computational cost scales with the size of the dataset, the dimensionality of the ODEs, and the complexity of the symbolic expressions. The authors should also discuss the memory requirements of the method and the hardware resources needed to run it efficiently. Furthermore, the authors should provide empirical measurements of training and inference time on different hardware configurations, and to compare these to other methods. This would provide a more practical understanding of the computational cost of using ODEFormer. Fourth, the authors should include a comparison of ODEFormer with other state-of-the-art methods for symbolic regression of dynamical systems, including $AI^{2}$ and DSR. This comparison should not only focus on the accuracy of the inferred ODEs but also on other relevant metrics, such as the computational cost and the robustness to noise. The authors should also provide a detailed analysis of the strengths and weaknesses of each method, highlighting the specific scenarios where ODEFormer performs better or worse than the other methods. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed method's capabilities and its position within the current landscape of SR techniques. Finally, the authors should provide a more rigorous definition of the complexity of the ODEs used in the benchmark and analyze how the model performs on different types of ODEs. This could involve categorizing the ODEs based on their linearity, stiffness, and dimensionality, and then analyzing the model's performance on each category. This would provide a more detailed understanding of the types of ODEs that the model can and cannot handle effectively. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly strengthen the paper and provide a more comprehensive assessment of the capabilities and limitations of ODEFormer.

## Questions

The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of this aspect, and it is unclear how the model's performance is affected by the amount of data available for training. Specifically, what is the minimum length of the trajectory needed for accurate inference, and how does the performance degrade as the trajectory length decreases? Second, how does the performance of ODEFormer vary with the dimensionality of the ODEs? While the paper evaluates on systems up to 4D, it is unclear how the model's performance would scale to higher-dimensional systems. What are the computational and memory requirements for higher-dimensional systems, and what are the limitations of the current approach in this context? Third, what are the specific types of ODEs that ODEFormer struggles with, and why? The paper mentions that the model struggles with chaotic systems, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the specific characteristics of these systems that make them challenging for the model. Are there other types of ODEs that the model struggles with, and what are the common features of these challenging systems? Fourth, how sensitive is ODEFormer to the choice of hyperparameters, and what are the optimal settings for different types of ODEs? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter choices, and it is unclear how the performance of the method varies with different settings of the learning rate, batch size, and the number of layers in the Transformer. What are the guidelines for choosing the optimal hyperparameter settings for different types of ODEs? Finally, how does the computational cost of ODEFormer compare to other methods for symbolic regression of dynamical systems? The paper reports inference times, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the method. How does the computational cost scale with the size of the dataset, the dimensionality of the ODEs, and the complexity of the symbolic expressions? These questions, if answered, would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the capabilities and limitations of ODEFormer and would help to guide future research in this area.
