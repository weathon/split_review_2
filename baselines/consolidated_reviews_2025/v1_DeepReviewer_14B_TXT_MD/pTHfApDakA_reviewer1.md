### Summary

This paper proposes a method, SELFCHECK, which uses LLMs to zero-shot check their own step-by-step reasoning. Specifically, instead of directly asking the LLM to check the correctness of a reasoning step, the method prompts the LLM to independently regenerate the step and then compare the generated step with the original one to determine its correctness. Experiments on several mathematical reasoning datasets demonstrate that this method can enhance the performance of LLMs.

1. The evaluation is insufficient. The experimental results indicate that SELFCHECK can improve the performance of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on mathematical reasoning datasets. However, the generalization ability of this method remains unclear. Specifically, the paper only evaluates on mathematical reasoning tasks, leaving it uncertain whether the method can be applied to other types of tasks that require step-by-step reasoning, such as logical deduction or planning. Furthermore, the method's performance on tasks with varying levels of complexity within the same domain is not explored. For instance, how would SELFCHECK perform on much simpler math problems, or problems requiring a significantly larger number of reasoning steps?
2. The method lacks novelty. The core idea of generating multiple solutions and selecting the most consistent answer is not new. This approach is similar to self-consistency methods already used in the field. The paper does not adequately address the similarities and differences between SELFCHECK and existing self-consistency approaches, nor does it provide a clear explanation of the specific scenarios where SELFCHECK would be more effective. The method's reliance on regenerating and comparing steps, while presented as a novel approach to error detection, is not fundamentally different from existing methods that use multiple generations to identify consistent outputs.

### Suggestions

The paper should provide a more thorough evaluation of the generalization capabilities of SELFCHECK. This should include experiments on a wider range of tasks that involve step-by-step reasoning, such as logical puzzles, planning problems, or tasks involving code generation. This would help to demonstrate the method's applicability beyond mathematical reasoning. Additionally, the evaluation should include tasks with varying levels of complexity to understand the method's performance across different problem difficulties. For example, the authors could include simpler math problems alongside more complex ones, or tasks requiring a varying number of reasoning steps. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the method's strengths and limitations.

The paper needs to more clearly articulate the novelty of the proposed method compared to existing self-consistency approaches. The authors should provide a detailed comparison of SELFCHECK with these methods, highlighting the specific differences in their approaches to error detection and correction. This should include a discussion of the scenarios where SELFCHECK is expected to outperform self-consistency methods, and vice versa. For example, the authors could explore whether SELFCHECK is more effective when the reasoning steps are highly structured, or when the errors are subtle and difficult to detect. A more rigorous analysis of the method's unique contributions is needed to justify its novelty.

Finally, the paper should explore the potential of combining SELFCHECK with self-consistency methods. If the core idea of SELFCHECK is to provide a more effective way to identify errors in individual reasoning steps, it would be interesting to see if this can be combined with the self-consistency approach of generating multiple solutions and selecting the most consistent one. This could potentially lead to even better performance. The authors should also consider exploring the use of majority voting across the solutions generated by SELFCHECK, as this could further improve the accuracy of the method. The paper should also provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of SELFCHECK compared to self-consistency methods, as this is an important factor to consider when evaluating the practical applicability of the method.
