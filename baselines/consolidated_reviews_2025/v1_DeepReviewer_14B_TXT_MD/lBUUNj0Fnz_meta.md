## Summary

This paper introduces an active learning framework for image segmentation, aiming to reduce the annotation burden by employing a binary query strategy. The core idea is to select the most informative image-class pairs and query the user with a simple 'yes' or 'no' question, indicating the presence or absence of a specific class within a given image. This approach contrasts with traditional active learning methods that often require more detailed annotations, such as pixel-level labels. The authors formulate the image and class selection process as a constrained optimization problem, which is then solved using a linear programming relaxation technique. The proposed method is evaluated on three challenging datasets: FlickrLandscapes, Cityscapes, and PASCAL VOC12. The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves competitive performance compared to several baselines, while significantly reducing the annotation effort. The authors also conduct a user study to estimate the time required for different annotation tasks, showing that binary queries are substantially faster than pixel-level or even region-level annotations. The paper claims to be the first to explore active learning for image segmentation using only binary queries, which is a key contribution. The method leverages uncertainty and redundancy metrics to select the most informative queries, aiming to maximize the information gained from each user interaction. The authors use an off-the-shelf LP solver to solve the optimization problem, and they evaluate the performance of the model using mean Intersection over Union (mIoU). Overall, the paper presents a novel and practical approach to active learning for image segmentation, with a focus on reducing annotation costs. However, the paper also has some limitations, which I will discuss in detail in the 'Weakness' section. The paper's focus on binary queries is a significant departure from traditional active learning methods, and it has the potential to make active learning more accessible and practical for real-world image segmentation tasks. The authors' approach to formulating the query selection as an optimization problem is also a notable contribution, and it provides a framework for further research in this area. The experimental results are promising, and they demonstrate the potential of the proposed method to achieve competitive performance with reduced annotation effort. However, the paper also has some limitations, which I will discuss in detail in the 'Weakness' section.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a novel approach to active learning for image segmentation, several weaknesses need to be addressed. First, the paper lacks a thorough comparison with existing active learning strategies that employ binary queries for image segmentation. Although the authors claim to be the first to use binary queries, the reviewer 1 points out that there are existing methods that use pointing and interactive refinement, which are more efficient than pixel-level annotation and are not discussed in the paper. This omission is significant because it fails to contextualize the proposed method within the broader landscape of active learning techniques. The paper's comparison is limited to basic baselines like random selection and entropy-based selection, but it does not include a detailed analysis of methods that, like the proposed approach, aim to minimize user annotation effort through binary feedback. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the true novelty and effectiveness of the proposed method. Second, the paper does not adequately address the potential for user error in the binary feedback. The method assumes that the user provides accurate 'yes' or 'no' answers, but in real-world scenarios, users may make mistakes, especially for complex images or classes. The paper does not discuss how such errors might propagate through the active learning process and affect the model's convergence and final performance. This is a critical limitation because the robustness of an active learning method to noisy feedback is essential for its practical applicability. The absence of any analysis or experimentation on the impact of noisy feedback raises concerns about the method's reliability in real-world settings. Third, the paper does not analyze the impact of the proposed method on different types of images and classes. The experiments report overall performance metrics, but there is no breakdown of performance based on image complexity or class difficulty. This is a significant limitation because the method's performance may vary depending on the characteristics of the images and classes. For example, the binary feedback mechanism might struggle with fine-grained segmentation tasks or images with small or thin objects. The lack of analysis on different subsets of the data makes it difficult to understand the method's limitations and potential failure modes. Fourth, the paper does not provide a clear analysis of the convergence rate of the proposed method.
