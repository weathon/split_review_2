## Summary

This paper introduces Concept-based Explainable Image Representation (CEIR), a novel approach aimed at enhancing the interpretability of self-supervised learning representations. The core idea is to project input images into a human-understandable concept space, thereby enabling attributions to semantically rich concepts while maintaining robustness for downstream tasks. The method begins by generating a concept pool using GPT-4, which is then filtered to include class-related concepts. Images are projected into this concept space using a linear layer trained to align with CLIP embeddings, leveraging CLIP's multi-modal capabilities to bridge natural language concepts and images. To address the high dimensionality and trivial variations in concept vectors, a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) is employed to distill these vectors into a more compact latent representation. This latent representation is then used for downstream tasks and for calculating concept importance scores using integrated gradients. The authors evaluate their method on unsupervised clustering and linear probe classification tasks, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on several benchmarks, including CIFAR10, CIFAR100, and STL10. The paper also explores the use of CEIR for open-world concept mining and label generation, showcasing its potential for automatic label generation and manipulation. The authors claim that CEIR achieves state-of-the-art unsupervised clustering performance and offers a new method for automatic label generation and manipulation. However, the paper also acknowledges that the transformation to a concept space may lead to some information loss, which is reflected in the lower linear probing performance compared to CLIP. Overall, the paper presents an interesting approach to explainable image representation learning, but it also has several limitations that need to be addressed.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. The core idea of combining concept bottleneck models with representation learning is both novel and interesting. The authors have successfully integrated the strengths of concept-based models and VAEs to learn explainable image representations, which is a significant contribution to the field. The use of GPT-4 for concept generation, while not without its limitations, is a creative approach to obtaining a diverse concept pool. The method's ability to project images into a concept space, allowing for attributions to human-understandable concepts, is a key strength. This approach enhances the interpretability of the learned representations, which is a crucial aspect of developing trustworthy AI systems. Furthermore, the paper demonstrates strong empirical results on unsupervised clustering tasks, achieving state-of-the-art performance on several benchmarks. This indicates that the learned representations are effective at capturing the underlying structure of the data. The authors also showcase the potential of CEIR for open-world concept mining and label generation, which is a promising direction for future research. The paper is generally well-written and easy to understand, which makes it accessible to a wider audience. The authors have clearly articulated their methodology and have provided sufficient details for others to reproduce their results. The use of integrated gradients for calculating concept importance scores is also a strength, as it provides a way to quantify the contribution of each concept to the final representation. The paper's exploration of the trade-off between interpretability and performance is also a positive aspect, as it highlights the challenges of developing methods that are both accurate and explainable. Finally, the authors have acknowledged some of the limitations of their method, which is a sign of intellectual honesty and a willingness to address these limitations in future work.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the method's reliance on a pre-defined concept set is a significant limitation. As the authors themselves acknowledge, the quality and completeness of this concept set are crucial for the performance of the method. The paper describes the process of generating the concept set using GPT-4 and filtering it, but it lacks a detailed methodology for ensuring that the selected concepts are both comprehensive and non-redundant. This reliance on a fixed set could lead to a bias towards the concepts included, potentially missing important nuances in the data. The paper states, "In the concept-based explanation model, we need to define the reference concept set for the model to inquire and get the related concept outputs." (Section 3). This dependence on a pre-defined set limits the method's ability to capture all relevant concepts in an image, and the lack of a clear methodology for ensuring the set's comprehensiveness and non-redundancy is a significant concern. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly evident from the paper's description of the method. Second, the method is likely sensitive to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the number of concepts and the threshold for concept activation. The paper mentions the number of concepts (M) in Section 3.2 and a threshold in the context of visualization in Section 4.3, but it lacks a thorough analysis of how these hyperparameters affect the performance and interpretability of the model. The paper does not include a dedicated section or analysis on hyperparameter sensitivity, and the experimental setup mentions using pre-trained models but doesn't detail the tuning process for the number of concepts or the activation threshold. Without a systematic exploration of the hyperparameter space, it is difficult to assess the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method. The optimal values for these parameters may also vary across different datasets, requiring careful tuning for each new application. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the absence of any hyperparameter sensitivity analysis in the paper. Third, the method may struggle to handle complex or abstract concepts that are difficult to represent with a vector. The paper relies on mapping images to a concept vector space where each dimension corresponds to a concept (Section 3.2). This inherently simplifies the representation of concepts. The examples in Figure 4 show concepts like "vehicle", "big cat", "rear hatchback", etc., which are relatively concrete. The paper doesn't explicitly evaluate the model's ability to handle abstract concepts. The method's reliance on a vector-based concept space and the lack of evaluation on abstract concepts raise concerns about its ability to capture the full complexity of human understanding. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly evident from the paper's methodology and the lack of evaluation on abstract concepts. This raises concerns about the trade-off between interpretability and performance. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the numerical results in Table 2. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly evident from the absence of Label-free CBM in the baseline comparisons. Finally, the paper lacks comparisons with other concept-based models on representation learning tasks. The "Experiments" section (Section 4) includes comparisons with clustering and representation learning models like SimCLR and MoCoV2 but doesn't include other concept-based models specifically designed for representation learning. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method compared to existing approaches.
