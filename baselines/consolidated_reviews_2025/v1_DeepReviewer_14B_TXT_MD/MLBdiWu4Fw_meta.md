## Summary

This paper introduces InternVid, a large-scale video-centric multimodal dataset designed to facilitate the learning of robust video-text representations for a variety of multimodal understanding and generation tasks. The dataset comprises over 7 million videos, totaling nearly 760,000 hours of content, which are segmented into 234 million clips, each accompanied by detailed textual descriptions, resulting in a corpus of 4.1 billion words. The authors employ a multi-scale captioning approach, leveraging both frame-by-frame captioning and video summarization techniques, to generate comprehensive and diverse textual descriptions of the video content. To learn video-text representations, the authors propose ViCLIP, a model based on the Vision Transformer (ViT)-Large architecture, which is trained using contrastive learning on InternVid. ViCLIP incorporates video masking to accelerate the learning process without compromising performance. The model demonstrates strong zero-shot action recognition capabilities and competitive video retrieval performance on standard benchmarks. The authors emphasize the broad applicability of both the dataset and the model in various fields, including autonomous driving, intelligent surveillance, and human-computer interaction. The core contribution of this work lies in the creation of a large-scale, high-quality video-text dataset and a corresponding model that can be used for a wide range of multimodal tasks. The dataset's scale and diversity, combined with the model's strong performance, make this a significant contribution to the field of multimodal learning and generation. The paper also highlights the potential of the dataset for video-centric dialogue systems, further showcasing its versatility. The authors also introduce a video-text representation learning model, ViCLIP, which is trained on InternVid via contrastive learning. The model demonstrates leading zero-shot action recognition and competitive video retrieval performance. The dataset and model have broad applications in various fields, including autonomous driving, intelligent surveillance, and human-computer interaction. The dataset can be used to learn video-text representations for multimodal understanding and generation, while the model can be used for tasks such as action recognition, video retrieval, and video-text matching.

## Strengths

The primary strength of this paper lies in the creation of InternVid, a large-scale video-centric multimodal dataset. The sheer scale of the dataset, encompassing over 7 million videos and 234 million clips with detailed textual descriptions, is a significant contribution to the field. This extensive dataset provides a valuable resource for training and evaluating video-text models, addressing a critical need for large, high-quality datasets in the multimodal domain. The authors' multi-scale captioning approach, which combines frame-by-frame captioning with video summarization, is another notable strength. This approach ensures that the textual descriptions are both comprehensive and diverse, capturing the nuances of the video content effectively. The proposed ViCLIP model, based on ViT-L, demonstrates strong zero-shot action recognition and competitive video retrieval performance, showcasing the effectiveness of the dataset and the model architecture. The use of video masking during training is a clever technique that accelerates the learning process without sacrificing performance. Furthermore, the paper highlights the broad applicability of both the dataset and the model, demonstrating their potential in various fields such as autonomous driving, intelligent surveillance, and human-computer interaction. The dataset's versatility is further emphasized by its potential use in video-centric dialogue systems. The authors also provide a detailed description of the data collection and annotation process, including the use of YouTube as a source, the filtering criteria for video duration and resolution, and the methods used for generating captions. This transparency is crucial for the reproducibility and reliability of the dataset. The inclusion of detailed hyperparameters for video retrieval fine-tuning in Table 7 is also a positive aspect, enhancing the reproducibility of the results. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the contributions and findings accessible to a broad audience. The combination of a large-scale dataset, a strong model, and a clear presentation makes this a valuable contribution to the field of multimodal learning and generation.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a significant contribution with the introduction of the InternVid dataset and the ViCLIP model, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. A primary concern, validated across multiple reviews, is the limitation imposed by the dataset's reliance on YouTube as its sole source. The authors acknowledge that the videos in the dataset are relatively short, with an average length of 6.4 minutes and a collection range of 10 to 30 minutes. This limitation, stemming from the nature of YouTube content, restricts the dataset's applicability to tasks that require longer videos, such as video summarization or long-term video understanding. As the reviewers correctly point out, the short average length of the videos may also limit the ability of models trained on this dataset to generalize to longer videos. This is a significant limitation, as many real-world video applications involve longer, more complex content. The paper itself acknowledges this limitation in the conclusion, stating, "Although YouTube is an extensive source encompassing a wide range of video categories, certain specific types of footage may be excluded or scarcely collected, including: public area surveillance, sports competitions, movies, documentaries, etc. The exclusion of such categories is often due to copyright restrictions or other limits imposed by the platform." This reliance on YouTube also introduces a potential bias towards content that is popular or trending on the platform, which may not be representative of the broader video landscape. This bias could impact the generalizability of models trained on this dataset to other video domains. The authors do not provide a detailed analysis of these potential biases, which is a significant oversight. The paper also lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the dataset and model. While the conclusion briefly mentions the limitations related to data diversity and representativeness, a more in-depth analysis of potential biases, weaknesses, and areas for improvement is needed. For instance, the paper does not discuss the geographical distribution of the videos, the types of activities represented, or the language used in the captions, all of which could introduce biases that affect the performance of models trained on this data. The absence of a detailed discussion of these limitations makes it difficult to assess the true scope and applicability of the dataset and model. Furthermore, while the paper provides details on the data collection and annotation process, it could benefit from even more granular details. For example, while the paper mentions the use of Tag2Text and BLIP2 for caption generation, it does not provide specific details about the configurations or parameters used with these models. Similarly, while the paper describes the filtering criteria for video duration and resolution, it does not provide specific details about the distribution of video lengths and resolutions in the final dataset. This lack of fine-grained details makes it challenging to fully understand the dataset's characteristics and to replicate the data collection process. Finally, while the paper provides some details on the training and evaluation of the ViCLIP model, it could be more comprehensive. For example, while Table 7 provides detailed hyperparameters for video retrieval fine-tuning, the paper does not provide a comprehensive list of hyperparameters used for all training stages. This lack of detail makes it difficult to fully understand the model's training process and to reproduce the results. The paper also does not provide a detailed analysis of the model's performance on different subsets of the data, such as different action categories or video lengths. This analysis would be helpful in identifying the strengths and weaknesses of the model and in guiding future research. In summary, the limitations related to the dataset's source, the lack of a thorough discussion of limitations, the absence of fine-grained details about the data collection and annotation process, and the lack of comprehensive training details are significant weaknesses that need to be addressed.

This would help to mitigate the limitations imposed by the current dataset's reliance on short YouTube videos and improve the generalizability of models trained on it. Furthermore, the authors should explore methods for artificially extending shorter videos, such as concatenating multiple clips or using video repetition techniques, to create a more balanced dataset with respect to video duration. This would help to address the limitations of the current dataset and improve the generalizability of models trained on it. The authors should also investigate the impact of video length on the performance of their proposed model and provide a more detailed analysis of how the model performs on videos of varying lengths. To address the potential biases introduced by relying solely on YouTube, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of the dataset's characteristics, including the geographical distribution of the videos, the types of activities represented, and the language used in the captions. This analysis should include quantitative measures where possible and should be used to identify and mitigate potential biases. The authors should also consider the ethical implications of these biases, especially when the dataset is used in sensitive applications like surveillance or autonomous driving. The authors should also discuss how these limitations might impact downstream tasks and what steps could be taken to mitigate them. This should include specific information about the configurations and parameters used with the captioning models (Tag2Text and BLIP2). The authors should also provide more details about the distribution of video lengths and resolutions in the final dataset. Finally, the paper should include a more comprehensive description of the training and evaluation of the ViCLIP model. This should include a detailed list of the hyperparameters used during training, such as the learning rate, batch size, and optimizer settings, for all training stages. The specific training data used, including any data augmentation or preprocessing techniques, should also be clearly defined. The evaluation metrics used to assess the performance of the model should be described in detail, including the specific formulas and any variations used. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the model's performance on different subsets of the data, such as different action categories or video lengths. This would help to identify the strengths and weaknesses of the model and to guide future research. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly enhance the value and impact of their work.

Several questions arise from my analysis of this paper, focusing on key uncertainties and methodological choices. First, I am curious about the specific criteria used for selecting videos from YouTube beyond the general categories mentioned (e.g., news, gaming). What specific search queries or filtering techniques were employed to ensure a diverse and representative sample of video content? How did the authors address the potential for bias in the YouTube algorithm's search results? Second, I would like to understand more about the multi-scale captioning approach. What specific configurations and parameters were used with the Tag2Text and BLIP2 models? How were the frame-by-frame captions and video summaries combined to generate the final textual descriptions?
