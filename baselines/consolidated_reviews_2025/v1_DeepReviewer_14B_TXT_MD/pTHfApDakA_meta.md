## Summary

This paper introduces SELFCHECK, a novel zero-shot verification schema designed to enhance the reliability of large language models (LLMs) in step-by-step reasoning tasks. The core idea behind SELFCHECK is to decompose the verification of a reasoning step into several stages: target extraction, information collection, step regeneration, and result comparison. Instead of directly asking the LLM to evaluate the correctness of a reasoning step, SELFCHECK prompts the LLM to independently regenerate the step and then compares the generated step with the original one to determine its correctness. This approach leverages the generative capabilities of LLMs to create a more robust verification mechanism. The method is evaluated on three mathematical reasoning datasets: GSM8K, MathQA, and MATH, demonstrating that SELFCHECK can improve the accuracy of final answers compared to baseline methods. The authors also explore the use of weighted voting to combine multiple generated solutions, further enhancing performance. The paper's primary contribution lies in its innovative approach to self-verification, which does not require additional training data or external resources. By breaking down the verification process into distinct stages and using regeneration as a comparison point, SELFCHECK offers a promising avenue for improving the trustworthiness of LLMs in complex reasoning tasks. The experimental results show that the method is effective in identifying errors and improving the overall accuracy of LLMs on mathematical problems. The authors also provide an ablation study to demonstrate the importance of each stage in the SELFCHECK process. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and clearly explained method that addresses a critical challenge in the field of LLMs, which is the need for reliable and accurate reasoning. The findings suggest that SELFCHECK has the potential to be a valuable tool for improving the performance of LLMs in various applications that require complex reasoning.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of the proposed method, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. A primary concern is the limited scope of the experimental evaluation. The paper focuses exclusively on mathematical reasoning datasets (GSM8K, MathQA, and MATH), and it is unclear whether SELFCHECK can be effectively applied to other domains that require step-by-step reasoning. As the authors themselves acknowledge, their focus on math problems is due to the ease of performance evaluation and dataset availability. However, this leaves a significant gap in our understanding of the method's generalizability. Specifically, it is uncertain how well SELFCHECK would perform on tasks involving commonsense reasoning, logical deduction, or code generation, where the nature of reasoning and the types of errors may differ significantly from mathematical problems. The method's reliance on step-by-step reasoning, while effective in math, may not translate well to domains where reasoning is less structured or involves different types of logical inferences. For example, commonsense reasoning often requires integrating diverse pieces of knowledge and making assumptions, which may not be easily captured by the proposed method's stages. Similarly, code generation involves syntax and semantic constraints that are not directly analogous to mathematical problem-solving. This lack of evaluation on diverse tasks is a significant limitation that needs to be addressed in future work. ## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should conduct a more thorough evaluation of SELFCHECK's generalizability across different domains. This should include experiments on tasks that involve commonsense reasoning, logical deduction, and code generation, in addition to mathematical reasoning. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's strengths and limitations and help to identify the types of tasks where SELFCHECK is most effective. For example, in commonsense reasoning, the 'target extraction' stage might need to identify implicit assumptions or background knowledge, while the 'information collection' stage would need to gather relevant contextual information. In code generation, the 'step regeneration' stage would need to ensure that the generated code is syntactically correct and semantically consistent with the problem description. Second, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of SELFCHECK. This should include a breakdown of the time taken for each stage, the number of tokens processed, and the overall resource consumption. A comparison with other verification methods, such as self-consistency, should be provided to contextualize the computational overhead. This analysis is crucial for assessing the practical viability of the method. Furthermore, the authors should explore techniques to reduce the computational overhead of their method, such as caching intermediate results or using more efficient LLMs for certain stages. Third, the authors should provide a more granular analysis of the performance of each stage of the SELFCHECK process. This should include accuracy metrics for each stage, such as the goal extraction, information identification, alternative answer generation, and comparison stages. This would help identify bottlenecks and areas for improvement. For example, if the goal extraction stage is frequently inaccurate, it would be a key area to focus on. Fourth, the authors should more clearly articulate the novelty of the proposed method compared to existing self-consistency approaches. This should include a detailed comparison of SELFCHECK with these methods, highlighting the specific differences in their approaches to error detection and correction. This should include a discussion of the scenarios where SELFCHECK is expected to outperform self-consistency methods, and vice versa. For example, the authors could explore whether SELFCHECK is more effective when the reasoning steps are highly structured, or when the errors are subtle and difficult to detect. A more rigorous analysis of the method's unique contributions is needed to justify its novelty. Fifth, the authors should provide a clear description of how the final answer is extracted from the multi-step reasoning of the LLMs. This should include examples to illustrate how the final answer is derived from the generated reasoning steps. This is essential for reproducibility and for understanding the method's overall effectiveness. Finally, the authors should explore the potential of combining SELFCHECK with self-consistency methods. If the core idea of SELFCHECK is to provide a more effective way to identify errors in individual reasoning steps, it would be interesting to see if this can be combined with the self-consistency approach of generating multiple solutions and selecting the most consistent one. This could potentially lead to even better performance. The authors should also consider exploring the use of majority voting across the solutions generated by SELFCHECK, as this could further improve the accuracy of the method. By addressing these points, the paper can be significantly strengthened and its contributions more clearly articulated.
