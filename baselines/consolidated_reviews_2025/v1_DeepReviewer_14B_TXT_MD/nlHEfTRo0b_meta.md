## Summary

This paper introduces Physics-Informed Dynamics representatiOn learner (PIDO), a novel framework for solving parametric partial differential equations (PDEs). The core idea behind PIDO is to project PDE solutions into a low-dimensional latent space using an auto-decoder, and then learn the dynamics of these latent embeddings using a neural ordinary differential equation (Neural ODE). This approach aims to achieve robust generalization across various aspects of PDE configurations, including initial conditions, PDE coefficients, and training time horizons. The authors propose two regularization techniques to enhance temporal extrapolation and training stability. The framework leverages a physics-informed loss function, which does not require exact solutions for training, and employs an implicit neural representation (INR) parameterized by Fourier features to represent the decoder. The empirical evaluation of PIDO is conducted on two benchmark problems: 1D combined equations and 2D Navier-Stokes equations. The results demonstrate that PIDO outperforms existing data-driven approaches in terms of generalization performance. Furthermore, the authors investigate the transferability of the learned representations to downstream tasks, such as long-term integration and inverse problems, showing that the pre-trained model can be fine-tuned to achieve good performance. The paper's main contribution lies in the specific combination of existing techniques, including latent space representation, Neural ODEs for latent dynamics, and physics-informed learning, along with the introduction of novel regularization techniques. While the individual components are not entirely novel, the authors argue that their specific integration and the proposed regularization techniques lead to improved performance and generalization capabilities. The paper provides a detailed description of the model architecture, training procedure, and experimental setup. However, it lacks a theoretical analysis of the proposed method, and the experimental evaluation is limited to a few benchmark problems and baselines. Despite these limitations, the paper presents a promising approach for solving parametric PDEs with improved generalization capabilities.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, while the authors present their framework as novel, I found that the individual components of their approach have been explored in prior work. Specifically, the idea of using a latent space to represent PDE solutions, as seen in [1,2], is not a new concept. Similarly, modeling latent dynamics with Neural ODEs, as in [3], has been previously investigated. The proposed regularization techniques, while helpful, are also not fundamentally novel and have been used in other physics-informed neural networks. While the authors combine these existing techniques in a novel way, the lack of a clear articulation of the specific novelty of their approach is a weakness. The paper does not adequately demonstrate the unique advantages of this particular combination over existing methods. Second, the experimental evaluation is not as comprehensive as it could be. The authors only compare their method with a few baselines, and the improvements are not always significant. For example, in Table 2, the proposed method only outperforms the baselines by a small margin. The authors should compare their method with more state-of-the-art approaches, such as [4], to demonstrate the superiority of their method. Furthermore, the experimental validation is limited to two benchmark problems: 1D combined equations and 2D Navier-Stokes equations. It is not clear how the framework performs on other PDEs, and the lack of evaluation on a wider range of problems limits the generalizability of the findings. The authors also do not include comparisons with other DeepONet-based methods for long-term integration, which is a significant omission. The long-term integration task is also not very challenging, and it is not clear how the framework performs on more challenging long-term integration tasks such as the Lorenz system or the Kuramoto-Sivashinsky equation. Third, the paper lacks any theoretical analysis of the proposed method. It is unclear why the proposed method can generalize well to unseen initial conditions, PDE coefficients, and training time horizons. The authors should provide some theoretical insights into the proposed method, such as convergence analysis or generalization bounds. This lack of theoretical grounding makes it difficult to assess the reliability and robustness of the proposed method. Fourth, the paper compares PIDO with PINN in the downstream task of inverse problems, but it does not compare with other PINN-based methods for inverse problems, such as [5,6,7,8]. This omission limits the evaluation of PIDO's performance in this task. The paper also compares PIDO with PI-DeepONet in the downstream task of long-term integration, but it does not compare with other DeepONet-based methods for long-term integration. Furthermore, the training initial conditions for the long-term integration task are sampled from the test data, meaning they are not strictly "outside" the training set for that specific downstream task setup. This raises concerns about the generalization capabilities of the method to completely unseen initial conditions. Finally, the paper does not provide any details about the tuning of the PINN baseline in the inverse problem task, and there is no comparison with well-tuned PINN baselines. This makes it difficult to assess the true performance of PIDO in this task. These weaknesses, taken together, limit the overall impact and generalizability of the paper's findings. I have high confidence in these identified limitations, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the lack of certain comparisons and analyses.
