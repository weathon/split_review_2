## Summary

This paper tackles the challenging problem of quantifying multimodal interactions in a semi-supervised setting, where labeled unimodal data and unlabeled multimodal data are available. The authors propose a novel approach based on information-theoretic measures, deriving lower and upper bounds on the amount of synergistic interactions between modalities. The core idea is to leverage the shared information between modalities and the disagreement between separately trained unimodal classifiers to estimate these bounds. Specifically, the lower bounds are derived using the shared information between modalities and the disagreement between unimodal classifiers, while the upper bound is obtained through connections to approximate algorithms for min-entropy couplings. The theoretical framework is grounded in the concept of partial information decomposition, which allows for the quantification of redundancy, uniqueness, and synergy in multimodal data. The authors validate their proposed bounds through experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets, demonstrating that the bounds accurately track true interactions. Furthermore, they explore the practical implications of their theoretical results, showing how these bounds can be used to estimate multimodal model performance, guide data collection strategies, and select appropriate multimodal models for various tasks. The paper's main contribution lies in providing a theoretical framework for understanding and quantifying multimodal interactions in a semi-supervised setting, which is a significant step towards more efficient and effective multimodal learning. The authors demonstrate the potential of their approach to inform critical decisions in multimodal machine learning, such as whether to collect additional multimodal data and which fusion models to use. The paper's approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where labeled multimodal data is scarce or expensive to obtain, making the semi-supervised setting a practical and important area of focus. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field by offering a principled way to analyze and leverage multimodal data in the absence of extensive labeled examples. The paper's findings suggest that the proposed bounds can serve as a useful tool for practitioners working with multimodal data, enabling them to make more informed decisions about data collection and model selection. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and theoretically sound approach to quantifying multimodal interactions, with promising implications for the field of multimodal learning.
