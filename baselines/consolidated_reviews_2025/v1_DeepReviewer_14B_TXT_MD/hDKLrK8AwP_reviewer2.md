### Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to enhancing the readability of Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) code generated by AI models. While existing models excel at creating visually accurate SVGs, they often produce code that is difficult for humans to read and edit. The authors propose a set of metrics to evaluate SVG readability, focusing on logical structuring, minimized complexity, and appropriate element use. They also introduce differentiable objectives that guide SVG generation models to produce more readable code without sacrificing visual accuracy. The paper demonstrates that their approach significantly improves code readability, making SVGs more accessible for manual adjustments and further development.

### Strengths

1. The paper addresses an important yet overlooked aspect of SVG generation—code readability—which is crucial for designers and developers who need to edit or optimize generated graphics.
2. The authors provide a clear set of desiderata for readable SVG code and develop specific metrics (SPI, ESS, RQ) to evaluate these aspects, contributing a valuable framework for future research.
3. The proposed differentiable objectives are designed to balance visual accuracy with code readability, and the results show that the model can achieve this balance effectively.
4. The paper includes comprehensive experiments and ablation studies, demonstrating the effectiveness of each proposed metric and loss function in improving SVG readability.

1. The paper's focus is primarily on synthetic shapes, and it's unclear how well the proposed methods generalize to more complex, real-world SVGs. 2. The evaluation of readability relies heavily on GPT-3.5, which, while effective, might not fully capture the nuances of human readability and editability. Human evaluation studies could provide more direct insights into the practical benefits of the proposed metrics. The reliance on an LLM for evaluating readability introduces a potential bias, as the model's understanding of readability might not align perfectly with that of human designers or developers who would be directly interacting with the generated SVG code. A more comprehensive evaluation should include human subjects to assess the practical impact of the proposed metrics on code comprehension and editing efficiency.

### Suggestions

To address the limitations of the current evaluation, future work should focus on expanding the dataset to include a wider variety of real-world SVGs. This should include SVGs with complex paths, gradients, transformations, and various levels of nesting. The evaluation should not only assess the visual fidelity of the generated SVGs but also the structural and semantic correctness of the code. This could involve metrics that measure the complexity of the generated code, such as the number of nested elements, the use of unnecessary groups, and the presence of redundant attributes. Furthermore, the evaluation should include a comparison with existing SVG optimization techniques to demonstrate the advantages of the proposed approach in terms of both readability and visual quality. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the method's practical applicability and its potential for real-world use cases.

To improve the evaluation of readability, it is crucial to incorporate human evaluation studies. These studies should involve designers and developers with varying levels of expertise in SVG coding. The participants should be asked to perform specific tasks, such as identifying and modifying specific elements within the generated SVG code. The evaluation should measure the time taken to complete these tasks, as well as the accuracy and ease of understanding reported by the participants. This would provide a more direct and reliable assessment of the practical benefits of the proposed metrics on code comprehension and editing efficiency. The study should also explore the impact of different readability metrics on the human evaluation results, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of the relationship between automated metrics and human perception of readability.

Finally, the paper should explore the potential of using more advanced LLMs, such as GPT-4, for evaluating readability. While GPT-3.5 is a capable model, newer models may offer improved performance in understanding and evaluating the nuances of code readability. The evaluation should compare the results obtained with different LLMs to determine the most effective model for this task. Additionally, the paper should investigate the potential of fine-tuning LLMs on a dataset of human-annotated SVG code to improve their ability to assess readability. This could involve training the model to predict human ratings of readability, which could lead to more accurate and reliable evaluations.

### Questions

1. How well do the proposed readability metrics and loss functions generalize to more complex, real-world SVGs beyond synthetic shapes?
2. Could the approach be adapted or extended to other vector graphics formats or programming languages where readability is crucial?
3. Are there any potential biases or limitations in using GPT-3.5 for evaluating SVG code readability, and how might these affect the results?
4. How do the authors plan to address the computational overhead introduced by the readability-focused loss functions, especially for real-time or large-scale applications?
