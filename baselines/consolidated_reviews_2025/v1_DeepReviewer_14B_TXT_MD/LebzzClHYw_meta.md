## Summary

This paper introduces Instructive Decoding (ID), a novel method designed to enhance the instruction-following capabilities of language models. The core idea behind ID is to leverage the anchoring effect, a cognitive bias where initial information disproportionately influences subsequent judgments. ID achieves this by contrasting the logits of the original instruction with those generated from a manipulated, noisy version of the instruction. This contrastive approach aims to guide the model towards generating responses that are more aligned with the intended instruction. The authors propose several methods for creating noisy instructions, including truncating and shuffling the original instruction, providing only input-output pairs, inserting random words, and using an 'opposite' instruction that prompts the model to generate responses that contradict the original intent. Through experiments on various instruction-tuned models and tasks, the authors demonstrate that ID consistently outperforms baseline models, particularly when employing the 'opposite' noisy instruction. The paper also provides a comprehensive analysis of the behavior of ID, showcasing its effectiveness in improving label adherence, coherence, and mitigating imbalances in the decoding process. The empirical findings suggest that ID offers a simple yet effective way to improve the performance of instruction-tuned language models without requiring additional training or fine-tuning. The method's reliance on contrasting logits from original and noisy instructions, inspired by contrastive decoding, presents a unique approach to refining model outputs. The authors' exploration of different noisy instruction variants further contributes to the understanding of how to effectively leverage the anchoring effect in language models. Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for the use of ID as a practical method for enhancing instruction-following in language models, with significant implications for various natural language processing tasks.

## Weaknesses

While I appreciate the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the novelty of the proposed method is somewhat limited. While the authors position ID as a novel approach, the core idea of using noisy instructions as negative examples for contrastive decoding is not entirely new. As Reviewer 1 pointed out, the 'opposite' instruction is similar to the negative prompts used in contrastive decoding, essentially acting as a filter to remove tokens that diverge from the intended instruction. The paper acknowledges the existence of Contrastive Decoding (CD) but does not fully address the similarities and differences between ID and existing methods that use negative prompts. The paper states, "Contrastive Decoding (CD) is a straightforward technique that improves the performance of LMs by comparing two sets of predictions" and "Despite its simplicity, the need for two models limits its broad applicability, and its utility in instruction-following scenarios remains largely unexplored." While this is true, the core mechanism of contrasting logits from original and noisy instructions is similar to the concept of negative prompts. This similarity diminishes the novelty of the approach, and the paper could benefit from a more thorough discussion of how ID differs from existing methods. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper's description of ID and its comparison to CD, along with the description of the 'opposite' instruction, clearly demonstrate the similarity to negative prompting techniques. Second, the paper lacks a detailed explanation of the mechanisms by which noisy instructions lead to improved instruction following. While the authors provide a motivation based on the anchoring effect, they do not fully explain why specific noisy instructions work and how they help the model better understand the original instructions. As Reviewer 1 noted, the paper describes *what* the noisy instructions do but could elaborate more on the underlying mechanisms. For example, the paper does not explain how the truncated-shuffled instruction forces the model to rely more on the initial parts of the instruction, or how the 'opposite' instruction helps the model better understand the nuances of the original instruction. This lack of mechanistic understanding limits the paper's contribution and makes it difficult to generalize the method to other contexts. My confidence in this assessment is medium, as the paper does provide a motivation based on the anchoring effect, but the lack of deeper explanation into the mechanisms is evident. Third, the paper does not adequately explore the sensitivity of ID to the choice of noisy instructions. While the authors evaluate ID with four different noisy instruction variants, they do not systematically investigate why some noisy instructions lead to better performance than others. As Reviewer 2 and 3 pointed out, the paper does not explore the impact of different types of noisy instructions, such as paraphrasing the original instruction, introducing typographical errors, or adding irrelevant information. This lack of systematic investigation limits the paper's ability to provide practical guidance on how to choose the most effective noisy instructions. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the experimental results show performance variations across different noisy instruction variants, and the paper does not delve deeply into *why* some work better than others. Fourth, the paper does not analyze the computational overhead introduced by the Instructive Decoding process. As Reviewer 2 and 3 noted, the paper does not compare the computational cost of ID with standard decoding methods. The additional forward pass required for the noisy instruction might introduce a significant overhead, especially for large models. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practicality of the method for real-world applications. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the paper does not include any discussion or analysis of the computational cost of ID. Finally, the paper lacks a thorough comparison with existing methods for improving the performance of instruction-tuned language models. As Reviewer 2 and 3 pointed out, the paper primarily compares ID against a standard baseline and does not benchmark against more advanced techniques, such as those that employ adversarial training or reinforcement learning. The paper also does not evaluate the robustness of ID to adversarial attacks or noisy inputs. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to establish the relative advantages and disadvantages of ID compared to other state-of-the-art techniques. My confidence in this assessment is high, as the experimental setup focuses on baseline comparisons and lacks discussion on adversarial robustness.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the mechanisms by which noisy instructions lead to improved instruction following. This could involve exploring how each type of noisy instruction (e.g., truncated-shuffled, opposite) affects the model's attention or internal representations. For instance, the authors could investigate whether the truncated-shuffled instruction forces the model to rely more on the initial parts of the instruction, thereby improving its ability to capture the core intent. Similarly, they could explore how the 'opposite' instruction helps the model better understand the nuances of the original instruction. A deeper dive into the model's internal behavior would strengthen the paper's claims and provide a more solid foundation for the proposed method. Second, the authors should conduct a more systematic investigation into the sensitivity of ID to the choice of noisy instructions. This could involve exploring various strategies for generating noisy instructions, such as paraphrasing the original instruction, introducing typographical errors, or adding irrelevant information. By systematically evaluating the performance of ID with these different types of noisy instructions, the authors could gain a deeper understanding of the method's sensitivity to the characteristics of the noisy input. This analysis could also reveal potential failure modes of ID and suggest ways to mitigate them. For instance, if certain types of noisy instructions consistently lead to worse performance, the authors could develop strategies to detect and avoid using such instructions during the decoding process. Furthermore, exploring the relationship between the degree of noise in the instruction and the resulting performance could provide valuable insights into the optimal level of noise for maximizing the effectiveness of ID. Third, the authors should include a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the Instructive Decoding process. This analysis should compare the time and memory requirements of ID with standard decoding methods, considering the impact of different factors, such as the size of the language model, the length of the input sequence, and the number of noisy instructions used during decoding. Furthermore, the authors could explore techniques for optimizing the implementation of ID to reduce its computational costs. For example, they could investigate the use of efficient algorithms for generating noisy instructions or explore methods for parallelizing the decoding process. Additionally, the authors could consider the trade-offs between the computational costs of ID and the resulting performance gains. Fourth, the authors should conduct a more thorough comparison with existing methods for improving the performance of instruction-tuned language models. This comparison should involve evaluating the performance of ID and these alternative methods on a common set of benchmarks and analyzing the strengths and weaknesses of each approach. For example, the authors could compare the robustness of ID to adversarial attacks or noisy inputs with the robustness of adversarial training methods. Additionally, the authors should compare the computational costs of ID with the computational costs of these alternative methods. This would provide a more complete picture of the relative advantages and disadvantages of ID and help to establish its position in the landscape of existing techniques. Finally, the authors should explore the possibility of combining ID with other methods to achieve even better performance. For instance, they could investigate the use of ID as a pre-processing step before applying adversarial training or reinforcement learning. By providing a comprehensive comparison with existing methods and exploring the potential for combining ID with other techniques, the authors can further demonstrate the value and versatility of their proposed method.
