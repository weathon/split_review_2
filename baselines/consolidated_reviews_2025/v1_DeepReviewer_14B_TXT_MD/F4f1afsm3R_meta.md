## Summary

This paper introduces SC-MCTS*, a novel Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) reasoning algorithm designed to enhance the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) in multi-step reasoning tasks. The authors address the limitations of previous MCTS-based methods, particularly their slow speed compared to Chain of Thought (CoT) prompting and the lack of in-depth studies on reward models. The core contribution of SC-MCTS* lies in three key areas: a highly interpretable reward model based on contrastive decoding, a significant speed improvement achieved through speculative decoding, and enhancements to the UCT node selection strategy and backpropagation process. The proposed reward model leverages the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the expert and amateur model distributions, log-likelihood, and self-evaluation to guide the search process. The authors demonstrate a 51.9% speed improvement per node using speculative decoding, which is crucial for practical applications of MCTS in LLMs. Furthermore, they refine the UCT strategy by optimizing the exploration parameter and improve backpropagation to enhance the overall search efficiency. The empirical evaluation is conducted on the Blocksworld dataset, a classic benchmark for multi-step reasoning, using Llama-3.1-70B as the expert model. The results show that SC-MCTS* outperforms the o1-mini model by an average of 17.4% on this dataset. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of each component of SC-MCTS* on the overall performance. The authors claim that their method is more interpretable, efficient, and generalizable than previous MCTS-based approaches. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in MCTS-based reasoning for LLMs, offering a practical and efficient approach to improve their performance on complex tasks. However, the paper's claims of generalizability and interpretability require further scrutiny, and the experimental evaluation could be strengthened by including a broader range of datasets and baselines.

Unlike previous methods that rely on external tools or datasets, this reward model is intrinsic to the LLM itself, making it more versatile and easier to implement. The authors' emphasis on interpretability is a welcome addition to the field, where black-box approaches often dominate. Furthermore, the authors' efforts to improve the speed of MCTS through speculative decoding are commendable. Achieving a 51.9% speed improvement per node is a significant practical advancement, making MCTS a more viable option for real-world applications. This speedup, combined with the improvements to the UCT node selection strategy and backpropagation, demonstrates a thorough understanding of the MCTS algorithm and its limitations. The empirical results, while limited to the Blocksworld dataset, are nonetheless impressive. The authors demonstrate that SC-MCTS* outperforms the o1-mini model by a substantial margin, showcasing the effectiveness of their proposed method. The ablation studies provide valuable insights into the contribution of each component of SC-MCTS*, further strengthening the paper's findings. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a wide audience. The authors clearly articulate their motivation, methodology, and results, which is essential for effective communication of scientific research. The combination of a novel reward model, significant speed improvements, and empirical validation makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field of LLM reasoning.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's experimental evaluation is limited to the Blocksworld dataset, which raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed SC-MCTS* method. As explicitly stated in the 'Experiments' section, 'The experiment was conducted on Blocksworld dataset across all steps and difficulties.' While Blocksworld is a useful benchmark for multi-step reasoning, it is a relatively constrained environment and does not fully capture the complexities of real-world scenarios or more diverse reasoning challenges. The lack of evaluation on datasets with varying reasoning structures, such as those involving temporal reasoning, common-sense reasoning, or mathematical problem-solving, makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the proposed algorithm. This limitation undermines the authors' claim that their method is more generalizable than previous approaches. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of SC-MCTS*. While the authors mention a speed improvement per node using speculative decoding, they do not provide a comprehensive analysis of the overall computational overhead, including memory usage and the impact of the reward model and speculative decoding on the total runtime. The 'Reasoning Speed' experiment only compares SC-MCTS* with vanilla MCTS, and there is no direct comparison of the computational cost of SC-MCTS* with other methods like CoT or RAP-MCTS in terms of absolute metrics. This makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method, especially when considering the computational demands of large language models. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the impact of different hyperparameters on the performance of SC-MCTS*. While the 'UCT Parameter Optimization' experiment explores the impact of the constant C, this is the only hyperparameter explicitly analyzed in detail. There is no systematic analysis of other MCTS hyperparameters like the number of simulations, exploration parameters (beyond the constant C), or parameters related to the reward models. This lack of sensitivity analysis makes it difficult to understand how these hyperparameters affect the performance of the method and provides limited guidance for selecting appropriate values. Fourth, the paper's claim of improving the UCT node selection strategy is not fully substantiated. The 'Node Selection Strategy' section describes the standard UCT formula, and the 'UCT Parameter Optimization' experiment focuses on finding the optimal value for the constant C. The authors state, 'However, \( C \) is a crucial part of UCT. Previous work~\citep{hao2023reasoninglanguagemodelplanning,zhang2024accessinggpt4levelmathematical} had limited thoroughly investigating its components, leading to potential failures of the UCT strategy.' This suggests the improvement is in the analysis and optimization of the existing UCT rather than a fundamental change to the algorithm itself. The claim of 'improving' could be interpreted as simply finding a better parameter value, which is a standard practice for applying UCT. Fifth, the paper's claim of improved interpretability is not strongly supported by the evidence provided. The 'Interpretability Study' section analyzes the distribution of reward functions, noting 'two distinct types of reward value distributions: normal distribution and half-normal distribution.' The authors argue that these distributions provide insights into the reasoning process. However, the link between these properties and *interpretability* of the reasoning process is not strongly established. The connection is more correlational (reward distribution shape and performance) than causal in terms of understanding the model's reasoning steps. Finally, the paper lacks a comparison with other methods that apply speculative decoding. The 'Reasoning Speed' experiment only compares SC-MCTS* with vanilla MCTS, not against other existing methods that utilize speculative decoding in the context of LLM reasoning. This makes it difficult to assess the true advantage of the proposed method in terms of speedup. These weaknesses, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact the conclusions and generalizability of the proposed method. My confidence in these identified issues is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and lack of supporting evidence.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should evaluate their SC-MCTS* algorithm on a more diverse set of datasets that cover a wider range of reasoning tasks. This should include datasets that involve different types of reasoning, such as temporal reasoning, common-sense reasoning, and mathematical problem-solving. For example, datasets like the MATH dataset, which requires mathematical problem-solving, or the PlanBench dataset, which focuses on planning tasks, could be used to evaluate the method's performance in different domains. Furthermore, the authors should analyze the performance of SC-MCTS* across these datasets to identify any specific strengths or weaknesses of the approach. This would provide a more robust assessment of the algorithm's generalizability and its potential for real-world applications. The analysis should also include a discussion of any modifications or adaptations that might be necessary to apply SC-MCTS* to different types of reasoning tasks. Second, the authors should provide a comprehensive breakdown of the computational cost of their method, including memory usage, the time spent on each component of the algorithm, and the impact of the reward model and speculative decoding on the total runtime. This analysis should include a comparison with other MCTS-based methods and Chain of Thought approaches, providing a clear understanding of the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. The authors should also investigate the scalability of their method with respect to the size of the language model and the complexity of the reasoning task. This would help to identify the practical limitations of the approach and guide future research in this area. The analysis should also consider the impact of different hardware configurations on the performance of the algorithm. Third, the authors should conduct a sensitivity analysis of the hyperparameters, such as the number of MCTS iterations, the exploration parameter in the UCT strategy, and the parameters of the reward model. This analysis should provide insights into how these hyperparameters affect the performance of the method and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values. For example, the authors could analyze the impact of different exploration parameters on the convergence of the MCTS algorithm and the quality of the solutions found. They could also analyze the impact of different reward model parameters on the interpretability and effectiveness of the reward signal. The authors should provide a clear explanation of how these hyperparameters were chosen and justify their choices based on empirical evidence. Fourth, the authors should clarify the specific limitations of the UCT implementation in previous works that they are addressing. If the issue is indeed just finding a better value for the exploration parameter, then it should be framed as such, rather than claiming a novel improvement to the UCT strategy itself. A more detailed analysis of how the optimal value was determined, including the range of values tested and the sensitivity of performance to this parameter, would be beneficial. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more rigorous justification for why their chosen value is optimal, rather than just stating that it yielded the best results. This could involve analyzing the exploration-exploitation trade-off at different stages of the search process and how the chosen parameter affects this balance. Without this level of detail, the claim of improving UCT remains unsubstantiated. Fifth, to support the claim of improved interpretability, the authors need to demonstrate how the statistical properties of the reward models provide insights into the reasoning process of the MCTS algorithm, beyond just indicating which reward model performs better. For example, they could show how specific statistical features of the reward distributions correlate with the quality of the chosen actions or the final outcome. This could involve analyzing the variance of the reward distributions at different nodes in the search tree and how this variance relates to the uncertainty in the model's predictions. Additionally, they could investigate whether certain statistical properties of the reward models can be used to identify biases or limitations in the model's reasoning process. Without such analysis, the statistical properties of the reward models are just descriptive, not interpretative. Finally, the authors should compare their method to other methods that utilize speculative decoding in the context of LLM reasoning. This would provide a more accurate assessment of the speedup achieved by SC-MCTS* and allow for a more meaningful comparison with existing approaches. These suggestions, which are directly linked to the identified weaknesses, would significantly strengthen the paper and enhance its impact on the field.
