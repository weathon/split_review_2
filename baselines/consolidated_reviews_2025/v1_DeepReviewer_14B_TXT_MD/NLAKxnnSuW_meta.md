## Summary

This paper introduces MEGA, a memory-efficient framework for 4D Gaussian Splatting (4DGS), aiming to address the high memory and storage requirements associated with representing complex dynamic 3D scenes. The core contribution of MEGA lies in its novel approach to compressing the color attributes of 4D Gaussians by decoupling them into a per-Gaussian direct color component and a shared, lightweight alternating current (AC) color predictor. This decomposition significantly reduces the memory footprint compared to traditional methods that rely on spherical harmonics coefficients. Furthermore, MEGA incorporates an entropy-constrained Gaussian deformation technique, which uses a deformation field to expand the action range of each Gaussian and integrates an opacity-based entropy loss to limit the number of Gaussians needed, thereby enhancing memory efficiency. The authors demonstrate that their framework achieves substantial storage reductions, up to 190x and 125x on the Technicolor and Neural 3D Video datasets, respectively, while maintaining comparable rendering speeds and scene representation quality. The method is evaluated on multiple datasets and compared with various competitive baselines, demonstrating its effectiveness and generalizability. The paper's significance lies in its potential to make 4DGS more practical for real-world applications by significantly reducing its memory demands. The authors present a well-structured approach, combining color decomposition and deformation techniques to achieve their memory efficiency goals. The experimental results, while showing impressive storage reductions, also reveal some limitations in rendering speed compared to certain baselines, which I will discuss in detail. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of dynamic scene representation by introducing a memory-efficient framework for 4DGS, but there are several areas that require further investigation and clarification to fully validate its claims and practical applicability.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. The core strength of MEGA lies in its innovative approach to compressing 4D Gaussian representations. The decomposition of color attributes into a direct current (DC) component and a shared, lightweight alternating current (AC) color predictor is a novel and effective method for reducing memory usage. This approach directly addresses a critical limitation of 4DGS, which is its high memory footprint, and the results, showing up to 190x storage reduction, are impressive. The use of a lightweight MLP for the AC color predictor is a clever way to capture temporal and viewpoint variations without incurring significant memory overhead. Furthermore, the introduction of an entropy-constrained Gaussian deformation technique is another significant contribution. This technique, combined with an opacity-based entropy loss, effectively limits the number of Gaussians required to represent a scene, further enhancing memory efficiency. The deformation field, which is temporal-viewpoint-aware, allows each Gaussian to cover a larger portion of the scene, reducing the overall number of primitives needed. The paper also demonstrates the effectiveness of MEGA through extensive experiments on multiple datasets, including the Technicolor and Neural 3D Video datasets. The quantitative results, using metrics such as PSNR, DSSIM, and LPIPS, show that MEGA achieves comparable rendering quality to the original 4DGS and other baselines, while significantly reducing memory usage. The authors also provide qualitative comparisons, which visually demonstrate the effectiveness of their method. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The method is clearly explained, and the experimental setup is well-described. The authors have also made an effort to compare their method with a range of recent baselines, which helps to contextualize their contributions. The combination of these technical innovations and empirical achievements makes MEGA a valuable contribution to the field of dynamic scene representation.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, while the paper claims comparable rendering speeds, my analysis of the experimental results reveals that MEGA's frames per second (FPS) are lower than some baselines, notably STG. Specifically, Table 1 shows that MEGA achieves an FPS of 83.14 on the Technicolor dataset, while STG achieves 141.73 FPS. Similarly, Table 2 shows that MEGA achieves an FPS of 77.42 on the Neural 3D Video dataset, while STG achieves 273.47 FPS. This discrepancy contradicts the paper's claim of comparable rendering speeds and indicates a significant performance gap. Furthermore, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the trade-off between memory efficiency and rendering speed. The authors do not investigate the impact of the reduced Gaussian count on rasterization throughput. It is possible that fewer Gaussians might lead to increased overhead in searching for relevant primitives, which could explain the lower FPS. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to fully understand the practical implications of the proposed method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the experimental results presented in the paper. Secondly, the paper does not provide sufficient visual evidence to demonstrate the effectiveness of the deformation field. While the paper includes qualitative comparisons in Figure 4, these are static images that do not show the deformation field's impact over time or across different viewpoints. The authors should have provided visualizations showing the same scene at different viewpoints or times, or visualizations of the motion trajectories of the Gaussians. Without these visualizations, it is difficult to assess the practical benefits of the proposed deformation model. The absence of such visualizations makes it challenging to understand how the deformation field affects the Gaussian primitives and their contribution to the final rendering. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks any visualizations that directly demonstrate the deformation field's effect. Thirdly, the paper lacks a comparative analysis of the chosen deformation strategy. The authors apply the deformation field to the 4D Gaussian parameters (centers, scales, and rotations), but they do not justify why this approach is superior to applying the deformation to the 3D Gaussians obtained by slicing the 4D Gaussians. This lack of comparison makes it unclear whether the chosen approach is indeed the most effective. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not discuss or compare this approach to applying deformation to 3D Gaussians after slicing. Fourthly, while the paper presents quantitative results showing comparable rendering quality, it lacks a detailed analysis of the trade-offs between memory savings and potential impacts on rendering quality, specifically regarding high-frequency details and subtle color variations. The paper does not quantify the potential loss in high-frequency details or subtle color variations due to the compressed color representation. A more rigorous analysis of how the direct color component and the shared AC predictor affect the reconstruction of complex scenes with intricate lighting and shading would be beneficial. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does not include a specific analysis of high-frequency detail loss or subtle color variations. Fifthly, the paper's comparison with existing state-of-the-art methods is limited. While the paper compares against several NeRF-based and Gaussian-based methods, it does not compare against other compression techniques specifically designed for 4DGS, or against alternative scene representation methods like neural radiance fields in terms of memory efficiency. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to contextualize the contributions of the proposed framework and highlight its advantages over other approaches. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks comparisons with specific 4DGS compression techniques and a broader range of alternative scene representations regarding memory efficiency. Sixthly, the paper's discussion on the scalability of the proposed framework to larger or more complex dynamic scenes is limited. The paper does not address how the memory savings and rendering quality scale with the number of Gaussians or the temporal duration of the scene. It is unclear if the proposed method would maintain its efficiency for very long or highly complex dynamic scenes, which is a crucial aspect for practical applications. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a dedicated analysis of scalability with increasing scene complexity and duration. Seventhly, the paper relies on several hyperparameters, such as the trade-off parameters in the loss function and the threshold parameters for densification and pruning. The sensitivity of the method to these hyperparameters is not thoroughly explored, and the chosen values may not be optimal for all datasets or scenes. Specifically, the impact of varying the weight of the entropy loss on the final rendering quality and the number of Gaussians used is unclear. Furthermore, the threshold parameters for densification and pruning, which control the trade-off between rendering quality and the number of Gaussians, are not analyzed in detail. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a dedicated sensitivity analysis for the hyperparameters. Finally, the paper introduces additional computational overhead due to the deformation field and entropy loss. The computational cost of these components, particularly the deformation field, is not analyzed in detail. The paper does not provide a breakdown of the time spent on different parts of the algorithm, making it difficult to assess the impact of the deformation field and entropy loss on the overall training and rendering time. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a detailed breakdown of computational costs for individual components.
