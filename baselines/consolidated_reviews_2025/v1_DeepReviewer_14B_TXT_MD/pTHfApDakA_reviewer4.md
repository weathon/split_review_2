### Summary

This paper proposes a zero-shot method to check the correctness of reasoning steps generated by LLMs. The proposed method consists of four stages: target extraction, information collection, step regeneration, and result comparison. The authors evaluate the proposed method on three math datasets and show that the proposed method can improve the performance of LLMs.

It is unclear whether the proposed method can be applied to other domains, such as commonsense reasoning, logical reasoning, and code generation. The method's reliance on step-by-step reasoning, while effective in math, may not translate well to domains where reasoning is less structured or involves different types of logical inferences. For example, commonsense reasoning often requires integrating diverse pieces of knowledge and making assumptions, which may not be easily captured by the proposed method's stages. Similarly, code generation involves syntax and semantic constraints that are not directly analogous to mathematical problem-solving.
2. The proposed method requires multiple calls to the LLMs, which may be time-consuming and costly. The computational overhead of the four-stage process, especially the regeneration step, could be a significant bottleneck for practical applications. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost, such as the number of tokens processed or the time taken for each stage, making it difficult to assess the method's efficiency. Furthermore, the cost may vary significantly depending on the LLM used, which should be explored in more detail.

### Suggestions

The authors should investigate the applicability of their method to other domains beyond math. Specifically, they should consider how the four stages of their method could be adapted to handle the unique challenges of commonsense reasoning, logical reasoning, and code generation. For example, in commonsense reasoning, the 'target extraction' stage might need to identify implicit assumptions or background knowledge, while the 'information collection' stage would need to gather relevant contextual information. In code generation, the 'step regeneration' stage would need to ensure that the generated code is syntactically correct and semantically consistent with the problem description. The authors should also provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of their method, including the number of tokens processed and the time taken for each stage. This analysis should be performed for different LLMs to understand how the cost varies depending on the model used. Furthermore, the authors should explore techniques to reduce the computational overhead of their method, such as caching intermediate results or using more efficient LLMs for certain stages.

To address the concern about the method's reliance on step-by-step reasoning, the authors could explore how to handle reasoning processes that are not strictly sequential. For example, in some problems, the reasoning steps may be iterative or involve backtracking. The authors could investigate how to adapt their method to handle such cases, perhaps by allowing for multiple regeneration attempts or by incorporating a mechanism to detect and correct errors in the reasoning process. Additionally, the authors should consider how to evaluate the method's performance on problems that require different types of logical inferences, such as deductive, inductive, or abductive reasoning. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's generalizability and robustness. The authors should also consider the impact of the quality of the initial reasoning steps on the performance of the proposed method. If the initial reasoning steps are poor, the regeneration process may not be able to correct the errors, and the overall performance may be limited. Therefore, it would be beneficial to investigate how to improve the quality of the initial reasoning steps, perhaps by using more advanced prompting techniques or by incorporating external knowledge sources.

Finally, the authors should provide more details about the specific prompts used for each stage of their method. This would allow other researchers to reproduce their results and to build upon their work. The authors should also discuss the limitations of their method and suggest directions for future research. For example, they could explore how to extend their method to handle more complex reasoning problems or how to integrate it with other reasoning techniques. The authors should also consider the ethical implications of their work, such as the potential for misuse of the method to generate misleading or harmful content. Addressing these limitations and ethical considerations would further strengthen the paper and make it more impactful.
