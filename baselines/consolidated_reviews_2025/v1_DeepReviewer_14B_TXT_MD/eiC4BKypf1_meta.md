## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to modeling human decision-making by leveraging large language models (LLMs). The core idea is to fine-tune a pre-trained LLM, specifically LLaMA, on datasets derived from cognitive psychology experiments, thereby creating a model termed CENTaUR. The authors demonstrate that this fine-tuned LLM exhibits improved alignment with human behavior compared to the base LLM, as well as other baseline models. The methodology involves presenting text-based descriptions of decision-making scenarios to the LLM, and then training a linear layer on top of the LLM's embeddings to predict human choices. The paper focuses on two distinct decision-making paradigms: 'decisions from descriptions,' where probabilities and outcomes are explicitly stated, and 'decisions from experience,' where individuals learn through repeated interactions. The empirical findings show that CENTaUR achieves lower negative log-likelihood (NLL) scores on held-out data, indicating a better fit to human behavior. Furthermore, the paper explores the model's ability to capture individual differences and its generalization to a hold-out task, demonstrating that CENTaUR can capture qualitative aspects of human behavior not present in the pre-training data. The authors also show that the model can be used to fit individual subjects, and that it generalizes to a hold-out task. Overall, this work presents a compelling case for the use of LLMs as cognitive models, offering a new perspective on how these models can be adapted to understand human decision-making processes. The paper's significance lies in its demonstration that LLMs, when fine-tuned on cognitive task data, can not only fit existing behavioral data but also exhibit human-like behavior in novel scenarios, suggesting a potential for broader applications in cognitive science.

## Strengths

I find the core idea of using LLMs as cognitive models to be both novel and inspiring. The paper's approach of fine-tuning a pre-trained LLM on cognitive task data is a creative way to bridge the gap between artificial intelligence and cognitive science. The experimental design is well-controlled and the results are clearly presented, making it easy to follow the authors' line of reasoning. The use of two distinct decision-making paradigms, 'decisions from descriptions' and 'decisions from experience,' provides a robust test of the model's capabilities. The fact that the fine-tuned model, CENTaUR, outperforms the base LLM and other baseline models on these tasks is a significant achievement. Furthermore, the paper's exploration of individual differences and generalization to a hold-out task adds to the strength of the findings. The demonstration that CENTaUR can capture qualitative aspects of human behavior, such as the overvaluation of described options, is particularly compelling. This suggests that the model is not simply memorizing the training data but is learning underlying patterns of human decision-making. The paper is also well-written and the figures are clear and informative, which makes the work accessible to a broad audience. The authors have successfully shown that LLMs can be adapted to model human behavior, which I believe will motivate more researchers in cognitive science to explore the potential of these models. The paper's contribution is significant in that it opens up a new avenue for cognitive modeling, and I believe this work will be an important contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

While I find the paper's core idea and results compelling, there are several weaknesses that I believe need to be addressed. My primary concern lies in the statistical rigor of the results, specifically regarding the magnitude of the effect and the lack of explicit statistical significance testing. For instance, in the 'Model Simulations' section, the paper presents regret values for the fine-tuned model (CENTaUR) and compares them to human regret. In the choices-13k dataset, CENTaUR has a regret of 1.35, while human regret is 1.24. Similarly, in the horizon task, CENTaUR has a regret of 2.38, while human regret is 2.33. While the paper provides standard errors for these values, it does not explicitly state the statistical significance of the differences. The numerical differences are quite small, and without a significance test, it is difficult to determine whether the observed improvements are due to the model's capabilities or simply due to chance or noise. This lack of statistical testing is a significant limitation, as it undermines the strength of the claims made about the model's performance. Furthermore, the paper does not include confidence intervals in Figure 2, which visually presents the performance comparison. The inclusion of confidence intervals would provide a visual representation of the uncertainty in the regret estimates and allow readers to assess the overlap between the confidence intervals of the two models. The absence of this visual representation makes it harder to assess the significance of the differences. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly presents the regret values and standard errors but lacks the statistical tests to validate the significance of the differences. Another significant weakness is the limited comparison to traditional cognitive models. The paper compares CENTaUR against a random guessing model, the base LLaMA model, and a domain-specific model (BEAST or a hybrid model). While these comparisons are useful, they do not provide a comprehensive evaluation of CENTaUR's performance relative to the existing literature on cognitive modeling. The paper does not include comparisons against more general and well-established cognitive models, such as those based on reinforcement learning, Bayesian inference, or prospect theory. For example, the reviewer suggested comparing against models from Daw et al. (2011), which are prominent in the field of decision-making. The lack of these comparisons makes it difficult to contextualize the performance of CENTaUR and understand its unique contributions. This limitation is a significant concern, as it prevents a thorough assessment of the model's strengths and weaknesses compared to existing approaches. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly lists the baseline models used for comparison, which do not include a broad range of traditional cognitive models. Finally, while the paper does include a hold-out task, the analysis of the model's ability to generalize to new tasks could be more rigorous. The paper demonstrates that CENTaUR can capture qualitative aspects of human behavior on the hold-out task, but it does not explore the model's ability to generalize to tasks with different levels of complexity, different types of uncertainty, or different types of feedback. A more thorough investigation of the model's generalization capabilities would strengthen the paper's claims. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does show generalization to a hold-out task, but the analysis could be more comprehensive.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First and foremost, the authors should conduct a more rigorous statistical analysis of their results. Specifically, they should perform statistical significance tests, such as paired t-tests or ANOVA, to compare the performance of CENTaUR with the base LLM and other baseline models across all evaluation metrics. This will help determine if the observed improvements are statistically significant or could have occurred by chance. The paper should report the p-values and effect sizes (e.g., Cohen's d) for each comparison. This will provide a more robust assessment of the model's performance and address the concern about the magnitude of the effect. Additionally, for the regret metric in Figure 2, the authors should calculate and display confidence intervals (e.g., 95% CI) for both the fine-tuned model and the base model. This will provide a visual representation of the uncertainty in the regret estimates and allow readers to assess the overlap between the confidence intervals of the two models. If the confidence intervals do not overlap, it would provide stronger evidence that the fine-tuned model's regret is significantly lower than the base model's. This will address the concern about the lack of visual representation of uncertainty. Second, the authors should expand their comparison to include a wider range of traditional cognitive models. Instead of relying on a single domain-specific model, they should compare against models based on prospect theory, reinforcement learning, Bayesian inference, or cognitive architectures. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the LLM-based approach and would allow for a more meaningful comparison with the existing literature. The authors should also provide a detailed justification for their choice of baseline models and explain why these models are representative of the state-of-the-art in cognitive modeling. This will address the concern about the limited comparison to traditional models. Third, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation of the model's generalization capabilities. This could involve testing the model on tasks with different levels of complexity, different types of uncertainty, or different types of feedback. They could also explore the model's ability to generalize to tasks that require explicit reasoning or planning. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the model's potential for broader applications in cognitive science. Finally, the authors should consider exploring the impact of different fine-tuning strategies on the model's performance. For example, they could experiment with varying the learning rate, batch size, or number of training epochs. They could also investigate the effect of using different optimization algorithms (e.g., Adam, SGD). Comparing the performance of models fine-tuned with different hyperparameters would provide insights into the sensitivity of the results to the fine-tuning process. Additionally, it would be beneficial to analyze the learning curves during fine-tuning to ensure that the model has converged and is not overfitting or underfitting the training data. This analysis could involve plotting the training and validation loss over epochs and examining the trend. If overfitting is observed, the authors could consider using regularization techniques (e.g., dropout, weight decay) to improve the model's generalization ability. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen the paper's contribution and impact.

## Questions

I have several questions that I believe would help clarify some of the key aspects of the paper. First, how likely is the fine-tuned model to transfer to new tasks that are significantly different from the ones it was trained on? While the paper demonstrates generalization to a hold-out task, I am curious about the model's ability to generalize to tasks with different levels of complexity, different types of uncertainty, or different types of feedback. Specifically, how would the model perform on tasks that require explicit reasoning or planning, rather than just decision-making based on past experiences or descriptions? Second, what is the meaning of the white region in Figure 3a? The figure shows the negative log-likelihood (NLL) difference to the best-fitting model for each participant, but the white regions are not explained in the caption or the text. Understanding what these regions represent is crucial for interpreting the results of the individual differences analysis. Third, what are the specific aspects of the LLM that contribute to its performance as a cognitive model? Is it the ability to learn complex representations from the text prompts, or is it the inherent structure of the LLM itself? Conducting ablation studies or analyzing the internal representations of the LLM could provide valuable insights into the mechanisms that underlie its behavior. Fourth, what are the limitations of the LLM approach? How does the model perform when faced with tasks that are outside of its training distribution, or tasks that require common-sense reasoning or real-world knowledge? Addressing these limitations would provide a more balanced and comprehensive assessment of the potential of LLMs as cognitive models. Finally, what are the potential applications of this model in real-world scenarios? How could the model be used to predict human decision-making in specific domains, such as finance or healthcare? Exploring these applications would enhance the paper's practical implications and demonstrate the broader impact of this work.
