## Summary

This paper introduces Delta, a novel inference-time approach designed to mitigate hallucinations in large language models (LLMs) without requiring model retraining or additional training data. The core idea behind Delta is to leverage contrastive decoding by masking portions of the input prompt and comparing the output distributions of the original and masked inputs. Specifically, the method computes the difference between the logits of the LLM's prediction for the unmasked prompt and a similar but corrupted (masked) prompt, resulting in a corrected distribution over the output tokens. This process aims to reduce the likelihood of hallucinations by amplifying contextually relevant tokens and suppressing those that are not grounded in the input. The authors evaluate Delta on several question-answering benchmarks, including SQuAD v1.1, SQuAD v2, TriviaQA, and Natural Questions, demonstrating improvements in exact match and F1 scores. The method shows particularly significant improvements in handling unanswerable questions, as evidenced by the no-answer exact match score on SQuAD v2. However, the paper also acknowledges that Delta's effectiveness is limited in tasks without explicit contextual information, such as CommonsenseQA and MMLU, where the method shows marginal performance declines compared to the baseline. The paper also incorporates Adaptive Plausibility Constraints (APC) to prevent the language model from generating semantically incorrect sequences, further refining the output. The authors claim that Delta is computationally efficient, as it does not require additional training data or model retraining. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach for reducing hallucinations in LLMs, particularly in context-rich question-answering scenarios, but also highlights the method's limitations and areas for further investigation. The method is inspired by contrastive decoding techniques, particularly those used in vision-language models, and adapts them to the text domain by using masking as a form of input corruption. The paper's findings suggest that manipulating the inference process through contrastive decoding can be an effective way to improve the reliability of LLMs, but also highlight the need for further research to address the method's limitations and improve its generalizability.
