## Summary

This paper delves into the critical issue of generalization in preference optimization (PO) when faced with noisy feedback, a common challenge in real-world applications of language models. The authors introduce a theoretical framework to analyze the generalization risk of Generalized Preference Optimization (GPO) under noisy feedback, where the preference between two samples is incorrectly labeled with a certain probability. The core contribution of the paper is the derivation of generalization guarantees that demonstrate how the risk increases with the noise level. These theoretical findings are supported by empirical validation on both simulated data and a real-world dataset, confirming the practical relevance of the results. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to both researchers and practitioners. However, the analysis relies on strong assumptions about the data distribution and the type of noise, which limits its applicability to more complex real-world scenarios. Despite these limitations, the paper provides valuable insights into the robustness of preference optimization methods and sets the stage for future research to address these challenges.

## Strengths

One of the key strengths of this paper is its clear and concise presentation, which makes the theoretical analysis and empirical results easily accessible to a broad audience. The authors effectively introduce the problem of noisy feedback in preference optimization and provide a solid theoretical foundation for understanding how noise affects generalization. The theoretical results, particularly the derivation of generalization bounds, are well-supported by the empirical validation on both simulated and real-world data. The experiments are comprehensive and well-designed, offering a robust evaluation of the theoretical claims. The paper also highlights the significance of its findings for the development of robust AI systems, which is a crucial consideration in the deployment of language models in real-world applications. The use of the von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution to model sample embeddings is a reasonable choice, given its prevalence in the literature on normalized embeddings in large language models (LLMs). The authors' ability to bridge the gap between theory and practice through their empirical work is commendable, as it provides a practical context for the theoretical findings.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations and assumptions in the theoretical analysis and experimental setup are worth noting. First, the assumption that sample embeddings follow a von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution is a significant limitation. While the vMF distribution is a reasonable approximation for normalized embeddings, it is a unimodal distribution, which may not accurately capture the complex, multi-modal structures often observed in real-world NLP data. This assumption is crucial for the proof of the generalization bounds, and the paper does not provide a sensitivity analysis or discuss how deviations from this assumption might impact the results. For instance, the paper states, 'Specifically, we consider the sample embeddings are from a hyperspherical distribution with unit norm. This closely approximates the structure of embeddings observed after the RMSNorm layer in practical models such as LLaMA (Zhang & Sennrich, 2019; Touvron et al., 2023). In particular, we consider the von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distribution, a classical and important distribution in directional statistics (Mardia & Jupp, 2009), which is analogous to spherical Gaussian distributions for features with unit norms.' (Section 3.3). However, the lack of discussion on the robustness of the results to different embedding distributions is a notable gap. I am highly confident in this limitation, as the vMF assumption is a fundamental part of the theoretical framework, and its restriction to unimodal distributions could significantly limit the applicability of the results to real-world scenarios where multi-modal distributions are more common.

Second, the paper's focus on random label flipping as the primary noise model is another limitation. While random label flipping is a common and well-understood form of noise, real-world noise is often more complex and can include systematic biases or adversarial examples. The theoretical analysis and experiments are tailored to this specific type of noise, and the paper does not explore how the results might generalize to other forms of noise. For example, the paper defines noisy preference data as, '$\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\epsilon}=\{(x_{i},\tilde{y}_{w,i},\tilde{y}_{l,i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$, which flips the preference label with probability $\epsilon$ from $y_{w}\succ y_{l}$ to $y_{l}\succ y_{w}$ for samples in the noise-free oracle dataset $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\;=\;\{(x_{i},y_{w,i},\dot{y}_{l,i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$.' (Section 3.2). The lack of discussion on the limitations of this noise model and the potential impact of other noise types is a valid concern. I am highly confident in this limitation, as the paper's scope is restricted to random label flipping, and the real-world applicability of the results could be enhanced by considering more diverse noise models.

Third, there is a potential disconnect between the theoretical results and the empirical success of preference optimization methods over supervised learning. The theoretical analysis shows that the generalization risk increases with the noise level, which is a valuable insight. However, the paper does not explicitly address the optimization dynamics that could lead to preference optimization methods outperforming supervised learning in practice. The reviewer's point that the empirical success of preference optimization might be due to overfitting on the training data, which is not captured by the theoretical analysis, is well-taken. The paper states, 'The theoretical results show that the generalization risk increases as the noise level increases.' (Theorem 3.1 and 3.2), but it does not discuss the optimization process in detail. I am highly confident in this limitation, as the theoretical focus on generalization risk might not fully explain the empirical phenomena observed in practice.

Finally, the paper lacks a discussion of the limitations of the proposed approach, particularly regarding the impact of different model architectures and training procedures on the derived bounds. The theoretical analysis is based on a general class of preference optimization losses, but the empirical validation is primarily conducted on a specific model architecture (LLaMA) and training procedure. The paper does not explore how the generalization bounds might change for other model architectures or training methods. For example, the paper mentions, 'The theoretical analysis is based on a general class of preference optimization losses, including DPO, IPO, and SLiC.' (Section 3.3), but the empirical results are limited to DPO. This limitation is significant, as the robustness of the theoretical findings to different model architectures and training procedures is crucial for their practical applicability. I am highly confident in this limitation, as the paper's scope is limited in this regard, and a more comprehensive analysis would enhance its value.

## Suggestions

To address the limitations identified in the paper, several concrete and actionable improvements can be made. First, the authors should explore relaxations of the vMF distribution assumption to enhance the practical relevance of the theoretical framework. For instance, considering a mixture of vMF distributions could better model multi-modal preference patterns, which are common in real-world data. This would require a more complex analysis but would significantly broaden the applicability of the results. The authors could introduce a parameter that quantifies the degree of deviation from the hyperspherical assumption and analyze how the generalization bounds change under different values of this parameter. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the robustness of the theoretical findings to variations in the data distribution.

Second, the paper should expand its discussion of the limitations of the proposed approach, particularly regarding the type of noise considered. The current analysis focuses on random label flipping, but real-world noise is often more complex and can include systematic biases or adversarial examples. The authors should discuss how their results might be affected by these other forms of noise and explore the potential for extending the theoretical framework to account for them. For example, they could analyze how the generalization bounds change if the noise is correlated with certain features of the input or the model's predictions. This would help to clarify the scope and limitations of the theoretical analysis and provide a more realistic assessment of the robustness of preference optimization methods.

Third, the authors should analyze the optimization dynamics of preference optimization methods under noisy feedback to bridge the gap between theory and practice. The current theoretical analysis focuses on the generalization risk but does not explicitly model the training process. Understanding how the model parameters evolve during training and how this evolution is affected by noise could provide insights into why preference optimization methods sometimes outperform supervised learning. The authors could investigate the gradient flow of the preference loss function and how it interacts with the noise distribution. Additionally, they could explore the role of regularization techniques in mitigating the effects of noise and improving generalization. Extending the theoretical framework to incorporate regularization terms and analyzing their impact on the generalization bounds would be a valuable addition.

Fourth, the paper should provide more concrete guidance on how to apply the theoretical findings in practice. While the theoretical analysis is valuable, it would be helpful to offer practical recommendations for how to use the derived bounds to improve the robustness of preference optimization algorithms. For example, the authors could discuss how to choose the appropriate hyperparameters or how to detect and mitigate the effects of noisy feedback. This would make the paper more accessible to practitioners and increase its impact on the field. The authors could also consider providing a case study or example to illustrate how their theoretical results can be used to guide the development of more robust preference optimization algorithms. This would help to translate the theoretical insights into practical applications.

Finally, the authors should conduct more extensive experiments on real-world datasets with varying levels of noise. The current experiments on simulated data validate the theoretical results, but real-world datasets with known annotation errors or synthetic noise could provide a more comprehensive evaluation. The experiments should explore the impact of different noise models, such as systematic biases or adversarial examples, and compare the performance of different preference optimization methods under noisy feedback. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the practical implications of the theoretical results and guide the development of more robust preference optimization algorithms.

## Questions

1. Can the authors provide more justification for the vMF distribution assumption? Specifically, how realistic is this assumption for real-world data, especially in NLP, where embeddings might exhibit more complex, multi-modal structures? Is there a way to quantify the degree of deviation from the vMF distribution in real-world datasets, and how would this affect the derived generalization bounds?

2. How does the choice of the function $f$ in the GPO objective affect the generalization guarantees? Are there specific properties of $f$ that are required for the analysis, and how do these properties influence the robustness of the results to noise?

3. Can the authors explain more about the apparent contradiction between the theoretical results and the empirical success of preference optimization methods over supervised learning? Specifically, how do the optimization dynamics and potential overfitting on the training data contribute to this phenomenon, and how can this be reconciled with the theoretical bounds on generalization risk?

4. How do the theoretical bounds change under different types of noise, such as systematic biases or adversarial examples? Is there a way to extend the current framework to account for these more complex noise models, and what are the key challenges in doing so?

5. What is the impact of different model architectures and training procedures on the derived generalization bounds? Are the bounds specific to certain types of models or training methods, or are they more generally applicable? How can the theoretical framework be adapted to account for these variations?
