## Summary

This paper introduces GeONet, a novel neural operator-based framework designed to learn the Wasserstein geodesic between two probability distributions. The core idea revolves around leveraging the dynamic formulation of the optimal transport (OT) problem, which is characterized by a system of coupled partial differential equations (PDEs) in both primal and dual spaces. Instead of directly solving these PDEs for each new pair of distributions, GeONet learns a neural operator that approximates the solution, effectively mapping pairs of initial and terminal distributions to their corresponding geodesic path. This is achieved by training a neural network, specifically a modified DeepONet architecture, to minimize a loss function that enforces the optimality conditions derived from the Benamou-Brenier formulation of OT. The loss function incorporates terms that penalize deviations from the continuity equation, the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, and the boundary conditions. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of GeONet through a series of experiments, primarily focusing on Gaussian mixture distributions in 1D and 2D settings. They compare the performance of GeONet against traditional OT solvers from the POT library, as well as other learning-based methods like Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) and Rectified Flow (RF) on point cloud data. The results indicate that GeONet achieves significant speedups compared to traditional solvers while maintaining reasonable accuracy. The paper also explores the application of GeONet to the MNIST dataset, using a latent space representation of the digits. However, the results on MNIST are less compelling, highlighting the challenges of applying the method to complex, real-world data. Overall, the paper presents an interesting and promising approach to learning Wasserstein geodesics using neural operators, offering a potentially more efficient alternative to traditional numerical solvers. However, the lack of theoretical analysis and the limited evaluation on high-dimensional and complex datasets raise questions about the generalizability and robustness of the proposed method.

The approach of framing the problem as learning a mapping between distribution pairs and their geodesics, rather than solving the PDEs from scratch for each new pair, is both novel and promising. This operator-learning perspective allows for a more efficient computation of geodesics, as demonstrated by the significant speedups achieved by GeONet compared to traditional solvers. The use of a modified DeepONet architecture is also a positive aspect, as it provides a flexible framework for approximating the solution operators of the coupled PDE system. The empirical results on Gaussian mixtures, while limited in dimensionality, do show that GeONet can effectively learn the geodesic path with reasonable accuracy. The comparison with traditional OT solvers, even though it's not a direct comparison of geodesic learning methods, highlights the computational advantage of the proposed approach. Furthermore, the inclusion of comparisons with other learning-based methods like CFM and RF on point cloud data provides some evidence of the method's broader applicability. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, which makes the core ideas and contributions accessible to a wider audience. The authors clearly articulate the problem, the proposed solution, and the experimental results. The use of figures and tables to present the results is also effective in conveying the key findings. The emphasis on the fast inference time of GeONet is another positive aspect, as it makes the method more practical for real-time applications. Finally, the authors' attempt to apply GeONet to the MNIST dataset, despite the challenges encountered, demonstrates a willingness to explore the method's applicability to more complex, real-world data.

My confidence in this limitation is high, as the experimental sections clearly focus on 1D and 2D examples, and there is no mention of strategies to mitigate the curse of dimensionality.
