## Summary

This paper provides a theoretical analysis of the neural collapse phenomenon in deep neural networks, specifically focusing on networks with at least two linear layers at the end. The authors prove that neural collapse occurs under certain conditions, such as low training error and balancedness of the linear layers. They demonstrate that these conditions are satisfied by gradient descent training with weight decay for networks with a wide first layer. The paper also presents numerical experiments that support the theoretical results, showing that neural collapse becomes more pronounced as the depth of the linear head increases. The core contributions of this paper include a rigorous theoretical framework for understanding neural collapse in deep linear networks and empirical validation of these theoretical findings. The significance of this work lies in its ability to provide a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving neural collapse, which is a critical phenomenon in the training of deep neural networks. However, the analysis is limited to specific architectural configurations, and the assumptions made may not always hold in practical scenarios, which restricts the generalizability of the results.

## Strengths

One of the key strengths of this paper is its rigorous theoretical analysis of neural collapse in deep neural networks. The authors provide a detailed and well-structured proof that neural collapse occurs under certain conditions, such as low training error and balancedness of the linear layers. This theoretical framework is a significant contribution to the field, as it moves beyond the simplified unconstrained features model (UFM) and considers the end-to-end training dynamics of deep networks. The paper is well-organized, with clear definitions and a logical flow of ideas, making it accessible to readers with a background in deep learning theory. The numerical experiments are well-designed and provide strong empirical support for the theoretical findings. For instance, the experiments show that as the depth of the linear head increases, the neural collapse metrics (NC1, NC2) improve, and the balancedness of the weight matrices is enhanced. These results are consistent with the theoretical predictions and help to validate the authors' claims. The paper also addresses an important problem in deep learning, which is the understanding of neural collapse, a phenomenon observed in the last layer of deep neural networks. By providing a theoretical understanding of this phenomenon, the authors contribute to a better grasp of the training dynamics and the properties of learned representations in deep networks.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's significant contributions, there are several limitations that I have identified and validated. First, the analysis is limited to deep networks with at least two linear layers at the end. This restriction is evident in the introduction, where the authors state, 'Our work addresses the issue, moving away from unconstrained features and studying DNNs that end with at least two linear layers.' The theoretical framework relies heavily on the properties of linear transformations, such as singular values and operator norms of weight matrices, which are well-defined for linear layers. However, in practice, many deep neural networks employ non-linear activation functions in the output layer, such as softmax or sigmoid, which are common in classification tasks. The paper does not provide a clear extension of its theoretical results to these more realistic scenarios, which limits the scope of the conclusions. This is a substantial limitation, as the behavior of neural collapse in networks with non-linear output layers might differ significantly from that in purely linear networks. The confidence level in this issue is high, as the paper explicitly states its focus on linear heads and the theoretical framework is tightly coupled to linear algebra concepts.

Second, the assumptions made in the paper, such as low training error and balancedness of the linear layers, may not always hold in practical scenarios. Theorem 3.1 explicitly states 'approximate interpolation, i.e., $\left\Vert Z_{L}-Y\right\Vert _{F}\leq\epsilon_{1}$' and 'approximate balancedness, i.e., $\left\Vert W_{\ell+1}^{\top}W_{\ell+1}-W_{\ell}W_{\ell}^{\top}\right\Vert _{op}\leq\epsilon_{2}$' as assumptions. While these conditions are often observed in practice, they are not guaranteed, and the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how deviations from these assumptions might affect the emergence of neural collapse. For example, different initialization schemes or optimization algorithms could lead to higher training errors or unbalanced weight matrices. The paper's focus on demonstrating the *possibility* of neural collapse under these conditions, rather than its universality, is a valid approach, but it leaves open questions about the robustness of the phenomenon in more diverse training settings. The confidence level in this issue is also high, as the assumptions are clearly stated and the paper does not explore their sensitivity in depth.

Third, the theoretical guarantees in Section 4 rely on the assumption of a wide first layer, which is not always the case in practical architectures. Theorem 4.1 states, 'Let the network satisfy Assumption 4.1 (Pyramidal network topology)... We consider a neural network as in (1), and we minimize the $\lambda$-regularized square loss... Assumption 4.1 includes the condition 'Let $n_1\geq N$'. This implies that the first layer's width must be at least the number of samples, which is a strong constraint. Many modern architectures, such as those used in computer vision or natural language processing, employ narrow or even single-layer networks. The assumption of a wide first layer may impose a specific inductive bias that is not present in other architectures, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. The paper does not provide sufficient justification for why this specific architecture is crucial for the analysis, nor does it explore how the results might change for networks with different widths. The confidence level in this issue is high, as the assumption is explicitly stated and the paper's theoretical results are built upon it.

These limitations highlight the need for further research to extend the theoretical framework to more general network architectures and to explore the sensitivity of the results to the assumptions made. While the paper makes a valuable contribution by providing a rigorous analysis of neural collapse in deep linear networks, the practical implications of these findings are somewhat restricted by the specific architectural and training conditions considered.
