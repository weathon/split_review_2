### Summary

This paper introduces a post-hoc calibration method for unsupervised domain adaptation called Pseudo-Calibration (PseudoCal). The method aims to address the issue of poorly calibrated predictive uncertainty in target data. PseudoCal generates a labeled pseudo-target set through inference-stage mixup and cluster assumptions, and then applies temperature scaling for calibration. The authors conduct extensive empirical evaluations across various UDA scenarios to demonstrate the effectiveness of PseudoCal.

### Suggestions

The paper's core weakness lies in its incremental contribution. While the combination of mixup, clustering, and temperature scaling is not inherently flawed, the paper does not sufficiently justify why this specific combination is novel or superior to other possible approaches. The method appears to be a straightforward application of existing techniques, lacking a deeper theoretical grounding or a novel technical insight. To strengthen the paper, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the method's behavior, perhaps by exploring the properties of the pseudo-labels generated by the mixup process within clusters. For instance, how does the distribution of these pseudo-labels compare to the true label distribution, and how does this impact the effectiveness of the subsequent temperature scaling? A more rigorous analysis of the interplay between these components is needed to demonstrate a genuine advancement over existing methods.

Furthermore, the ablation study is inadequate to fully assess the contribution of each component. The comparison with "PseudoCal-same" is insufficient to isolate the effects of mixup and temperature scaling. The authors should conduct a more comprehensive ablation study by systematically removing or replacing each component. For example, what happens if mixup is replaced with a simpler interpolation method, or if temperature scaling is replaced with a different calibration technique? It would also be beneficial to analyze the sensitivity of the method to the choice of mixup parameters and the number of clusters. Such an analysis would provide a more complete understanding of the method's strengths and limitations, and would help to identify potential areas for improvement. The current ablation study leaves open the question of whether the observed performance gains are truly due to the specific combination of techniques, or if they can be attributed to other factors.

Finally, the paper would benefit from a more thorough comparison with existing calibration methods in domain adaptation. While the authors mention that their method is designed for unsupervised domain adaptation, they do not provide a detailed comparison with other calibration techniques that have been applied to this problem. A more comprehensive literature review and a more detailed comparison with existing methods would help to better position the paper within the existing body of work and would highlight the specific advantages of the proposed approach. This would also help to clarify the novelty of the method and its contribution to the field. The authors should also consider exploring the limitations of their method, such as its performance under extreme domain shifts or with limited data, to provide a more balanced assessment of its applicability.
