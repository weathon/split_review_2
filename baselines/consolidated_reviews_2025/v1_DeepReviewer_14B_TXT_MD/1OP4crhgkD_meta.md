## Summary

This paper introduces Semantically Aligned Task Decomposition in Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (SAMA), a novel approach designed to tackle the credit assignment problem in cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) with sparse rewards. The core idea behind SAMA is to leverage the power of pre-trained language models (PLMs) to generate semantically meaningful subgoals, which are then used to guide the learning process of individual agents. The method employs a multi-step process, beginning with the generation of a task manual from the environment's LaTeX source code. This manual, along with the current state of the environment, is then used to prompt a PLM to generate high-level subgoals. These subgoals are subsequently decomposed into individual agent subgoals, which are allocated to each agent. The agents then learn their policies conditioned on these natural language subgoals, using a language-grounded RL approach. A key component of SAMA is its self-reflection mechanism, which allows the PLM to revise its subgoal generation based on the success or failure of the agents. The authors evaluate SAMA on two challenging sparse-reward MARL environments: Overcooked and MiniRTS. The experimental results demonstrate that SAMA significantly improves sample efficiency compared to state-of-the-art automatic subgoal generation (ASG) methods. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative use of PLMs for semantically aligned subgoal generation and its integration with a language-grounded RL framework for MARL. The approach is promising, but the paper also acknowledges several limitations, including the reliance on the quality of PLMs and prompts, the computational cost of using PLMs, and the need for further investigation into the method's sensitivity to various design choices. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to addressing a critical challenge in MARL, with strong empirical results, but also highlights areas for future research and improvement.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the core idea of using PLMs to generate semantically aligned subgoals for MARL is both novel and promising. By leveraging the inherent knowledge encoded in PLMs, the authors are able to create subgoals that are more meaningful and interpretable than those generated by traditional methods. This approach directly addresses the credit assignment problem by providing agents with clear, high-level objectives, which facilitates more efficient learning. The use of chain-of-thought prompting further enhances the quality of the generated subgoals, ensuring that they are logically consistent and relevant to the task at hand. Second, the integration of language-grounded RL is a significant strength. By conditioning the agents' policies on natural language subgoals, the authors create a more flexible and adaptable learning framework. This allows the agents to generalize across different subgoals and tasks, which is a crucial aspect of effective MARL. The experimental results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The authors demonstrate that SAMA significantly outperforms state-of-the-art ASG methods on two challenging sparse-reward tasks, Overcooked and MiniRTS. These results highlight the practical benefits of using PLMs for subgoal generation in MARL. The ablation studies, while not exhaustive, provide some insights into the importance of the self-reflection mechanism and the interactive object extraction step. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors clearly articulate the problem, the proposed solution, and the experimental setup. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the key concepts and results. The inclusion of a limitations section also demonstrates a balanced and critical perspective on the work.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the method's heavy reliance on the quality of the PLMs and the specific prompts used to generate subgoals is a significant concern. While the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of SAMA using specific PLMs and prompts, it lacks a detailed analysis of the method's sensitivity to variations in these critical components. The paper does include an ablation study comparing GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, which provides some insight into the impact of different PLMs. However, this analysis is limited in scope and does not explore a wider range of PLMs or systematically vary the prompts themselves. The prompts used to elicit subgoals from the PLMs are a crucial factor in the method's success, and the paper does not provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the method is robust to variations in prompt design. This lack of sensitivity analysis raises concerns about the generalizability of the results and the potential for the method to fail in scenarios where the PLMs or prompts are not well-suited to the task. Second, the paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison of SAMA to non-PLM-based methods for addressing the credit assignment problem in MARL. While the paper compares SAMA to several baselines, including some ASG methods, it does not explicitly compare against a representative set of non-PLM-based approaches. This makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of SAMA compared to existing methods. The paper should include a comparison to methods that rely on intrinsic motivation, reward shaping, or other techniques for guiding agent behavior. This comparison should not only focus on final performance metrics but also consider the computational cost and sample efficiency of the different methods. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of SAMA. The use of PLMs can be computationally expensive, and it is important to understand the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. The paper mentions the hardware used for experiments and efforts to reduce PLM calls, but it does not provide quantitative metrics on training time, inference time, or resource consumption compared to baselines. This lack of computational analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the method, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Fourth, while the paper includes a section on limitations, I find that the analysis of the method's limitations is not as thorough as it could be. The paper discusses limitations related to prompt selection, PLM errors, the scope for goal-conditioned learning, and the cost of repeated PLM prompting. However, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the scenarios where SAMA may not perform well or may be computationally infeasible. For example, the paper does not discuss the impact of the complexity of the environment, the number of agents, or the quality of the PLMs on the method's performance. Finally, the paper does not provide sufficient detail on the implementation of the baseline methods, particularly regarding the use of population-based training. The paper mentions that some baselines use population-based training, but it is not clear whether SAMA also uses this technique. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to assess the validity of the comparisons and raises concerns about the fairness of the experimental setup. The paper should provide a detailed description of the implementation details of the baseline methods and ensure that all methods are compared under similar conditions.
