## Summary

This paper introduces Secure-FLOATING, a novel framework designed to establish trust in real-time mobility data for connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs), micro-mobility devices, and smartphone users. The core contribution lies in the synergistic integration of federated learning, blockchain technology, and secure multi-party computation (SMPC) to ensure data privacy, integrity, and real-time validation. The framework aims to enable nearby nodes to coordinate and learn to trust each other's mobility data without compromising privacy, addressing a critical challenge in the deployment of intelligent transportation systems. The authors propose a system where nodes locally train machine learning models on their own data and then securely share model updates using SMPC. These updates are aggregated on a permissioned blockchain, which also stores endorsement information in an immutable and transparent manner using the InterPlanetary File System (IPFS). The framework leverages zero-knowledge proofs as a "trust-but-verify" mechanism to filter out noisy or malicious data, enhancing the robustness of the trust establishment process. The paper presents a theoretical analysis of the framework's privacy guarantees and scalability, demonstrating its potential for real-world applications. The authors evaluate Secure-FLOATING using realistic trajectories in New York City, simulating scenarios with up to 8,000 nodes. The experimental results demonstrate the framework's ability to achieve lower delays and overhead while maintaining high accuracy in data validation, even with a significant portion of malicious actors. Specifically, the framework is shown to achieve up to 75% successful endorsement even with 50% attacker penetration. The paper's significance lies in its potential to enable secure and privacy-preserving data sharing in intelligent transportation systems, paving the way for more reliable and efficient mobility solutions. By combining federated learning, blockchain, and SMPC, the framework offers a robust approach to trust establishment that addresses the limitations of traditional centralized and decentralized systems. The authors position Secure-FLOATING as a scalable solution suitable for real-world deployment, highlighting its ability to handle a large number of nodes with linear growth in communication overhead. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach to addressing the challenges of trust and privacy in intelligent transportation systems, with potential implications for the widespread adoption of CAVs and other connected mobility solutions.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, several weaknesses have been identified through careful analysis. A primary concern is the lack of detailed information regarding the implementation of the Secure Multi-Party Computation (SMPC) protocol. While the paper mentions the use of additive secret sharing, it fails to specify the exact cryptographic primitives employed, the precise communication patterns between nodes, and the mechanisms for handling potential vulnerabilities such as node collusion or data poisoning attacks. For instance, the paper states that "Each node splits the local model updates into shares, ensuring that any subset of less than k shares reveals no information about the update. The shares are distributed among neighboring nodes, and each node sums up the shares it receives to get the aggregated model update," but it does not elaborate on how these shares are generated, distributed, and reconstructed to form the global model. This lack of detail makes it difficult to assess the security and robustness of the SMPC protocol. The paper also does not discuss the impact of node failures or malicious nodes on the overall performance and security of the SMPC protocol. This omission is significant, as node failures and malicious behavior are common challenges in real-world vehicular networks. The absence of a detailed analysis of these aspects raises concerns about the framework's practical applicability and reliability. The paper's discussion of zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) is also lacking in detail. While it mentions their use as a "trust-but-verify" mechanism, it fails to specify the type of ZKPs used (e.g., interactive or non-interactive), how they are integrated into the framework, and their impact on performance. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the ZKPs or how this overhead scales with the number of nodes and the complexity of the model. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the practicality of using ZKPs in large-scale deployments. The paper also lacks a comprehensive comparison with existing trust models and security frameworks for CAVs. The experimental evaluation only compares the proposed framework against internally defined baselines, without benchmarking against established state-of-the-art solutions. This omission makes it difficult to gauge the relative advantages and disadvantages of Secure-FLOATING and its suitability for different application scenarios. A more comprehensive comparison would have provided a clearer understanding of the framework's strengths and weaknesses compared to existing approaches. Furthermore, the paper does not adequately address the limitations of the proposed framework, particularly its performance in highly dense or sparse networks and its handling of node heterogeneity. The paper lacks an analysis of how the framework's performance degrades with increasing network density, especially concerning communication overhead and latency. While the paper mentions that "the total communication overhead for n nodes in the network is represented by the function f(n) = 2n - 1, which exhibits a linear growth with respect to n," it does not delve into the practical implications of this in highly dense or sparse networks. Additionally, the paper does not address the impact of varying computational capabilities and data availability across different nodes, which is a common challenge in real-world vehicular networks. The paper only briefly mentions heterogeneity as a future research direction, stating that "The heterogeneity of data across different vehicles (e.g., varying sensor quality, driving patterns, and environmental conditions) can pose challenges for the convergence and generalization of the federated trust model," but it does not provide a detailed analysis of its impact. Finally, the paper lacks specific details on the practical implementation of the framework, including the specific hardware and software requirements for deployment in real-world scenarios. While it mentions the use of IPFS, permissioned blockchain, and communication standards, it does not specify the computational resources required, such as CPU, memory, and storage, or the complete software stack needed for implementation. This lack of detail makes it harder to assess the practical feasibility and resource requirements of the framework. These weaknesses, which have been independently validated through careful analysis, significantly impact the paper's conclusions and limit the understanding of the framework's real-world applicability. The lack of detail in the SMPC implementation, the insufficient discussion of ZKPs, the absence of comprehensive comparisons, and the limited analysis of network density and heterogeneity are all critical issues that need to be addressed to fully evaluate the framework's potential.
