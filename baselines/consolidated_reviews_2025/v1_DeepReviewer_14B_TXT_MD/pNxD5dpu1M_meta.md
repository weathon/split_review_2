## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to modeling large-scale multi-agent systems interacting on sparse networks by leveraging the framework of cooperative mean field games (MFGs) on Chung-Lu graphs. The authors' core contribution lies in extending the traditional MFG framework to accommodate the specific structural properties of Chung-Lu graphs, which are known to exhibit power-law degree distributions and sparsity, characteristics often found in real-world networks. The paper's methodology centers around developing a tractable approximation scheme for the mean field transition probabilities, which is crucial for making the problem computationally feasible. This approximation, termed the "extensive approximation," aims to capture the dynamics of agents changing states over time, considering the network's structure. The authors then propose two learning algorithms: one based on solving a limiting mean field control Markov decision process (MFC MDP) and another that directly interacts with the real graph. The theoretical analysis provides convergence results for the empirical mean fields to the limiting mean fields, and the paper demonstrates the effectiveness of the proposed approach through experiments on synthetic and real-world networks, comparing their algorithms against existing MFG methods based on Lp graphons and graphexes. The empirical findings suggest that the proposed approach outperforms these existing methods in the considered scenarios. Overall, the paper presents a significant step towards modeling large-scale multi-agent systems on sparse networks, addressing a gap in the literature where traditional MFG models often assume dense interactions. The use of Chung-Lu graphs allows for a more realistic representation of network structures, and the development of scalable learning algorithms makes the approach applicable to large-scale problems. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in the clarity of the extensive approximation and the depth of the experimental analysis, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths

The paper's primary strength lies in its innovative application of Chung-Lu graphs within the mean field game framework. This is a significant contribution as it addresses the limitations of traditional MFG models that often assume dense interactions, which are not representative of many real-world networks. By incorporating Chung-Lu graphs, the authors are able to model sparse networks with power-law degree distributions, making the approach more relevant to practical applications. The theoretical analysis, which demonstrates the convergence of empirical mean fields to limiting mean fields, provides a solid foundation for the proposed approach. This rigorous analysis is crucial for establishing the validity of the mean field approximation in the context of sparse networks. Furthermore, the development of scalable learning algorithms is another notable strength. The authors propose two algorithms, one based on solving a limiting MFC MDP and another that directly interacts with the real graph, providing flexibility in implementation. These algorithms are designed to handle the specific characteristics of Chung-Lu graphs, such as their sparsity and power-law degree distributions, which is a non-trivial task. The empirical results, although limited in scope, demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. The experiments on synthetic and real-world networks show that the proposed algorithms outperform existing MFG methods based on Lp graphons and graphexes, suggesting the potential of the approach in practical scenarios. The paper's focus on scalability is also a key strength, as it makes the approach applicable to large-scale multi-agent systems, which is essential for many real-world applications. The authors' ability to bridge the gap between theoretical analysis and practical implementation is commendable, and the paper's overall contribution to the field of multi-agent systems and mean field games is significant.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. The most significant issue is the lack of clarity surrounding the "extensive approximation" introduced in Section 4. The authors present a complex formula for this approximation without providing a step-by-step derivation or a clear explanation of the underlying assumptions. The formula itself involves multiple summations and products over sets like $\mathcal{G}^k$, $\mathcal{X}$, $\mathcal{U}$, $\mathcal{C}^k$, $A_2^k$, and $A_3^k$, and the notation used is not consistently defined within the text. For instance, the terms $p_{2,j}$, $p_{3,jm}$, and $s_j$ are not explicitly defined, making it difficult to understand their meaning and role in the approximation. This lack of clarity makes it challenging for the reader to grasp the intuition behind the approximation and to assess its validity. The paper states that the approximation goes beyond the multinomial assumption, but the precise way in which it does so is not made clear. The connection between the multinomial sampling of neighborhoods and the subsequent approximation of the mean field transition probabilities is not explicitly explained. This lack of transparency is a major limitation, as it hinders the reader's ability to fully understand the core methodology of the paper. My confidence in this assessment is high, as it is based on a direct examination of the paper's content and the lack of explicit definitions and derivations.

This lack of a rigorous complexity analysis makes it difficult to assess the scalability of the proposed algorithms and to compare them with existing methods.
