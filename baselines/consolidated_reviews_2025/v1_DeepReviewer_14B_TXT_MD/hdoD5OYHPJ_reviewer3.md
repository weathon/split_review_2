### Summary

This paper proposes a simple yet effective method to improve the zero-shot performance of CLIP. The key idea is to adaptively adjust the weights of different prompt templates for each image, rather than using a fixed average. The weights are determined by the similarity between the class descriptors and the image embedding. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their method on various datasets and models, showing consistent improvements over the baseline.

1. The performance improvement is relatively small, with an average gain of only 0.45 percentage points. While the method is consistent across different settings, the magnitude of the improvement might not be significant enough to justify the added complexity in some applications.
2. The method relies on the quality of the prompt templates. If the prompt templates are not diverse or informative enough, the adaptive weighting might not be effective. 3. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. While the authors claim it has minimal overhead, a quantitative comparison with the baseline method would be helpful to understand the trade-off between performance and efficiency. Specifically, the cost of computing the similarity scores and updating the weights should be analyzed in relation to the overall inference time.
4. The paper does not explore the potential of combining the proposed method with other techniques for improving zero-shot performance, such as prompt ensembling or knowledge distillation. It is unclear whether the proposed method is complementary to these existing techniques or if it is redundant.

### Suggestions

The authors should investigate the sensitivity of their method to the choice of prompt templates more thoroughly. While the paper demonstrates results with a few different template sets, a more systematic analysis is needed. This could involve varying the number of templates, the diversity of the templates, and the quality of the templates. For example, the authors could explore using templates generated by different methods, such as those from large language models or templates created through manual effort. It would also be beneficial to analyze the correlation between the performance gain and the characteristics of the prompt templates. This analysis would provide a better understanding of the method's limitations and potential for improvement. Furthermore, the authors should explore the impact of the number of prompt templates on the performance and computational cost. It is possible that using a large number of templates might lead to diminishing returns in terms of performance, while significantly increasing the computational overhead. A detailed analysis of this trade-off would be valuable for practical applications.

To better understand the computational cost, the authors should provide a breakdown of the time spent on different steps of the proposed method. This should include the time for computing the similarity scores between class descriptors and image embeddings, the time for updating the weights, and the time for the final classification. This analysis should be compared to the time spent on the baseline method to quantify the additional overhead. The authors should also investigate the scalability of the method with respect to the number of classes and the size of the input images. It is important to know how the computational cost scales with the problem size. Additionally, the authors should explore the potential of optimizing the implementation of the method to reduce the computational overhead. This could involve using more efficient algorithms or data structures, or leveraging hardware acceleration. A detailed analysis of the computational cost would help to determine the practical applicability of the method.

Finally, the authors should explore the potential of combining their method with other techniques for improving zero-shot performance. For example, they could investigate whether combining their method with prompt ensembling or knowledge distillation leads to further performance gains. It is also possible that their method could be used as a pre-processing step for other techniques. The authors should also explore the potential of using their method to generate more informative prompt templates. For example, they could use the learned weights to identify the most informative templates and use them to generate new templates. This could lead to a more efficient and effective way of improving zero-shot performance. A thorough investigation of the complementarity of the proposed method with existing techniques would be valuable for the community.
