## Summary

This paper tackles the challenging problem of estimating heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE) in the presence of delayed responses, a scenario often encountered in real-world applications but frequently overlooked in existing literature. The authors introduce a novel framework, Counterfactual Regression with Delayed Feedback (CFR-DF), which explicitly models potential response times alongside eventual outcomes. This is a crucial distinction, as it acknowledges that the effect of a treatment may not be immediately observable. The core methodological approach involves adapting the counterfactual regression framework to handle delayed feedback by treating the eventual potential outcomes as latent variables. To estimate these latent variables, the authors employ a modified Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. This algorithm iteratively estimates the posterior probabilities of the latent outcomes and updates the model parameters to maximize the likelihood of the observed data. The authors provide theoretical identifiability results, demonstrating that under certain assumptions, the potential outcomes and response times can be uniquely identified. The paper presents a rigorous theoretical analysis, including proofs of identifiability, which is a significant contribution. The proposed CFR-DF method is evaluated through extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets. The synthetic datasets allow for controlled experiments where the ground truth is known, while the real-world datasets demonstrate the practical applicability of the method. The empirical results generally show that CFR-DF outperforms existing methods that do not account for delayed feedback, highlighting the importance of explicitly modeling response times in HTE estimation. The authors' work makes a valuable contribution by formalizing the delayed feedback problem in HTE estimation and proposing a practical method to address it. The paper's significance lies in its ability to bridge the gap between theoretical causal inference and practical applications where delayed responses are common. By explicitly modeling response times, the proposed method provides a more accurate and nuanced understanding of treatment effects in these scenarios. The paper's focus on both theoretical grounding and empirical validation makes it a valuable contribution to the field of causal inference.
