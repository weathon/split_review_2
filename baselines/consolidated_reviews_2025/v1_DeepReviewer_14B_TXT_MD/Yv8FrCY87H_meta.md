## Summary

This paper introduces Tree-of-Table, a novel approach designed to enhance the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) when dealing with large and complex tabular data. The core idea revolves around a hierarchical decomposition of tables into a tree-like structure, facilitating a more manageable and structured reasoning process. The method begins with Table Condensation, where relevant portions of the tables are identified and merged, reducing the cognitive load on the LLM. This is followed by Table Decomposition, which breaks down the complex reasoning task into a series of intermediate steps. Finally, a Table-Tree is constructed, representing the hierarchical reasoning process, and the LLM traverses this tree in a depth-first manner to generate the final answer. The authors evaluate their method on several datasets, including WikiTQ, TableFact, FeTaQA, and BIRD, demonstrating that Tree-of-Table achieves state-of-the-art performance, particularly on the large-scale BIRD dataset. The experimental results show that this approach not only improves accuracy but also enhances efficiency, as measured by the number of generated samples. The authors also conduct ablation studies to analyze the impact of table size and different table formats on the method's performance. Overall, the paper presents a well-structured and empirically validated method for improving LLM-based table reasoning, addressing a critical challenge in the field of natural language processing. The approach is intuitive and the results are promising, suggesting a significant step forward in handling complex tabular data with LLMs.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the proposed Tree-of-Table method is both novel and well-designed to address the challenges of large-scale table understanding. The use of Table Condensation and Decomposition to distill and reorganize relevant data into a manageable format is a valuable contribution. This approach effectively reduces the complexity of the input, making it easier for LLMs to process and reason over the information. The hierarchical tree structure, inspired by the Tree-of-Thought concept, provides a structured way to decompose complex reasoning tasks into smaller, more manageable steps. This is a significant improvement over methods that attempt to process the entire table at once. Second, the authors conduct extensive experiments on multiple datasets, demonstrating the effectiveness of their approach. The results show that Tree-of-Table outperforms existing methods in terms of both accuracy and efficiency, particularly on the large-scale BIRD dataset. The inclusion of ablation studies further strengthens the paper by providing insights into the impact of different factors, such as table size and format, on the method's performance. The consistent performance across different datasets and LLMs highlights the robustness and generalizability of the approach. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow. The authors provide clear explanations of their method and experimental results, making it accessible to a broad audience. The figures and tables are well-organized and effectively communicate the key findings. The overall presentation of the paper is excellent, which enhances its impact and readability.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the method's reliance on the LLM's inherent reasoning capabilities is a significant limitation. While the Tree-of-Table approach provides a structured framework for reasoning, it does not fundamentally address the limitations of the underlying LLM. The paper explicitly states that the method uses LLMs for schema-linking, table merging, and thought generation, indicating a strong dependence on the LLM's ability to perform these tasks effectively. The experimental results, which show performance variations across different LLMs, further highlight this dependency. This reliance on the LLM's capabilities means that the method's performance is inherently capped by the LLM's reasoning abilities, and the paper lacks a detailed discussion of this limitation and potential solutions. Second, the paper lacks crucial implementation details that are necessary for reproducibility. While the authors describe the overall method, they do not provide specific examples of the prompts used for each step of the Tree-of-Table method, including the exact wording and any specific formatting requirements. The paper mentions the use of 'Thought Decomposition Prompt' and 'Operation Sample Prompt,' but the content of these prompts is not provided. Additionally, the hyperparameter settings used for the LLMs are not specified. This lack of detail makes it difficult for other researchers to replicate the results and build upon the proposed method. For example, how does the depth and branching factor of the tree affect performance? Are there specific types of questions or tables where a deeper or wider tree is more beneficial? This analysis could provide valuable insights into the design choices of the method. Finally, the authors should include a detailed failure analysis to provide a deeper understanding of the method's limitations. This analysis should go beyond simply stating that the method fails in certain cases. Instead, it should provide a detailed explanation of the specific reasons why the method fails, including the types of questions or tables that pose the most challenges. For example, the authors could analyze the cases where the method fails to correctly identify the relevant columns or rows, or where it fails to correctly aggregate or compare data. This analysis should also include examples of the failure cases, along with a discussion of the underlying causes. This would provide valuable insights into the method's limitations and suggest potential directions for future research.
