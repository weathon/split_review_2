## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to model inversion attacks (MIAs) by leveraging the power of diffusion models, specifically addressing the limitations of traditional GAN-based methods. The core contribution is the Diffusion Distillation Model Inversion Attack (DDMI) framework, which replaces the GAN generator with a single-step generator distilled from a pre-trained diffusion model. This approach aims to enhance the stability and fidelity of reconstructed samples in MIA scenarios. The paper also extends the application of generative MIAs to multimodal models like CLIP, demonstrating the potential for privacy leakage in these systems. The authors conduct extensive experiments, showing that DDMI outperforms state-of-the-art GAN-based MIAs in both white-box and black-box settings, particularly in terms of visual fidelity and reconstruction accuracy. However, the paper's focus on GAN-based baselines and the lack of detailed computational cost analysis and qualitative evaluation of the reconstructed samples are notable limitations. Despite these issues, the paper provides a valuable contribution to the field of privacy-preserving machine learning, highlighting the need for robust defenses against advanced attack methods.

## Strengths

One of the key strengths of this paper is its well-structured and clear presentation, making it easy to follow the methodology and experimental results. The authors effectively articulate the limitations of GAN-based MIAs, such as instability and low fidelity, and propose a compelling alternative using diffusion models. The DDMI framework is innovative in its approach, leveraging the superior generative capabilities of diffusion models to enhance the inversion process. The paper provides a detailed explanation of the distillation process, which is crucial for understanding how the single-step generator is derived from a pre-trained diffusion model. The empirical results are robust, with extensive experiments demonstrating that DDMI outperforms existing GAN-based methods in both white-box and black-box settings. The authors also explore the application of MIAs to multimodal models like CLIP, which is a novel and important contribution, revealing new privacy vulnerabilities in these systems. The paper's focus on improving the visual fidelity and accuracy of reconstructed samples is particularly noteworthy, as it addresses a significant challenge in the field of MIAs. The authors' use of quantitative metrics like FID and KNN distance to evaluate the performance of DDMI is thorough and provides a solid foundation for their claims. Overall, the paper makes a valuable contribution to the research community by advancing the state-of-the-art in generative MIAs and highlighting the need for robust privacy defenses in machine learning models.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, several limitations are evident. First, the paper's novelty is somewhat limited, as the initial parts of the methodology section primarily apply existing techniques to the MIA problem. The authors acknowledge that DDMI builds on the existing framework of generative model inversion attacks (GMI) but replaces the GAN-based generator with a single-step generator distilled from a pre-trained diffusion model. This reliance on the existing GMI framework, while beneficial for the research community, does not fully establish the unique contributions of DDMI. The paper also lacks a detailed comparison between the single-step generator and the full DDMI framework, which is crucial for understanding the trade-offs between computational cost and inversion quality. For instance, the paper does not discuss the computational cost or inversion quality differences between using a pre-trained single-step generator and the full DDMI framework, nor does it analyze the number of diffusion steps required for DDMI to achieve comparable results to the single-step method. This omission makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of DDMI, especially when considering the potential for scaling to larger models or datasets. The computational cost analysis is a significant gap, as the paper does not provide a detailed breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each stage of the DDMI framework, including the distillation process and the inversion process itself. This lack of information hinders the reader's ability to evaluate the practicality of the proposed method. Additionally, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed methods. For example, the performance degradation of DDMI under increasing noise in the target model's output probabilities is not explored, nor is the sensitivity of the methods to the choice of hyperparameters, particularly in the diffusion process. The paper also does not investigate the potential for the methods to fail when the target model has been trained with robustness techniques such as adversarial training. This omission is crucial, as it leaves the reader with an incomplete understanding of the method's robustness and applicability. Furthermore, the evaluation metrics used in the experiments are primarily quantitative, such as FID and KNN distance, but lack a qualitative analysis of the reconstructed samples. While quantitative metrics provide a numerical assessment, they do not fully capture the perceptual quality and semantic fidelity of the reconstructed images. A more detailed qualitative analysis, perhaps including a user study or a more in-depth visual inspection, would strengthen the evaluation. The paper also does not explore potential defense mechanisms against DDMI, which is essential for providing a balanced view of the framework's practical implications and limitations. The authors should at least acknowledge the existence of potential defenses and discuss how they might impact the effectiveness of the proposed attack. These weaknesses, while not undermining the overall contribution of the paper, significantly impact the completeness and practical relevance of the proposed method.

## Suggestions

To address the identified limitations, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison of the single-step generator and the full DDMI framework. This should include experiments that directly compare the computational costs and inversion quality of both methods, such as measuring the inference time for each and plotting inversion quality against computational efficiency. The authors should also discuss the scenarios where each method is most effective, such as the robustness of the single-step method to noise in the target model's output probabilities or the performance of DDMI with more complex target models. A detailed analysis of the trade-offs between the two approaches would be beneficial. To enhance the computational cost analysis, the authors should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each stage of the DDMI framework, including the pretraining of the diffusion model, the distillation process, and the inversion process. This analysis should consider how these costs scale with the size of the model and the dataset, providing a table that shows the training time and memory usage for different model sizes and dataset sizes. The authors should also discuss potential optimizations for the computational cost of DDMI, such as more efficient distillation techniques or model compression methods. To strengthen the comparison with other generative models, the authors should include a more thorough discussion of the potential benefits and drawbacks of using alternative generative models for MIAs, such as normalizing flows or autoregressive models. This should involve adapting these models for generative MIA tasks and providing a comparative analysis of their performance against DDMI. The authors should also discuss the challenges associated with adapting these models for MIA tasks, such as the need for specific training strategies or the potential for increased computational cost. To address the lack of qualitative analysis, the authors should include a more in-depth visual inspection of the reconstructed samples, focusing on perceptual quality and semantic fidelity. This could involve a user study where participants rate the quality of the reconstructed images or a detailed visual analysis by the authors, highlighting specific features and details. The authors should also discuss the limitations of the quantitative metrics used in the paper and explain why a qualitative analysis is necessary to fully evaluate the performance of DDMI. Finally, to provide a more balanced view of the framework's practical implications, the authors should explore potential defense mechanisms against DDMI. This could include techniques such as differential privacy or adversarial training, and the authors should discuss how these defenses might impact the effectiveness of the proposed attack. A thorough discussion of these limitations and potential defenses would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the proposed methods and their applicability.
