## Summary

This paper explores the hypothesis that intrinsic behavioral variability (IBV), such as spontaneous muscle activations (SMAs), plays a crucial role in the development of flexible motor representations, enabling adaptation to novel motor tasks and changes in the body. The authors propose a computational framework where agents, simulated as four-joint finger-like structures, are trained using three distinct methods: a training-only approach (H0), a pre-training IBV approach (H1), and an intermittent IBV approach (H2). The core idea is that exposing agents to variability in their movements, either before or during training, enhances their ability to adapt to new situations, such as reaching for new targets, adapting to simulated amputation, or coping with simulated neural damage. The agents are controlled by a simple feedforward neural network, and the training involves both a self-supervised IBV phase and a supervised reaching phase. The results indicate that agents trained with the intermittent IBV approach (H2) outperform those trained with the other two methods in all three adaptation tasks. The authors interpret this as evidence that IBV promotes the development of more flexible and robust motor representations, which are better able to accommodate novel demands. The paper draws inspiration from developmental neuroscience, particularly the role of SMAs in early motor development, and attempts to translate these biological concepts into a computational framework. The authors argue that their findings provide a computational basis for understanding how IBV might contribute to motor learning and adaptation in biological systems. While the paper presents an interesting and novel approach to exploring the role of IBV, it suffers from several limitations, particularly in the simplicity of the model, the lack of detailed parameter specifications, and the absence of a thorough comparison with existing computational methods. The paper's core contribution lies in its attempt to bridge biological concepts with machine learning, offering a fresh perspective on how intrinsic variability can enhance learning and adaptation in artificial agents. However, the limitations in the methodology and presentation hinder the paper's ability to fully support its claims and to place its findings within the broader context of motor learning and adaptation research.

The simulated agent, a four-joint structure resembling a finger, and the single-layer neural network, are far removed from the complexity of human neuromotor systems. As noted by multiple reviewers, this simplicity raises questions about the generalizability of the findings to more complex motor tasks or different types of neural architectures. The paper does not adequately address how the observed benefits of intermittent IBV might translate to more realistic scenarios involving complex dynamics and coordination. This limitation is further compounded by the lack of a thorough discussion of the model's limitations and its potential impact on the generalizability of the results. This lack of detail makes it difficult to understand the specific conditions under which IBV is most effective and hinders the reproducibility of the results. ## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should **significantly enhance the model's complexity**. This could involve using simulated agents with a greater number of joints, more complex body structures, and more sophisticated neural networks. Exploring different neural architectures, such as recurrent or convolutional networks, could also provide valuable insights into the role of IBV in different learning scenarios. Second, the authors should provide a **detailed description of the IBV parameters**. This should include the magnitude, frequency, and duration of the variability introduced during the IBV training phase. A sensitivity analysis exploring the impact of different IBV schedules on both behavioral performance and neural weight variability would be highly beneficial. This analysis should include a range of variability parameters and should clearly demonstrate how the chosen parameters lead to the observed results. Third, the authors should provide a **more mechanistic explanation of how neural weight variability contributes to improved behavioral performance**. This could involve analyzing the specific changes in neural weights during different phases of learning and adaptation, and relating these changes to the observed behavioral outcomes. For example, visualizing the weight changes in a lower-dimensional space could help to understand the structure of the learned representations and how they evolve with IBV. Fourth, the authors should address the potential for **overfitting to the specific experimental conditions**. This could be done by testing the model on a wider range of motor tasks and environmental conditions, and by comparing the performance of the IBV-trained agents with agents trained without IBV under these varied conditions. Fifth, the authors should **compare their approach with existing methods** in reinforcement learning and motor control. This comparison should highlight the unique contributions of their framework and its potential advantages over existing methods. For example, the authors could compare their approach with methods that use exploration noise or curriculum learning, and discuss how their approach differs in terms of its biological plausibility and its ability to facilitate adaptation to novel motor tasks and changes in the body. Sixth, the authors should provide a **more detailed explanation of the self-supervised task** used in H1 and H2. This explanation should include a clear description of the task's objectives, the specific inputs and outputs of the neural network during this task, and how this task relates to the target-reaching task. A visual representation of the self-supervised task, such as a diagram or a short video, would be helpful. Additionally, the authors should discuss the rationale behind choosing this particular self-supervised task and how it is expected to promote motor adaptation. Seventh, the authors should **redraw the figures** using a plotting library like matplotlib, ensuring consistent formatting, appropriate font sizes, and clear labeling. The figures should be designed to clearly highlight the key findings of the study, rather than simply showing the simulation environment. For example, instead of showing the agent moving, the figures could show the performance of the agent over time, or the learned representations of the body. Eighth, the authors should include a **dedicated limitations section**, explicitly addressing the limitations of the model's simplicity, the potential impact of these limitations on the generalizability of the findings, and the potential for overfitting to the specific experimental conditions. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the **potential implications of their work**, exploring the broader impact and future directions. The authors should also proofread their paper to correct any grammatical errors or typos.
