## Summary

This paper introduces SAMRefiner, a novel approach to refining coarse segmentation masks by leveraging the Segment Anything Model (SAM). The core idea is to adapt SAM, a model primarily designed for prompt-based segmentation, to the task of mask refinement. Instead of directly predicting masks from scratch, SAMRefiner uses the information contained within a coarse mask to generate multiple prompts, which are then used to guide SAM's segmentation process. This is achieved through a multi-prompt excavation strategy that employs distance-guided point prompts, context-aware elastic bounding boxes (CEBoxes), and Gaussian-style mask prompts. These diverse prompts are designed to address the inherent noise and imperfections in coarse masks. The method also includes a split-then-merge (STM) pipeline to handle multi-object scenarios in semantic segmentation, where individual objects within a mask are refined separately and then recombined. Furthermore, the authors propose an enhanced version, SAMRefiner++, which incorporates an Intersection over Union (IoU) adaptation step. This step involves training a small adapter to refine SAM's IoU predictions, leading to further performance improvements without requiring additional annotations. The method is evaluated on a variety of benchmarks, including DAVIS-585, COCO, and PASCAL VOC 2012, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving mask quality for both semantic and instance segmentation tasks. The results show that SAMRefiner and SAMRefiner++ outperform existing refinement techniques while maintaining efficiency. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative adaptation of SAM for mask refinement, its robust multi-prompt strategy, and the introduction of an effective IoU adaptation technique. The authors demonstrate the versatility of their approach by applying it to different segmentation tasks and datasets, highlighting its potential as a practical tool for improving the quality of coarse segmentation masks.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The most compelling is the innovative adaptation of the Segment Anything Model (SAM) for the task of mask refinement. Instead of using SAM for direct segmentation, the authors cleverly leverage its prompt-based nature to refine existing coarse masks. This approach is both practical and effective, as it allows the model to focus on improving the quality of existing segmentations rather than learning to segment from scratch. The multi-prompt excavation strategy is another significant strength. By generating diverse prompts, including distance-guided points, context-aware elastic bounding boxes, and Gaussian-style masks, the method effectively addresses the noise and defects often present in coarse masks. This strategy ensures that the refinement process is robust to various types of errors and imperfections. The context-aware elastic bounding boxes (CEBoxes) are particularly noteworthy, as they adapt to the shape of the object, providing a more accurate prompt than simple bounding boxes. The split-then-merge (STM) pipeline is also a valuable contribution, as it allows the method to handle multi-object scenarios in semantic segmentation effectively. By refining individual objects separately and then merging them, the method avoids the problem of over-segmentation or under-segmentation. Furthermore, the introduction of SAMRefiner++, which incorporates an IoU adaptation step, is a significant improvement. This step allows the method to further refine the masks without requiring additional annotations, making it more practical for real-world applications. The experimental results presented in the paper are also compelling. The method demonstrates strong performance across various benchmarks, outperforming existing refinement techniques while maintaining efficiency. The authors have also shown the versatility of their approach by applying it to different segmentation tasks and datasets. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the methodology and experimental results. The authors have successfully presented a complex method in a way that is accessible and understandable.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further consideration. First, the paper's evaluation is limited by the datasets used. While the authors evaluate SAMRefiner on DAVIS-585, COCO, and PASCAL VOC 2012, these datasets do not fully capture the diversity of real-world segmentation challenges. Specifically, the paper lacks an evaluation on datasets with highly cluttered backgrounds, significant occlusions, or extreme variations in object scale. This absence limits the assessment of the method's robustness in more complex scenarios. For instance, datasets with medical images, satellite imagery, or high-resolution natural images could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the method's generalization capabilities. The lack of such evaluations makes it difficult to ascertain how well SAMRefiner would perform in real-world applications where these challenges are common. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper explicitly lists the datasets used, and these datasets are not representative of all real-world scenarios. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for SAMRefiner. While the authors provide the total time cost for refining a large dataset, they do not provide a breakdown of the inference time per image or batch, nor do they discuss the memory footprint of the method. This information is crucial for researchers who want to use the method in their own work, especially in resource-constrained environments. The paper mentions that experiments were conducted on a 3090 GPU, but there is no analysis of how the method performs on different hardware configurations. This lack of information makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper does not provide the necessary details regarding computational cost. Third, the paper does not adequately explore the sensitivity of the method to the quality of the initial coarse masks. While the method is designed to handle noisy prompts, there is no systematic evaluation of how performance degrades with increasing levels of noise and incompleteness in the initial masks. The paper uses pseudo masks generated from different methods, which inherently have varying quality, but it does not provide a controlled analysis of how SAMRefiner's performance is affected by specific types of noise, such as random pixel noise, boundary fuzzing, or object occlusion. The paper also does not explore the impact of different types of initial mask generation methods beyond the ones used. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to understand the robustness of the approach under various real-world conditions. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper does not provide a controlled study on the sensitivity to noise and incompleteness. Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed method, specifically the time required for prompt generation and mask refinement. While the paper provides the total time cost for refining a large dataset, it lacks a breakdown of the time spent on individual components, such as prompt generation, mask refinement, the split-then-merge pipeline, and the IoU adaptation step. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the approach for large-scale applications. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper does not provide the necessary details regarding the computational cost of each component. Finally, the paper does not adequately address the potential for overfitting in the IoU adaptation step of SAMRefiner++. The paper states that SAMRefiner++ uses coarse masks as priors for IoU adaptation, but it does not discuss the potential for overfitting to the specific dataset used for adaptation. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the impact of the size of the coarse mask dataset used for adaptation on the final performance. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the generalization ability of SAMRefiner++ on unseen datasets. My confidence in this limitation is high, as the paper does not provide any analysis or discussion on the potential for overfitting or the impact of dataset size.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should expand their evaluation to include a broader range of datasets that present more challenging segmentation scenarios. Specifically, they should consider datasets with highly cluttered backgrounds, significant occlusions, and extreme variations in object scale. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's robustness and generalization capabilities. For instance, datasets with medical images, satellite imagery, or high-resolution natural images could be included. Furthermore, the authors should analyze the performance of SAMRefiner on datasets with varying levels of annotation quality, as this would reflect real-world scenarios where coarse masks may not be uniformly accurate. This analysis should include a discussion of the types of errors that SAMRefiner tends to make on different datasets, which could provide insights into its limitations and potential areas for improvement. Second, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational resources required for SAMRefiner. This analysis should include a breakdown of the inference time per image or batch, the memory footprint of the method, and the performance on different hardware configurations. The authors should also compare the computational cost of SAMRefiner with other mask refinement methods, which would help to assess its efficiency. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the impact of different optimization techniques on the computational performance of SAMRefiner, such as model quantization or pruning. This would provide valuable guidance for researchers who want to deploy the method in resource-constrained environments. The authors should also specify the software and library versions used in their experiments to ensure reproducibility. Third, the authors should conduct a systematic evaluation of their method using a range of coarse masks with varying levels of noise and incompleteness. This evaluation should include quantitative metrics that measure the accuracy of the refined masks compared to the ground truth, as well as metrics that quantify the degree of noise and incompleteness in the initial masks. The authors should also analyze the impact of different types of noise (e.g., random pixel noise, boundary fuzzing, object occlusion) and incompleteness (e.g., missing object parts, truncated objects) on the final refinement quality. This analysis should provide insights into the limitations of the method and help guide future research in developing more robust mask refinement techniques. The authors should also explore the use of data augmentation techniques to improve the robustness of the method to noisy initial masks. Fourth, the authors should provide a detailed breakdown of the time required for each step of their method, including prompt generation (distance-guided points, elastic boxes, and Gaussian masks), the split-then-merge pipeline, and the IoU adaptation step. This analysis should be performed on a standard hardware setup and should include the memory footprint of each step. The authors should compare these results against existing mask refinement techniques, providing a clear understanding of the trade-offs between performance and computational cost. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the potential for optimizing the prompt generation process, such as using more efficient algorithms for distance transform or elastic box computation, to reduce the overall computational overhead. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the IoU adaptation step in SAMRefiner++. Specifically, they should investigate the potential for overfitting to the specific dataset used for adaptation and how this might affect the generalization performance on unseen data. This could be achieved by evaluating the performance of SAMRefiner++ on a held-out dataset that is different from the dataset used for adaptation. The authors should also explore the impact of the size of the coarse mask dataset used for adaptation on the final performance. This could be done by training SAMRefiner++ with different sizes of coarse mask datasets and analyzing the corresponding changes in the refinement performance. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the limitations of the IoU adaptation step and provide guidelines for when it is appropriate to use this step and when it is not.
