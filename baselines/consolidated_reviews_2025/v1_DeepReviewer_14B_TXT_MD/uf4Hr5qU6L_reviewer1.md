### Summary

The paper proposes a new prompting strategy, PRECOT, to enhance the reasoning ability of LLMs. The proposed method requires LLMs to first construct a problem representation containing the initial and goal states, and then solve the problem. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method on three reasoning tasks, including arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning.

### Suggestions

The authors should more clearly articulate the distinction between their approach and existing methods, particularly zero-shot Chain-of-Thought (CoT). While the paper positions PRECOT as a more structured approach by explicitly defining initial and goal states, the core mechanism of guiding the LLM through a step-by-step reasoning process is fundamentally similar to zero-shot CoT. To strengthen the paper, the authors need to provide a more rigorous analysis of how the explicit problem representation in PRECOT leads to qualitatively different reasoning behaviors compared to simply prompting with 'Let's think step by step'. This could involve a detailed examination of the intermediate representations generated by the model under both approaches, highlighting the specific ways in which PRECOT's structured representation facilitates more accurate or efficient reasoning. Furthermore, the authors should explore the limitations of PRECOT, such as scenarios where the initial and goal states are difficult to define or where the problem requires a more flexible reasoning approach that doesn't fit into a strict initial-to-goal state framework.

To address the lack of comparative analysis, the authors should include a more comprehensive evaluation that incorporates other reasoning strategies like decomposition and planning. The current evaluation only compares PRECOT with CoT, which is insufficient to demonstrate the unique advantages of the proposed method. The authors should investigate how PRECOT compares to methods that explicitly decompose problems into sub-problems or use planning algorithms to guide the reasoning process. This would involve not only comparing the final performance metrics but also analyzing the reasoning trajectories generated by each method. For example, the authors could examine whether PRECot's structured approach leads to more efficient search or better handling of complex problems compared to decomposition or planning methods. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of PRECOT and its place within the broader landscape of LLM reasoning techniques.

Finally, the authors should consider exploring the potential for combining PRECOT with other reasoning strategies. For instance, could PRECOT be used as a pre-processing step to structure the problem, followed by a decomposition method to break down the problem into smaller sub-problems? Or could a planning algorithm be used to guide the reasoning process within the framework of PRECOT's initial and goal states? By exploring these possibilities, the authors could demonstrate the versatility of their approach and its potential for integration with other reasoning techniques. This would also help to address the concern that PRECOT is simply a more detailed version of zero-shot CoT, by showing that it can be used as a building block for more complex reasoning systems.
