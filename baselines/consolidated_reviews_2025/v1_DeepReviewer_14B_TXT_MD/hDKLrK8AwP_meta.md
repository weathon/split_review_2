## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to generating Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) with a focus on improving the readability of the resulting code, a crucial aspect often overlooked in favor of visual fidelity. The authors propose a set of three distinct, differentiable loss functions designed to enhance specific aspects of SVG readability: structural consistency, element appropriateness, and redundancy reduction. The structural consistency loss aims to ensure that the order of SVG elements in the code reflects their spatial arrangement in the image, while the element appropriateness loss encourages the use of simpler SVG primitives (like rectangles and circles) over more complex paths when possible. The redundancy reduction loss penalizes unnecessary elements that do not significantly contribute to the visual output. These losses are incorporated into a variational autoencoder (VAE) framework, which is trained to generate SVG code from input images. The authors evaluate their approach using both quantitative metrics, including the proposed readability metrics, and a qualitative assessment using GPT-3.5 to gauge the understandability of the generated code. The experiments are conducted on synthetic shape datasets and font reconstruction tasks, demonstrating that the proposed method can improve code readability without significantly sacrificing visual accuracy. The core contribution of this work lies in the explicit consideration of code readability as a key objective in SVG generation, offering a practical approach to creating SVGs that are not only visually accurate but also easier for humans to understand and edit. This is a significant step towards making AI-generated vector graphics more accessible and useful for designers and developers.

## Weaknesses

While I appreciate the paper's contributions, I've identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the Structural Proximity Index (SPI), as defined in Section 2.2.1, appears overly simplistic. The SPI calculates the Euclidean distance between the centroids of consecutive SVG elements in the rendered image, using the formula: `mathbf{SPI}=1/(1+e^{-\Sigma_{i=1}^{N-1}(|P(e_{i+1})-P(e_{i})|-|i+1-i|)}}`. This approach fails to account for the hierarchical nature of SVGs, where elements can be nested within each other. As a result, the spatial distance between two elements does not necessarily reflect their structural relationship. For example, two nested paths might be spatially close, but the SPI would not capture their parent-child relationship. This limitation is acknowledged in Section 3.2.1, where the authors state, "SVG elements can overlap, repeat, and appear in various other configurations that complicate the definition of their 'position' in the rendered image." This oversimplification undermines the SPI's ability to accurately measure structural consistency. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly evident from the definition of the SPI and the paper's own acknowledgment of the issue. 

Second, the Structural Consistency Loss, defined in Section 3.2.1 as `mathcal{L}_{S C}=1/(1+e^{-\Sigma_{i=1}^{N-1}((x_{i+1}-x_{i})^{2}+(y_{i+1}-y_{i})^{2})}}`, suffers from a similar limitation. This loss function only considers the spatial distance between the positions of consecutive elements, neglecting the parent-child relationships that are fundamental to SVG structure. This means that the loss function does not penalize scenarios where nested elements are not grouped together in the code, even if they are spatially close. This is a significant oversight, as the hierarchical structure is a key aspect of readable SVG code. My confidence in this weakness is also high, as it is directly evident from the mathematical definition of the loss function and the inherent nature of SVG structure. 

Third, the Element Simplicity Score (ESS) and Element Appropriateness Loss (L_EA), while aiming to encourage the use of simpler SVG elements, may not always align with the goal of accurate shape representation. The ESS assigns lower complexity scores to basic elements like `<rect>`, `<circle>`, and `<line>`, and higher scores to complex elements like `<path>`. The Element Appropriateness Loss, defined in Section 3.2.2 as `mathcal{L}_{E A}=1/(1+e^{-\Sigma edge_map(rasterized_SVG)})`, penalizes irregular shapes, indirectly promoting simpler elements. However, the model might prioritize simplicity over accuracy, leading it to choose a circle over a path even when a path is necessary to accurately represent the desired shape. While the overall objective function includes accuracy terms, the tension between simplicity and accuracy is not fully addressed. This could lead to suboptimal results where the generated SVGs are readable but not visually faithful to the input. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as it is a logical consequence of the definitions of ESS and L_EA, but the extent of the issue would require further empirical analysis. 

Fourth, the use of total edge length as a measure of shape simplicity in the Element Appropriateness Loss is problematic. As the reviewer points out, a circle has a longer edge length than a square, and a rough square has a longer edge length than a perfect square. This suggests that edge length is not an appropriate metric for encouraging the use of simpler elements. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Section 3.2.2, stating that the loss "would also penalize any irregular shapes, even if they are necessary to accurately reproduce the input image." This indicates that the edge length metric is not robust and can lead to inaccurate representations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is based on a clear counterexample and the paper's own acknowledgment of the issue. 

Fifth, the Redundancy Quotient (RQ), as described in Section 2.2.3, is computationally expensive and not directly differentiable. The RQ measures redundancy by perturbing each SVG element and observing its impact on the rendered image. This process requires rendering the SVG multiple times, which is computationally intensive. Furthermore, this approach is not differentiable, which is a significant limitation for gradient-based optimization. While the authors propose a differentiable proxy loss in Section 3.2.3, they do not provide experimental results for this proxy, making it difficult to assess its effectiveness. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is based on the definition of RQ and the lack of experimental results for the proposed proxy. 

Finally, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed method. The authors do not provide a breakdown of the time spent on calculating each loss component, nor do they discuss the memory footprint of the additional loss functions. This makes it difficult to assess the practical overhead of the proposed approach, especially when scaling to more complex images or larger datasets. This is a significant oversight, as the computational cost is a crucial factor in the practical applicability of any method. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks any discussion of computational complexity, training time, or memory usage.

For example, the distance between two nested elements should be smaller than the distance between two non-nested elements, even if they are spatially far apart. This could be achieved by incorporating a term that penalizes deviations from the expected parent-child relationships. Furthermore, the model should be able to learn the importance of different structural relationships, rather than treating all relationships equally. This could be done by introducing learnable weights for different types of relationships, such as nesting, grouping, and ordering.
