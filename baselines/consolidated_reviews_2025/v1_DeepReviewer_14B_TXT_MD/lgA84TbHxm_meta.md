## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to self-supervised contrastive learning by proposing a dynamically scaled temperature function for the InfoNCE loss. The core idea is to adjust the temperature parameter based on the cosine similarity between sample embeddings, aiming to mitigate the adverse effects of hard negative samples during training. The authors argue that a fixed temperature, as commonly used in methods like SimCLR, can lead to suboptimal performance by either disrupting local cluster structures with overly high temperatures or failing to adequately separate distinct classes with overly low temperatures. To address this, they propose a temperature scaling function that assigns higher temperatures to samples with low cosine similarity (presumably false negatives) and lower temperatures to samples with high cosine similarity (true positives and true negatives). The specific form of the temperature function is a cosine function of the cosine similarity, chosen to satisfy four criteria derived from the analysis of the InfoNCE loss and the desired properties of the learned representations. These criteria include ensuring high temperatures for false negatives, low temperatures for true positives, and a balance between local and global structure preservation. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of the temperature function, demonstrating that it satisfies the derived criteria. Empirically, the proposed method, termed DySTreSS, is evaluated on several benchmark datasets, including ImageNet-1K and ImageNet-100, as well as on transfer learning tasks involving medical images and sentence embeddings. The results demonstrate that DySTreSS consistently outperforms the baseline SimCLR and other state-of-the-art self-supervised learning methods, particularly in linear classification accuracy. The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different temperature ranges and shifted temperature profiles, providing insights into the sensitivity of the method to its hyperparameters. Overall, this paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to improving contrastive learning through dynamic temperature scaling, contributing a valuable technique to the field of self-supervised representation learning. The core contribution lies in the novel temperature scaling function and its theoretical justification, which addresses a key limitation of existing contrastive learning methods that rely on a fixed temperature parameter. The empirical results further solidify the practical significance of this approach, demonstrating its effectiveness across various datasets and tasks.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the authors provide a theoretical analysis of the proposed temperature function, they do not provide a theoretical analysis of the convergence properties of the proposed method. The paper focuses on the design and motivation of the temperature function, demonstrating that it satisfies the derived criteria, but it lacks a formal analysis of the convergence of the training process with the dynamically scaled temperature. This is a significant omission, as convergence is a critical aspect of any optimization-based method. Without a convergence analysis, it is difficult to fully assess the stability and reliability of the proposed approach. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly lacks any such analysis. Second, the paper assumes a fixed distribution for cosine similarity, which may not be valid in practice. As shown in Figure 2, the distribution of cosine similarities for TP, FN, and TN pairs changes significantly during pre-training. The paper uses these distributions to motivate the design of the temperature function, implying an assumption about the distribution. However, the changing distribution during training suggests that this assumption may not hold, and the effectiveness of the proposed method could be affected by this. This is a valid concern, as the method's performance relies on the initial distribution of cosine similarities, and the paper does not address the implications of this changing distribution. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not address this issue and the evidence from Figure 2 is clear. Third, the paper claims that the cosine function satisfies the four criteria mentioned in Section 3.4, but it does not provide a detailed explanation of how the cosine function satisfies each criterion. The authors state that the cosine function does not violate the criteria and refer to Figure 4, but they do not provide a point-by-point explanation of how the function's properties align with each criterion. This lack of detailed explanation makes it difficult to fully understand the rationale behind the choice of the cosine function and its suitability for the task. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a detailed explanation linking the cosine function to each of the four criteria. Fourth, the proposed temperature scaling function introduces additional hyperparameters, specifically the minimum and maximum temperature values. While the paper includes ablation studies on temperature range, it does not provide a definitive guide for choosing the optimal values for these hyperparameters. The ablation studies show that the performance is sensitive to these values, but the paper does not offer a systematic approach for selecting them. This lack of guidance makes it difficult to use the proposed method in practice, as the optimal hyperparameter values may vary depending on the dataset and task. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper acknowledges the sensitivity to these parameters but does not provide a comprehensive guide for their selection. Fifth, the paper lacks a comprehensive comparison with other temperature scaling methods. While the paper compares against MACL, which is a temperature scaling method, it does not compare against a broader range of explicit temperature scaling techniques. This makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method compared to existing approaches. A more comprehensive comparison would provide a more complete picture of the method's strengths and weaknesses. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does compare against MACL, but a broader comparison is missing. Sixth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by the proposed temperature scaling function. While the authors mention that the method is computationally efficient, they do not provide a quantitative analysis of the additional computational cost. This makes it difficult to assess the practical implications of using the proposed method, particularly when scaling to larger batch sizes and embedding dimensions. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks any quantitative analysis of computational cost. Finally, the paper primarily focuses on the InfoNCE loss and does not explore the potential limitations or adaptations required when applying the proposed scaling function to other contrastive loss functions. This limits the generalizability of the proposed approach, as other contrastive losses may have different properties and requirements. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the method is presented specifically for InfoNCE loss and no discussion of other losses is present.
