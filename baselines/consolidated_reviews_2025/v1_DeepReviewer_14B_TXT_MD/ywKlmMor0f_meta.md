## Summary

This paper introduces the Multimodal Ambiguity (MMA) benchmark, a novel evaluation suite designed to assess the ability of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) to handle ambiguous queries by integrating visual context. The benchmark comprises 261 textual contexts and questions, each exhibiting lexical, syntactic, or semantic ambiguity, and paired with two images that suggest different interpretations, leading to distinct correct answers. The authors rigorously evaluate 24 MLLMs, encompassing both proprietary and open-source models, and find that these models often struggle to effectively leverage visual information to resolve textual ambiguities. Specifically, the study reveals that MLLMs tend to rely heavily on textual cues, even when visual context is available, and that they perform worst on syntactic ambiguities. The paper also highlights a significant performance gap between proprietary and open-source models, with Claude 3.5 Sonnet achieving the highest accuracy at 74.32%. The authors' analysis indicates that MLLMs often exhibit a high error consistency rate, suggesting a bias towards textual information and a failure to fully integrate visual cues. This work underscores the limitations of current MLLMs in handling ambiguous queries and provides a valuable resource for future research in this area. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of their benchmark, including the relatively small dataset size, the use of generated images, and the potential for improvements in question design. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution by introducing a new benchmark that specifically targets the challenge of visual disambiguation in MLLMs, and by providing a comprehensive evaluation of current models, highlighting areas for future improvement. The study's findings suggest that while MLLMs have made significant progress, they still struggle with the nuanced task of integrating visual and textual information to resolve ambiguity, a critical capability for real-world applications. The authors' work provides a clear direction for future research, emphasizing the need for models that can more effectively leverage visual context to disambiguate textual queries. The paper's detailed analysis and comprehensive evaluation make it a valuable resource for researchers working in the field of multimodal language modeling.

## Strengths

The primary strength of this paper lies in the introduction of the MMA benchmark, a novel and much-needed resource for evaluating MLLMs' ability to handle ambiguous queries using visual information. This is a significant contribution because, as the authors rightly point out, previous benchmarks have not specifically targeted this aspect of MLLM performance. The benchmark's design, which pairs ambiguous questions with two images suggesting different interpretations, is both creative and effective in forcing models to integrate both textual and visual data. The categorization of ambiguities into lexical, syntactic, and semantic types further enhances the benchmark's utility, allowing for a more detailed analysis of model performance across different types of ambiguity. The paper also presents a comprehensive evaluation of 24 MLLMs, including both proprietary and open-source models. This thorough evaluation provides a broad view of the current state of MLLM performance on ambiguous contexts, allowing for direct comparisons between different models. The authors' analysis of the Error Consistency Rate (ECR) is another strength, as it provides valuable insights into the models' tendency to rely on textual cues even when visual information is available. This analysis is supported by the high accuracy achieved by the models when presented with text-only inputs, which further highlights their bias towards textual information. The paper is also well-organized and easy to follow, with clear explanations of the different types of ambiguities, the experimental setup, and the results. The authors provide a detailed description of the data collection process, the image selection and generation methods, and the evaluation metrics. This level of detail makes the paper easy to understand and replicable. Furthermore, the authors acknowledge the limitations of their benchmark, including the dataset size, the use of generated images, and the potential for improvements in question design. This transparency is commendable and helps to put the results of the paper in context. The inclusion of a dedicated section on future work, outlining potential directions for improvement, further strengthens the paper's contribution to the field. The authors suggest incorporating additional modalities, languages, and multiple images per sentence, which are all valuable avenues for future research. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel benchmark, comprehensive evaluation, insightful analysis, and clear presentation.

## Weaknesses

While the paper makes a valuable contribution, several weaknesses warrant attention. Firstly, while the authors provide evidence that MLLMs struggle to integrate visual information, the analysis could be more in-depth. The paper presents the Error Consistency Rate (ECR) and text-only input performance as evidence for this claim, which is valid, but a more granular analysis of the models' internal workings, such as attention maps, could provide a more nuanced understanding of why models fail to leverage visual information effectively. The paper notes that models often choose the same incorrect answer even when presented with different visual contexts, indicating a bias towards textual information, but it does not delve into the specific reasons for this bias. This lack of deeper analysis limits our understanding of the underlying mechanisms that cause MLLMs to struggle with visual disambiguation. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's analysis, while insightful, could be more detailed. Secondly, the benchmark size is relatively small, with only 261 questions, and the distribution of ambiguity types is not balanced, with only 81 semantic ambiguity questions compared to 100 lexical and 80 syntactic. This imbalance limits the comprehensiveness of the benchmark and may not fully represent the complexity of real-world scenarios. The small number of semantic ambiguity questions, in particular, limits the ability to draw strong conclusions about model performance in this area. This is a valid concern, as the authors themselves acknowledge in the limitations section, and it impacts the generalizability of the findings. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly acknowledges the limited dataset size and the imbalance in the distribution of ambiguity types. Thirdly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the difficulty of each question. While the paper presents overall accuracy and accuracy per ambiguity type, it does not provide a difficulty score for individual questions. This makes it difficult to understand the specific challenges that each question poses to the models and limits our ability to identify which types of ambiguities are most challenging. This is a valid weakness, as the paper focuses on aggregate performance metrics rather than individual question difficulty. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include any analysis of individual question difficulty. Finally, while the paper presents the Error Consistency Rate (ECR) as a metric, it does not provide a detailed analysis of the error cases. The paper does not categorize the errors into different types, such as errors due to misinterpretation of the text, errors due to misinterpretation of the visual information, or errors due to misintegration of the text and visual information. This lack of detailed error analysis makes it difficult to understand the specific types of errors that the models are making and limits our ability to identify areas for improvement. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include a detailed qualitative error analysis.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the authors should conduct a more in-depth analysis of the failure modes of the MLLMs on the MMA benchmark. This should include examining the attention maps of the models to see which parts of the image are being attended to when making a decision, and analyzing the internal representations of the models to see how they are encoding the ambiguous text and the visual information. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the limitations of current MLLMs in handling ambiguous queries. Specifically, the authors should investigate whether the models are failing to recognize the ambiguity in the text, or if they are failing to correctly associate the visual information with the appropriate interpretation of the text. This could involve categorizing instances where the model chooses the incorrect answer due to a misinterpretation of the visual context, versus instances where the model fails to resolve the textual ambiguity even when presented with the correct visual context. Secondly, the authors should expand the benchmark to include a more balanced distribution of ambiguity types, especially for semantic ambiguity, which is currently underrepresented. The small number of semantic ambiguity questions limits the ability to draw strong conclusions about model performance in this area. A larger, more balanced dataset would provide a more robust evaluation of MLLMs' ability to handle different types of ambiguities. Furthermore, the authors should investigate the possibility of creating more fine-grained categories within each ambiguity type to capture the nuances of different types of ambiguities. For example, within syntactic ambiguity, they could differentiate between prepositional phrase attachment and coordination ambiguity, as these may pose different challenges to MLLMs. This would allow for a more detailed analysis of model performance and a better understanding of the specific types of ambiguities that are most challenging for MLLMs. Thirdly, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the difficulty of each question. This could involve assigning difficulty scores to each question based on the performance of the models and analyzing the correlation between difficulty and ambiguity type. It would also be helpful to analyze the performance of the models on different types of questions to identify the specific strengths and weaknesses of each model. For example, some models may perform better on lexical ambiguities, while others may perform better on syntactic ambiguities. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the performance of the models and would help to identify areas for improvement. The authors should also consider including more challenging questions in the benchmark to better evaluate the limits of MLLMs' ability to handle ambiguities. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the error cases. This could involve categorizing the errors into different types, such as errors due to misinterpretation of the text, errors due to misinterpretation of the visual information, and errors due to misintegration of the text and visual information. This would provide a more detailed understanding of the specific types of errors that the models are making and would help to identify areas for improvement. Additionally, the authors should consider conducting ablation studies to determine the contribution of different components of the MLLMs to performance on the benchmark. For example, they could remove the visual encoder or the text encoder to see how this affects performance. This would help to identify the specific components that are most important for handling ambiguities.

## Questions

Several questions arise from my analysis of this paper. Firstly, I am curious about the specific methods used to generate the images for the benchmark. The paper mentions that some images were generated using generative models, but it does not provide details on the specific algorithms or techniques used. I would like to know how the authors ensured that the generated images accurately represented the different interpretations of the ambiguities, and whether any measures were taken to avoid introducing biases or inaccuracies through the image generation process. Secondly, I would like to understand more about the Error Consistency Rate (ECR) metric. While the paper provides a definition of the metric, I am interested in seeing examples of cases where the models showed high ECR, as well as cases where the models showed low ECR. This would help me to better understand what the metric tells us about the models' ability to handle ambiguities. I would also like to know if the ECR was calculated across all ambiguity types or separately for each type, and if there were any notable differences in ECR across the different ambiguity types. Thirdly, I am interested in the performance gap between proprietary and open-source models. The paper notes that proprietary models generally outperform open-source models, but it does not provide a detailed analysis of the key differences between these models that may explain their different performance on the MMA benchmark. I would like to know if the authors have any insights into the architectural or training differences that may be responsible for this performance gap. It would be helpful to see a more detailed comparison of the architectures and training procedures of the different models. Finally, I would like to know more about the process of selecting the text-only questions for the benchmark. The paper mentions that the questions were designed to be ambiguous, but it does not provide details on the specific methods used to ensure the quality and accuracy of the questions. I would like to know if the authors conducted any pilot studies or expert reviews to validate the questions, and if there were any specific criteria used to ensure that the questions were indeed ambiguous and that the different interpretations were valid.
