## Summary

This paper introduces a novel framework for simulating physical systems in a continuous spatio-temporal domain, addressing the limitations of traditional numerical methods and existing data-driven approaches. The core idea revolves around formulating the simulation task as a double observation problem, employing two interconnected dynamical systems. The first system operates on sparse observations in a discrete setting, while the second system functions in a continuous domain, enabling both forecasting and interpolation of solutions. The practical implementation leverages recurrent Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to model the discrete dynamics and a spatio-temporal attention observer for the continuous dynamics. This architecture allows for the evaluation of the physical system's state at arbitrary spatial and temporal locations. The authors provide a theoretical analysis, deriving error bounds for their approach, and demonstrate its effectiveness through experiments on three standard fluid dynamics datasets. The results show that their method outperforms existing baselines, highlighting its potential for real-world applications. The paper's key contribution lies in its unique approach to combining discrete and continuous dynamical systems, enabling continuous spatio-temporal simulations from sparse data. The theoretical analysis provides a foundation for understanding the method's behavior, and the empirical results demonstrate its practical effectiveness. The authors claim that their method achieves superior performance compared to state-of-the-art methods on three standard datasets in fluid dynamics. The method's ability to handle sparse observations and generalize to new initial conditions is a significant advancement in the field of data-driven PDE simulation. The paper's approach of using a latent space for discrete dynamics and a state observer for continuous dynamics is a notable contribution to the field. The authors also provide ablation studies to justify their design choices, further strengthening the paper's claims. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and technically sound approach to continuous spatio-temporal simulation of physical systems, with promising results and potential for further development.

For example, the authors could compare their method to other latent space time-stepping methods, highlighting the specific architectural choices and theoretical justifications that lead to improved performance. This would strengthen the contribution of the paper by clearly delineating the novel aspects of their approach. Second, the experimental evaluation needs to be significantly expanded to include comparisons with the most recent state-of-the-art methods. This would help to understand the trade-offs between accuracy and computational efficiency. Third, the authors should broaden the scope of their experimental evaluation to include a wider range of PDEs beyond fluid dynamics. The current evaluation is limited to three datasets in fluid dynamics, which does not provide sufficient evidence for the generalizability of the proposed method. The authors should include results on datasets from other domains, such as solid mechanics or electromagnetism, to demonstrate the applicability of their method to different types of physical phenomena. The analysis should also consider the memory requirements of the model, particularly when dealing with large-scale simulations. Furthermore, the authors should compare the computational cost of their method with that of existing data-driven approaches and traditional numerical methods, providing a clear understanding of the trade-offs between accuracy and computational efficiency. Finally, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the scenarios where the method might fail or perform poorly. This should include a discussion of the sensitivity of the method to the choice of hyperparameters, the quality of the training data, and the complexity of the underlying physical system. It would also be beneficial to explore the limitations of the method in handling non-linear or chaotic systems, where small errors can quickly propagate and lead to inaccurate predictions.
