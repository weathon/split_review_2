## Summary

This paper introduces a cost-efficient framework for leveraging large language models (LLMs) in reasoning tasks, employing a cascade architecture that dynamically routes queries between a weaker, less expensive model and a stronger, more capable one. The core idea is to utilize the weaker model for simpler questions and escalate to the stronger model only when necessary, thereby reducing overall computational costs. The authors propose several methods for determining when to route a query to the stronger model, primarily based on the consistency of the weaker model's answers. These methods include a vote-based approach, where a threshold on the consistency score is used, and a verification-based approach, which checks if the most consistent answers from different prompts match. Furthermore, the authors introduce a mixture of thought representations, sampling answers from both chain-of-thought and program-of-thought prompts to enhance the performance of the weaker model. The empirical evaluation, conducted on six reasoning benchmark datasets, demonstrates that the proposed approach achieves comparable performance to using the stronger model alone, while significantly reducing costs, requiring only 40% of the stronger model's resources. The paper's main contribution lies in its practical approach to cost-effective LLM utilization, offering a valuable strategy for deploying these powerful models in resource-constrained environments. The authors also explore the impact of different prompting strategies and demonstrate the effectiveness of their proposed mixture of thought representations. The findings suggest that by carefully managing the use of weaker and stronger models, it is possible to achieve a balance between accuracy and cost-efficiency in LLM-based reasoning tasks. The paper's focus on practical cost reduction makes it a valuable contribution to the field, providing a concrete strategy for deploying LLMs in real-world applications.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the paper addresses a very practical and important problem: the high cost of using large language models. The proposed cascade approach, which dynamically routes queries between weaker and stronger models based on answer consistency, is both intuitive and effective. The idea of using answer consistency as a signal of question difficulty is a clever way to leverage the capabilities of weaker models while still maintaining high accuracy. The paper's exploration of different consistency measures, including sampling with different chain-of-thought and program-of-thought prompts, is also a valuable contribution. The introduction of the mixture of thought representations, which samples answers from both chain-of-thought and program-of-thought prompts, is a novel approach that enhances the performance of the weaker model. The empirical evaluation is comprehensive, with experiments conducted on six reasoning benchmark datasets. The results clearly demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves comparable performance to using only the stronger model while requiring significantly fewer resources. The paper also includes a comparison with other baselines, including using only the weaker or stronger models, and shows that the proposed cascade approach outperforms them in terms of cost and accuracy. The additional studies, which explore the use of different weaker models, further demonstrate the potential of the proposed approach. Finally, the paper is well-written and clearly explains the proposed methods and experimental results, making it accessible to a wide audience. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field by providing a practical and effective strategy for cost-efficient LLM utilization.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the paper relies heavily on the assumption that the weaker LLM is consistently incorrect on certain hard questions. This assumption, as noted by multiple reviewers, is not always true in practice. The method does not account for scenarios where the weaker LLM might produce correct answers by chance, or when the consistency metric fails to capture the nuances of question difficulty. This could lead to suboptimal routing, where the weaker LLM is used even when it should not be. For example, the paper does not analyze cases where the weaker model produces correct answers with low consistency, and the stronger model produces wrong answers with high consistency. This is a significant limitation because it undermines the core premise of the proposed approach. The paper's reliance on consistency as a perfect indicator of correctness is a potential weakness, and the experimental results, particularly the analysis of consistency scores for easy questions, suggest potential limitations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the paper's own analysis of consistency scores and the lack of discussion of inconsistent correct answers. Second, the paper's choice of GPT-3.5-turbo and GPT-4 as the weaker and stronger LLMs, respectively, introduces a significant performance gap that might not be representative of real-world scenarios where the choice of models is often constrained by availability or cost. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the proposed method to the choice of the weaker and stronger LLMs, or the possibility of using other LLMs or other types of models. The experiment with LLAMA2-13B as the weaker LLM shows mixed results, indicating that the performance gap matters. This is a crucial limitation because it makes it difficult to generalize the findings to situations where the choice of weaker LLM is more constrained. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the paper's experimental results and the lack of exploration of other LLM combinations. Third, the paper lacks a theoretical analysis of the proposed method. There is no formal justification for why the answer consistency of the weaker LLM is a good indicator of question difficulty, nor is there a proof that the proposed routing strategy minimizes the overall cost. The paper also lacks a theoretical framework for understanding how the mixture of thought representations leads to improved performance. This is a significant weakness because it limits the paper's ability to provide a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms and the optimality of the proposed approach. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the absence of any theoretical analysis in the paper. Fourth, the paper does not compare the proposed approach with other heuristics for routing questions to the stronger model. For example, the paper does not explore the possibility of routing questions based on the length of the questions or the presence of specific keywords. This is a weakness because it does not provide a baseline to understand the added complexity and benefit of the proposed consistency-based approach. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the lack of comparison with simpler heuristics in the experimental section. Finally, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the proposed method, such as the potential for error propagation or the dependence on the quality of the demonstrations. For instance, the paper does not analyze how errors in the weaker LLM's answers might propagate to the stronger LLM, or how the method would perform if the demonstrations were noisy or irrelevant. The paper also does not address the potential for the weaker LLM to be overconfident in its answers, leading to incorrect routing decisions. This is a weakness because it does not provide a complete picture of the method's practical implications. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is supported by the lack of discussion of these limitations in the paper.

Third, what is the impact of different prompt engineering techniques on the performance of both the weaker and stronger models? The paper explores different prompting strategies, but a more thorough investigation of this factor would be beneficial. Fourth, how does the proposed method handle ambiguous or complex questions that might have multiple valid answers or require multiple steps of reasoning? The paper does not explicitly address this issue, and it is important to understand the limitations of the method in these scenarios. Fifth, how does the computational overhead of the consistency checking process impact the overall cost of the proposed method? The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of this factor, and it is important to understand the trade-offs between the cost savings achieved by using the weaker LLM and the computational overhead of the consistency check. Finally, how does the proposed method compare to other methods for reducing the cost of using LLMs, such as pruning, quantization, or knowledge distillation? The paper does not explore these alternative approaches, and it would be valuable to understand the relative advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method compared to these techniques.
