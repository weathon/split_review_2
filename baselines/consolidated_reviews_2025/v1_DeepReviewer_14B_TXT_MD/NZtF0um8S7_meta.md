## Summary

This paper delves into the relatively unexplored territory of in-context learning capabilities within sequence-to-sequence (seq2seq) models, a domain predominantly dominated by decoder-only architectures. The authors undertake a comprehensive investigation, comparing the performance of seq2seq models against decoder-only models across a diverse range of tasks. A central contribution of this work lies in the introduction of two novel methods designed to enhance the in-context learning abilities of seq2seq models. The first method, termed 'objective-aligned prompting,' involves strategically placing the target input on the encoder side and incorporating mode tags and sentinel tokens, aligning the prompts with the pretraining objectives of the model. The second method, a 'fusion-based approach,' processes each few-shot example independently through the encoder and then aggregates these representations at either the encoder or decoder level. The authors' empirical findings demonstrate that, when properly configured, seq2seq models can indeed be effective few-shot learners, even surpassing larger decoder-only models in certain scenarios. This challenges the prevailing notion that decoder-only models are inherently superior for in-context learning. The paper's significance lies in its expansion of the scope of few-shot learning to include seq2seq models, providing valuable insights into their potential and offering practical techniques to improve their performance. By demonstrating that seq2seq models can be competitive with decoder-only models, the authors open up new avenues for research and application in the field of natural language processing. The paper's focus on prompt design and configuration highlights the importance of these factors in achieving optimal performance with seq2seq models, offering a valuable contribution to the growing body of knowledge on in-context learning. The authors also provide a detailed experimental setup and a thorough evaluation across a diverse set of tasks, which strengthens the validity of their findings and demonstrates the generalizability of their proposed methods. Overall, this paper makes a compelling case for the potential of seq2seq models in few-shot learning and provides a solid foundation for future research in this area.

## Weaknesses

While this paper makes several valuable contributions, there are some notable weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. One significant limitation lies in the lack of detail regarding the experimental setup, particularly concerning the selection of few-shot examples. The paper mentions that five experiments were conducted with different random seeds, implying random selection, but it does not explicitly state whether the examples were randomly chosen or if a specific strategy was employed. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to assess the validity and reproducibility of the experiments. The selection process for few-shot examples can significantly impact the results, and without a clear explanation, it is hard to determine the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, the paper lacks a detailed explanation of how the model outputs for the encoder-decoder models are transformed into predictions for the NLU tasks. While the authors mention that they select the option with the lowest cross-entropy loss, they do not specify how the cross-entropy loss is calculated for each option. It is unclear whether the input to the model includes the question, the options, and the target answer, or just the question and the options. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to understand the experimental results and reproduce them. Similarly, for generation tasks, the paper does not specify how the ROUGE scores are computed. It is unclear whether the model outputs are used directly or if any preprocessing steps are applied before calculating ROUGE. This lack of detail makes it challenging to reproduce the results and compare them with other methods. Another weakness is the absence of a zero-shot baseline for the seq2seq models in Table 2. The paper compares the performance of seq2seq models with decoder-only models in few-shot settings, but it does not include the zero-shot performance of the seq2seq models on the same SuperGLUE benchmark. This omission makes it difficult to assess the absolute improvement achieved by the few-shot prompting strategies. Including the zero-shot performance would provide a crucial reference point for evaluating the effectiveness of the proposed methods. Additionally, the paper makes a bold claim in the abstract that their approach outperforms a decoder-only model that is six times larger. However, the results in Table 2 show that the base T5-11B model performs better than OPT-66B on specific tasks such as WiC, CB, and StoryCloze. This discrepancy undermines the claim in the abstract and suggests that the proposed methods do not consistently outperform larger decoder-only models across all tasks. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the reasons behind the performance differences between decoder-only and encoder-decoder models. While the paper presents performance comparisons, it does not delve into the architectural differences that might lead to these variations. For instance, the attention mechanisms in decoder-only models allow for direct access to all tokens in the context, whereas encoder-decoder models separate the context into an encoder and decoder, potentially limiting the flow of information. A more thorough investigation into how these architectural choices impact the models' ability to learn in-context would be valuable. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison of the proposed methods with existing approaches for in-context learning. It would be helpful to compare the proposed methods with other techniques, such as prompt tuning or meta-learning, to better understand their strengths and weaknesses. The paper should include a more detailed discussion of how the proposed methods relate to and differ from existing techniques. For example, how does objective-aligned prompting compare to other methods of prompt engineering, and what are the specific advantages of the fusion-based approach over other methods of combining information from multiple examples? The paper also lacks a sensitivity analysis of the proposed methods to different hyperparameter settings. It would be beneficial to conduct a sensitivity analysis to understand how the performance of the proposed methods varies with different hyperparameter settings, such as the number of layers, the hidden size, and the attention heads. This analysis should also consider the computational cost associated with different hyperparameter settings. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed methods. It would be helpful to compare the computational cost of the proposed methods with existing approaches to better understand their practicality. The paper should include a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed methods, including the training time, inference time, and memory requirements. This analysis should also consider the scalability of the proposed methods to larger datasets and models. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight the need for further investigation and refinement of the proposed methods.
