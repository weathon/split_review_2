## Summary

This paper investigates the potential of using monocular depth estimation as a pre-training task to enhance the performance of semantic segmentation models. The central hypothesis is that pre-training on depth prediction can provide a geometric understanding of scenes, which can then be leveraged to improve semantic understanding. To test this hypothesis, the authors conduct a series of experiments using various datasets and architectures, comparing depth pre-training to traditional methods like ImageNet classification. The core methodology involves pre-training a network on monocular depth estimation, then fine-tuning it for semantic segmentation. The depth pre-training is performed using the Monodepth2 architecture, and the semantic segmentation is performed using various encoders such as ResNet18, ResNet50, DeepLabV3, and ViT-L. The authors explore the impact of different factors, such as dataset size, resolution, and architecture, on the effectiveness of depth pre-training. They also compare depth pre-training to other pre-training methods, including ImageNet classification and contrastive learning approaches like DINO and MOCO V2. The main empirical findings indicate that depth pre-training can indeed improve semantic segmentation performance, particularly in scenarios with limited labeled data. The authors also find that pre-training on depth is more effective than ImageNet pre-training for semantic segmentation. They also show that the learned representations from depth pre-training align well with semantic boundaries, as visualized through Grad-CAM. The paper also explores the robustness of depth pre-training to object scale and out-of-domain transfer. The authors find that pre-training on higher resolution images is beneficial and that depth pre-training can transfer well to other datasets. Overall, this paper presents a compelling case for the use of monocular depth estimation as a pre-training task for semantic segmentation, offering a potential alternative to traditional ImageNet pre-training. The findings suggest that geometric understanding gained from depth pre-training can be effectively transferred to semantic tasks, which could be particularly beneficial in domains where labeled data is scarce. The paper's exploration of various factors influencing transferability and comparisons with other pre-training methods provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of depth pre-training. The results also highlight the potential of using depth pre-training to reduce the reliance on large, human-annotated datasets for pre-training, which could be particularly beneficial for specialized domains where labeled data is scarce.
