## Summary

This paper introduces Longitudinal Ensemble Integration (LEI), a novel framework designed for sequential classification using multimodal longitudinal data. The core idea behind LEI is to extend the capabilities of existing Ensemble Integration (EI) methods by incorporating Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to handle temporal dependencies. The authors propose that by first applying base predictors to individual data modalities at each time point, and then integrating these intermediate predictions using an LSTM stacker, they can effectively capture both modality-specific and temporal information. The base predictors are trained to predict the diagnosis at each time point, and their outputs are then used as input for the LSTM stacker, which learns to aggregate these predictions over time. The authors evaluate LEI's performance on the early detection of dementia using the TADPOLE challenge dataset, derived from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). They compare LEI against several baselines, including multi-layered LSTMs with time-distributed and longitudinal classifiers, and the PPAD method. The empirical results suggest that LEI outperforms these baselines, demonstrating the potential of integrating intermediate base predictions from individual data modalities. The authors also highlight that the LEI framework enables the identification of important features for dementia-related diagnoses. The paper's contribution lies in its proposed framework for handling longitudinal multimodal data, which they argue is an extension of existing EI methods. However, the paper's lack of detailed methodological explanations, limited experimental comparisons, and absence of theoretical analysis raise significant concerns about the robustness and generalizability of the proposed approach. The paper's findings, while promising, require further validation and analysis to fully establish the effectiveness and novelty of the LEI framework.

## Strengths

The paper's primary strength lies in its attempt to address the challenging problem of sequential classification using multimodal longitudinal data, a task with significant implications in healthcare and other domains. The proposed LEI framework, which combines ensemble integration with LSTM networks, presents a potentially useful approach for handling complex temporal and multimodal data. The idea of using intermediate base predictions from individual modalities, rather than directly integrating raw data, is a conceptually interesting approach that could offer advantages in terms of interpretability and modularity. The authors' focus on the early detection of dementia using the TADPOLE dataset is also a relevant and important application area. The empirical results, while limited to a single dataset, do suggest that LEI can outperform some baseline methods, indicating the potential of the proposed framework. The authors also highlight the feature importance analysis enabled by LEI, which could be valuable for understanding the underlying factors contributing to dementia progression. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, which facilitates understanding of the proposed method and its application. The authors' attempt to extend existing ensemble integration methods to handle longitudinal data is a logical and potentially valuable contribution. The use of LSTMs to model temporal dependencies is also a well-established and appropriate technique for this type of data. The paper's focus on a real-world problem and its attempt to provide a practical solution are also positive aspects. The authors' claim that LEI enables the identification of important features for dementia-related diagnoses is also a potentially valuable contribution, as it could lead to new insights into the disease progression. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach that warrants further investigation and development.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I've identified several significant weaknesses that undermine the validity and generalizability of the proposed LEI framework. Firstly, the paper suffers from a severe lack of methodological detail, particularly concerning the implementation of the base predictors and the LSTM stacker. The authors mention using a library of base predictor models, including KNN, Logistic Regression, SVM, Random Forest, and XGBoost, but they fail to provide specific details about the algorithms used, their hyperparameter settings, or the training procedures. This lack of information makes it impossible to reproduce the results and hinders a deeper understanding of the method's inner workings. For example, the paper states that base predictors are trained to predict the diagnosis at each time point, but it does not explain how these predictions are generated or how they are used by the LSTM stacker. The description of the LSTM stacker is also vague, lacking details on the number of layers, hidden size, activation functions, or optimizer used. This lack of clarity is a major obstacle to understanding and evaluating the proposed method. The paper also suffers from a limited experimental evaluation. The authors only use the TADPOLE dataset, which raises concerns about the generalizability of the findings. The TADPOLE dataset, while comprehensive, may have specific biases or characteristics that could lead to overfitting or an overestimation of the model's performance. The absence of evaluation on other datasets limits the confidence in the robustness of the LEI framework. Furthermore, the paper's comparison with baselines is not comprehensive. The authors compare LEI against two multi-layered LSTMs and the PPAD method, but they fail to include other relevant methods for processing longitudinal and multimodal data, such as GRU, temporal convolutional networks (TCNs), and attention-based mechanisms. This limited comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed LEI framework. The paper also lacks a thorough analysis of the proposed method. There is no ablation study to understand the contribution of different components of LEI, no discussion of the computational complexity, and no exploration of the sensitivity of the method to hyperparameter settings. The absence of these analyses makes it difficult to assess the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed framework. For example, it is unclear how the performance of LEI would change with different choices of base predictors or LSTM architectures, and how the method scales with increasing data size. The paper also lacks a theoretical analysis of the proposed framework, such as convergence or generalization bounds. This absence of theoretical grounding makes it difficult to understand the underlying principles of the method and its limitations. The paper's discussion of related work is also inadequate. The authors do not compare their method to other methods for longitudinal multimodal data in sufficient detail. The paper mentions several existing approaches in the introduction, but it does not provide a thorough comparison of how LEI's approach to handling multimodal data differs from methods using attention mechanisms or other fusion techniques. This lack of detailed comparison makes it harder to understand LEI's unique contributions in the context of existing multimodal methods. Finally, the paper's claim of providing code for the method is not fully supported by the reviewer's experience, which suggests that the code might not be readily available. This lack of publicly available code hinders the reproducibility of the results and makes it difficult for other researchers to build upon this work. These weaknesses, taken together, significantly undermine the paper's contribution and the confidence in its findings. The lack of methodological detail, limited experimental evaluation, inadequate comparison with baselines, and absence of theoretical analysis all point to a need for substantial improvements before the paper can be considered a significant contribution to the field. I have high confidence in these identified weaknesses as they are directly supported by the paper's content and the lack of specific details in the method and experimental sections.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete and actionable improvements. First and foremost, the authors must provide a more detailed description of the LEI framework, including the specific implementation details of the base predictors and the LSTM stacker. This should include the specific algorithms used for each base predictor, their hyperparameter settings, and the training procedures. The authors should also clarify the choice of the LSTM stacker and its configuration, including the number of layers, the hidden size, and the activation functions. Furthermore, the authors should provide a detailed explanation of how the intermediate base predictions are integrated and how the final prediction is made. This could involve providing pseudocode or a detailed algorithm description. This level of detail is crucial for reproducibility and for a deeper understanding of the method's inner workings. Second, the authors should significantly expand the experimental evaluation of the LEI framework. This should include evaluating LEI on additional datasets that exhibit different characteristics, such as varying sample sizes, modalities, and temporal resolutions. For example, datasets from other biomedical domains, such as electronic health records or wearable sensor data, could be used to assess the generalizability of the proposed method. Furthermore, the authors should perform a more rigorous analysis of the TADPOLE dataset itself, investigating potential biases and limitations that could affect the results. This could involve examining the distribution of data across different time points and patient groups, as well as assessing the impact of missing data patterns. Such analysis would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's performance and its limitations. Third, the authors should include a more comprehensive comparison with state-of-the-art methods for longitudinal and multimodal data processing. Specifically, they should compare the LEI framework with methods such as GRU, temporal convolutional networks (TCNs), and attention-based mechanisms. These methods represent different approaches to modeling temporal dependencies and could provide valuable insights into the relative performance of the proposed framework. Moreover, the authors should consider comparing against methods that explicitly handle multimodal data, such as those based on tensor factorization or graph neural networks. The comparison should not only focus on overall performance metrics but also on computational efficiency, parameter sensitivity, and interpretability. A detailed analysis of these aspects would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the LEI framework. Fourth, the authors should conduct a thorough ablation study to understand the contribution of each component of LEI. This should include experiments where different base predictors are used, and the impact of varying the LSTM architecture is assessed. For example, the authors could compare the performance of LEI with different types of base predictors, such as decision trees, support vector machines, or simple neural networks, and analyze how the choice of base predictor affects the overall performance. Furthermore, the impact of the number of LSTM layers and the size of the hidden states should be investigated to understand the sensitivity of the method to these hyperparameters. This analysis would provide valuable insights into the design choices and help to identify the optimal configuration of LEI for different datasets and tasks. Fifth, the authors should include a detailed discussion of the computational complexity of LEI. This should include an analysis of the time and memory requirements of the method, as well as a comparison with the computational cost of the baseline methods. The authors should also explore the scalability of LEI to larger datasets and longer time series. This analysis would help to assess the practical applicability of the method and identify potential bottlenecks. For example, the authors could provide a breakdown of the computational cost of each step in the LEI pipeline, such as the training of base predictors, the computation of intermediate predictions, and the training of the LSTM. This would allow readers to understand the computational trade-offs of using LEI and make informed decisions about its use. Sixth, the authors should provide a theoretical analysis of the proposed framework, including convergence and generalization bounds. This would provide a deeper understanding of the method's properties and limitations. Finally, the authors should ensure that the code for the method is publicly available and well-documented to ensure reproducibility and to allow other researchers to build upon this work. These suggestions, if implemented, would significantly improve the quality and impact of the paper.
