## Summary

This paper introduces a novel diffusion-based generative model designed to perform both music synthesis and source separation within a single framework. The core idea is to model the joint distribution of individual music sources, enabling the model to generate new mixtures, separate sources from a given mixture, and even impute missing sources. The proposed method, termed MSDM (Multi-Source Diffusion Model), leverages a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) architecture and is trained on the Slakh2100 dataset, a large collection of synthetically mixed multi-track music. The model's training involves learning the score function of the joint source distribution, which is then used for both generation and separation tasks. For source separation, the authors introduce a novel inference method based on Dirac likelihood functions, which they claim improves performance. The empirical evaluation includes experiments on total music generation, partial generation (imputation), and source separation. The results show that the proposed method achieves competitive performance in source separation, comparable to existing models like Demucs, and demonstrates the ability to perform partial generation. However, the paper's primary focus is on demonstrating the unified framework for generation and separation, rather than achieving state-of-the-art performance in either task individually. The authors argue that their approach represents a step towards general audio models by unifying these two traditionally separate tasks within a single model. While the paper presents a compelling idea and demonstrates its feasibility, the experimental evaluation and analysis have some limitations that need to be addressed to fully validate the proposed approach. The paper's contribution lies in its novel approach to jointly modeling music generation and source separation, but the empirical results and analysis require further refinement to fully support the claims made.

## Strengths

I find the core idea of using a single diffusion-based model to handle both music generation and source separation to be a significant strength of this paper. The concept of modeling the joint distribution of sources is both innovative and theoretically sound, providing a unified framework for tasks that are often addressed separately. The proposed method, MSDM, demonstrates the feasibility of this approach, showcasing its ability to perform total generation, partial generation (imputation), and source separation. The introduction of a Dirac likelihood function for source separation is another notable contribution, which, according to the authors, enhances the model's performance. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the proposed method accessible to a broad audience. The authors provide a clear explanation of the methodology, including the mathematical formulation and the implementation details. The experiments, while not without limitations, provide a reasonable initial evaluation of the model's capabilities. The qualitative results, although not extensively analyzed in the paper itself, offer some support for the model's ability to generate coherent music and separate sources effectively. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of their work and suggest potential avenues for future research, which I appreciate. The paper's focus on bridging the gap between music generation and source separation is a valuable contribution to the field, and the proposed method offers a promising direction for future research in this area. The fact that the model can perform all these tasks within a single framework is a significant advantage, potentially leading to more efficient and versatile audio processing systems. The paper's contribution is not in achieving state-of-the-art performance in either generation or separation, but in demonstrating the feasibility of a unified approach, which I believe is a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I've identified several key weaknesses that significantly impact my assessment of its contributions. First, the paper's evaluation of music generation is not as comprehensive as it could be. While the authors present experiments on total and partial generation, these experiments lack a direct comparison to state-of-the-art music generation models. The paper compares against a 'Mixture Model' with the same architecture, but this comparison does not address the performance of the proposed method relative to models like Singsong, MusicLM, or AudioLDM, which are specifically designed for music generation. This omission makes it difficult to assess the true novelty and performance of the proposed method in the context of the rapidly evolving field of music generation. The experimental section dedicated to music generation is also less extensive than the one for source separation, further suggesting that the primary focus of the paper is not on achieving state-of-the-art results in music generation. This lack of comprehensive evaluation in the music generation domain is a significant limitation, as it leaves the reader uncertain about the model's capabilities in this area. Second, the paper's source separation performance, while competitive, does not surpass the baseline Demucs model. Table 3 clearly shows that Demucs + Gibbs achieves a higher SI-SDR_I score (17.73) than the proposed MSDM Dirac (correction) (16.48). Even the standard Demucs model achieves a higher score (16.11) than the best performing MSDM variant. This underperformance in source separation, compared to a well-established baseline, raises concerns about the practical utility of the proposed method for this task. The claim in the abstract that the proposed method is a 'step towards general audio models' seems overstated given that it does not outperform existing methods in source separation. The paper's focus on unifying generation and separation does not justify the claim of being a step towards general audio models if it does not achieve competitive performance in both tasks. Third, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method. The 'Experiments' section does not include any information about training time, inference time, FLOPs, or memory usage. This omission is particularly problematic given that diffusion models are known to be computationally expensive. Without this information, it is difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the proposed method and compare it to other approaches. The lack of computational cost analysis makes it hard to determine whether the proposed method offers a good trade-off between performance and computational resources. Fourth, the paper's evaluation is limited to the Slakh2100 dataset, which is a synthetic dataset. While Slakh2100 is a valuable resource for source separation research, it does not fully capture the complexities of real-world recordings, which include reverberation, microphone characteristics, and other artifacts. The lack of evaluation on real-world datasets like MUSDB18 raises concerns about the generalizability of the proposed method to more realistic scenarios. The paper's claim of being a step towards general audio models is further weakened by the lack of evaluation on diverse datasets. Fifth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the qualitative results. While the authors mention qualitative results and provide a link to listen to examples, the paper itself lacks a detailed in-text analysis of the generated music and separated sources, including specific examples of successes and failures. This lack of qualitative analysis makes it difficult to fully understand the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method. Finally, the paper does not explore the impact of different architectural choices on the performance of the model. The 'Method' section describes the architecture but does not include experiments varying architectural choices such as the number of layers or the type of activation functions. This lack of ablation study makes it difficult to understand the importance of different design choices and how they affect the model's performance. These weaknesses, taken together, significantly limit the impact of the paper and raise questions about the practical utility and generalizability of the proposed method.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly expand the evaluation of their music generation capabilities. This should include a direct comparison to state-of-the-art music generation models, such as Singsong, MusicLM, and AudioLDM. The comparison should not only include quantitative metrics but also qualitative analysis, such as listening tests, to assess the musicality and coherence of the generated samples. The authors should also explore different evaluation metrics that are more specific to music generation, such as metrics that measure the musical structure or the emotional content of the generated music. Second, the authors should investigate methods to improve the source separation performance of their model. This could involve exploring different architectures for the score network, such as incorporating attention mechanisms or using more efficient convolutional layers. They could also investigate different training strategies, such as using a larger batch size or employing data augmentation techniques. A more detailed analysis of the failure cases of the model would also be beneficial, helping to identify the limitations of the method and guide future research. Third, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost of their method. This should include the training time, inference time, FLOPs, and memory usage. This information is crucial for assessing the practical feasibility of the method and comparing it to other approaches. The authors should also compare the computational cost of their method to that of other state-of-the-art methods, such as Demucs. Fourth, the authors should evaluate their method on more diverse datasets, including real-world recordings with varying degrees of reverberation and noise. Datasets such as MUSDB18, which contains studio-recorded music, or datasets from the DCASE challenges, which include environmental sounds and speech, would be appropriate. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's generalizability and robustness. Fifth, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the qualitative results. This should include a more in-depth analysis of the generated music and separated sources, including specific examples of both successful and unsuccessful cases. This analysis should include a discussion of the musical characteristics of the generated samples, such as the melody, harmony, and rhythm, as well as the quality of the separated sources. A visual analysis of the separated sources, such as spectrograms, would also be beneficial. Finally, the authors should explore the impact of different architectural choices on the performance of the model. This could include varying the number of layers, the size of the hidden layers, and the type of activation functions used. This analysis would provide valuable insights into the design choices that are most important for achieving good performance. By addressing these limitations, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and provide a more compelling argument for the proposed method.
