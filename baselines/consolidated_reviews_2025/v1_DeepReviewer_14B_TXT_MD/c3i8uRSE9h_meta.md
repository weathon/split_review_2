## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach for solving inverse problems using pre-trained diffusion models, focusing on a fast, training-free method for constrained sampling. The core idea revolves around approximating the gradient of the denoising process using a numerical method, which significantly reduces the computational cost compared to backpropagation-based approaches. Specifically, the authors propose to leverage the Jacobian of the denoising function with respect to the noisy data, approximating it through a numerical derivative. This allows for efficient gradient descent in the noisy latent space, facilitating the solution of inverse problems such as inpainting and super-resolution. The method is presented as a way to leverage the power of pre-trained diffusion models without requiring additional training or fine-tuning. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach on ImageNet for inpainting and super-resolution tasks, comparing their results against several existing methods. The key empirical finding is that the proposed method achieves competitive results, often outperforming other training-free methods in inpainting, while being significantly faster. However, in super-resolution, the method's performance is more mixed, achieving comparable but not consistently superior results. The paper also introduces a new task called 'layer inference,' which demonstrates the method's ability to decompose an image into different layers based on text prompts. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach for fast, training-free inverse solving with diffusion models, highlighting the potential of numerical approximations for efficient gradient estimation. However, the paper also has some limitations, including a limited scope of experimental evaluation and a lack of theoretical analysis, which I will discuss in detail below.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be quite compelling. The most significant strength is the proposed method's simplicity and efficiency. By using a numerical approximation of the Jacobian, the authors have developed a way to perform gradient descent in the latent space of diffusion models without relying on backpropagation. This is a notable technical innovation that significantly reduces the computational cost of solving inverse problems. The method is also training-free, which means it can be readily applied to any pre-trained diffusion model without the need for additional fine-tuning. This is a significant advantage in terms of practical applicability. The empirical results, particularly for inpainting, are also quite impressive. The proposed method achieves competitive results, often outperforming other training-free methods in terms of FID score, while being significantly faster. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the numerical approximation approach. The introduction of the 'layer inference' task is another strength, showcasing the method's versatility and potential for novel applications. This task, while not rigorously quantified, demonstrates the method's ability to decompose an image into different layers based on text prompts, which is a unique and potentially valuable capability. The paper is also well-written and easy to follow, making the method accessible to a wider audience. The authors clearly explain the core idea and the implementation details, which is crucial for reproducibility. Overall, the paper presents a novel and efficient approach for solving inverse problems with diffusion models, with promising empirical results and potential for further development.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that I believe significantly impact its overall contribution. First, the experimental evaluation is quite limited in scope. The authors primarily focus on ImageNet for inpainting and super-resolution tasks, which, while a standard benchmark, does not provide a comprehensive assessment of the method's generalizability. As Reviewer 1 correctly pointed out, it would be beneficial to see how the method performs on other types of datasets, such as faces (CelebA) or text-rich images (TextImageNet), and on other inverse problems, such as colorization or image editing. The lack of such evaluations makes it difficult to assess the robustness and applicability of the proposed method across different domains. The 'layer inference' task, while interesting, is not rigorously evaluated with quantitative metrics, which further limits the scope of the experimental analysis. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper explicitly states the use of ImageNet for the primary quantitative evaluations (Section 4.1), and the lack of quantitative results for other datasets or inverse problems is evident. Second, the qualitative results for super-resolution reveal some noticeable artifacts. As noted by Reviewer 1, the super-resolution results in Figure 5 (Figure 6 in the provided text) exhibit some blurriness and lack fine details compared to the ground truth. This is further supported by the quantitative results in Table 1 (Figure 6 in the provided text), where the proposed method's FID score for super-resolution is higher (worse) than other methods, indicating a lower image quality. This suggests that the proposed method may not be as effective for super-resolution as it is for inpainting. This is a medium-confidence concern, as the artifacts are visually apparent, and the quantitative results support this observation. Third, the quantitative results for super-resolution also show that the proposed method does not consistently outperform prior works. As Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 2 both noted, the PSNR achieved by the proposed method is slightly lower than that of P2L and LDPS, and the FID score is also higher. This indicates that the proposed method is not state-of-the-art in super-resolution. This is a high-confidence concern, as the numerical results in Table 1 directly support this claim. Fourth, the paper lacks a proper ablation study. As Reviewer 1 correctly pointed out, the authors do not analyze the impact of different design choices on the performance of the method. For example, it would be interesting to see how the performance changes with different numerical approximation schemes (e.g., forward or central differences) or with different numbers of function evaluations. The absence of such an analysis makes it difficult to understand the sensitivity of the method to different parameters and to identify the optimal settings. This is a high-confidence concern, as the 'Experiments' section (Section 4) does not include any ablation studies. Fifth, the paper lacks a theoretical analysis of the proposed method. As Reviewer 3 correctly pointed out, the authors do not analyze the convergence properties of the method, nor do they provide any theoretical guarantees on the quality of the solutions. This lack of theoretical grounding makes it difficult to understand the fundamental limitations and potential of the proposed method. Without a convergence analysis, it is unclear under what conditions the method is guaranteed to produce a satisfactory solution, and how the solution quality is affected by the choice of hyperparameters. This is a high-confidence concern, as the paper primarily focuses on the algorithmic description and empirical results, lacking a theoretical analysis section. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed explanation of how to choose the hyperparameters of the proposed method. As Reviewer 3 noted, the paper mentions specific hyperparameters like step size and learning rate in the algorithm description and experiment setup but doesn't provide a detailed explanation of how these were chosen or how they should be tuned. This lack of guidance makes it difficult to reproduce the results and to apply the method to new tasks and datasets. This is a medium-confidence concern, as while the paper does mention the hyperparameters, it lacks a detailed explanation of the selection process.

## Suggestions

Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for this paper. First, the authors should significantly expand the scope of their experimental evaluation. This should include testing the method on a wider range of datasets, such as CelebA for face super-resolution and TextImageNet for text-rich image restoration. They should also explore other inverse problems beyond inpainting and super-resolution, such as colorization, image editing, and deblurring. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the method's generalizability and robustness. Second, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the super-resolution results. This should include a more thorough investigation of the causes of the observed artifacts and a comparison with other state-of-the-art methods that achieve higher PSNR and FID scores. They should also explore different optimization strategies that could potentially improve the quality of the super-resolution results. Third, the authors should conduct a thorough ablation study to analyze the impact of different design choices on the performance of the method. This should include a sensitivity analysis of the numerical approximation scheme, the number of function evaluations, the step size, and the number of iterations. They should also investigate the trade-offs between computational cost and performance. This would provide a better understanding of the method's sensitivity to different parameters and help to identify the optimal settings for different tasks. Fourth, the authors should include a theoretical analysis of the proposed method. This should include an analysis of the convergence properties of the method, as well as an investigation of its limitations and potential. This would provide a deeper understanding of the method's behavior and its relationship to other existing approaches. Fifth, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how to choose the hyperparameters of the proposed method. This should include clear guidelines on how to tune these parameters for different tasks and datasets. This would make the method more accessible to other researchers and facilitate its practical use. Finally, the authors should consider comparing their method with DDIM and DDNM, as suggested by Reviewer 3. While DDIM is used as the underlying sampling method, it should also be included as a baseline for comparison in the inverse problem solving context. The lack of comparison with DDNM, which directly addresses the inverse problem using a different optimization strategy, also needs to be addressed. These comparisons would provide a more complete picture of the method's performance relative to the current state of the art.

## Questions

I have several questions that I believe are crucial for a deeper understanding of the proposed method. First, how does the proposed method compare to other state-of-the-art methods, such as DDIM and DDNM, when applied to the same inverse problems? While the paper compares against P2L, LDPS, and PSLD, the absence of comparisons with DDIM and DDNM is a significant oversight, as these are relevant baselines for solving inverse problems with pre-trained diffusion models. Second, how do the hyperparameters, such as the step size, the number of iterations, and the number of function evaluations, affect the performance of the proposed method? The paper lacks a detailed explanation of how these hyperparameters should be chosen, and an ablation study is needed to understand their impact on the method's performance. Third, what are the convergence properties of the proposed method? The paper lacks a theoretical analysis of the method, and it is unclear under what conditions the method is guaranteed to produce a satisfactory solution. A convergence analysis would provide a deeper understanding of the method's behavior and its limitations. Fourth, how does the proposed method's performance scale with different noise schedules? The paper uses a specific pre-trained Stable Diffusion 1.5 model and doesn't explore different noise schedules. Given that diffusion models are sensitive to the noise schedule, it is important to investigate how the proposed method performs with different noise schedules. Finally, what are the limitations of the proposed method, and under what conditions is it likely to fail? The paper does not discuss the potential limitations of the method, and it is important to understand its failure modes and the scenarios where it may not be applicable. Addressing these questions would significantly enhance the paper's contribution and provide a more complete understanding of the proposed method.
