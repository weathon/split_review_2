## Summary

This paper introduces MultiIoT, a large-scale benchmark dataset designed to facilitate research in multimodal learning for the Internet of Things (IoT). The authors present a collection of over 1.15 million samples spanning 12 different sensor modalities and 8 distinct tasks. These modalities include, but are not limited to, IMU data, camera images, depth maps, and LiDAR point clouds, while the tasks encompass activities such as activity recognition, gesture classification, and object detection. The authors' primary aim is to address the challenges inherent in processing diverse sensory data, capturing long-range temporal dependencies, and managing the heterogeneity characteristic of real-world IoT sensor data. To establish a foundation for future research, the paper provides a set of baseline results using a variety of existing multimodal learning techniques, ranging from modality-specific models to multisensory and multitask architectures. These baselines include CNNs for image data, RNNs for time-series data, and adapter-based models. The experimental results demonstrate the potential of multimodal and multitask learning approaches for IoT applications. The authors also explore the impact of modality combinations and task interactions on model performance. The paper emphasizes the importance of this benchmark for advancing research in IoT data processing and multimodal machine learning. The authors plan to release the MultiIoT benchmark to the research community, along with the baseline code. The paper's contribution lies in the creation of a comprehensive dataset and the establishment of initial baseline results, which serve as a starting point for further exploration of multimodal learning in the IoT domain. However, the paper also acknowledges the need for further research to address the challenges posed by the complexity and heterogeneity of IoT data. The authors highlight the potential of this benchmark to drive innovation in areas such as smart homes, healthcare, and industrial automation. The paper's overall significance is in providing a valuable resource for the research community and in identifying key challenges that need to be addressed to fully leverage the potential of multimodal learning in IoT applications.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, several weaknesses significantly impact its overall contribution. Firstly, the benchmark is essentially a collection of existing datasets, as explicitly stated in section 2.2, where the authors list the datasets used for each modality, such as "2,940 IMU gaze samples (Kong et al., 2021)" and "41,000 instances from KITTI (Geiger et al., 2013)." This approach, while practical, raises concerns about the novelty and the specific relevance of the benchmark to the IoT domain. The datasets were not originally collected with the intention of being used for multimodal learning, and the connection to IoT is not always clear. The tasks defined, while relevant, seem somewhat arbitrary and lack a deeper explanation of their interconnectedness or the specific challenges they pose in the IoT context. This is evident in section 2.3, where the tasks are described individually without a clear articulation of their significance within the IoT domain beyond general descriptions. This lack of a cohesive narrative around the IoT theme and the tasks undermines the benchmark's potential impact. Secondly, the paper lacks sufficient detail regarding the machine learning techniques used in the modeling baselines. While the authors mention using CNNs for image data and RNNs for time-series data in section 4.1, they fail to provide specific architectural details, such as the exact CNN architecture (e.g., ResNet, VGG), the number of layers, filter sizes, or activation functions. Similarly, for time-series data, it is unclear whether RNNs, LSTMs, or Transformers were used, and what their specific configurations were. This lack of detail, as noted in the analysis of Reviewer 2, makes it difficult to assess the validity of the baselines and to build upon them. The absence of specific hyperparameter settings, such as learning rates, batch sizes, and optimization algorithms, further hinders reproducibility. This lack of transparency in the modeling baselines is a significant limitation. Thirdly, the paper does not provide sufficient details about the data collection process, the data format, and the tools and resources available for researchers. While section 2.2 mentions the datasets used and some of the hardware, it lacks a comprehensive overview of the data collection process for the entire MULTIIOT benchmark. The paper does not specify the sampling rates of different sensors, the synchronization method used across modalities, or the specific hardware used for data acquisition. Furthermore, the paper does not specify the format of the released data (e.g., CSV, HDF5), the structure of the files, or the naming conventions used. This lack of information, as highlighted by Reviewer 2, makes it difficult for researchers to effectively use the benchmark. The absence of a README file with clear instructions on how to download, preprocess, and use the data further exacerbates this issue. Fourthly, the paper could benefit from a more detailed exploration of the potential applications of MultiIoT. While the paper mentions applications like smart homes and healthcare, it does not provide specific scenarios or use cases that demonstrate how the benchmark can be used to solve practical problems. For example, it would be beneficial to show how MultiIoT can be used to develop a system for activity recognition in smart homes or for patient monitoring in healthcare settings. The lack of concrete examples, as noted by Reviewer 2, limits the paper's ability to highlight the significance and impact of the benchmark. Finally, the paper suffers from issues with writing quality and figure clarity. As noted by Reviewer 1, the language is redundant, and the figures are of low quality. Figure 1, in particular, is blurry and difficult to read. The descriptions of the datasets and tasks are not clear and concise, making it hard to understand the specifics of the benchmark. This lack of clarity hinders the paper's overall readability and impact. These weaknesses, all of which are supported by direct evidence from the paper and the reviewers' analyses, significantly limit the paper's contribution and its potential to drive further research in the field.
