## Summary

This paper introduces Real3D, a novel self-training framework designed to train single-view large reconstruction models (LRMs) using real-world images. The core contribution of Real3D lies in its ability to leverage both synthetic and real-world data, addressing the limitations of existing methods that primarily rely on synthetic or multi-view datasets. The framework employs two unsupervised losses: a cycle consistency rendering loss at the pixel level and a semantic loss at the image level, which enable the model to learn from single-view images without ground-truth 3D data. Additionally, the authors propose an automatic data curation method to collect high-quality examples from in-the-wild images, which is crucial for scaling up the training data. The empirical findings demonstrate that Real3D consistently outperforms prior work in various evaluation settings, including both real and synthetic data, showcasing its effectiveness in leveraging real-world data for improved 3D reconstruction. The significance of this work lies in its potential to advance the field of 3D reconstruction by enabling the training of robust models using readily available real-world images, thus bridging the gap between synthetic and real-world performance.

The proposed unsupervised losses, specifically the cycle consistency rendering loss and the semantic loss, are innovative and effective in supervising the model without ground-truth 3D data. These losses provide a mechanism for the model to learn from single-view images, which is a challenging task. The experiments conducted in this paper are extensive and well-designed. The results demonstrate that Real3D outperforms prior work in various evaluation settings, which is a strong indicator of the method's effectiveness. The paper is also well-written, with clear explanations of concepts and methodologies, making it easy for readers to follow the paper's logic. ## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, the paper lacks sufficient detail regarding the data curation approach. While the authors mention the goal of selecting unoccluded instances and the use of instance segmentation and depth estimation, they do not provide specifics on the instance segmentation model used, its training, performance, or how segmentation errors are handled. This lack of detail is concerning because the quality of the segmented instances directly impacts the training of the LRM. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss potential biases introduced by selecting unoccluded instances, which could affect the model's generalization capability. For example, the paper states, 'Specifically, we find it is important to train the model with un-occluded instances. Thus, we develop an automatic occlusion detection method leveraging the synergy between instance segmentation and single-view depth estimation.' (Section 3.2, paragraph 1), but it does not elaborate on the specific criteria or thresholds used to filter out undesirable data points. This omission makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the data curation process. My confidence in this issue is high, as the lack of detail is evident throughout the data curation section (Section 3.2). Secondly, the paper could provide more insights into the choice of the specific unsupervised losses used for training LRMs. While the authors explain the motivation and formulation of the cycle consistency and semantic losses, they do not discuss the limitations of these losses or explore other potential unsupervised losses. For instance, the cycle consistency loss might not be sufficient to enforce geometric accuracy, and the semantic loss might not capture fine-grained details. The paper briefly mentions a limitation of the semantic loss: 'A simple strategy for applying the loss is rendering multiple novel views of a reconstruction and calculating the loss on all of them. However, this leads to the multi-head problem, a trivial solution to minimize the loss (see Fig.~\ref{fig: ablation}). The reason is that CLIP is not fully invariant to the camera viewpoint.' (Section 3.1, Semantic-level Guidance), but it does not delve into the limitations of the cycle consistency loss or explore other unsupervised loss functions like adversarial or perceptual losses. This lack of discussion limits the understanding of the chosen losses' effectiveness and potential alternatives. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper does not address these limitations in Section 3.1. Thirdly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the scalability of the proposed method. While the authors demonstrate the benefit of increasing real-world data, they do not provide specific details on the computational cost associated with this scaling. Information on training time, memory requirements, and model parameters is missing, as is a discussion of potential bottlenecks. The paper mentions an experiment on 'Effect of Data Amount' (Section 4.3), showing that 'performance improves as more real images are used for training' (Section 4.3, Analysis), but it does not provide the necessary computational details. This omission makes it difficult to assess the practicality of the method for real-world applications. My confidence in this issue is high, as the absence of these details is clear in the experimental section (Section 4). Fourthly, the paper mentions using a constant canonical pose for input views during self-training on real images but does not provide a detailed explanation of how this canonical pose is defined or how it affects the model's performance. It is unclear how the model handles variations in object pose during training, and whether this canonical pose is learned or predefined. The paper states, 'For our base model, we use a fine-tuned TripoSR~\cite{tochilkin2024triposr}, an LRM without input pose and intrinsics conditioning.' (Section 3.1, Overview) and 'the canonical pose of the input view (introduced in Sec.~\ref{sec: lrm}).' (Section 3.1, Pixel-level Guidance), but it does not clarify the definition or handling of this pose. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the method to different object orientations. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper does not provide the necessary details in Sections 3.1 and 2.1. Finally, the paper states that the model is initialized on a synthetic dataset before being trained on real-world images but does not provide sufficient details on the initialization process. The specific synthetic dataset used, the training procedure, and the impact of initialization on the final performance are not discussed. The paper mentions using 'our collected \dataname{} real single-view data and the synthetic multi-view renderings of Objaverse~\cite{deitke2023objaverse} jointly.' (Section 4, Datasets) and 'a filtered subset consisting of 260K high-quality shapes for training.' from Objaverse (Section 4, Datasets), but it does not provide the necessary details on the initialization process. This lack of detail makes it difficult to evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed approach. My confidence in this issue is high, as the paper does not provide these details in Sections 3 and 4.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of the instance segmentation process used for data curation. This should include details about the segmentation model used, its training procedure, and its performance on the real-world images. The authors should also discuss how they handle segmentation errors and potential biases introduced by the selection of unoccluded instances. For example, they could explore using a robust segmentation model trained on a diverse dataset and implement a data augmentation strategy to mitigate biases. Furthermore, the paper should include a quantitative analysis of the quality of the segmented instances and how this quality affects the performance of the LRM. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the data curation process and its impact on the overall results. Secondly, the paper should provide a more thorough discussion of the chosen unsupervised losses and their limitations. The authors should explore other potential unsupervised losses, such as adversarial losses or perceptual losses, and discuss why they were not chosen. They should also analyze the limitations of the cycle consistency and semantic losses, and discuss how these limitations might affect the performance of the model. For example, they could explore using a more robust geometric loss to enforce accuracy and a more fine-grained semantic loss to capture more details. Furthermore, the paper should include an ablation study to evaluate the contribution of each loss function to the overall performance of the model. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the effectiveness of the chosen losses and their potential limitations. Thirdly, the paper should include a more detailed analysis of the scalability of the proposed method. This should include information about the training time, memory requirements, and the number of parameters of the model. The authors should also discuss the potential challenges of scaling the method to larger datasets and more complex models. For example, they could explore using more efficient training techniques, such as distributed training or mixed-precision training. Furthermore, the paper should include an analysis of the performance of the model as the amount of training data increases. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the scalability of the proposed method and its potential limitations. Fourthly, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of how the constant canonical pose is defined and used during self-training. Specifically, it should clarify whether this pose is a fixed, predefined value or if it is learned during the initialization phase on the synthetic dataset. The authors should also discuss how the model handles variations in object pose during training, and whether any specific techniques are used to ensure robustness to different object orientations. For example, data augmentation techniques or pose normalization methods could be explored. A more thorough analysis of the impact of the canonical pose on the model's performance would be beneficial, including experiments with different canonical poses to demonstrate the sensitivity of the method to this parameter. This would provide a better understanding of the method's limitations and potential for improvement. Finally, the paper should include a comprehensive description of the synthetic dataset used for initialization, including its size, diversity, and the specific 3D representations used. The training procedure should be described in detail, including the optimization algorithm, learning rate schedule, and any regularization techniques employed. Furthermore, the authors should provide an analysis of the impact of the initialization on the final performance, including experiments with different initialization strategies. For example, they could compare the performance of the model when initialized with different synthetic datasets or with a random initialization. This would help to understand the importance of the initialization phase and its contribution to the overall performance of the model. It would also be beneficial to analyze the transferability of the learned representations from the synthetic domain to the real-world domain.
