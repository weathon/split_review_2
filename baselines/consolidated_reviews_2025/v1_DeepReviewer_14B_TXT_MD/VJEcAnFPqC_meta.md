## Summary

This paper introduces a synthetic graph navigation task as a framework for investigating stepwise inference in transformers. The core idea is to represent problems as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), where a model must navigate from a start node to a goal node, potentially through intermediate nodes. This framework allows for controlled experimentation on various aspects of stepwise reasoning. The authors explore several phenomena, including the 'stepwise inference reasoning gap,' which refers to the performance difference between models trained with and without explicit intermediate steps. They also investigate the diversity-accuracy tradeoff, observing that models tend to favor shorter paths, a phenomenon they term 'simplicity bias.' Furthermore, the paper examines the model's navigation preferences and its ability to generalize to new, unseen graph structures, particularly when guided by in-context exemplars. The experiments are conducted using a 2-layer transformer trained from scratch on synthetic graph navigation tasks, allowing for a detailed analysis of the model's behavior. The findings reveal that transformers can learn to perform stepwise inference, and that the structure of the training data significantly impacts the model's ability to generalize. The paper also identifies a primacy bias, where the model tends to favor the first path it encounters in the context. Overall, this work provides a valuable synthetic framework for studying stepwise inference, offering insights into the mechanisms underlying this type of reasoning and highlighting several biases that can affect model performance. The authors acknowledge the limitations of their synthetic approach and suggest future work to test their hypotheses in large language models and more complex scenarios.

## Strengths

I find the paper's core strength lies in its innovative use of a synthetic graph navigation task to study stepwise inference. This approach provides a controlled environment for isolating and analyzing specific aspects of reasoning, which is a significant contribution to the field. The paper's methodology is well-designed, and the experiments are clearly presented, making it easy to follow the authors' line of reasoning. The identification of several phenomena, such as the stepwise inference reasoning gap, the diversity-accuracy tradeoff, and the simplicity bias, is a valuable contribution to our understanding of how transformers perform stepwise inference. The mechanistic analysis, particularly the explanation of the simplicity bias through the correlation between inner product and distance, is insightful and provides a deeper understanding of the model's behavior. The exploration of the model's navigation preferences and its ability to generalize to new graph structures, especially when guided by in-context exemplars, is also a notable strength. The paper's focus on understanding the underlying mechanisms of stepwise inference, rather than just achieving high performance, is commendable. The authors' willingness to acknowledge the limitations of their approach and suggest future research directions demonstrates a thoughtful and rigorous approach to scientific inquiry. The use of both Bernoulli and hierarchical DAGs provides a good starting point for exploring the model's behavior under different graph structures. The paper's clear and concise writing style makes it accessible to a wide audience, and the figures effectively illustrate the key findings. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its novel methodology, its clear presentation of results, and its insightful analysis of the mechanisms underlying stepwise inference.

## Weaknesses

While I appreciate the paper's contributions, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's reliance on a synthetic graph navigation task raises concerns about the generalizability of its findings to real-world natural language processing (NLP) tasks. As the authors themselves acknowledge, the connection between navigating a graph and the complexities of natural language understanding and generation is not well-established. The paper does not provide a detailed explanation of how the specific structure of the graph navigation task, with its directed acyclic graph (DAG) structure, directly translates to the sequential, often ambiguous nature of language. While the introduction mentions that tasks benefiting from stepwise inference can be cast as graph navigation problems, it lacks concrete examples of how this mapping occurs in practical NLP scenarios. This limitation is further highlighted by the fact that the experiments are conducted on a 2-layer transformer trained from scratch, which is far removed from the scale and complexity of large language models (LLMs) where stepwise inference is most commonly used. The paper's motivation is rooted in the success of stepwise inference methods in LLMs, but the experimental setup does not directly address the challenges and nuances of these models. This gap between the motivation and the experimental setup makes it difficult to assess the practical relevance of the findings. The paper also lacks a thorough evaluation of the proposed method compared to existing stepwise inference techniques. While the authors compare the model's performance with and without stepwise inference, this internal comparison does not provide a benchmark against established methods like chain-of-thought prompting or scratchpad techniques. The absence of such comparisons makes it difficult to assess the significance of the reported results and to position the proposed method within the broader landscape of stepwise inference techniques. Furthermore, the paper's conclusion that a model can learn to perform stepwise inference better than direct inference seems somewhat unsurprising, given that the training data for stepwise inference explicitly contains intermediate steps. This is a rather trivial observation, as the model is explicitly trained with the solution path, which is not the case for direct inference. The performance difference is therefore not surprising and does not provide much insight into the mechanisms of stepwise inference in more complex models. The paper also identifies a strong bias toward choosing a path defined by the first chain over the second when presented with conflicting exemplars. This primacy bias, while interesting, is a limitation that could hinder the model's performance in real-world scenarios where exemplars may not always be consistent. Additionally, the paper acknowledges that the model fails to generalize to reasoning chains longer than those present in its training data, which is another significant limitation. The paper also notes that the model has a simplicity bias towards shorter paths, which is a limitation that could hinder the model's performance in real-world scenarios where the optimal solution is not always the simplest one. Finally, while the paper provides explanations for the observed phenomena, such as the stepwise inference reasoning gap, the diversity-accuracy tradeoff, and the simplicity bias, the analysis could be more detailed. For instance, the 'simplicity bias' is observed, but the paper does not explore why the model favors shorter paths, or how this relates to the model's architecture or training process. The diversity-accuracy tradeoff is also presented without a clear explanation of the factors influencing this relationship, such as the specific sampling strategies used. The paper does provide a mechanistic explanation for the simplicity bias, but a deeper dive into the underlying mechanisms could be beneficial. In summary, the paper's weaknesses stem from the gap between the synthetic task and real-world NLP, the lack of comparison to existing methods, the somewhat trivial nature of some findings, and the limited analysis of the underlying mechanisms.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the paper should provide a more detailed explanation of how the synthetic graph navigation task relates to real-world NLP challenges. The authors should provide concrete examples of how the graph navigation problem maps to specific NLP tasks, such as question answering or text summarization. For instance, they could explain how the nodes in the graph represent different concepts or entities in a text, and how the edges represent the relationships between them. This would help clarify the relevance of the proposed task and make the findings more applicable to practical NLP problems. Furthermore, the authors should discuss the limitations of the graph navigation task in capturing the full complexity of natural language, acknowledging the differences between the structured nature of graphs and the more ambiguous nature of language. Second, the paper needs a more comprehensive evaluation of the proposed method. The authors should compare their method to existing approaches for graph navigation or similar tasks, and they should also evaluate the method on a wider range of graph structures and input data. This would help to establish the significance of the reported results and to identify the limitations of the proposed method. The evaluation should also include an analysis of the model's performance on more complex graph structures, such as those with cycles or multiple paths between nodes. The authors should also explore the model's sensitivity to different types of input data, such as noisy or incomplete graphs. This would provide a more complete picture of the model's capabilities and limitations. Third, the paper should include a comparison to established baselines or alternative approaches for stepwise inference. This comparison should not be limited to just performance metrics, but also include an analysis of the computational complexity, memory requirements, and the interpretability of the different methods. For example, how does the proposed method compare to chain-of-thought prompting or other methods that explicitly guide the model through intermediate steps? A detailed comparison would help position the proposed method within the broader landscape of stepwise inference techniques and provide a more complete picture of its strengths and weaknesses. Fourth, the authors should consider extending their analysis to include more complex graph structures and navigation tasks. The current focus on relatively simple graph navigation scenarios may not fully capture the challenges encountered in real-world applications. For example, the authors could explore tasks that involve graphs with varying degrees of connectivity, cycles, or hierarchical structures. Additionally, the paper could investigate how the model's performance is affected by the presence of noisy or ambiguous information in the input data. This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's limitations and its ability to generalize to diverse scenarios. Fifth, the paper would benefit from a more thorough exploration of how the observed simplicity bias and primacy bias could be mitigated in practical applications. While the synthetic graph navigation task provides a controlled environment for studying stepwise inference, the identified biases could significantly hinder the performance of transformers in real-world tasks that require more complex reasoning. For instance, in tasks involving multi-hop reasoning or planning, the tendency to favor shorter paths or the first encountered solution could lead to suboptimal outcomes. The authors should consider investigating techniques such as training-based methods or prompt engineering to reduce the impact of these biases. This could involve incorporating specific training examples that explicitly demonstrate the need for longer or more complex reasoning chains, or designing prompts that encourage the model to explore multiple potential solutions before committing to one. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the potential trade-offs between mitigating these biases and other performance metrics, such as accuracy and efficiency. Finally, the authors should delve deeper into the implications of the observed diversity-accuracy tradeoff for practical applications. While the tradeoff is an interesting finding, the paper does not fully explore how this tradeoff might affect the performance of transformers in different real-world scenarios. For example, in some applications, it may be more important to prioritize accuracy over diversity, while in others, the opposite may be true. The authors should consider investigating how the sampling temperature can be tuned to achieve the desired balance between diversity and accuracy for specific tasks. This could involve developing adaptive sampling strategies that dynamically adjust the temperature based on the characteristics of the input data or the desired outcome. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the potential limitations of using temperature tuning as the sole mechanism for controlling the diversity-accuracy tradeoff, and explore alternative approaches that may be more effective in certain contexts.

## Questions

Several questions arise from my analysis of the paper. First, how can the simplicity bias and primacy bias be mitigated in practical applications? Are there any training-based methods or prompt engineering techniques that can be used to reduce the impact of these biases? Second, how well do the findings from the synthetic graph navigation task generalize to other stepwise inference protocols and real-world tasks? Are there any plans to extend the study to include more complex graph structures and navigation tasks? Third, can you provide more details on the synthetic graph navigation task and how it relates to real-world natural language processing tasks? Specifically, how do the nodes and edges in the graph correspond to elements of natural language, and how does the navigation process mirror the reasoning process in NLP? Fourth, can you provide a more detailed analysis of the results, and explain the stepwise inference reasoning gap, the diversity-accuracy tradeoff, and the simplicity bias in more detail? What are the underlying mechanisms that cause these phenomena, and how do they relate to the model's architecture and training process? Fifth, can you provide a more thorough evaluation of the proposed method, and compare it to existing methods? How does the proposed method perform compared to established stepwise inference techniques, and what are its strengths and weaknesses relative to these methods? Finally, the paper says that "we can empirically reproduce and analyze several phenomena observed at scale: (i) the stepwise inference reasoning gap, the cause of which we find in the structure of the training data". I'm confused by this statement because it seems that the stepwise inference reasoning gap is caused by the lack of intermediate steps in the training data for direct inference, which is different from the structure of the training data for stepwise inference. Could you clarify this?
