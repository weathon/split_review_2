## Summary

This paper introduces LayerDAG, a novel generative model for directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) that combines autoregressive and diffusion-based techniques. The core idea is to decompose the DAG generation process into a sequence of layer-wise generation steps, where each layer is generated conditioned on the preceding ones. This approach effectively addresses the challenge of strong node dependencies inherent in DAGs. The authors propose a unique tokenization strategy that interprets the partial order of nodes as a sequence of bipartite graphs, allowing the model to capture both directional and logical dependencies. Specifically, the model first predicts the number of nodes in the next layer, then generates node attributes, and finally generates the edges within that layer. This process is repeated autoregressively for each layer. Within each layer, a diffusion model is employed to generate both node attributes and edges, conditioned on the previously generated layers. The authors also introduce a flexible quality-efficiency trade-off scheme by adjusting the number of denoising steps based on the layer index. The model's performance is evaluated on both synthetic and real-world datasets, including TPU Tile and HLS, demonstrating its ability to generate DAGs that adhere to complex logical rules and exhibit good statistical properties. The experiments show that LayerDAG outperforms existing DAG generative models in terms of validity, expressiveness, and generalization. Furthermore, the synthetic DAGs generated by LayerDAG are shown to enhance the training of machine learning-based surrogate models, leading to improved accuracy in predicting performance metrics of real-world DAGs across diverse computing platforms. The authors also demonstrate the effectiveness of their proposed flexible quality-efficiency trade-off scheme. Overall, this paper presents a significant contribution to the field of DAG generation by introducing a novel and effective approach that combines the strengths of autoregressive and diffusion models, and by demonstrating its practical utility in real-world applications.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The core innovation lies in the novel combination of autoregressive and diffusion models for DAG generation. The layer-wise tokenization approach, which interprets the partial order of nodes as a sequence of bipartite graphs, is a creative solution to the challenge of strong node dependencies in DAGs. This approach allows the model to effectively capture both directional and logical dependencies, which is a significant advancement over existing methods. The use of a diffusion model within each layer to generate node attributes and edges is also a notable contribution, as it allows for the generation of complex and diverse DAG structures. The authors' decision to generate the number of nodes in each layer before generating the node attributes and edges is a clever way to decouple the node dependencies, which significantly improves the efficiency of the generation process. The experimental results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of LayerDAG on both synthetic and real-world datasets, showing that it outperforms existing DAG generative models in terms of validity, expressiveness, and generalization. The fact that the synthetic DAGs generated by LayerDAG can be used to enhance the training of machine learning-based surrogate models is a particularly important finding, as it highlights the practical utility of the proposed approach. The authors also provide a thorough evaluation of their model, including comparisons with existing models and an analysis of its performance on both synthetic and real-world datasets. The results demonstrate the model's effectiveness and potential for practical applications. The paper is also well-written and clearly explains the proposed method, the experimental setup, and the results. The use of figures and tables effectively supports the textual content. The authors have clearly put a lot of thought into the design of their model and the evaluation of its performance. The layer-index-based denoising schedule is another strength, as it provides a flexible way to balance the quality and efficiency of the generated DAGs. This is a practical consideration that is often overlooked in generative models, and I appreciate that the authors have addressed it.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the paper describes the overall architecture of LayerDAG, the description of the model's components and their interactions lacks sufficient detail. Specifically, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of how the autoregressive component conditions on previous layers when generating a new layer. While the paper states that the generation of the (l+1)-th layer is conditioned on the entire graph generated up to layer l, the exact mechanism by which this conditioning is achieved is not fully explained. For example, the paper mentions that the denoising network for node attributes, denoted as \(\phi_{\theta_X}\), augments the representations of the node attributes with a context vector \(\mathbf{h}_t^{(\leq l)}\) derived from the previous layers, but the precise method for generating this context vector and how it is incorporated into the denoising process is not explicitly stated. Similarly, the paper mentions that the denoising network for edge diffusion, denoted as \(\phi_{\theta_A}\), augments the input with the node attributes and edge states from the previous layers, but the specific details of this augmentation are not provided. This lack of clarity makes it difficult to fully understand the model's inner workings and to assess its potential limitations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper lacks a detailed explanation of the conditioning mechanism. Second, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed model. It is unclear how the computational cost scales with the size of the DAGs, the number of layers, and the number of denoising steps. The paper does not provide a formal complexity analysis, such as Big O notation, for the different components of the model. This analysis is crucial for understanding the practical applicability of the model, especially for large-scale DAG generation. The absence of a detailed analysis makes it difficult to compare the proposed model's efficiency with other potential approaches. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not include any discussion of computational complexity. Third, the paper does not provide sufficient details about the architecture of the denoising network used in the diffusion process. This lack of detail makes it difficult to assess the model's complexity and potential limitations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide specific architectural details of the denoising network. While the authors claim that this is sufficient for benchmarking, it is unclear how the model would perform on much larger DAGs, which are common in many real-world applications. The lack of experiments on larger graphs raises concerns about the model's scalability and generalizability. The paper also does not discuss the potential for mode collapse in the diffusion process, and how this might affect the diversity of generated DAGs.
