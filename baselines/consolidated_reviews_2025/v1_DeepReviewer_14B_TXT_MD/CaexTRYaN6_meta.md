## Summary

This paper introduces CONCORD, a novel dataset distillation method that leverages the conceptual understanding of large language models (LLMs) to enhance the quality of distilled datasets. The core idea is to use LLMs to retrieve category-specific concepts, which then guide a diffusion model in generating images that accurately represent essential object details. Unlike traditional dataset distillation methods that primarily focus on matching the overall distribution of the original data, CONCORD aims to improve instance-level control by incorporating fine-grained conceptual information. The method begins by prompting LLMs to retrieve relevant concepts for each category, which are then evaluated for their validity using a CLIP model. These concepts are used to guide the diffusion process, ensuring that the generated images not only look realistic but also semantically align with the target categories. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of CONCORD by integrating it with existing diffusion-based distillation methods, such as Minimax and unCLIP Img2Img. The experimental results, primarily on ImageNet-1K and its subsets, show that CONCORD achieves state-of-the-art performance, improving both the visual quality and training effectiveness of the distilled datasets. The paper also includes ablation studies to validate the contribution of different components of the method, such as the concept retrieval process and the contrastive objective used for concept matching. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to dataset distillation by incorporating LLMs to enhance the conceptual richness of the distilled data, which is a significant step forward in addressing the limitations of traditional distribution-matching methods. The method's flexibility, allowing it to be integrated into various diffusion-based pipelines, further enhances its practical applicability. However, as I will discuss in the weaknesses section, there are some limitations that need to be addressed in future work.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First and foremost, the innovative use of LLMs for conceptual guidance is a significant contribution. By leveraging the rich conceptual knowledge of LLMs, the authors have introduced a novel approach that enhances the controllability and quality of generated images. This addresses a key limitation of previous dataset distillation techniques, which often lack instance-level control and may produce images with incomplete or inaccurate object details. The method's ability to explicitly guide the image generation process with fine-grained concepts is a clear advancement over methods that primarily focus on matching the overall distribution of the original data. Second, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. CONCORD achieves state-of-the-art performance on ImageNet-1K and its subsets, demonstrating its effectiveness in generating high-quality distilled datasets. The method's ability to maintain performance across different datasets and settings highlights its robustness and generalization capabilities. The authors also provide extensive experimental results, including ablation studies and comparisons with state-of-the-art methods, which thoroughly validate the effectiveness of CONCORD and provide insights into the contributions of each component of the method. The flexibility of the method is another notable strength. The fact that CONCORD can be easily integrated into existing diffusion-based generative pipelines makes it a versatile tool for dataset distillation. This flexibility allows researchers and practitioners to apply CONCORD to a wide range of tasks and models without requiring extensive modifications. Finally, the paper is well-written and easy to follow, making the technical details accessible to a broad audience. The authors provide a clear explanation of how the conceptual information from LLMs is integrated into the diffusion process, and the experimental results are presented in a clear and concise manner. The inclusion of ablation studies and visualizations further enhances the paper's clarity and impact.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the novelty of the method is somewhat limited. While the integration of LLMs for concept retrieval is a valuable contribution, the paper's core architecture and training process are largely similar to the Minimax method (Gu et al., 2024). The authors apply their concept-informed approach to the Minimax framework, and while they show performance improvements, they do not provide a detailed analysis of the specific modifications and their individual impact on performance. This lack of deep architectural comparison makes it difficult to fully assess the unique contributions of CONCORD beyond the use of LLMs. This is evident in the experimental section where the comparison is primarily focused on performance gains rather than a detailed breakdown of the architectural differences. Second, the paper lacks a thorough analysis of the computational cost associated with the proposed method. While the authors mention using a single NVIDIA A100 GPU, they do not provide a detailed breakdown of the time spent on each step of the pipeline, including concept retrieval, diffusion processing, and any other overhead. This makes it difficult to assess the practical scalability of the method, especially for large-scale datasets or real-time applications. The reliance on LLMs for concept retrieval could introduce significant latency, and the impact of this on the overall distillation time needs further investigation. The paper does not provide a direct comparison of computational time or efficiency with other methods, such as DREAM, which is known for its efficiency. This lack of efficiency analysis is a significant limitation, as it makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the cases where the proposed method performs worse than the Minimax method in the 1 IPC case. In Tables 1 and 2, the base Minimax method sometimes outperforms the CONCORD-enhanced versions at 1 IPC. The authors acknowledge the performance gains of CONCORD generally but do not delve into the reasons for the underperformance in these specific cases. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to fully understand the limitations of the method and identify potential areas for improvement. Fourth, the analysis of the results in Table 3, particularly regarding the performance of the unCLIP baseline, is somewhat superficial. While the authors note the poor performance of the unCLIP baseline at 50 IPC, they do not explore the underlying reasons for this poor performance. The fact that it performs worse than random selection at 50 IPC is concerning and requires further investigation. The authors should analyze the quality of the images generated by unCLIP at different IPC values and provide insights into why its performance degrades so significantly. Fifth, the method's dependence on the CLIP model for concept validity evaluation introduces a potential bottleneck. The authors use CLIP to evaluate the validity of the retrieved concepts by calculating the activation of text descriptions on real images. While CLIP is a powerful model, its performance can be influenced by the specific pre-training data and the nature of the text embeddings. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the method to different CLIP models or alternative approaches for concept validity evaluation, which could be a limitation. Sixth, the paper does not thoroughly explore the potential limitations or failure cases of the proposed method. For instance, it would be beneficial to analyze scenarios where the LLM-derived concepts are ambiguous or misleading, leading to poor image generation. The paper should also explore the sensitivity of the method to the quality of the LLM's output, and how this impacts the final distilled dataset. Finally, the generalizability of the method to other data modalities beyond images is not explored. The current approach is heavily reliant on the visual nature of images and the ability of LLMs to provide descriptive text. It is unclear how this method could be adapted for other data types, such as audio, video, or time-series data, where the concept acquisition process would require a different approach. These limitations, which I have verified through my analysis of the paper, highlight areas where further research is needed to fully realize the potential of the proposed method.

## Suggestions

Based on the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements for future work. First, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the architectural differences between CONCORD and the Minimax method. This should include a breakdown of the specific modifications made to the Minimax framework and their individual impact on performance. A more rigorous comparison is needed, detailing the specific changes and their individual impact on performance. Second, the authors should conduct a thorough analysis of the computational cost associated with each step of their pipeline, including concept retrieval, diffusion processing, and any other overhead. This analysis should include a breakdown of the time spent on LLM queries, CLIP embedding calculations, and the diffusion denoising steps. Furthermore, the authors should explore techniques to reduce the computational cost of concept retrieval, such as caching frequently used concepts or employing more efficient LLM prompting strategies. It would also be beneficial to investigate the trade-off between the number of diffusion steps and the quality of the generated images, as reducing the number of steps could significantly decrease the computational cost, albeit potentially at the expense of image quality. The authors should also consider exploring alternative, less computationally intensive methods for concept integration, such as using lightweight attention mechanisms or feature injection techniques. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the cases where the proposed method performs worse than the Minimax method in the 1 IPC case. This analysis should go beyond simple accuracy metrics and explore the quality and diversity of the generated samples. For example, are the generated images more prone to mode collapse or artifacts compared to Minimax? Are there specific categories where the proposed method struggles more than others? A detailed investigation into these aspects would provide a more nuanced understanding of the method's limitations. Fourth, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of the unCLIP baseline's performance in Table 3. The fact that it performs worse than random selection at 50 IPC is concerning and requires further investigation. The authors should analyze the quality of the images generated by unCLIP at different IPC values and provide insights into why its performance degrades so significantly. This analysis should include a discussion of the limitations of the unCLIP model and how the proposed method overcomes these limitations. Fifth, to mitigate the dependence on the CLIP model, the authors should explore alternative methods for concept validity evaluation. This could involve using other pre-trained models or developing a custom evaluation metric that is less reliant on a specific model. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the method to different CLIP models and provide a comparative analysis of their performance. Furthermore, the authors could explore the possibility of using a ensemble of models for concept validity evaluation, which could potentially improve the robustness and accuracy of the method. It would also be beneficial to investigate the impact of different text prompts on the CLIP embeddings and explore techniques to generate more robust and consistent embeddings. Sixth, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of scenarios where the proposed method performs poorly. This should include a detailed examination of the types of images that are difficult to generate using the LLM-guided diffusion process, and the reasons why these images are challenging. For example, the authors could analyze cases where the LLM-derived concepts are too abstract or ambiguous, or where the diffusion model struggles to translate these concepts into realistic images. The authors should also investigate the impact of noisy or misleading concepts on the quality of the distilled dataset, and explore methods to mitigate these issues. This could involve techniques such as using multiple LLMs or incorporating a feedback mechanism to refine the concepts based on the generated images. Finally, the authors should explore the potential for extending their method to other data modalities. This could involve investigating how to adapt the concept acquisition process for non-visual data types, such as audio or time-series data. For example, the authors could explore the use of audio transcription models or time-series analysis techniques to extract relevant concepts from these data types. The authors should also consider how the diffusion process could be adapted to generate data in these modalities, and whether the proposed approach can be generalized to handle the unique characteristics of these data types. This would significantly broaden the applicability of the proposed method and demonstrate its potential for a wider range of applications.
