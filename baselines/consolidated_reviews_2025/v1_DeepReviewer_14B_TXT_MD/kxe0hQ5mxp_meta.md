## Summary

This paper introduces a novel class of activation functions, termed 'elephant activation functions,' designed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in neural networks. The core idea revolves around generating both sparse representations and sparse gradients, which the authors theoretically link to improved continual learning capabilities. The proposed elephant activation function is mathematically defined with two parameters, 'a' and 'd', which control the width and slope of the function, respectively. The authors provide a theoretical analysis, primarily focused on a simple regression task, to demonstrate that these functions can satisfy the properties required for continual learning, specifically by promoting local elasticity. Empirically, the paper evaluates the performance of neural networks incorporating elephant activation functions (EMLP and ECNN) across various tasks, including streaming learning for regression, class incremental learning, and reinforcement learning. The results show that these networks outperform classical activation functions in these continual learning scenarios, particularly in settings without replay buffers or task boundary information. The paper's main contribution lies in the introduction of a new activation function that can be easily integrated into existing neural network architectures, offering a potential solution to the persistent problem of catastrophic forgetting. The experimental results, while promising, are primarily focused on relatively small datasets and simple architectures, leaving room for further investigation into the method's scalability and generalizability. Overall, the paper presents a novel and interesting approach to continual learning, but it would benefit from more extensive experimentation and a deeper exploration of the practical implications of the proposed method. The theoretical analysis, while providing a foundation for the method, is somewhat limited in its scope and applicability to more complex scenarios. The paper's findings suggest that the elephant activation function is a promising avenue for future research in continual learning, but further work is needed to fully realize its potential.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I've identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's experimental validation is limited by the scale of the datasets used. While the authors present results on Split MNIST, Split CIFAR10, Split CIFAR100, and Split Tiny ImageNet, they do not include experiments on the full ImageNet dataset. This is a significant omission, as ImageNet is a standard benchmark for evaluating the scalability and generalizability of deep learning models. The absence of ImageNet experiments makes it difficult to assess how the proposed elephant activation function would perform on more complex tasks with a larger number of classes and more diverse data. This lack of experimentation on large-scale datasets raises concerns about the practical applicability of the method in real-world scenarios. Second, the theoretical analysis, while providing a foundation for the method, is primarily based on a simple regression task. The authors start their analysis with a scalar function and use a Taylor expansion to investigate the training dynamics. While the paper extends the method to more complex architectures (MLP, CNN) and tasks (class incremental learning, RL), the core theoretical justification is rooted in this simplified scenario. The applicability of this initial analysis to more complex, deep architectures is an assumption that could be further explored. The paper does not provide a detailed explanation of how the analysis extends to these more complex settings, and this lack of theoretical rigor raises questions about the generalizability of the findings. Third, the paper lacks a systematic approach for selecting the parameters 'a' and 'd' of the elephant activation function. The authors use fixed values for 'd' in different experiments (d=8 for streaming regression, d=4 for class incremental learning and RL) without providing a clear guideline on how to choose these values or exploring the sensitivity of the results to changes in these parameters. The value of 'a' is also not explicitly explored or varied in the experiments. This lack of parameter sensitivity analysis is a significant weakness, as it makes it difficult to determine the optimal settings for the elephant activation function and raises concerns about the robustness of the method. Fourth, the paper does not include a comprehensive discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. While the paper presents the advantages and experimental results, it lacks a dedicated section or substantial discussion outlining the drawbacks of the elephant activation functions. This omission makes it difficult to fully assess the practical implications of the method and its potential drawbacks. Fifth, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with other activation functions specifically designed to promote sparsity. While the paper compares against SR-NN, which aims for sparse representations, it does not compare against other activation functions explicitly designed to induce sparsity, such as those with built-in thresholding or regularization mechanisms. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to determine the specific benefits of the elephant activation function compared to other sparsity-inducing methods. Sixth, the paper does not include a comparison with other baseline continual learning methods in the RL experiments. The RL experiments only compare against a standard MLP with varying buffer sizes and do not include comparisons with other continual learning algorithms designed to mitigate catastrophic forgetting in RL. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the performance of the proposed method relative to the state-of-the-art in continual learning. Finally, the paper lacks a detailed discussion on the practical implications of the proposed method, such as the computational cost of the elephant activation function compared to other activation functions. The paper does not address whether there are specific scenarios where the elephant activation function might not be the best choice. This lack of practical analysis makes it difficult to assess the real-world applicability of the method.
