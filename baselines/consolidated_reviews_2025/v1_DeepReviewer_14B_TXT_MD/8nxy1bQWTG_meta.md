## Summary

This paper introduces DiffEnc, a novel framework that extends traditional diffusion models by incorporating a time-dependent encoder within the forward diffusion process. The core idea is to parameterize the mean of the diffusion process with an encoder whose output is a function of both the data and the diffusion timestep. This approach aims to enhance the flexibility of diffusion models without impacting sampling time, as the encoder is only utilized during training. The authors also delve into the theoretical underpinnings of diffusion models, specifically examining the assumption that the variances of the forward and reverse diffusion processes are equal. They demonstrate that relaxing this assumption leads to a weighted diffusion loss and prove that the optimal Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) is achieved when these variances are equal in the continuous-time limit. The authors further propose a parameterization of the encoder that approximately eliminates additional terms in the loss resulting from the generalized encoder. Empirically, the paper demonstrates that DiffEnc achieves a statistically significant improvement in likelihood on the CIFAR-10 dataset, and also shows improvements in latent loss across other datasets without compromising the diffusion loss. The authors conduct experiments on CIFAR-10, MNIST, and ImageNet32, comparing their method against several baselines, including VDM and other generative models. The key finding is that the proposed DiffEnc framework, with its time-dependent encoder, can lead to better likelihoods compared to standard diffusion models. The paper's contribution lies in its novel approach to incorporating a time-dependent encoder, its theoretical analysis of the variance assumption, and its empirical validation of the proposed framework. The authors' work provides a valuable contribution to the field of diffusion models by exploring new ways to enhance their flexibility and performance. However, as I will discuss in the weaknesses section, the paper could benefit from a more thorough analysis of the computational costs, a more comprehensive comparison with existing state-of-the-art diffusion models, and a clearer articulation of the novelty of the proposed generalizations.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be commendable. The introduction of DiffEnc, a framework that incorporates a time-dependent encoder into the diffusion process, is a significant contribution. This approach enhances the flexibility of diffusion models without affecting sampling time, a crucial advantage. The authors' theoretical analysis of the assumption that the variances of the forward and reverse diffusion processes are equal is also a notable strength. They rigorously prove that relaxing this assumption leads to a weighted diffusion loss and that the optimal ELBO is achieved when the variances are equal in the continuous-time limit. This theoretical insight provides a deeper understanding of the underlying principles of diffusion models. The authors also propose a parameterization of the encoder that approximately eliminates additional terms in the loss resulting from the generalized encoder, which is a clever technical innovation. The experimental results, particularly the statistically significant improvement in likelihood on CIFAR-10, are compelling. The authors also demonstrate that DiffEnc improves latent loss across datasets without compromising diffusion loss, showcasing the practical effectiveness of their framework. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, and the authors do a good job of highlighting how their proposed generalizations relate to standard variational diffusion models. The discussion of the various parameterizations of the encoder is also very helpful. The authors' meticulous details in the appendices are also appreciated, providing a comprehensive view of their work. Overall, the paper presents a novel and well-executed approach to enhancing diffusion models, supported by both theoretical analysis and empirical validation. The combination of theoretical insights and practical improvements makes this paper a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational costs associated with the DiffEnc framework. While the authors mention that the encoder is exclusively employed during the training phase, they do not quantify the increase in training time or memory usage compared to standard diffusion models with similar architectures. The paper states, "Crucially, this encoder is exclusively employed during the training phase and not utilized during the sampling process," but this statement does not address the computational overhead during training. The paper describes the encoder architecture as having "the same overall U-Net architecture, but with downsampling to resolutions 16x16 and 8x8," but it does not provide a detailed breakdown of the training time, memory usage, or inference time, nor does it compare these metrics to standard diffusion models. The paper also mentions training steps, such as "We trained all our models with either 3 or 5 seeds depending on the computational cost of the experiments," but this does not provide a clear picture of the computational overhead. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method, as the computational cost is a crucial factor in real-world scenarios. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the absence of any detailed computational analysis is clearly evident in the paper. Secondly, the paper does not provide a comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art diffusion models. While the authors compare DiffEnc to VDM and other generative models in Table 1, this comparison is limited to likelihood (BPD) and does not include other metrics like FID or computational cost. The paper states, "Comparison of average bits per dimension (BPD) over 3 seeds on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet32 with other work," but this is not sufficient to fully evaluate the performance of DiffEnc in the context of recent advancements. The paper also mentions that diffusion models are state-of-the-art in various domains, but it does not provide a detailed comparison with models that incorporate similar techniques, such as those using learnable noise schedules or adaptive sampling methods. The paper does not discuss the trade-offs between DiffEnc and these other models in terms of performance, computational cost, and implementation complexity. This lack of a thorough comparison makes it difficult to assess the relative advantages and disadvantages of DiffEnc compared to other recent advancements in the field. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does provide some comparison, but it is not comprehensive enough. Thirdly, and perhaps most importantly, the paper does not adequately address the novelty of the proposed generalizations. The authors introduce a time-dependent encoder and explore relaxing the assumption of equal forward and backward variances, but these ideas are not entirely novel. As Reviewer 2 pointed out, the generalization of the noise schedule appears to be a special case of blurring diffusion models, and the generalization of the forward process with a time-dependent encoder is also done in the context of blurring diffusion models and in Daras et al. 2022. The paper cites Hoogeboom et al. 2022, Rissanen et al. 2022, and Daras et al. 2022 in the Related Work section, but it does not provide a detailed comparison of the proposed method with these existing approaches in terms of methodology and theoretical underpinnings. The paper states, "We introduce a time-dependent encoder that parameterizes the mean of the diffusion process," and "In addition, we investigate the relaxation of a common assumption in diffusion models," but it does not adequately differentiate its approach from existing methods. This lack of a detailed comparison makes it difficult to assess the unique contributions of the paper. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide a sufficient discussion of the similarities and differences between its approach and existing methods. Finally, the paper does not provide enough evidence that the proposed generalizations are useful in practice. While the experiments show that DiffEnc can improve likelihood on some datasets, it is not clear how the results compare to existing methods, particularly blurring diffusion models. The paper demonstrates an improvement in likelihood on CIFAR-10 and provides a statistical significance test, stating, "We note that the improvement in total loss is significant, since we get a p-value of 0.03 for a t-test," but it lacks a direct comparison with blurring diffusion models. This makes it difficult to assess the practical utility of the proposed generalizations in the broader context of existing methods. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper does provide some evidence of improvement, but it is not comprehensive enough.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. Firstly, the authors should include a more comprehensive analysis of the computational costs associated with the DiffEnc framework. This should include a detailed breakdown of the training time, memory usage, and inference time, comparing it to standard diffusion models. It would be beneficial to provide a theoretical analysis of the computational complexity, as well as empirical results on various hardware configurations. This analysis should also explore the sensitivity of the model to the architecture and hyperparameters of the time-dependent encoder. This could involve conducting ablation studies to understand the impact of different encoder architectures, layer sizes, and activation functions on the overall performance of the model. This analysis should also explore the potential for overfitting or underfitting when using different encoder configurations, and provide guidelines for selecting appropriate hyperparameters. Secondly, the authors should provide a more detailed comparison of the DiffEnc framework with other state-of-the-art diffusion models. This comparison should go beyond simply stating that diffusion models are state-of-the-art and should include a discussion of how DiffEnc compares to models that incorporate similar techniques, such as those using learnable noise schedules or adaptive sampling methods. The authors should also discuss the trade-offs between DiffEnc and these other models in terms of performance, computational cost, and implementation complexity. This could involve conducting experiments on a common set of benchmarks and providing a detailed analysis of the results. It would also be valuable to discuss the potential for combining DiffEnc with other techniques, such as those used in latent diffusion models, to further improve performance. Thirdly, the authors should more clearly position their work within the existing literature on diffusion models, specifically in relation to blurring diffusion models and other generalizations of the forward process. While the paper introduces a generalized framework, it lacks a thorough comparison to established methods. For instance, the proposed time-dependent encoder, while presented as a novel contribution, bears a strong resemblance to the time-dependent transformations used in blurring diffusion models. A more detailed analysis is needed to differentiate the proposed approach from these existing methods, highlighting the specific advantages and disadvantages of each. The authors should provide a more rigorous comparison, both theoretically and empirically, to justify the novelty and practical benefits of their approach. This should include a discussion of the limitations of existing methods that their approach overcomes, or a demonstration of improved performance or efficiency. Finally, the authors should provide more evidence that the proposed generalizations are useful in practice. The current experiments, while demonstrating some improvement in likelihood, do not provide sufficient evidence of the practical utility of the proposed generalizations. The authors should compare their method against a wider range of existing diffusion models, including blurring diffusion models, on standard benchmark datasets. This comparison should not only focus on likelihood but also on other relevant metrics such as sample quality and computational cost. It is crucial to demonstrate that the proposed method offers a significant improvement over existing approaches, not just a marginal gain in likelihood. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of their method to different choices of the time-dependent encoder and its parameterization, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the method's behavior and limitations. Furthermore, the theoretical analysis, while interesting, needs to be more tightly integrated with the empirical findings. The authors should explore how the theoretical results, such as the analysis of the continuous-time limit, inform the design and performance of their method. For example, they could investigate whether the theoretical conditions for well-defined continuous-time limits are met in their experiments and how deviations from these conditions affect the results. The authors should also consider extending their theoretical analysis to cover more general cases, such as non-Gaussian forward processes, to further demonstrate the significance of their framework.
