## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to imitation learning (IL) by leveraging the forward-backward (FB) framework as a behavior foundation model (BFM). The core idea is to train an FB model using unsupervised data, thereby learning a representation of successor measures that can be used for various IL tasks without requiring additional reinforcement learning (RL) routines. The authors demonstrate that with appropriate choices of reward functions, the FB model can be effectively applied to several IL paradigms, including behavior cloning (BC), reward-based IL, distribution matching, feature matching, and goal-based IL. The experimental results, conducted on the DeepMind Control Suite, show that the proposed FB-IL method achieves state-of-the-art performance with significantly less computation time compared to other baselines. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative use of the FB framework as a BFM, which allows for efficient imitation learning from a single demonstration. The authors provide a clear theoretical justification for their approach and present a comprehensive set of experiments to validate their claims. The method's efficiency and ability to leverage unsupervised data are particularly noteworthy. However, the paper's claims of generalizability are not fully supported by the experimental evidence, which is limited to continuous control tasks. The paper's findings suggest that the FB framework offers a promising direction for developing efficient and versatile IL methods, but further investigation is needed to fully realize its potential in more complex and diverse scenarios. The authors' approach to using the FB framework as a BFM is a significant step forward in the field of imitation learning, offering a computationally efficient alternative to traditional methods. The paper's emphasis on using unsupervised data to pre-train the FB model is also a valuable contribution, as it reduces the need for large amounts of labeled data. The experimental results, while limited in scope, demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed method in the tested environments. The paper's theoretical analysis provides a solid foundation for the proposed approach, and the authors' discussion of different IL paradigms highlights the versatility of the FB framework. Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for the use of FB models as BFMs for imitation learning, and its findings have important implications for the development of more efficient and generalizable IL methods.

Overall, the paper presents a compelling case for the use of FB models as BFMs for imitation learning, and its findings have important implications for the development of more efficient and generalizable IL methods. The clarity of the presentation, the theoretical rigor, and the strong experimental results all contribute to the paper's overall quality and significance.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach to imitation learning using the forward-backward (FB) framework, several limitations warrant careful consideration. The most significant weakness lies in the limited scope of the experimental validation. The paper claims that the proposed method can use few demonstrations to imitate any behavior, but the experiments are exclusively conducted on continuous control benchmark tasks from the DeepMind Control Suite. As I've verified, the experiments are performed on the 'Maze', 'Walker', 'Cheetah', and 'Quadruped' domains, all of which involve continuous action spaces and state-based observations. There is no evidence presented to support the claim that the method can generalize to more complex tasks, such as manipulation tasks that often require discrete action spaces or long-term planning. This is a critical limitation because the ability to handle discrete actions and hierarchical structures is essential for many real-world applications. The absence of experiments on tasks with discrete action spaces raises concerns about the method's applicability to a broader range of problems. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the method's performance with image-based observations. The current experiments rely on state-based observations, which are significantly different from the high-dimensional image inputs that are common in many real-world scenarios. The lack of experiments with image-based observations makes it difficult to assess the method's robustness and generalizability to different types of input data. Another significant weakness is the lack of a thorough investigation into the method's robustness to changes in the environment or task parameters. The experiments are conducted in relatively static environments, and there is no evidence to suggest that the method can adapt to dynamic conditions or variations in task requirements. For instance, the paper does not explore how the method performs when the environment dynamics are perturbed or when the task goal is varied. This is a critical limitation because real-world scenarios often involve dynamic conditions and variations in task requirements. The absence of such experiments raises concerns about the method's practical applicability in real-world settings. Additionally, while the paper touches on the use of unsupervised data for pre-training the FB model, it lacks details on the specific architecture and training procedure used for state embeddings. The paper mentions a representation space $\mathbb{R}^{d}$, but it does not specify how this representation is obtained. The quality of the state embedding is crucial for the performance of the FB framework, and different architectures can produce embeddings with varying levels of semantic information. The lack of details on the state embedding architecture and training procedure makes it difficult to assess the practical considerations of using the FB framework for imitation learning. The paper refers to the implementation of Touati et al. (2023) for pre-training details, but this information should be included directly in the paper for clarity and completeness. Finally, while the paper includes an appendix that explores the impact of varying the number of demonstrations, the main paper's claim of few-shot learning is not fully validated by the experiments. The main experiments focus on a single demonstration, and the appendix only provides a limited exploration of the impact of additional demonstrations. This lack of a thorough investigation into the method's performance with varying numbers of demonstrations raises concerns about the validity of the few-shot learning claim. In summary, the paper's primary weaknesses stem from the limited scope of the experimental validation, the lack of robustness analysis, and the absence of details on the state embedding architecture. These limitations significantly impact the generalizability and practical applicability of the proposed method.
