## Summary

This paper introduces Swin4TS, a novel approach to long-term time series forecasting (LTSF) that leverages the Swin Transformer architecture, originally designed for computer vision tasks. The core idea is to adapt the Swin Transformer's window-based attention and hierarchical representation mechanisms to effectively model temporal data. The authors propose two variants of Swin4TS: a channel-dependent (CD) version, which captures correlations across both channel and time dimensions, and a channel-independent (CI) version, which processes each channel independently for improved efficiency. The CD variant employs a shifted window attention mechanism to capture both local and global dependencies, while the CI variant processes each channel separately, leading to a reduced computational cost. The authors evaluate Swin4TS on eight benchmark datasets, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance compared to both Transformer-based and non-Transformer-based baselines. The empirical results highlight the effectiveness of the proposed approach, with the CI variant often achieving superior performance on datasets with a large number of channels, while the CD variant excels on datasets with fewer channels and complex inter-channel dependencies. The paper also includes ablation studies and visualizations of attention maps to provide further insights into the model's behavior. Overall, this work presents a compelling case for the application of vision transformer architectures to time series forecasting, showcasing the potential for cross-domain transfer of advanced modeling techniques. The authors successfully demonstrate that the Swin Transformer's core mechanisms can be effectively adapted to the temporal domain, achieving state-of-the-art results on a range of long-term forecasting tasks. This work contributes to the growing body of research exploring the use of transformer architectures for time series analysis and provides a valuable reference for future studies in this area.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the authors claim that Swin4TS has linear computational complexity, the analysis provided is not sufficiently rigorous. Although the paper states that the complexity is O(ML) (where M is the number of channels and L is the sequence length), this claim is based on the window-based attention mechanism. The paper states in Section 3.2 that the complexity within a window is O(L) and then extends this to O(ML) for M independent channels. However, the paper does not provide a detailed breakdown of the computational cost for each component of the model, such as the attention layers, feed-forward networks, and the shift-window attention operation. This lack of a formal analysis makes it difficult to fully assess the scalability of the proposed method, especially when considering the hierarchical representation. The paper also lacks a comparison of the computational complexity of Swin4TS with other state-of-the-art methods, such as Transformer, Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, PatchTST, Crossformer, and TimesNet, in terms of their theoretical complexity. While Table 4 in Section 5 provides a comparison of inference time and memory usage, it does not include a theoretical analysis of the computational complexity of these models. This makes it difficult to understand the computational advantages and disadvantages of Swin4TS relative to other approaches. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not provide a formal mathematical proof of the linear complexity claim, and the analysis is based on the assumption that the window-based attention mechanism is the dominant factor in determining computational cost. Second, the paper's evaluation is primarily focused on long-term time series forecasting (LTSF), and it does not explore the applicability of Swin4TS to other time series tasks such as anomaly detection, classification, and imputation. This limits the scope of the proposed model and its potential impact on the broader field of time series analysis. The paper does not include any experiments or discussion on how Swin4TS would perform on these other tasks. This lack of evaluation leaves a gap in understanding the model's generalizability and its ability to handle different types of time series problems. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly states its focus on LTSF and mentions other tasks only as future work. Third, the paper acknowledges that the performance of Swin4TS/CD degrades on datasets with hundreds of channels, but it does not provide a detailed analysis or potential solutions to address this issue. Section 4.2 states that on datasets like Traffic and Electricity, which contain hundreds of channels, the performance of Swin4TS/CD is significantly inferior to Swin4TS/CI. The authors suggest that this may be due to the complex correlations between these hundreds of channels, making it challenging for Swin4TS/CD to uncover the underlying correlations in limited data. However, the paper lacks a thorough investigation into the reasons behind this performance drop, such as potential overfitting due to the increased number of parameters or the difficulty in capturing complex inter-channel dependencies with the current architecture. Furthermore, the absence of experiments with datasets having varying numbers of channels makes it difficult to pinpoint the exact threshold where the CD strategy starts to falter. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper explicitly acknowledges the performance issue and lacks a deeper analysis or proposed solutions. Fourth, the paper primarily compares Swin4TS with Transformer-based and non-Transformer-based baselines. While the paper includes a comparison with PatchTST, Crossformer, and TimesNet, it does not include comparisons with other CV models that have been adapted for time series analysis, such as CNN-based models or RNN-based models. This makes it difficult to assess the true novelty and effectiveness of the proposed approach in the context of time series analysis. The lack of comparison with established time-series specific models, such as those utilizing temporal convolutions or recurrent mechanisms, makes it difficult to assess the true novelty and effectiveness of the proposed approach in the context of time series analysis. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper's comparison is mainly against recent deep learning models for time series, particularly Transformer-based ones, and lacks a comparison with more traditional CV or time series models. Finally, while the paper touches upon some limitations, it lacks a broader discussion on other potential limitations, such as the sensitivity of the model to the quality and quantity of training data, or the complexity of the underlying time series patterns. The paper mentions the limitation of Swin4TS/CD on high-dimensional data but lacks a detailed discussion on other potential limitations. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper does not delve into a comprehensive discussion of factors like sensitivity to data quality, quantity, or specific types of time series patterns.

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should provide a more rigorous analysis of the computational complexity of Swin4TS. This analysis should include a detailed breakdown of the computational cost for each component of the model, such as the window-based attention, shift-window attention, and hierarchical representation. The analysis should explicitly show how the complexity scales with respect to the input sequence length (L), the number of channels (M), and the number of patches (N). Furthermore, the authors should provide a comparison of the computational complexity of Swin4TS with other state-of-the-art methods, such as Transformer, Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, PatchTST, Crossformer, and TimesNet. This comparison should be presented in a table format, clearly showing the order of complexity for each method. This will allow readers to better understand the computational advantages and disadvantages of the proposed method. Second, the authors should extend the evaluation of Swin4TS to include other time series tasks such as anomaly detection, classification, and imputation. For anomaly detection, the model could be trained on normal time series data and then used to identify anomalous patterns by measuring the reconstruction error or the likelihood of the observed sequences. For classification, the model could be adapted to extract relevant features from time series data and then fed into a classification layer to predict categorical labels. For imputation, the model could be trained to predict missing values in time series data by leveraging its understanding of temporal dependencies. These additional experiments would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the model's capabilities and limitations beyond long-term forecasting. Third, to address the performance degradation of Swin4TS/CD on high-dimensional datasets, the authors should conduct a more detailed analysis of the model's behavior with varying numbers of channels. This could involve experiments with synthetic datasets where the number of channels can be controlled, allowing for a systematic investigation of the model's scalability. The authors should also explore potential solutions, such as incorporating channel-wise attention mechanisms or using dimensionality reduction techniques before feeding the data into the model. Additionally, regularization techniques, such as dropout or weight decay, could be explored to mitigate potential overfitting issues. A thorough analysis of the computational complexity of the CD strategy with respect to the number of channels would also be valuable. Fourth, the authors should include comparisons with other CV models that have been adapted for time series analysis, such as Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) and various RNN-based architectures. This would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of Swin4TS's performance and its advantages over existing approaches. The comparison should not only focus on forecasting accuracy but also on computational efficiency and model complexity. Furthermore, the authors should consider including a discussion of the limitations of the proposed approach and potential directions for future research, such as exploring different attention mechanisms or incorporating external knowledge into the model. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This discussion should include a consideration of the factors that may affect the performance of Swin4TS, such as the quality and quantity of the training data, the complexity of the underlying time series patterns, and the presence of noise or outliers in the data. The authors should also discuss the potential failure cases of the proposed method, such as when the time series data is highly non-stationary or contains abrupt changes. The authors should also consider providing some guidelines on how to choose between Swin4TS/CD and Swin4TS/CI, based on the characteristics of the dataset. For example, the authors could discuss the trade-offs between the two methods in terms of computational cost and accuracy, and provide some recommendations on when to use each method.
