## Summary

This paper delves into the relatively unexplored area of watermark coexistence and ensembling within the realm of deep learning-based image watermarking techniques. The core contribution lies in demonstrating that multiple, independently developed watermarking methods can be applied to the same image with less interference than might be intuitively expected. This observation is significant because it suggests a pathway for embedding multiple layers of information or protection within a single image without severely compromising the integrity of each individual watermark. The authors further explore the concept of ensembling, where multiple watermarking methods are combined to increase the overall message capacity and create new trade-offs between capacity, accuracy, robustness, and image quality. This is achieved without retraining the base models, which is a notable advantage. The paper's methodological approach is primarily empirical, involving a comprehensive set of experiments where various open-source watermarking methods are applied sequentially to images. The authors meticulously measure the impact of each additional watermark on the decoding accuracy, robustness, and image quality. They explore two main ensembling strategies: series, where one watermark is applied after another, and parallel, where both watermarks are applied independently and then combined. The empirical findings reveal that different watermarking methods exhibit varying degrees of coexistence, with some pairs showing minimal impact on each other's decoding accuracy, while others demonstrate more significant interference. The experiments also show that ensembling can indeed increase the message capacity, but often at the cost of reduced accuracy and robustness. The paper also explores the use of error-correcting codes (ECC) to mitigate some of the negative impacts of ensembling. The overall significance of this work lies in its challenge to the assumption that multiple watermarks will inevitably interfere with each other. It opens up new possibilities for multi-layered watermarking and information embedding, which could have implications for copyright protection, data storage, and content authentication. However, the paper also highlights the complexities involved in managing the trade-offs between capacity, accuracy, robustness, and image quality when multiple watermarks are involved. The authors acknowledge that a deeper theoretical understanding of the underlying mechanisms of coexistence is needed to fully harness the potential of this phenomenon. In essence, this paper is a valuable exploration of a novel area, providing empirical evidence that challenges existing assumptions and opens up new avenues for research and application in the field of image watermarking. It lays the groundwork for future investigations into the theoretical underpinnings of watermark coexistence and the development of more sophisticated ensembling techniques.

## Strengths

One of the primary strengths of this paper is its exploration of a relatively uncharted territory in the field of deep learning-based image watermarking: the coexistence and ensembling of multiple watermarks. To my knowledge, this is one of the first studies to systematically investigate how different watermarking methods interact when applied to the same image. This novel contribution is significant because it challenges the common assumption that multiple watermarks will inevitably interfere with each other, potentially rendering them unusable. By demonstrating that various open-source watermarking methods can coexist with minimal impact on image quality and decoding robustness, the authors open up new possibilities for multi-layered watermarking and information embedding. This is a valuable contribution that could have implications for various applications, including copyright protection, data storage, and content authentication. Another notable strength is the comprehensive experimental analysis conducted by the authors. They meticulously test a wide range of watermarking methods, both individually and in various combinations, providing a wealth of empirical data on their coexistence and ensembling behavior. The experiments are well-designed, measuring key metrics such as decoding accuracy, robustness, and image quality. The use of both series and parallel ensembling strategies further enriches the analysis, offering insights into different approaches to combining multiple watermarks. The inclusion of error-correcting codes (ECC) to mitigate the negative impacts of ensembling is another positive aspect, demonstrating a practical approach to improving the reliability of watermarks in coexistence scenarios. The paper is generally well-written and organized, making it easy to follow the authors' arguments and findings. The use of figures and tables effectively enhances the presentation of the results, allowing for a clear visualization of the complex interactions between different watermarking methods. The authors also provide a clear explanation of their methodology, including the choice of watermarking methods, evaluation metrics, and experimental setup. This transparency is crucial for the reproducibility of the study and allows other researchers to build upon their work. Finally, the paper's exploration of ensembling as a means to increase message capacity without retraining the base models is a significant contribution. This finding has practical implications for scenarios where it is not feasible or desirable to retrain existing watermarking models, offering a flexible and efficient way to enhance their capacity.

## Weaknesses

Despite the paper's strengths, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, while the paper demonstrates the feasibility of watermark coexistence and ensembling, it lacks a deep theoretical analysis of why certain watermarking methods coexist better than others. The authors offer a "Channel coding interpretation" and a "geometric explanation" in Appendix A, but these are high-level and lack the rigor needed to fully understand the underlying mechanisms. For instance, the paper does not explore the orthogonality of the embedding spaces used by different watermarking algorithms or the statistical properties of the noise they introduce. This theoretical gap is significant because it limits our ability to predict which watermarking methods will be compatible and to design new methods that are inherently more suitable for coexistence. The absence of a solid theoretical foundation makes it difficult to generalize the findings beyond the specific set of watermarking methods tested in the paper. This weakness is supported by the observation that different pairs of watermarking methods exhibit varying degrees of coexistence (Table 1), yet the paper does not provide a theoretical explanation for these differences. The implications of this limitation are that the paper's findings are primarily empirical, and further research is needed to develop a more comprehensive understanding of watermark coexistence. I have a high confidence level in this weakness because it is consistently pointed out by both reviewers and is evident from the paper's content. Secondly, the paper does not thoroughly investigate the potential negative impacts of watermark coexistence. While it acknowledges that coexistence can lead to reduced accuracy and robustness (Figure 2), it does not delve into specific scenarios where these negative impacts are amplified. For example, the paper does not explore what happens when the watermark embedding capacities are pushed to their limits, nor does it analyze the potential bias or artifacts introduced by the super watermark. This lack of in-depth analysis is concerning because it suggests that the paper may be presenting an overly optimistic view of watermark coexistence. The implications are that in real-world applications, where watermarks may be subjected to various attacks or operate at high embedding capacities, the negative impacts of coexistence could be more significant than reported in the paper. I have a high confidence level in this weakness, as it is supported by the experimental results showing accuracy and robustness degradation (Figure 2 and 3) and the absence of experiments exploring extreme conditions. Thirdly, the paper's focus on image watermarking limits the generalizability of its findings. While the authors suggest that the same principles might apply to other media types like video, audio, or text, they do not provide any empirical evidence or a detailed discussion of the challenges and adaptations needed for these domains. This limitation is significant because it restricts the applicability of the paper's findings and leaves open the question of whether watermark coexistence is a universal phenomenon or specific to images. The implications are that further research is needed to explore the applicability of these findings in other domains, and the current paper's conclusions should be interpreted with caution when considering non-image media. I have a high confidence level in this weakness because it is explicitly acknowledged by the authors and is evident from the paper's experimental setup. Fourthly, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational overhead associated with ensembling multiple watermarking methods. While it briefly mentions that ensembling has a computational cost (Discussion section), it does not provide any quantitative measurements or discuss the implications for real-time systems or resource-constrained devices. This omission is significant because it leaves a critical gap in our understanding of the practicality of the proposed approach. The implications are that in applications where computational resources are limited, the overhead of ensembling may render it impractical, despite its benefits in terms of increased capacity. I have a high confidence level in this weakness because it is supported by the absence of any computational analysis in the experimental section and the brief mention in the discussion. Finally, the paper does not address the potential security implications of watermark coexistence. It does not explore whether the presence of multiple watermarks makes the watermarked content more vulnerable to attacks or whether an attacker could exploit the interaction between watermarks to develop more effective attacks. This omission is a significant oversight, as security is a paramount concern in any watermarking application. The implications are that the paper's findings may not be directly applicable to security-sensitive scenarios, and further research is needed to assess the robustness of coexisting watermarks against adversarial attacks. I have a high confidence level in this weakness because it is not addressed at all in the paper, and both reviewers independently identified it as a significant concern. In summary, while the paper makes a valuable contribution to the field, these weaknesses limit the scope and generalizability of its findings and highlight areas where further research is needed.
