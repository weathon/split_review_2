### Summary

The paper proposes a generic framework for learning language-informed visual concepts by distilling pretrained vision-language models. The trained concept encoders extract disentangled concept embeddings along various concept axes specified by language, which can be remixed or edited to generate images with novel concept compositions.

### Suggestions

The paper's reliance on synthetic data generated by a pre-trained Text-to-Image (T2I) model raises concerns about the potential for bias and limitations in the diversity of concepts the model can learn. While the authors argue that this approach avoids the need for human annotations, it implicitly assumes that the T2I model's understanding of visual concepts is perfect, which is unlikely to be the case. The T2I model may have its own biases or may not be able to generate images that cover the full spectrum of real-world variations in color, shape, and texture. For example, the T2I model might struggle with generating images of objects with unusual colors or materials, which could limit the ability of the proposed method to generalize to such cases. Furthermore, the reliance on a single T2I model could introduce a bottleneck, where the performance of the proposed method is limited by the capabilities of the T2I model. It would be beneficial to explore methods to mitigate these potential biases, such as using multiple T2I models or incorporating real-world data into the training process.

The lack of clarity regarding the architecture of the concept encoders is a significant issue. The paper mentions that the encoders share the same architecture but maintain separate weights, but it does not provide sufficient detail about the specific architecture used. It is unclear what type of layers are used, how many layers there are, and what the activation functions are. This lack of detail makes it difficult to reproduce the results of the paper and to understand the inner workings of the proposed method. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a comparison of the number of parameters used by the proposed method and a baseline method that does not use the concept encoders. This makes it difficult to assess the computational overhead of the proposed method. A detailed description of the concept encoder architecture and a comparison of the number of parameters with a baseline method would greatly improve the clarity and reproducibility of the paper. It would also be helpful to provide an ablation study to analyze the impact of different architectural choices on the performance of the proposed method.

The paper acknowledges that the proposed method is not robust to concepts unseen during training and proposes a test-time fine-tuning procedure to address this issue. However, the fact that this procedure is time-consuming raises concerns about the practicality of the method. The paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the time required for fine-tuning, nor does it compare the fine-tuning time with other methods. It would be beneficial to explore alternative approaches that do not require fine-tuning, such as using a more robust model architecture or incorporating techniques for handling out-of-distribution data. Furthermore, the paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the limitations of the proposed method, including specific examples of cases where the method fails to generalize to unseen concepts. This would help to identify areas for future research and to provide a more realistic assessment of the capabilities of the proposed method.
