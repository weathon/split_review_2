## Summary

This paper introduces a novel approach to training deep neural networks with differential privacy guarantees, termed Clipless DP-SGD, which leverages Lipschitz-constrained networks to circumvent the need for per-sample gradient clipping. The core idea revolves around bounding the sensitivity of gradient computation by enforcing Lipschitz constraints on the network's layers, specifically using Gradient Norm Preserving (GNP) networks. This is achieved by controlling the Lipschitz constant of each layer with respect to its parameters, which allows for a computable sensitivity bound that can be used within the Gaussian mechanism for differential privacy. The authors establish a theoretical link between the Lipschitz constant with respect to network inputs and parameters, demonstrating that bounding the Lipschitz constant of each layer enables differentially private training. The method involves a backpropagation process to compute layer-wise sensitivity bounds, which are then used to add noise to the gradients. The paper presents a detailed algorithm for this process, which includes a projection step to enforce the Lipschitz constraints. Empirically, the authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach on several datasets, showing that Clipless DP-SGD achieves competitive privacy-utility trade-offs compared to existing DP-SGD implementations. They also provide a speed and memory consumption analysis, highlighting the computational advantages of their method over per-sample clipping. The authors also provide an open-source implementation of their method, which enhances the reproducibility and accessibility of their work. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of differentially private deep learning by offering a computationally efficient alternative to per-sample gradient clipping, while maintaining strong privacy guarantees. The approach is grounded in a theoretical framework that connects the Lipschitz properties of the network to the sensitivity of the gradient computation, making it a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a compelling approach to differentially private training, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, while the paper claims to avoid per-sample gradient clipping, it does not fully address the practical challenges associated with enforcing Gradient Norm Preserving (GNP) constraints, particularly within convolutional neural networks. The paper acknowledges that strict orthogonality, a requirement for GNP networks, is difficult to enforce, especially for convolutions, and cites several works that address this issue. However, the paper does not delve into the numerical instability that can arise from attempting to enforce orthogonality with floating-point precision. This can lead to oscillations or divergence during training, which is a significant practical concern. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss how the proposed method handles non-linearities within the convolutional layers, which can further complicate the enforcement of GNP constraints. This lack of detail regarding the practical implementation of GNP constraints is a significant limitation. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the paper itself acknowledges the difficulty of enforcing strict orthogonality, and the lack of discussion on numerical instability and non-linearities is a clear omission. Secondly, the paper lacks a detailed comparison with other existing approaches for differentially private training of deep neural networks, specifically those that use regularization to enforce Lipschitz constraints. While the paper compares against standard DP-SGD implementations, it does not provide a direct comparison with methods that use explicit regularization terms to enforce Lipschitz constraints. This omission makes it difficult to understand the specific advantages and disadvantages of the proposed approach compared to these alternatives. For instance, it is unclear how the proposed method compares in terms of computational cost, memory requirements, and convergence speed to methods that use regularization. This lack of comparison is a significant weakness, as it limits the ability to contextualize the proposed method within the broader landscape of differentially private deep learning. My confidence in this weakness is high, as the experimental section clearly lacks a direct comparison with regularization-based Lipschitz constraint methods. Finally, while the paper provides a theoretical analysis of the proposed method, it does not fully explore the limitations of the method, particularly in scenarios with highly non-convex loss landscapes. The paper focuses on the application of Lipschitz constraints for DP training, but it does not discuss how the method performs in highly non-convex loss landscapes, which is a common challenge in deep learning. The focus is more on the privacy aspect enabled by Lipschitz constraints. This lack of discussion is a limitation, as it does not provide a complete picture of the method's applicability and potential challenges. This is a valid weakness, as the tightness of the bounds directly impacts the privacy-utility trade-off. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the paper acknowledges the NP-hard nature of finding the exact Lipschitz bound, but does not fully explore the implications of this on the method's performance.
