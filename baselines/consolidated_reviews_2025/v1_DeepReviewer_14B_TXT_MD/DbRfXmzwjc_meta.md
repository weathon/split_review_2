## Summary

This paper introduces MAGNet, a novel approach to molecular generation that leverages a coarse-to-fine strategy, moving from abstract shapes to specific atom and bond types. The core idea is to represent molecules as a combination of substructures, specifically rings, chains, and junctions, which are then assembled into a complete molecular graph. This approach contrasts with traditional methods that often generate molecules atom by atom, which can struggle with complex ring systems and large cycles. The authors propose a variational autoencoder (VAE) framework where the generation process is conditioned on a latent code, allowing for the generation of diverse molecules. The model first generates a shape graph, representing the connectivity of these substructures, and then uses this shape graph to guide the generation of the atom and bond types. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of MAGNet through a series of experiments, showing that it outperforms existing methods in terms of reconstruction and sampling of shapes, as well as overall generative performance on standard benchmarks. Specifically, the model shows improved expressivity, leading to molecules with more topologically distinct structures and diverse atom and bond assignments. The paper also includes an ablation study to investigate the impact of removing each component of the model, and explores the model's ability to generalize to unseen datasets. The authors highlight the model's ability to generate molecules with large rings and complex junctions, which are often difficult for other methods to handle. Overall, the paper presents a significant advancement in the field of molecular generation by introducing a novel shape-based approach that addresses the limitations of existing methods and demonstrates promising results on standard benchmarks. The use of a coarse-to-fine generation process, conditioned on a latent space, allows for greater control and diversity in the generated molecules. The paper's findings suggest that this approach could be valuable for various applications, including drug discovery and materials science.

By decomposing molecules into substructures like rings, chains, and junctions, the authors address a key limitation of atom-by-atom generation methods, which often struggle with complex ring systems and large cycles. This shape-based approach allows the model to capture the global context of a molecule more effectively, leading to more structurally diverse outputs. The use of a variational autoencoder (VAE) framework is also a strength, as it allows for the generation of diverse molecules by sampling from the latent space. The model's ability to generate molecules conditioned on the latent code provides a level of control that is not present in many other generative models. The experimental results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate that MAGNet outperforms existing methods on standard benchmarks, showing improved reconstruction and sampling of shapes, as well as overall generative performance. The model's ability to generate molecules with large rings and complex junctions is particularly noteworthy, as these are often challenging for other methods to handle. The inclusion of an ablation study, while not perfect, provides some insight into the importance of each component of the model. The authors also demonstrate the model's ability to generalize to unseen datasets, which suggests that it is not overfitting to the training data. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, with a clear explanation of the proposed method and the experimental results. The figures and tables are well-designed and help to convey the key findings of the paper. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field of molecular generation, and I believe that the proposed approach has the potential to be highly impactful.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the model's limitations and potential failure cases. While the authors mention that uncommon rings are challenging for the model during atom allocation, they do not provide a comprehensive discussion of the types of molecules that MAGNet struggles to generate. For example, it would be beneficial to know if the model has difficulty with specific ring systems, such as fused rings or macrocycles, or if there are certain chemical properties that it cannot capture effectively. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to fully assess the model's capabilities and limitations. The paper also does not discuss the potential ethical implications of using the model for molecule generation. While the authors focus on the technical aspects of the model, they do not address the potential for misuse, such as the generation of harmful or toxic compounds. This is a significant oversight, as the ability to generate novel molecules could be used for malicious purposes if not properly regulated. The paper also lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed approach. The authors do not provide information on the time and memory requirements for both training and inference, nor do they compare the model's complexity to existing methods. This makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the model, especially when dealing with large datasets or complex molecules. The paper also does not analyze the importance of the order in which the components are added during the generation process. While the authors describe a specific order of generation (shape multiset, connectivity, atom-level), they do not explore the impact of changing this order. This is a significant oversight, as the order of generation could have a substantial impact on the model's performance. The ablation study, while present, lacks depth and does not provide significant insights into the model's architecture. The removal of each component and the resulting performance drop is predictable, and the study fails to explore the nuanced interactions between different parts of the model. A more detailed ablation study should investigate the specific contributions of each component and their impact on the generated molecular structures. Finally, the inclusion of SM-LSTM as a baseline is questionable. As the paper itself shows, SM-LSTM achieves perfect validity, uniqueness, and novelty scores, making it an unsuitable comparison for evaluating MAGNet's core generative capabilities, particularly regarding validity. The paper does not provide a clear rationale for including this baseline, especially when the primary focus is on the structural and chemical validity of generated molecules. These weaknesses, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact my overall assessment of the work. My confidence in these identified issues is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and lack of discussion.

This should include a discussion of the potential for misuse, such as the generation of harmful or toxic compounds, and the safeguards that are in place to prevent such misuse. The authors should also consider the potential for bias in the training data to be reflected in the generated molecules, and how this could impact the fairness and equity of the model's applications. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed approach. This should include the time and memory requirements for both training and inference, as well as a comparison to existing methods. The authors should also discuss the scalability of the approach to larger datasets and more complex molecules. This analysis should include empirical runtime measurements on molecules of varying sizes. Fourth, the authors should investigate the importance of the order in which the components are added during the generation process. This could involve experimenting with different generation sequences and analyzing the impact on the model's performance. The authors should also analyze the generation performance under different orderings to reveal whether certain substructures are more crucial to establish early in the process. Fifth, the ablation study should be significantly enhanced to provide more meaningful insights into the model's architecture. Instead of simply removing components, the authors should investigate the specific contributions of each component and their impact on the generated molecular structures. This could involve analyzing how the removal of the shape representation module affects the diversity of generated molecules, or how the atom and bond allocation module influences the chemical validity of the results. The ablation study should also include a quantitative analysis of the generated molecules, such as the distribution of molecular weights, ring sizes, and bond types. Finally, the authors should reconsider the inclusion of SM-LSTM as a baseline. Given its perfect validity, uniqueness, and novelty scores, it does not provide a meaningful comparison for evaluating MAGNet's core generative capabilities. The authors should either provide a clear justification for its inclusion or replace it with a more appropriate baseline. These suggestions are directly connected to the identified weaknesses and are aimed at improving the overall quality and impact of the paper. By addressing these points, the authors can provide a more comprehensive and robust evaluation of their proposed method.
