## Summary

This paper introduces Neuro2Semantic, a novel framework designed to reconstruct the semantic content of perceived speech from intracranial EEG (iEEG) recordings. The core contribution lies in its two-phase approach: first, an LSTM-based adapter is trained to align neural embeddings with pre-trained text embeddings, and second, a corrector module, based on the Vec2Text model, is employed to generate continuous text from these aligned embeddings. The authors leverage transfer learning, utilizing pre-trained models for both the text embeddings and the corrector module, which is a key aspect of their methodology. The empirical findings demonstrate that Neuro2Semantic achieves strong performance, particularly in low-data settings, outperforming a baseline Bayesian decoding method. The model's ability to generalize in zero-shot settings is also highlighted as a notable strength. The experimental setup involves three subjects who listened to naturalistic conversations, and the evaluation is based on several metrics, including BLEU, ROUGE, and BERT Score. While the paper presents a promising approach to neural decoding, it also acknowledges certain limitations, such as the dependence on pre-trained models and the potential for biases inherited from these models. The overall significance of this work lies in its potential to advance the field of brain-computer interfaces and neural decoding, particularly in scenarios where data is limited. However, the paper's claims regarding the reconstruction of 'continuous language' are not fully supported by the experimental results, which are based on a listening task rather than a more general language production or comprehension task. The paper's focus on a specific listening task, while valuable, limits the generalizability of its findings and the scope of its claims. The authors' emphasis on low-data performance is a significant contribution, given the challenges of acquiring large amounts of neural data. However, the reliance on pre-trained models and the lack of a more comprehensive comparison with other state-of-the-art methods raise questions about the true novelty and robustness of the proposed framework. The paper's exploration of zero-shot generalization is a positive aspect, but further investigation is needed to fully understand the model's capabilities in this area. In summary, Neuro2Semantic presents a valuable contribution to the field of neural decoding, but its claims and limitations need to be carefully considered in the context of its specific experimental setup and methodological choices.

## Strengths

The primary strength of this paper lies in its innovative application of transfer learning to the challenging problem of neural decoding, particularly in low-data scenarios. The authors have effectively combined an LSTM-based adapter for aligning neural data with pre-trained text embeddings and a corrector module for generating continuous text. This approach demonstrates a practical method for leveraging existing resources to enhance performance in a data-constrained domain. The use of pre-trained models, specifically the text-embedding-ada-002 and the Vec2Text corrector, is a significant technical innovation that allows the model to achieve strong performance with limited iEEG data. This is particularly valuable in neuroscience, where acquiring large amounts of neural data is often difficult and expensive. The paper also demonstrates a clear and well-structured methodology, with detailed descriptions of the training process, loss functions, and evaluation metrics. The experimental setup, while limited to a listening task, is clearly defined, and the results are presented in a straightforward manner. The authors' emphasis on zero-shot generalization is another notable strength, as it suggests the model's potential to adapt to new semantic contexts without requiring extensive retraining. The paper's clear writing style and organization make it easy to understand the proposed framework and its empirical findings. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of their approach, which is a sign of intellectual honesty and rigor. The paper's focus on a specific, well-defined task allows for a clear evaluation of the proposed method's performance. The use of multiple evaluation metrics, including BLEU, ROUGE, and BERT Score, provides a comprehensive assessment of the model's reconstruction capabilities. The authors' decision to combine electrode data from multiple subjects for analysis is a pragmatic approach to address the limited availability of data, although it also raises questions about the generalizability of the results. Overall, the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field of neural decoding, with a clear focus on practical applications and a strong emphasis on low-data performance. The combination of transfer learning, a well-defined methodology, and a clear presentation of results makes this a significant contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough review of the paper and the provided analyses, several key weaknesses have been identified and validated. First, the paper's claims regarding the reconstruction of 'continuous language' are overly broad given the limited scope of the experimental evaluation. The title, 'Neuro2Semantic: A Transfer Learning Framework for Semantic Reconstruction of Continuous Language from Human Intracranial EEG,' and the introduction's discussion of potential applications in augmentative and alternative communication technologies suggest a more general capability than what is demonstrated. The experiments are based on a specific listening task, where participants listened to podcast-like conversations, and the model reconstructs the semantic content of what was heard. This is a significant limitation, as it does not address the complexities of language production or comprehension in more diverse contexts. The paper's claims imply a broader applicability to continuous language, but the empirical evidence is confined to a narrow listening task. This overreach in claims is a significant weakness, as it misrepresents the true capabilities of the proposed framework. Second, the evaluation metrics used in the paper, while standard, do not fully capture the semantic accuracy of the reconstructed text. The BLEU scores reported are below 10%, indicating poor word-level overlap with the ground truth. While the authors also report BERT Score, which is a better measure of semantic similarity, the lack of more nuanced semantic metrics is a concern. The paper does not include metrics based on contextualized word embeddings or semantic role labeling, which could provide a more detailed assessment of the model's ability to capture the meaning of the reconstructed text. Furthermore, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the types of errors made by the model, going beyond aggregate scores. This makes it difficult to understand the specific strengths and weaknesses of the approach. The example outputs provided in Figure 2B, while showing some semantic similarity, also highlight the model's tendency to generate text that does not exactly match the ground truth. This lack of detailed error analysis is a significant weakness, as it limits the insights that can be gained from the empirical results. Third, the comparison against the baseline is unfair due to the proposed framework's reliance on pre-trained models. The baseline method, a Bayesian decoding approach, does not leverage pre-trained language models in the same way as Neuro2Semantic. This gives the proposed framework an inherent advantage, especially in low-data regimes. The use of pre-trained models is a key aspect of the proposed method, but it also makes the comparison with a non-pre-trained baseline less informative. A more robust comparison would involve either adapting the baseline to also leverage pre-trained models or using a different baseline that does not rely on pre-training. The lack of a more fair comparison makes it difficult to assess the true contribution of the proposed architecture. Fourth, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of related work, particularly concerning iEEG-based language decoding methods and sequence-to-sequence models for text reconstruction from neural signals. While the authors mention some relevant studies, they do not provide a comprehensive review of existing approaches. This makes it difficult to assess the novelty and significance of their contribution. The paper should discuss methods that use similar neural data (iEEG) for language decoding, and those that employ sequence-to-sequence models for text reconstruction from neural signals. A more detailed comparison with these methods is needed to contextualize the proposed approach. Fifth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the model's limitations, particularly concerning its performance on different types of speech, its sensitivity to noise and artifacts in the iEEG data, and its dependence on electrode quality and placement. The paper does not analyze the model's performance on different types of speech, such as spontaneous speech versus scripted speech. It also does not investigate the model's sensitivity to noise or artifacts in the iEEG data, which is a critical factor in real-world applications. Furthermore, the paper does not explore the model's sensitivity to the quality and placement of electrodes, which can vary significantly across subjects and recording sessions. This lack of analysis of the model's limitations is a significant weakness, as it limits the understanding of its practical applicability. Finally, the paper's reliance on the Vec2Text corrector module introduces a single point of failure and limits the model's effectiveness in handling domain-specific or rare vocabulary. The model's performance is heavily dependent on the accuracy of this corrector, which may not be well-suited to handle out-of-vocabulary words or domain-specific terms. This dependency also introduces potential biases inherent in the corrector model, which could affect the accuracy and reliability of the text reconstruction. The paper acknowledges this limitation, but it is a significant concern that needs to be addressed. These weaknesses, all validated through careful analysis, significantly impact the overall conclusions and generalizability of the paper's findings. The overstatement of claims, the limitations of the evaluation, the unfair comparison, the lack of related work, the limited analysis of limitations, and the dependence on a single corrector module all contribute to a less robust and less informative study than it could be.

To address the identified weaknesses, several concrete and actionable improvements can be made. First, the authors should significantly temper their claims regarding the capabilities of the Neuro2Semantic framework. The title should be revised to reflect the narrow scope of the current evaluation, perhaps focusing on the specific task of reconstructing listened speech rather than making broad claims about continuous language reconstruction. The introduction should also clearly state the limitations of the current study and avoid suggesting immediate applications in augmentative and alternative communication technologies. It is crucial to acknowledge that the current results are a preliminary step towards these broader goals, rather than a demonstration of their immediate feasibility. Second, the authors should expand their evaluation metrics to include more nuanced measures of semantic similarity. This could involve using metrics based on contextualized word embeddings, such as ELMo or BERT embeddings, which can capture the meaning of words in context. They could also consider using metrics based on semantic role labeling, which can assess the accuracy of the reconstructed text in terms of its semantic structure. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the types of errors made by the model, going beyond aggregate scores. This could include a qualitative analysis of the reconstructed text, identifying common errors such as word substitutions, omissions, or additions. This would provide a more nuanced understanding of the model's strengths and weaknesses. Third, the authors should provide a more robust comparison against the baseline by either adapting the baseline to also leverage pre-trained models or using a different baseline that does not rely on pre-training. The authors could also consider performing an ablation study to quantify the contribution of the pre-trained models to the overall performance of the framework. This would help to isolate the specific benefits of the proposed architecture from the benefits of pre-training. Additionally, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the performance of the baseline, including the specific parameters used and the training procedure. This would help to ensure that the comparison is as fair as possible. Fourth, the authors should significantly expand the related work section to include a more comprehensive review of existing methods for neural decoding and semantic reconstruction, particularly those using iEEG data and sequence-to-sequence models. This should include a detailed comparison of the proposed approach with these methods, highlighting the specific advantages and disadvantages of each. For example, the authors should discuss how their LSTM-based adapter compares to other methods for aligning neural data with text embeddings, such as those based on canonical correlation analysis or other dimensionality reduction techniques. They should also discuss how their corrector module compares to other sequence-to-sequence models used for text reconstruction, such as those based on transformers or other recurrent neural networks. This would provide a more solid foundation for assessing the novelty and significance of their contribution. Fifth, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the model's limitations, including its performance on different types of speech, its sensitivity to noise and artifacts in the iEEG data, and its dependence on electrode quality and placement. This could involve experiments with different types of speech, such as spontaneous speech and scripted speech, as well as experiments with simulated noise and artifacts. The authors should also investigate the model's performance across different subjects, and how this might be affected by differences in electrode placement and data quality. This would provide a more realistic assessment of the model's practical applicability and its potential for generalization to new datasets. Sixth, to address the dependency on the Vec2Text corrector, the authors should explore methods to make the model more robust to out-of-vocabulary words and domain-specific terms. This could involve incorporating a mechanism for handling unknown words, such as using sub-word embeddings or a character-level model, or fine-tuning the corrector module on a more diverse dataset that includes a wider range of vocabulary. Additionally, the authors could investigate the use of ensemble methods, where multiple corrector modules are trained and their outputs are combined to improve robustness and reduce the impact of individual module biases. This would also help to mitigate the single point of failure issue. Finally, the authors should provide a more detailed description of the dataset used in the experiments, including the size of the dataset, the characteristics of the speakers, and the recording conditions. This information is crucial for assessing the generalizability of the results and to compare them with other studies. The paper should also specify how the data was preprocessed, including any filtering or artifact removal steps. By addressing these weaknesses, the authors can significantly improve the rigor and impact of their work.
