## Summary

This paper introduces OC-STORM, an object-centric model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) pipeline designed to enhance sample efficiency in visually complex environments. The core idea is to leverage recent advances in computer vision, specifically pre-trained segmentation models, to extract object-centric representations that are then used to train a world model and policy. The method builds upon the STORM algorithm, integrating the Cutie model for object feature extraction. The pipeline involves four main steps: first, key objects are annotated with segmentation masks in a small number of frames; second, these masks are used to extract object features via the pre-trained Cutie model; third, both the object features and raw observations are used to train an object-centric world model that predicts environmental dynamics; and finally, the policy is trained using imagined trajectories generated by the world model. The authors evaluate OC-STORM on the Atari 100k benchmark and the game Hollow Knight, demonstrating that it outperforms the baseline STORM algorithm in many cases. The empirical results suggest that incorporating object-centric representations can improve sample efficiency and overall performance in visually complex RL tasks. The paper argues that this approach addresses the limitations of traditional reconstruction-based MBRL methods, which often overlook decision-relevant details. The authors claim that their method provides a practical way to integrate recent advances in computer vision with MBRL, leading to more efficient and effective learning in complex visual environments. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to object-centric MBRL, but it also reveals several limitations that warrant further investigation. The integration of pre-trained vision models with MBRL is a promising direction, but the paper's findings also highlight the need for more robust and generalizable methods that can handle complex environments and reduce reliance on manual annotations.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several significant weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the technical novelty of the proposed method is limited. As the authors themselves acknowledge, OC-STORM is primarily an integration of the existing STORM algorithm with the pre-trained Cutie model. While this integration is practically relevant, it does not introduce significant theoretical or algorithmic advancements in the field of reinforcement learning or computer vision. The core idea of using object-centric features within an MBRL framework is not entirely new, and the paper does not sufficiently highlight the unique aspects of this specific combination compared to existing object-centric RL methods. The paper explicitly states that it builds upon STORM and uses Cutie, and this lack of fundamental innovation is a valid concern. Second, the paper lacks a thorough comparison with other object-centric representation methods. Specifically, the authors do not compare OC-STORM with methods like Focus or Slot Attention, which are relevant baselines in the field. This omission makes it difficult to assess the true performance gains of the proposed method relative to other approaches that explicitly model objects. The absence of these comparisons is a significant limitation, as it leaves open the question of whether OC-STORM offers any advantages over existing object-centric techniques. The paper only compares against STORM, and this lack of comparison is a clear weakness. Third, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by incorporating the Cutie vision model. While the authors mention that Cutie is used to extract object features, they do not quantify the additional computational cost compared to the baseline STORM model. This is a critical aspect for practical applications, as the increased computational burden might outweigh the performance gains in resource-constrained settings. The paper lacks any discussion of training time, inference time, or memory usage, making it difficult to evaluate the method's efficiency. Fourth, the proposed method requires prior knowledge, such as the number of objects and segmentation masks, which may limit its applicability in real-world scenarios. The method explicitly relies on manual annotation of objects and pre-defining the number of objects, which is a significant limitation. This reliance on manual annotation makes the method less scalable and less applicable to scenarios where such annotations are difficult or impossible to obtain. The paper clearly states the need for manual annotation and pre-defined object numbers, which supports this concern. Fifth, the paper does not address how the proposed method would handle more complex environments, such as Minecraft. The experiments are conducted on Atari 100k and Hollow Knight, but there is no mention of experiments on more complex environments. This lack of evaluation on more challenging environments raises concerns about the method's generalizability and scalability. Sixth, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. Specifically, the reliance on annotated segmentation masks for key objects raises concerns about scalability and applicability to more complex environments where such annotations are not readily available or are computationally expensive to obtain. Furthermore, the paper does not address the potential for error propagation when the object features extracted by Cutie are imperfect or when the world model struggles to accurately predict the dynamics of the environment based on these features. The paper mentions the annotation requirement but does not delve into the implications for scalability or the potential for error propagation, which is a significant oversight. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of how segmentation errors propagate through the system and affect the overall performance. The method relies on Cutie for object segmentation, and the paper does not analyze how segmentation errors affect the overall performance. This is a critical concern, as segmentation errors could significantly impair the learned dynamics model and lead to suboptimal policy learning. The paper uses Cutie but lacks an analysis of how its potential errors affect the overall performance, which is a significant weakness. In summary, while the paper presents a promising approach to object-centric MBRL, these limitations significantly impact the overall conclusions and practical applicability of the method. Second, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead introduced by incorporating the Cutie vision model. This should include a comparison of training time, inference time, and memory usage with the baseline STORM model. This analysis is essential for assessing the practicality of the method in resource-constrained settings. The authors should also investigate techniques for optimizing the computational performance of their method, such as using model compression or parallel processing. Third, the authors should explore methods to reduce or eliminate the need for manual annotations. One approach could be to investigate unsupervised or self-supervised object discovery techniques that can identify relevant objects without explicit labels. This would make the method more scalable and applicable to a wider range of real-world scenarios. Another direction could be to explore methods for learning object representations from interaction data, where the agent learns to identify and track objects based on their influence on the environment. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the method to the quality or quantity of these annotations, providing guidelines for when and how annotations should be used. Fourth, the authors should expand the evaluation of OC-STORM to more complex and diverse environments. This could include environments with more realistic physics, more complex object relationships, or more diverse visual appearances. Additionally, comparing the performance of OC-STORM with other state-of-the-art model-based reinforcement learning methods on these more complex environments would provide a more rigorous assessment of its effectiveness. While the use of Cutie for object feature extraction is a practical choice, the paper should delve deeper into how these features are leveraged within the MBRL framework. A visualization of the learned object features and their impact on the predicted dynamics could also provide valuable insights. Finally, the authors should more clearly articulate the specific technical contributions of their work beyond the simple combination of existing models. A more detailed comparison with existing object-centric reinforcement learning methods is needed to highlight the unique aspects of their approach. This should include a discussion of how their method addresses the limitations of previous approaches and what specific challenges they overcome with their particular integration of Cutie and STORM. These suggestions are all directly related to the identified weaknesses and are aimed at improving the robustness, generalizability, and practical applicability of the proposed method.
