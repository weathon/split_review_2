## Summary

This paper introduces ChEF, a Comprehensive Evaluation Framework for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), designed to provide a structured and standardized approach to assessing these models' capabilities. The framework is built upon four modular components: Scenario, Instruction, Inferencer, and Metric, which allows for versatile evaluations across different MLLMs. The authors also present six new 'recipes' for evaluating desirable capabilities, including calibration, in-context learning, instruction following, language performance, hallucination, and robustness. These recipes are intended to quantify the desiderata of MLLMs for real-world applications. The paper includes a large-scale evaluation of nine prominent MLLMs across nine scenarios and the six desiderata, aiming to provide insights into the generalizability and composite capabilities of these models. The experimental results are presented in a series of tables and figures, showcasing the performance of each model on the various evaluation tasks. The authors emphasize the modularity and scalability of ChEF, suggesting it can be adapted to include new scenarios, models, and metrics. The core contribution of this work lies in the proposed evaluation framework and the comprehensive empirical study of existing MLLMs, rather than the introduction of novel models or techniques. The paper's findings highlight the diverse strengths and weaknesses of current MLLMs, underscoring the need for a standardized approach to their evaluation. The authors argue that ChEF provides a valuable tool for researchers and practitioners to assess the capabilities of MLLMs in a more systematic and rigorous manner. The paper's focus is on establishing a robust evaluation methodology, which they believe is crucial for the continued development and deployment of MLLMs. The authors also acknowledge the limitations of their work, noting that the framework is still in its early stages and that further research is needed to address its scalability and comprehensiveness. Overall, the paper presents a significant contribution to the field by providing a structured framework for evaluating MLLMs, which is a critical step towards the development of more reliable and capable models.

## Strengths

This modularity is a significant advantage, as it enables researchers to easily modify or extend the framework to accommodate new models, tasks, and evaluation metrics. The authors' emphasis on standardization is also commendable, as it addresses the need for a more rigorous and consistent approach to evaluating MLLMs. The introduction of six new 'recipes' for evaluating desirable capabilities is another notable contribution. These recipes, which include calibration, in-context learning, instruction following, language performance, hallucination, and robustness, are crucial for assessing the practical utility of MLLMs. By quantifying these desiderata, the authors provide a more nuanced understanding of model performance beyond simple accuracy metrics. The large-scale evaluation of nine prominent MLLMs across nine scenarios and the six desiderata is a valuable contribution in itself. The empirical results provide valuable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of different models, highlighting the trade-offs between various capabilities. This comprehensive evaluation is essential for understanding the current state of the art in MLLMs and for identifying areas where further research is needed. The authors' commitment to open-source software and data is also a positive aspect, as it facilitates reproducibility and encourages further research in this area. The paper is well-written and clearly explains the methodology and experimental results. The figures and tables are well-organized and easy to understand, which enhances the overall readability of the paper. The authors have successfully identified a critical gap in the field, which is the lack of a standardized evaluation framework for MLLMs, and they have proposed a viable solution. The framework's focus on both performance and desirable capabilities makes it a valuable tool for researchers and practitioners alike. The paper's emphasis on the practical implications of MLLM evaluation is also a strength, as it highlights the importance of developing models that are not only accurate but also reliable and robust.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a valuable contribution to the field, several weaknesses warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's primary focus is on evaluating existing MLLMs using the proposed ChEF framework, and it does not introduce any new models or techniques for improving MLLMs. This is not a flaw in the framework itself, but it does limit the paper's overall impact. The paper's stated goal is to introduce an evaluation framework, and it successfully achieves this goal, but the lack of any novel model or technique means that the paper does not directly contribute to the advancement of MLLM capabilities. This is a valid concern, as the field would benefit from both robust evaluation frameworks and novel model architectures. Second, the evaluation framework, while comprehensive, may not cover all possible aspects of MLLM performance. Specifically, the framework does not explicitly address the reasoning capabilities of MLLMs in complex scenarios, such as those requiring multi-step inference or symbolic reasoning. While the framework includes scenarios like ScienceQA, which involve some level of reasoning, it does not incorporate benchmarks specifically designed for complex reasoning tasks. This is a significant limitation, as reasoning is a crucial aspect of MLLM performance, and the lack of explicit evaluation in this area means that the framework may not fully capture the capabilities of these models. The framework's reliance on specific datasets for each scenario also raises concerns about its ability to generalize to unseen tasks or data distributions. The paper does not include experiments that explicitly test the generalization capabilities of the framework or the models being evaluated. This is a critical oversight, as the ability to generalize to new tasks and data distributions is a key requirement for real-world applications of MLLMs. Furthermore, the paper does not discuss the limitations of the proposed framework in detail. While the conclusion briefly mentions that the framework is in its nascent stage and supports a limited number of scenarios and models, it does not delve into the potential shortcomings of the framework. For example, the framework's reliance on specific metrics might not fully capture the nuances of MLLM performance. The paper does not discuss the potential biases in the datasets used for evaluation, which could affect the validity of the results. The paper also does not address the scalability of the framework to a larger number of scenarios and models. This is a significant limitation, as the ability to scale to a larger number of scenarios and models is essential for the framework to be useful in practice. The lack of a detailed discussion of these limitations undermines the credibility of the framework and its potential impact. The paper also lacks a thorough discussion of the computational resources required to evaluate a large number of models across multiple scenarios. This is a critical oversight, as the computational cost of evaluation is a significant factor in the practicality of any evaluation framework. The paper does not provide any information on the time or resources required to run the experiments, which makes it difficult to assess the feasibility of using the framework in practice. Finally, the paper does not discuss the potential for adversarial attacks on the models being evaluated. This is a significant limitation, as adversarial robustness is an important aspect of MLLM performance, and the lack of any evaluation in this area means that the framework may not fully capture the vulnerabilities of these models. The paper's failure to address these limitations significantly weakens its overall contribution. ## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should expand the framework to include more complex reasoning tasks. This could involve incorporating datasets that specifically target multi-step inference, symbolic reasoning, and logical deduction. For example, the framework could be extended to include tasks from the BIG-bench benchmark, which includes a variety of challenging reasoning tasks. Additionally, the authors could explore the use of automated theorem proving or program synthesis as a way to evaluate the reasoning capabilities of MLLMs. This would require the development of new metrics that can accurately assess performance on these tasks. Second, the authors should conduct experiments to evaluate the generalization capabilities of the framework and the models being evaluated. This could involve using few-shot learning or zero-shot learning settings, where the MLLMs are evaluated on tasks that they have not been explicitly trained on. The authors should also investigate the potential biases in the datasets used for evaluation and develop methods to mitigate these biases. This could involve using debiasing techniques or evaluating the models on datasets that are specifically designed to test for biases. Third, the authors should provide a more detailed discussion of the limitations of the proposed framework. This should include a discussion of the potential shortcomings of the chosen metrics, the scalability of the framework, and the potential biases in the datasets used for evaluation. The authors should also discuss the computational resources required to evaluate a large number of models across multiple scenarios. This should include an analysis of the time and resources required to run the experiments, as well as a discussion of the potential for parallelization. Fourth, the authors should consider incorporating adversarial examples to test the robustness of MLLMs against subtle perturbations in the input. This would require the development of new metrics that can accurately assess the robustness of the models. Fifth, the authors should explore the use of more nuanced metrics that can capture the quality of the generated responses, beyond simple accuracy or F1 scores. For example, metrics that measure the coherence, consistency, and fluency of the generated text could be incorporated. The authors could also consider using human evaluation to assess the quality of the generated responses, particularly for tasks that require creative or open-ended generation. Sixth, the authors should develop a more modular and extensible architecture for the framework, which would make it easier to add new scenarios, metrics, and models. This could involve using a plugin-based architecture or a distributed computing framework. Finally, the authors should consider releasing the framework as open-source software, which would allow other researchers to contribute to its development and use it for their own research. These suggestions are directly connected to the identified weaknesses and are aimed at improving the comprehensiveness, reliability, and scalability of the framework. By addressing these limitations, the authors can significantly enhance the impact of their work.
