## Summary

This paper introduces a novel graph data augmentation method, Dual-Prism (DP), which leverages spectral graph theory to generate augmented graphs while preserving essential structural properties. The core idea revolves around the observation that low-frequency components of a graph's Laplacian spectrum are more closely tied to its inherent properties, such as connectivity and diameter, than high-frequency components. Based on this, the authors propose two augmentation strategies: DP-Noise, which adds Gaussian noise to high-frequency eigenvalues, and DP-Mask, which randomly masks these eigenvalues. The method begins by performing an eigen-decomposition of the graph Laplacian, identifying the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The augmentation is then applied in the spectral domain by modifying the high-frequency eigenvalues, while leaving the low-frequency ones untouched. The augmented graph is subsequently reconstructed using the modified eigenvalues and the original eigenvectors. The authors conduct extensive experiments across 21 real-world datasets from three different domains, evaluating the performance of DP in various learning paradigms, including supervised, semi-supervised, unsupervised, and transfer learning. The results demonstrate that DP consistently outperforms several baseline augmentation methods, suggesting that preserving the low-frequency spectrum is crucial for maintaining the core properties of graphs during augmentation. The paper also provides some theoretical insights into the relationship between the eigenvalues and graph properties, and it empirically shows that changes in graph properties are primarily reflected in the low-frequency components of the spectrum. Overall, the paper presents a well-motivated and empirically validated approach to graph data augmentation, offering a new perspective on how to generate diverse yet structurally consistent augmented graphs.

The methodology is explained in detail, with clear notation and illustrations, and the experimental section presents the results and analysis in a concise and understandable manner. Second, the core idea of using the spectral approach for graph data augmentation is both novel and interesting. The authors' observation that low-frequency components of the graph Laplacian spectrum are more closely tied to graph properties is a key insight that motivates their method. This perspective offers a fresh approach to graph augmentation, moving beyond traditional spatial-based methods. The proposed Dual-Prism (DP) augmentation strategies, DP-Noise and DP-Mask, are simple yet effective, and they are well-motivated by the spectral analysis. Third, the paper provides a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the proposed method. The authors evaluate DP on 21 real-world datasets across three different domains, using four different learning settings. The results consistently demonstrate that DP outperforms the baselines, providing strong empirical evidence for the effectiveness of the proposed method. The inclusion of various learning paradigms, including supervised, semi-supervised, unsupervised, and transfer learning, further strengthens the paper's claims. Finally, the paper offers some theoretical insights into the relationship between the eigenvalues and graph properties, which adds to the paper's overall contribution. The authors link the second smallest eigenvalue, the Fiedler value, to graph connectivity and diameter, providing a theoretical foundation for their approach. The empirical observation that changes in graph properties are primarily reflected in the low-frequency components of the spectrum is also a significant contribution. These theoretical and empirical insights, combined with the strong experimental results, make this paper a valuable contribution to the field of graph learning.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. First, while the paper presents some spectral analysis insights, some of these observations are well-known in spectral graph theory literature. For instance, the observation that low-frequency components are more resilient to edge alterations is a common theme in the field. While the paper does cite relevant works, particularly for observations 1 and 4, the connection to existing literature could be more explicit, especially for observation 2. The paper could benefit from a more detailed discussion of how its specific observations build upon or differ from existing results in spectral graph theory. This lack of explicit connection to existing literature weakens the novelty of the spectral insights. Second, the proposed methods are based on the assumption that the low-frequency part of the spectrum is more important than the high-frequency part for preserving graph properties. While the empirical results support this assumption, a more rigorous theoretical justification is needed. The paper does provide some theoretical links between low-frequency eigenvalues and graph properties, but a more formal analysis of why preserving low-frequency components is crucial for maintaining graph properties would be beneficial. The paper relies more on empirical observation than a rigorous theoretical proof for this assumption. This lack of a strong theoretical foundation makes the method appear somewhat ad-hoc. Third, the paper lacks a clear explanation of how to choose the hyperparameters of the proposed method, such as the frequency ratio, the augmentation ratio, the standard deviation, and the augmentation type. While the paper provides the hyperparameter settings used in the experiments and shows the impact of some hyperparameters, it lacks a systematic explanation or guidelines for choosing these values for different datasets or tasks. The selection process appears to be primarily empirical, and the paper does not provide any guidance or intuition on how to select them for different datasets or tasks. This lack of guidance makes it difficult to apply the method effectively in practice. Fourth, the paper does not provide a clear comparison of the proposed method with other spectral-based graph augmentation methods. While the paper mentions GCL-SPAN in the related work section, a more detailed comparison is needed to understand the relative strengths and weaknesses of the proposed method. The paper includes GCL-SPAN as a baseline in the unsupervised experiments, but a more thorough comparison with other relevant spectral-based methods, especially in the supervised and semi-supervised settings, is missing. This lack of comparison makes it difficult to assess the novelty and contribution of the proposed method in the context of existing spectral-based graph augmentation techniques. Fifth, the paper does not provide a clear analysis of the limitations and potential drawbacks of the proposed method. The paper mentions limitations in the conclusion, such as the computational cost, the dependence on the quality of the eigen-decomposition, and the possibility of generating unrealistic or invalid graphs. However, it lacks a detailed analysis of these limitations throughout the paper and a discussion of potential solutions or mitigations. The paper should provide a more detailed analysis of the limitations and potential drawbacks of the proposed method, and discuss how they can be addressed or mitigated. Finally, the proposed method is based on the eigen-decomposition of the Laplacian matrix, which is computationally expensive. The paper acknowledges this but doesn't provide a detailed analysis of its performance on large graphs or compare its computational efficiency with other methods. The paper also does not provide a clear explanation of how the proposed method can be applied to directed graphs or dynamic graphs. The paper focuses on undirected graphs and doesn't provide any explanation or methodology for applying the proposed method to directed or dynamic graphs. These limitations restrict the applicability of the method to a specific class of graphs and learning scenarios.
