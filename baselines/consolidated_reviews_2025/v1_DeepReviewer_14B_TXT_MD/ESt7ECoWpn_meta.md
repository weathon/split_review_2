## Summary

The paper introduces a novel game-theoretic approach to achieve per-instance differential privacy (pDP) by proposing the Noise Variance Optimization (NVO) game. In this game, each data instance is treated as a player, and the strategy involves selecting the noise variance to be added to that specific instance. The utility function is defined as a weighted sum of a privacy assurance term, which encourages the game to satisfy pDP, and a utility preservation term, which aims to maintain the utility of the data. The authors claim that the Nash Equilibrium (NE) of this game ensures pDP across all data instances while enhancing the statistical utility of the dataset. They propose a Best Response Dynamics (BRD) algorithm to find the NE and test the performance of this algorithm on two real datasets, comparing it with the standard Laplace mechanism. The paper's core contributions lie in its innovative use of game theory to address pDP and the theoretical proof that the NE ensures pDP. However, the paper has several significant limitations, including the lack of comparison with state-of-the-art pDP mechanisms, the absence of a rigorous proof for Theorem 4.1, and the lack of a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the proposed algorithms. These limitations undermine the practical relevance and robustness of the proposed method, making it difficult to assess its true value in the context of existing differential privacy techniques.

## Strengths

The paper makes several valuable contributions to the field of differential privacy (DP). Firstly, it introduces a novel game-theoretic approach to achieving per-instance differential privacy (pDP), which is a significant departure from traditional methods that apply uniform noise across all data instances. This approach allows for the assignment of different noise variances to each data point, potentially leading to better utility preservation while maintaining privacy. The authors provide a theoretical analysis of their method, demonstrating that the Nash Equilibrium (NE) of the NVO game ensures pDP across all data instances. This theoretical foundation is crucial for understanding the privacy guarantees of the proposed mechanism. Additionally, the paper includes experiments on two real datasets, which demonstrate the practical applicability of the NVO game. The empirical results show that the proposed method can achieve better utility compared to the standard Laplace mechanism, particularly in terms of the root mean squared error (RMSE) for regression tasks and the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence for distribution preservation. The introduction of the NVO game and the BRD algorithm to find the NE is a technical innovation that adds to the paper's value. The authors also provide a clear motivation for the need to move beyond the one-size-fits-all approach of traditional DP mechanisms, highlighting the limitations of the Laplace mechanism in terms of utility preservation. Overall, the paper's strengths lie in its innovative approach, theoretical analysis, and empirical validation, which together provide a solid foundation for further research in pDP.

## Weaknesses

Despite its novel contributions, the paper has several significant weaknesses that need to be addressed. One of the most critical issues is the lack of comparison with state-of-the-art pDP mechanisms. The paper only compares the proposed NVO game with the conventional Laplace mechanism, which is a basic and widely known method. The current state-of-the-art for achieving pDP involves optimizing noise levels for each data point, and the paper fails to demonstrate how the proposed game-theoretic approach compares to these more advanced techniques. This omission makes it difficult to assess the practical significance and advantages of the NVO game. For instance, the optimal solution for pDP mechanisms, which involves solving an optimization problem to determine the noise levels for each data point, is not discussed or compared against. This is a significant oversight, as it leaves the reader unsure of the method's relative performance and utility. Another major weakness is the absence of a rigorous proof for Theorem 4.1. The theorem claims that at the Nash Equilibrium, the game satisfies pDP for all data instances. However, the proof provided in the paper is insufficient and relies on the assumption that if a player can improve their utility by deviating from their current strategy, then the current strategy profile is not a Nash Equilibrium. This logic is flawed because it is possible for a player to deviate and either improve or worsen their utility, and the game would still be at a Nash Equilibrium as long as no player can improve their utility. The paper needs to rigorously show that at equilibrium, all data points simultaneously satisfy the pDP condition, which is not the case in the current version. The lack of a formal proof undermines the theoretical foundation of the paper and raises doubts about the validity of the privacy guarantees.
