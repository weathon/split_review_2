## Summary

This paper introduces MotionRL, a novel multi-reward optimization framework designed for text-to-motion generation, with a specific focus on incorporating human preferences. The core idea revolves around fine-tuning existing text-to-motion models using reinforcement learning, guided by a multi-objective reward function that balances text adherence, motion quality, and human perceptual preferences. The authors leverage a pre-trained computational perception model, drawing from prior work in the field, to provide a quantitative measure of human preference. To manage the multi-objective nature of the problem, they employ a Pareto-based optimization strategy, which aims to identify a set of non-dominated solutions that represent the best trade-offs between the different objectives. The method also incorporates reward-specific identifiers, which are intended to allow users to control the trade-offs between different objectives during inference. The experimental evaluation of MotionRL is conducted on the HumanML3D dataset, where the authors demonstrate that their approach achieves state-of-the-art performance in terms of both quantitative metrics and human evaluations. Specifically, they report improvements in FID and R-Precision scores compared to baseline methods, and they also present user study results that suggest their method generates motions that are more aligned with human preferences. The authors also conduct an ablation study to assess the impact of different reward combinations on the overall performance. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to text-to-motion generation by explicitly considering human perception, which has been largely overlooked in previous methods. The use of reinforcement learning to optimize for multiple objectives, along with the introduction of reward-specific identifiers, represents a significant step forward in the field. However, as I will discuss in detail, there are several areas where the paper could be strengthened, particularly in terms of providing more detailed explanations of the methodology, conducting more comprehensive experiments, and addressing some of the limitations of the proposed approach.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be commendable. First and foremost, the paper's core contribution—applying reinforcement learning to incorporate human perception in text-to-motion generation—is a significant and novel step. The authors have identified a critical gap in the field, where most existing methods focus on optimizing numerical metrics without explicitly considering human preferences. By introducing a multi-objective optimization framework that balances text adherence, motion quality, and human preferences, they have addressed this gap in a creative and effective manner. The use of a pre-trained computational perception model, while not entirely novel in itself, is a clever way to quantify human preferences and integrate them into the reinforcement learning process. This allows the model to learn not only to generate motions that are semantically aligned with the input text and of high quality but also to generate motions that are visually appealing and natural to human observers. The Pareto-based multi-objective optimization strategy is another strength of the paper. By using this approach, the authors are able to identify a set of non-dominated solutions that represent the best trade-offs between the different objectives. This is particularly important in the context of text-to-motion generation, where there is often no single optimal solution that simultaneously maximizes all objectives. The introduction of reward-specific identifiers is also a valuable contribution, as it allows users to control the trade-offs between different objectives during inference. This adds a layer of flexibility and control that is often lacking in existing methods. Finally, the experimental results, including both quantitative metrics and human evaluations, demonstrate that MotionRL achieves state-of-the-art performance on the HumanML3D dataset. The authors have shown that their method not only improves numerical metrics but also generates motions that are more aligned with human preferences. The ablation study, while not as comprehensive as I would have liked, does provide some evidence that the different reward components contribute to the overall performance of the method. In summary, the paper presents a well-motivated, technically sound, and empirically validated approach to text-to-motion generation that makes a significant contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that I believe need to be addressed. First, the motivation for the study, while present, is not as clearly articulated as it could be. The introduction states that existing methods require motion length as input, which may lead to motion quality degradation, but it does not provide specific examples of what these problems might be. Similarly, the authors mention that existing methods ignore human preferences, but they fail to provide a detailed explanation of the issues that arise from this oversight. This lack of clarity weakens the motivation for the proposed method. Second, while the paper introduces a novel approach to text-to-motion generation, the novelty of the proposed method is somewhat limited by the use of existing techniques. The authors acknowledge that reward-specific tokens and batch-wise Pareto-optimal selection are borrowed from previous works, but they fail to articulate how their approach differs from these existing techniques. It is not sufficient to simply state that these techniques are used in a different context. The authors should explain the specific modifications or adaptations they have made to these techniques to suit the text-to-motion domain. This lack of detailed explanation makes it difficult to assess the true novelty of the proposed method. Third, the experimental results, while showing improvements over the baseline, are not as compelling as they could be. In Table 1, the proposed method shows only a marginal improvement over MoMask, with a 0.002 increase in FID and a 0.010 increase in Top-1 R-Precision. While the authors also present user study results, the visual presentation of these results in Figure 3(b) makes it difficult to distinguish the success rates of different methods. This lack of clear and substantial improvements raises questions about the practical significance of the proposed method. Fourth, the ablation study is not as comprehensive as it should be. The study focuses on the impact of different reward combinations but does not analyze the impact of individual components within each reward or the sensitivity to hyperparameters. This lack of detailed analysis makes it difficult to understand the relative importance of each reward component and the robustness of the method to different hyperparameter settings. Fifth, the paper mentions using reward-specific tokens to control trade-offs during inference but does not provide much detail on how these tokens are designed or how users would specify their preferences. This lack of practical details makes it difficult to assess the usability and practical value of this aspect of the method. Sixth, the paper lacks a discussion of the computational cost of the proposed method compared to baselines. This is a significant omission, especially given the use of reinforcement learning, which can be computationally expensive. Without a clear understanding of the computational cost, it is difficult to assess the practical feasibility of the proposed method. Seventh, the paper does not adequately address the potential biases in the human preference data used to train the perception model. The authors rely on a pre-trained perception model without acknowledging its inherent limitations or discussing the demographics of the human raters. This lack of discussion raises concerns about the generalizability of the method to different populations and contexts. Eighth, the paper does not explore the impact of different perception models or the possibility of fine-tuning the perception model jointly with the motion generator. This is a missed opportunity, as different perception models may have different biases and strengths, and joint training could potentially lead to a more tightly coupled system. Finally, the paper does not explicitly address the scalability of the proposed method to more complex motion sequences. The experiments are limited to the HumanML3D dataset, and there is no discussion of how the method would perform on datasets with more complex or longer sequences. These limitations, taken together, suggest that while the paper presents a promising approach, there are several areas where further work is needed to fully validate its effectiveness and practical value.
