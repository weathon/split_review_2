## Summary

This paper introduces the concept of model kinship as a metric to guide the merging of large language models (LLMs), drawing an analogy to biological kinship. The authors propose that model kinship, quantified by the similarity of weight differences between models, correlates with performance gains in merged models. They first demonstrate this correlation empirically, showing that models with higher kinship tend to exhibit greater average performance gains when merged. Based on this finding, they introduce a novel merging strategy called Top-k Greedy Merging with Model Kinship. This strategy iteratively selects and merges models with high kinship, aiming to improve multitask performance and avoid local optima. The core idea is that merging models with similar 'genetic' information (i.e., weight differences) leads to more effective combinations. The authors conduct experiments using Mistral-7B based models and evaluate the merged models on a set of benchmark datasets, including Winogrande, GSM8K, and TruthfulQA MC2. They report that their proposed merging strategy achieves better performance compared to a greedy merging approach, and that model kinship can be used as an early stopping criterion, improving the efficiency of the merging process. The paper's main contribution lies in the introduction of model kinship as a guiding metric for model merging and the demonstration of its effectiveness in improving merging performance. However, the paper also has several limitations, including a narrow experimental scope, a lack of theoretical justification for the kinship metric, and a marginal performance improvement. Despite these limitations, the paper presents an interesting approach to model merging and provides a starting point for further research in this area. The authors' work highlights the potential of leveraging model similarity to guide the merging process, and their findings suggest that model kinship could be a useful tool for improving the performance of merged LLMs. However, further investigation is needed to validate the generalizability of their findings and to address the identified limitations.

## Strengths

I found the introduction of model kinship as a concept to be a novel and interesting contribution. The analogy to biological kinship provides an intuitive way to think about model merging, and the authors' attempt to quantify this concept using weight differences is a creative approach. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, which makes the core ideas accessible to a broad audience. The authors provide a clear description of their proposed merging strategy, Top-k Greedy Merging with Model Kinship, and they present their experimental results in a well-organized manner. The empirical analysis, while limited in scope, does provide some evidence to support the claim that model kinship correlates with performance gains in model merging. The authors' demonstration that model kinship can be used as an early stopping criterion is also a valuable finding, as it suggests a way to improve the efficiency of the merging process. The paper's focus on improving the efficiency of model merging is a practical and relevant goal, and the authors' proposed method offers a potential solution to this problem. The use of multiple benchmark datasets for evaluation, while not exhaustive, provides a reasonable assessment of the proposed method's performance. The authors also provide a clear explanation of their experimental setup and methodology, which allows for a fair evaluation of their results. Overall, the paper presents a novel approach to model merging that is grounded in empirical analysis and offers a promising direction for future research.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I have identified several significant weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, the paper's experimental evaluation is notably limited in scope. The authors conduct all experiments using models based on the Mistral-7B architecture, as explicitly stated in Section 3.1: "All models are either fine-tuned or merged from the *Mistral-7B* architecture." This lack of diversity in model architectures raises serious concerns about the generalizability of the findings. The Mistral model family, while effective, may possess unique characteristics that make the observed kinship correlations less applicable to other model families. For example, models with different attention mechanisms or pre-training datasets might exhibit different kinship patterns and merging behaviors. This limitation is further compounded by the fact that the authors only use a specific set of three benchmarks, Winogrande, GSM8K, and TruthfulQA MC2, for the core merging experiments, as mentioned in Section 4.1: "Each model demonstrates distinct strengths across three benchmarks: Winogrande (5-shot), GSM8k (5-shot), and TruthfulQA MC2 (0-shot)." While the paper mentions using a set of six tasks (ARC, HellaSwag, MMLU, TruthfulQA, Winogrande, GSM8K) for initial correlation analysis, the core merging experiments focus on the average performance across a specific set of tasks. This limited dataset selection primarily focuses on specific types of reasoning and question-answering tasks, and it does not fully capture the potential benefits or limitations of the proposed merging approach across different task domains. The absence of evaluation on a broader range of tasks, such as natural language understanding, common sense reasoning, or code generation, makes it difficult to assess the robustness of the proposed method. This narrow experimental scope significantly undermines the paper's claims of general applicability. Second, the paper lacks a strong theoretical justification for the proposed model kinship metric. The authors motivate the concept by drawing an analogy to biological kinship, as stated in Section 2.2: "Motivated by the parallel drawn between biological reproduction and the process of model evolution (as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:compare}), we propose that the concept of \emph{kinship}, which is central to evolutionary biology for understanding breeding relationships and human genealogies \citep{thompson1985}, can be conceptually extended to the field of model merging, specifically to describe the kinship between models." While this analogy provides an intuitive starting point, it does not provide a rigorous theoretical link to why the similarity of weight differences should be a good indicator for merging success. The paper does not delve into existing theories on model parameter spaces or transfer learning to provide a clear rationale for the proposed metric. This lack of theoretical grounding makes it difficult to understand the underlying mechanisms that drive the observed kinship correlations and how they relate to the merging performance. The authors should provide a more rigorous argument, potentially drawing from existing theories on model parameter spaces and transfer learning, to justify why weight difference similarity would be a good indicator for merging success. Third, the performance improvement achieved by the proposed method is marginal. In Table 2, the best model achieved an average task performance of 69.13 using the proposed method, while the greedy strategy stabilized at 68.72. This difference of only 0.41% is not substantial, and the authors do not provide any statistical analysis to determine if this difference is statistically significant. Without this, it's difficult to assess the practical implications and whether the added complexity of the kinship-based method is justified by the performance gain. The lack of statistical significance testing makes it impossible to determine if the observed improvement is due to the proposed method or simply due to random chance. Furthermore, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the practical implications of such a small gain. It is unclear if this minor improvement justifies the complexity introduced by the kinship-based selection process. Fourth, the paper focuses primarily on merging models derived from the same base model. As stated in Section 2.2: "For a model merging process where models have been fine-tuned or merged from the same base model, the model kinship $r$ can be defined using the similarity metric function $sim (\cdot, \cdot)$, which measures the similarity between two model weight differences." The authors do not discuss or evaluate the applicability of their method to models with different initializations or architectures. This limitation restricts the generalizability of the findings and raises questions about the robustness of the proposed method in more diverse scenarios. Fifth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with calculating model kinship and performing the merging process. This information is crucial for assessing the practicality of the proposed approach, especially when dealing with large language models. While the paper mentions a 30% improvement in time efficiency due to early stopping, it does not provide a comprehensive breakdown of the time and memory requirements for the proposed method compared to baselines. Finally, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of potential limitations or failure cases of the proposed merging strategy. While the experimental results show improvements in certain scenarios, the paper doesn't explore situations where the method might underperform or fail. This omission makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the proposed method and its potential limitations. These weaknesses, taken together, significantly limit the impact and generalizability of the paper's findings. My confidence in these identified issues is high, as they are directly supported by the paper's content and lack of analysis.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, the authors should significantly expand the scope of their experimental evaluation. This should include using a wider range of model architectures, such as the Llama family, which have different pre-training procedures and architectural designs. This would help to determine whether the observed kinship correlations and the effectiveness of the proposed merging strategy are consistent across different model families. Furthermore, the authors should consider using a wider range of evaluation datasets that cover various tasks, including natural language understanding, common sense reasoning, and code generation. This would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the proposed merging strategy's capabilities and limitations. For example, datasets like GLUE or SuperGLUE could be used to evaluate the model's performance on a variety of natural language understanding tasks, while datasets like BigBench could be used to assess its performance on a wide range of reasoning tasks. Additionally, code generation datasets could be used to evaluate the model's ability to generate code. By expanding the evaluation to include these diverse datasets, the authors can provide a more robust and comprehensive assessment of their proposed merging strategy. Second, the authors should provide a more robust theoretical foundation for the proposed model kinship metric. They should explore existing literature on model parameter spaces, transfer learning, and feature representations to provide a clear rationale for why the similarity of weight differences should be a good indicator for model merging potential. For instance, they could investigate whether the proposed metric correlates with the alignment of feature spaces or the compatibility of learned representations. A deeper analysis of the underlying mechanisms that drive successful model merging would strengthen the motivation for using model kinship. Furthermore, the authors should consider exploring alternative metrics for measuring model similarity, such as those based on activation patterns or gradient flow, and compare their effectiveness with the proposed weight-based metric. Third, to address the marginal performance gains, the authors should conduct a more rigorous statistical analysis of their results. This should include reporting confidence intervals or p-values to determine the significance of the observed improvements. Additionally, they should provide a more detailed analysis of the practical implications of the performance gains. For example, they could investigate whether the proposed method leads to improvements in specific downstream tasks or applications. It would also be beneficial to explore the sensitivity of the method to different hyperparameter settings and to analyze the computational overhead introduced by the kinship-based selection process. The authors should also consider comparing their method with other model merging techniques to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of its effectiveness. Fourth, the authors should investigate the applicability of model kinship to models with different initializations or architectures. This could involve exploring techniques for aligning or normalizing model weights before calculating kinship, or adapting the kinship metric to account for architectural differences. For example, one could consider using a layer-wise kinship measure, where each layer is compared independently and then aggregated, which might be more robust to architectural variations. Fifth, the authors should provide a more detailed analysis of the computational cost associated with calculating model kinship and performing the merging process. This should include a breakdown of the time and memory requirements for each step, as well as a comparison to other merging techniques. It would be beneficial to explore methods for reducing the computational overhead, such as using low-rank approximations or efficient distance metrics. This analysis should also consider the scalability of the approach to larger models and datasets. Finally, the authors should conduct experiments to identify scenarios where the proposed merging strategy might fail or underperform. This could involve testing the approach on models with different levels of kinship, or on tasks that require specific expertise not captured by the kinship metric. For example, it would be useful to examine the performance of the merged models on tasks that are significantly different from the tasks used to calculate kinship. The authors should also investigate the sensitivity of the merging strategy to the choice of hyperparameters, such as the value of k in Top-k Greedy Merging. A thorough analysis of these limitations would provide a more complete understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of the proposed approach and guide its practical application. By addressing these points, the authors can significantly strengthen their paper and make a more compelling case for the effectiveness and generalizability of their proposed model merging strategy.
