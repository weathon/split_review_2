## Summary

This paper introduces a novel graph-based framework for addressing the dual challenges of out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization and detection. The core idea revolves around constructing a graph where nodes represent data samples, and edges capture relationships between them. The framework leverages spectral learning with wild data (SLW), which involves performing spectral decomposition on the graph's adjacency matrix. A key contribution is the demonstration that minimizing the SLW objective is equivalent to performing this spectral decomposition, enabling the quantification of OOD generalization and detection performance. The paper proposes a spectral contrastive loss derived from this decomposition, which aims to learn representations that are robust to distribution shifts. The method is evaluated on several image classification datasets, including CIFAR-10, CIFAR-10-C, SVHN, LSUN, Places365, and Textures, and compared against existing OOD generalization and detection methods. The empirical results suggest that the proposed method achieves improvements in both OOD generalization and detection compared to the baselines. The paper also provides a theoretical analysis, attempting to connect the spectral properties of the graph to the performance of OOD generalization and detection. The authors argue that their approach provides a unified framework for handling both OOD generalization and detection, which are often addressed separately in the literature. The paper's significance lies in its novel graph-based approach and its attempt to provide a theoretical foundation for understanding the interplay between OOD generalization and detection. However, as I will discuss in detail, there are several limitations in the theoretical analysis, experimental evaluation, and the clarity of the presentation that need to be addressed to fully realize the potential of this work.

## Weaknesses

After a thorough examination of the paper, I have identified several weaknesses that significantly impact its overall contribution. Firstly, the paper makes a strong assumption about the composition of the unlabeled data, which is a critical component of the proposed method. The paper explicitly states that the unlabeled data is a mixture of in-distribution (ID), covariate-shifted OOD, and semantically shifted OOD data, with a specific mixture ratio. This assumption, as stated in Section 2, is that the unlabeled set $\mathcal{D}_{u}$ consists of ID, covariate OOD, and semantic OOD data, drawn from the mixture distribution $\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{wild}}:=(1\ \bar{-}\,$ $\pi_{s}\,-\,\pi_{c})\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{in}}\,+\,\pi_{c}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{covariate}}\,+\,\pi_{s}\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{semantic}}$. The paper also mentions in Section 5.1 that the mixture ratio is set to $\pi_{c}=0.5$ and $\pi_{s}=0.1$, following the setup of Bai et al. (2023). This assumption is problematic because, in real-world scenarios, it is often difficult to guarantee that unlabeled data will contain all three types of data in such a specific ratio. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the proposed method to deviations from this specific mixture ratio. For example, what happens if the unlabeled data is predominantly in-distribution with very little OOD data, or vice-versa? The paper lacks a detailed analysis of how the performance degrades under different mixture ratios, which is a significant oversight. This limitation undermines the robustness and generalizability of the proposed method, as its performance may be highly dependent on the specific composition of the unlabeled data. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's explicit assumptions and the lack of any sensitivity analysis. 

Secondly, the paper's choice of using KNN distance for OOD detection, while simple and aligned with the theoretical analysis, is a limitation. As stated in Section 5.1, the paper uses a simple nonparametric KNN distance, which does not impose any distributional assumption on the feature space. While the paper acknowledges that this method aligns with the theoretical analysis, it does not explore other OOD detection methods or discuss the limitations of KNN distance. The choice of KNN distance, while simple, might not capture complex relationships in the feature space, and a more sophisticated distance metric or a learned distance function could potentially yield better results. The paper lacks a discussion on the limitations of KNN distance and how it might affect the overall performance, especially in high-dimensional spaces where KNN can suffer from the curse of dimensionality. This is a significant limitation because the performance of the OOD detection component is directly tied to the choice of distance metric. The paper's failure to explore alternative methods or discuss the limitations of KNN distance weakens the overall evaluation of the proposed framework. My confidence in this weakness is high, as it is directly supported by the paper's explicit choice of KNN distance and the absence of any discussion of its limitations or alternative methods. 

Thirdly, the paper's evaluation is limited in scope, focusing primarily on image classification tasks with specific datasets. While this statement is mathematically correct, the paper does not provide a clear explanation of the relationship between the low-rank approximation of the adjacency matrix and the singular value decomposition.
