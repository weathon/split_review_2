## Summary

This paper introduces Empowerment through Causal Learning (ECL), a novel framework that integrates causal structure learning with empowerment-driven exploration in model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL). The core idea is that by understanding the causal relationships within an environment, an agent can achieve better controllability and learning efficiency. ECL operates in three main steps: first, it learns a causal dynamics model from collected data, using a causal mask to identify relevant state dimensions; second, it optimizes this model through empowerment-driven exploration, where the agent seeks to maximize the difference in empowerment between the causal and dense dynamics models; and third, it learns policies for downstream tasks, incorporating a curiosity reward to balance causality and mitigate overfitting. The framework is designed to be method-agnostic, allowing for the integration of various causal discovery techniques. The authors evaluate ECL across six environments, including both simple and complex tasks, as well as pixel-based environments, demonstrating its superior performance compared to other causal MBRL methods in terms of causal discovery accuracy, sample efficiency, and asymptotic performance. The empirical results suggest that ECL's approach to combining causal understanding with empowerment-based exploration leads to more effective learning and control. The paper's main contribution lies in its innovative approach to leveraging causal structures to guide exploration and improve controllability, addressing limitations of existing methods. By iteratively refining the causal model through empowerment-driven exploration, ECL aims to create a more controllable and efficient learning process. The inclusion of a curiosity reward further enhances the framework's ability to learn robust policies. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to model-based reinforcement learning that has the potential to advance the field.

## Strengths

I find several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. First, the core idea of integrating causal structure learning with empowerment-based exploration in model-based reinforcement learning is both novel and compelling. This approach addresses a critical limitation of existing methods by actively leveraging causal structures to guide exploration and improve controllability. The paper clearly articulates the motivation behind this approach, arguing that empowerment, when coupled with causal understanding, can lead to more efficient and effective learning. Second, the paper is well-organized and clearly written, making it easy to follow the proposed framework and its components. The authors provide a detailed explanation of the ECL framework, including the three main steps: model learning, model optimization, and policy learning. The use of figures and examples, such as the robot manipulation task, helps to illustrate the concepts and the overall workflow of the framework. The mathematical formulations are presented clearly, and the overall structure of the paper is logical and easy to follow. Third, the empirical results presented in the paper are compelling. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of ECL through extensive experiments across six environments, including both simple and complex tasks, as well as pixel-based environments. The results show that ECL outperforms other causal MBRL methods in terms of causal discovery accuracy, sample efficiency, and asymptotic performance. The inclusion of ablation studies, while not perfect, provides some insight into the contribution of different components of the framework. The paper also includes a comparison with several baselines, further strengthening the empirical evidence. Finally, the method-agnostic design of ECL is a significant strength. The framework's ability to integrate various causal discovery methods makes it adaptable to different types of environments and tasks. This flexibility is a valuable asset and enhances the potential impact of the work. The authors have successfully demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of their approach, paving the way for further research in this area.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant careful consideration. First, while the paper claims that the ECL framework is method-agnostic, the experimental evaluation only demonstrates its effectiveness with two specific causal discovery methods: constraint-based (ECL-Con) and score-based (ECL-Sco). Although these methods represent different approaches, the paper does not provide evidence that ECL can be effectively combined with a wider range of causal discovery techniques. This limitation undermines the claim of method-agnosticism to some extent, as it remains unclear how ECL would perform with other causal discovery methods based on different principles. Second, the paper lacks a detailed, algorithmic comparison with existing causal model-based RL methods, particularly CDL. While the paper mentions CDL and includes it as a baseline, it does not provide a granular comparison of the specific mechanisms by which ECL's empowerment-driven exploration interacts with and refines the causal model during learning. The paper describes ECL's components and workflow, but it does not explicitly contrast these mechanisms with CDL's approach, which relies on conditional independence tests for causal discovery without the empowerment-driven exploration loop. This lack of detailed comparison makes it difficult to fully understand the specific advancements and unique aspects of ECL. Third, the ablation studies, while present, are not comprehensive enough. While the paper includes ablation studies on different reward components in the appendix, the main paper lacks a detailed explanation of how the empowerment objective is optimized and how this optimization affects the learned policies. The paper does not explore the sensitivity of the results to different curiosity reward coefficients, nor does it provide a step-by-step breakdown of the optimization process for the empowerment objective. This lack of detail makes it difficult to fully understand the contribution of each component and how they interact. Fourth, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational complexity of the ECL framework. While the paper describes the different steps of the framework, it does not provide a breakdown of the time complexity for each stage or compare it to other model-based reinforcement learning methods. This lack of analysis makes it difficult to assess the scalability and practicality of the method, especially in more complex environments. Fifth, the paper lacks a thorough discussion of the limitations of the ECL framework. While the conclusion mentions future work on handling changing dynamics, the main paper does not discuss the potential failure modes of the causal discovery process, such as when the underlying assumptions of the causal model are violated or when the data is insufficient for reliable causal inference. The paper also does not discuss how the performance of ECL degrades in environments with highly stochastic transitions or when the causal structure changes over time. Finally, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the sensitivity of the ECL framework to hyperparameters. While the appendix contains some hyperparameter analysis, the main paper does not provide a comprehensive sensitivity analysis for various hyperparameters. This lack of analysis makes it difficult for practitioners to implement the framework and adapt it to their specific needs. These weaknesses, taken together, highlight areas where the paper could be improved to provide a more complete and robust analysis of the proposed framework.

## Suggestions

To address the identified weaknesses, I recommend several concrete improvements. First, to strengthen the claim of method-agnosticism, the authors should evaluate the ECL framework with a wider range of causal discovery techniques, including those based on different principles, such as score-based methods with different optimization strategies and constraint-based methods with different assumptions about the underlying data distribution. This would involve not only implementing these methods but also analyzing the performance of the overall framework under different causal discovery settings. For example, how does the performance vary when using methods with different levels of sensitivity to noise or different assumptions about the underlying data distribution? This would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the framework's robustness and generalizability. Second, to provide a more detailed comparison with existing methods, the authors should include a table that explicitly contrasts ECL with other causal model-based RL methods, such as CDL, highlighting the key differences in their approaches to causal discovery, empowerment-driven exploration, and policy learning. This table should include specific details on the mathematical formulations, algorithmic steps, and the interaction between causal structure learning and exploration. For example, the table could compare how each method initializes the causal structure, how they update the structure during learning, and how they use the learned structure for exploration and policy learning. Furthermore, the authors should provide a more detailed explanation of how ECL's empowerment-driven exploration differs from standard exploration techniques used in other model-based RL methods, and how this difference contributes to the improved performance. Third, to improve the ablation studies, the authors should conduct a more thorough analysis of the impact of the curiosity reward and the empowerment-driven exploration on the overall performance and learning efficiency. This should include experiments with different curiosity reward coefficients to assess the sensitivity of the results to this parameter. The authors should also provide a detailed explanation of how the empowerment is calculated and how it is used to guide exploration. This should include a discussion of the optimization process for the empowerment objective and how this optimization affects the learned policies. Furthermore, the authors should analyze the exploration behavior of the agent with and without the empowerment objective to demonstrate the benefits of this approach. The ablation study should also include a comparison of the performance of the agent when trained with and without the curiosity reward to isolate the impact of each component. Fourth, the authors should provide a more rigorous analysis of the computational demands of the ECL framework. Specifically, a breakdown of the time complexity for each stage—model learning, model optimization, and policy learning—would be valuable. For instance, the causal discovery step, which involves constraint-based or score-based methods, can have varying computational costs depending on the chosen algorithm and the size of the state space. Similarly, the empowerment calculation, which involves entropy estimation, can be computationally intensive, especially in high-dimensional state spaces. A detailed analysis should include not only the asymptotic time complexity but also the practical runtime measurements on the environments used in the experiments. This would allow for a better understanding of the scalability of ECL and its suitability for different types of problems. Furthermore, it would be beneficial to compare the computational cost of ECL with other model-based reinforcement learning methods, both causal and non-causal, to provide a clearer picture of its relative efficiency. Fifth, the paper should also explore the robustness of the ECL framework under various challenging conditions. For example, the performance of causal discovery algorithms can be significantly affected by noisy or incomplete data. It would be important to analyze how ECL performs when the observed data is limited or when the environment contains stochastic transitions that are not captured by the causal model. Furthermore, the paper should investigate the sensitivity of ECL to violations of the assumption of a static causal structure. In many real-world scenarios, the underlying causal relationships may change over time, and it is important to understand how ECL would adapt to such non-stationarities. This could involve experiments in environments with time-varying causal structures or by introducing perturbations to the environment that alter the causal relationships. A thorough analysis of these limitations would provide a more complete picture of the applicability of ECL. Finally, a more detailed hyperparameter analysis is needed to ensure the practical applicability of the ECL framework. The paper should provide a sensitivity analysis of the key hyperparameters, such as the learning rates for the different models, the regularization parameters for causal discovery, and the parameters related to the empowerment calculation. This analysis should include not only the final performance metrics but also the convergence speed and the stability of the learning process. It would be beneficial to show how the performance of ECL varies across different hyperparameter settings and to provide guidelines for selecting appropriate values for different types of environments. This would make it easier for practitioners to implement ECL and to adapt it to their specific needs. Furthermore, the paper should discuss the potential interactions between different hyperparameters and how these interactions can affect the overall performance of the framework.

First, how does the computational complexity of the ECL framework compare to other model-based reinforcement learning methods, both causal and non-causal? A detailed analysis of the time complexity for each stage of the framework, along with practical runtime measurements, would be valuable. Second, what are the limitations of the ECL framework, and how does its performance degrade in environments with highly stochastic transitions or changing causal structures? A thorough discussion of the potential failure modes of the causal discovery process and the framework's robustness under various challenging conditions is needed. Third, how sensitive is the ECL framework to hyperparameters, and how do different hyperparameter settings affect the performance of the framework? A comprehensive sensitivity analysis of the key hyperparameters, along with guidelines for selecting appropriate values, would be beneficial. A granular comparison of the specific mechanisms by which ECL's empowerment-driven exploration interacts with and refines the causal model during learning is needed. Fifth, can the authors conduct more comprehensive ablation studies to evaluate the impact of the curiosity reward and empowerment-driven exploration on the overall performance and learning efficiency? A detailed explanation of how the empowerment is calculated and how it is used to guide exploration, along with experiments with different curiosity reward coefficients, would be valuable. Finally, what are the specific mechanisms by which ECL's empowerment-driven exploration interacts with and refines the causal model during learning, and how does this differ from the approach used in CDL? A more detailed explanation of the mathematical formulations and algorithmic steps that differentiate ECL from CDL is needed. These questions target key uncertainties and methodological choices, seeking clarification of critical assumptions and limitations of the proposed framework.
