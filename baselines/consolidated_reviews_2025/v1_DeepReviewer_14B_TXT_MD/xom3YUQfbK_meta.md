## Summary

This paper introduces "Model Manager," a novel framework designed to leverage large language models (LLMs) for comparing and explaining the differences between machine learning (ML) models. The core idea is to use LLMs to generate human-understandable explanations of the discrepancies in predictions made by two models when presented with the same input data. The framework operates by sampling input instances from a dataset and then serializing these instances, along with the corresponding predictions from the two models being compared, into a JSON format. This JSON data is then fed into an LLM via a zero-shot prompt, which instructs the LLM to analyze the patterns and inconsistencies in the model outputs and summarize these differences in natural language. The authors evaluate their framework on three classification datasets—Blood, Diabetes, and Car—using three different ML models: Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). They employ three state-of-the-art LLMs—GPT-4, Claude, and Llama 2—as verbalizers and conduct ablation studies to analyze the impact of various design choices, such as the inclusion of model internals in the prompt. The primary evaluation metric is the accuracy with which an external LLM can infer the predictions of the second model based on the verbalization generated by the framework and the input data. The results indicate that the framework is particularly effective in verbalizing the differences between parametric models like LR and DT, while it faces more challenges with non-parametric models like KNN. The authors also explore the impact of including model-specific information, such as decision rules for decision trees and feature importance scores for logistic regression, on the quality of the generated verbalizations. Overall, the paper presents a promising approach for enhancing the interpretability and comparability of ML models, with potential applications in model management and debugging. However, the study is limited by its focus on classification models and tabular data, and the evaluation methodology relies on an LLM's ability to accurately interpret the model differences, which may introduce biases.
