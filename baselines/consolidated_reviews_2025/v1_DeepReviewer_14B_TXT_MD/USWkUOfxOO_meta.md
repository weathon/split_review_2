## Summary

This paper introduces Pseudo-Calibration (PseudoCal), a novel post-hoc framework designed to address the issue of poorly calibrated predictive uncertainty in unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA). The core idea behind PseudoCal is to treat UDA calibration as a target-domain-specific unsupervised problem, rather than a covariate shift problem across domains. This perspective allows the authors to focus on the unique challenges of calibration within the target domain itself. The key methodological innovation of PseudoCal lies in its use of inference-stage mixup to generate a labeled pseudo-target dataset. Specifically, the method applies mixup to unlabeled target samples during inference, creating synthetic samples with corresponding pseudo-labels. This process is grounded in the cluster assumption, which posits that samples within the same cluster are likely to share the same label. By mixing samples from different clusters, the method generates a diverse set of pseudo-labeled target samples that are intended to mimic the correct-wrong prediction statistics of the real target data. Once this pseudo-labeled dataset is created, PseudoCal employs temperature scaling to calibrate the model's predictions. Temperature scaling is a well-established post-hoc calibration technique that adjusts the model's output probabilities by dividing the logits by a learned temperature parameter. The authors argue that by applying temperature scaling to the synthesized pseudo-target set, they can effectively calibrate the model's predictions for the real target domain. The empirical evaluation of PseudoCal is extensive, covering a range of UDA scenarios and datasets. The authors demonstrate that PseudoCal consistently outperforms alternative calibration methods, including source-only calibration and other UDA-aware calibration techniques. The experiments are conducted across various benchmark datasets for domain adaptation, including Office-31, Office-Home, and DomainNet, among others. The results show that PseudoCal achieves superior calibration performance, as measured by metrics such as Expected Calibration Error (ECE). The paper also includes ablation studies to analyze the impact of different components of the method, such as the mixup ratio and the use of different augmentation techniques for generating the pseudo-target set. These studies provide insights into the effectiveness of the proposed approach and the importance of the different design choices. Overall, the paper makes a significant contribution to the field of unsupervised domain adaptation by addressing the important problem of predictive uncertainty calibration. The proposed PseudoCal framework offers a novel and effective approach to this problem, and the extensive empirical evaluation provides strong evidence for its effectiveness. The paper is well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience of researchers and practitioners in the field of machine learning.

This shift in perspective allows for the development of a more tailored and effective calibration technique. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow. The authors provide clear explanations of the proposed method, the experimental setup, and the results. The use of figures and tables effectively illustrates the key concepts and findings, making the paper accessible to a broad audience. The paper's contribution to the field of unsupervised domain adaptation is significant. ## Weaknesses

While I appreciate the novelty and empirical success of the proposed PseudoCal method, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, the paper's claim of novelty is somewhat undermined by its reliance on a combination of existing techniques. As the authors themselves acknowledge, PseudoCal leverages inference-stage mixup, the cluster assumption, and temperature scaling. While the specific combination of these techniques for UDA calibration is novel, the individual components are not. This raises concerns about the overall technical contribution of the paper. The authors do not provide a sufficiently detailed justification for why this particular combination is superior to other possible approaches. A more rigorous analysis of the interplay between these components is needed to demonstrate a genuine advancement over existing methods. For instance, the paper lacks a theoretical analysis of how the distribution of pseudo-labels generated by the mixup process compares to the true label distribution and how this impacts the effectiveness of temperature scaling. This lack of theoretical grounding weakens the paper's claims of novelty and makes it difficult to fully understand the method's behavior. Secondly, the ablation study is inadequate to fully assess the contribution of each component. The comparison with "PseudoCal-same" is insufficient to isolate the effects of mixup and temperature scaling. The authors should have conducted a more comprehensive ablation study by systematically removing or replacing each component. For example, what happens if mixup is replaced with a simpler interpolation method, or if temperature scaling is replaced with a different calibration technique? The absence of such experiments makes it difficult to determine whether the observed performance gains are truly due to the specific combination of techniques or if they can be attributed to other factors. The current ablation study leaves open the question of whether the observed performance gains are genuinely due to the specific combination of techniques, or if they can be attributed to other factors. This lack of a thorough ablation study significantly weakens the paper's empirical claims. Thirdly, the paper lacks a comprehensive analysis of the method's limitations. While the authors mention that PseudoCal can be applied in limited source access scenarios, they do not explicitly investigate the performance of PseudoCal with limited unlabeled target data. This is a crucial aspect, as real-world applications often involve scenarios with scarce target data. Furthermore, the analysis of performance under extreme domain shifts is limited. The paper includes experiments on partial-set UDA, which can be considered a form of domain shift, but it does not explore scenarios where the target domain exhibits significant changes in data distribution, such as variations in lighting, viewpoint, or background. Such experiments would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the limitations of PseudoCal and its applicability in diverse settings. The absence of a thorough investigation into these limitations makes it difficult to assess the practical applicability of the method in real-world scenarios. Fourthly, the paper does not provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of PseudoCal compared to other calibration methods. While the authors mention that the method is efficient, they do not provide quantitative comparisons of the runtime and memory usage. This is an important consideration, especially when dealing with large-scale datasets or resource-constrained environments. A quantitative comparison of the computational cost of PseudoCal with other calibration methods would be valuable. This should include a breakdown of the time spent on pseudo-label generation, clustering, and temperature scaling. The lack of such an analysis makes it difficult to assess the practical implications of using PseudoCal in real-world applications. Finally, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the hyperparameter sensitivity of PseudoCal. While the authors mention that the performance is relatively stable across a range of mixup ratios, they do not provide a rigorous analysis of the impact of different mixup ratios on the quality of the pseudo-labels and the final calibration performance. Similarly, the authors do not provide sufficient guidance on how to choose the optimal number of clusters for k-means. While they mention that the number of clusters is set to the number of classes in the target domain, they do not investigate the sensitivity of the method to this parameter, especially when the true number of classes is unknown or when there is a class imbalance in the target domain. This lack of a thorough hyperparameter analysis makes it difficult to reproduce the results and to apply the method effectively in practice. In summary, the paper's weaknesses stem from a lack of theoretical grounding, an inadequate ablation study, a limited analysis of limitations, an absence of computational overhead analysis, and a lack of hyperparameter sensitivity analysis. These weaknesses undermine the paper's claims of novelty and make it difficult to fully assess the practical applicability of the proposed method. The confidence level in these identified issues is generally high, as they are based on direct observations from the paper's content and the absence of crucial experiments or analyses.

The ablation study should also include a comparison with other calibration methods applied to the pseudo-labeled data to determine if the performance gains are specific to temperature scaling. Thirdly, the authors should conduct a more thorough investigation into the robustness of PseudoCal under various challenging conditions. Furthermore, they should explore scenarios where the target domain exhibits significant changes in data distribution, such as variations in lighting, viewpoint, or background. Such experiments would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the limitations of PseudoCal and its applicability in diverse settings. The authors could also consider experiments with noisy target data or scenarios where the target domain contains novel classes not present in the source domain. Fourthly, the authors should provide a detailed analysis of the computational overhead of PseudoCal compared to other calibration methods. This should include a quantitative comparison of the runtime and memory usage, as well as a breakdown of the time spent on each step of the method (pseudo-label generation, clustering, and temperature scaling). The authors should also analyze the scalability of PseudoCal with respect to the size of the target dataset and discuss the potential for parallelizing the different steps to improve efficiency. This analysis would help readers understand the practical implications of using PseudoCal in resource-constrained environments.
