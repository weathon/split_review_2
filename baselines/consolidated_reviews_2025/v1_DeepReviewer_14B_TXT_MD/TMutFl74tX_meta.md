## Summary

This paper introduces a meta-learning approach designed to address the challenge of learning from multiple noisy annotators, particularly in scenarios with limited labeled data. The core idea revolves around embedding data points into a latent space using a neural network and then employing a probabilistic model, specifically a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), to learn task-specific classifiers while simultaneously estimating the reliability of different annotators within this latent space. The method leverages the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm to adapt the classifier to new tasks using a small amount of annotated data, and the entire process is meta-learned to improve performance on unseen tasks. The authors demonstrate the effectiveness of their approach on real-world datasets, including those with synthetic noise and crowdsourced data. The method's key contribution, as presented by the authors, lies in its integration of classifier learning from noisy annotators directly into the meta-learning phase, which they argue distinguishes it from prior work that often uses pre-trained models as feature extractors and then applies methods for noisy labels. The experimental results show that the proposed method achieves competitive performance compared to several baselines, including methods specifically designed for noisy labels and meta-learning adaptations of these methods. The paper's significance lies in its attempt to bridge the gap between meta-learning and learning from noisy labels, a crucial area for real-world applications where labeled data is often scarce and unreliable. However, the paper also has some limitations, particularly in the lack of a dedicated discussion of limitations and the use of relatively small datasets, which I will elaborate on in the following sections.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be technically sound and promising. The core idea of combining meta-learning with a probabilistic model for handling noisy labels is a valuable contribution. The authors' approach of embedding data points into a latent space and then using a GMM to model the data distribution and annotator reliability is a well-established technique, and its application in this context is both logical and effective. The use of the EM algorithm for adapting the classifier to new tasks with limited data is also a sensible choice, and the fact that the EM steps are differentiable allows for efficient backpropagation through the entire model, which is a key advantage. Furthermore, the paper's experimental setup is quite comprehensive, including comparisons against a range of baselines, including methods specifically designed for noisy labels and meta-learning adaptations of these methods. The inclusion of both synthetic and real-world datasets, such as the LabelMe dataset, provides a good evaluation of the method's performance under different conditions. The authors also clearly articulate the differences between their approach and existing methods, highlighting the integration of classifier learning from noisy annotators during the meta-learning phase as a key distinction. This is a crucial point, as it addresses a limitation of some prior work that uses pre-trained models as feature extractors and then applies methods for noisy labels. The paper's attempt to bridge the gap between meta-learning and learning from noisy labels is a significant contribution, as it addresses a critical challenge in real-world applications where labeled data is often scarce and unreliable. The technical soundness of the proposed method, combined with its empirical evaluation, makes this a valuable contribution to the field.

## Weaknesses

While the paper presents a technically sound approach, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further discussion. Firstly, while the authors claim novelty in integrating classifier learning from noisy annotators during the meta-learning phase, the core idea of using meta-learning to handle noisy labels has been explored in previous works, as acknowledged by the authors themselves. Although the paper distinguishes itself by directly learning classifiers on target tasks during meta-learning, the incremental contribution over existing meta-learning approaches for noisy labels is not entirely clear. The authors should have provided a more detailed comparison to highlight the unique aspects of their approach and to clearly articulate the specific challenges they address that are not covered by existing methods. For instance, a more thorough analysis of how the proposed method handles different types of annotator noise compared to existing meta-learning techniques would have been beneficial. Secondly, the paper lacks a dedicated section or explicit discussion of the limitations of the proposed method. This is a significant oversight, as it is standard practice in academic papers to discuss the assumptions made by the method, the potential failure cases, and the computational cost. While these datasets are appropriate for the problem setting, the paper could have benefited from including experiments on larger and more complex datasets, such as Tiered-ImageNet, to further demonstrate the scalability and generalizability of the proposed method. The use of larger datasets would have also allowed for a more robust evaluation of the method's performance under different conditions. My confidence in this weakness is medium, as the datasets used are standard, but the point about larger datasets is valid. The absence of experiments on larger datasets limits the conclusions that can be drawn about the method's scalability and generalizability.
