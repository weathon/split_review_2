## Summary

This paper introduces Maestro, a novel framework for training low-rank decompositions of deep neural networks (DNNs) by integrating a low-rank ordered decomposition directly into the training process. The core idea is to progressively find the optimal decomposition for each layer of a DNN while training, using a low-rank ordered decomposition that allows for dynamic selection of ranks at a layer granularity. This approach builds upon the concept of ordered dropout, extending it to the low-rank factorization of weights. The method aims to address the challenges of finding optimal ranks for each layer during training, which is often a manual and time-consuming process. Maestro employs a hierarchical group lasso penalty to encourage entire columns/rows to become zero, effectively reducing the rank of the decomposed weight matrices. This process is integrated into the training loop, allowing the low-rank structure to adapt to the data distribution. The authors evaluate Maestro on several models, including ResNet-18, VGG-19, and a 6-layer transformer, across tasks such as MNIST, CIFAR-10, and English-German translation. The experimental results demonstrate that Maestro achieves competitive or better performance compared to existing low-rank methods like Pufferfish and Cuttlefish, while also achieving comparable or better accuracy than these methods on ImageNet-1k. The paper also explores the trade-off between accuracy and model size by varying the hierarchical group lasso penalty. The authors argue that their method is superior to static low-rank methods like SVD because it adapts to the data during training, allowing for a more optimal low-rank representation. Overall, the paper presents a compelling approach to training low-rank DNNs, offering a dynamic and data-driven alternative to static decomposition methods. The integration of low-rank factorization into the training process is a key contribution, potentially leading to more efficient and accurate models. However, several aspects of the method and its evaluation require further clarification and analysis, which I will discuss in detail in the following sections.

## Strengths

I found several aspects of this paper to be particularly strong. The core idea of integrating low-rank decomposition directly into the training process, rather than applying it as a post-processing step, is a significant contribution. This approach allows the low-rank structure to adapt to the data distribution, potentially leading to more optimal representations compared to static methods like SVD. The authors clearly articulate the motivation for their method, highlighting the limitations of existing low-rank techniques that rely on iterative decompositions of layers. The use of a low-rank ordered decomposition, which allows for dynamic selection of ranks at a layer granularity, is another notable innovation. This approach provides a flexible way to control the trade-off between model size and accuracy. The experimental results, while not without limitations, demonstrate the potential of the proposed method. The authors evaluate Maestro on a variety of models and datasets, showing that it can achieve competitive or better performance compared to existing low-rank methods. Specifically, the results on ImageNet-1k are encouraging, demonstrating that Maestro can be applied to large-scale datasets. The inclusion of an ablation study, which analyzes the impact of different components of the method, is also a strength. This study provides insights into the contribution of each component to the overall performance of Maestro. The paper is generally well-written and easy to follow, making it accessible to a broad audience. The authors clearly explain the technical details of their method, and the experimental setup is well-described. The use of figures and tables to present the results is effective. Finally, the authors provide a theoretical analysis that shows how Maestro recovers SVD for uniform data and PCA for identity mapping, which provides a theoretical foundation for their method. Overall, I believe that the paper presents a novel and promising approach to training low-rank DNNs, with several technical innovations and empirical achievements.

## Weaknesses

Despite the strengths of this paper, I have identified several weaknesses that warrant further attention. First, while the paper explicitly states that Maestro is an extension of ordered dropout and provides the correct citation, the relationship between the two methods could be made clearer in the abstract and introduction. The paper mentions that Maestro is a "non-trivially extended version of Ordered Dropout," but the specific modifications and improvements over the original ordered dropout approach are not explicitly stated in the abstract and introduction. This lack of clarity could lead to confusion for readers unfamiliar with ordered dropout. Second, the paper compares Maestro to Pufferfish and Cuttlefish, but it does not provide a detailed explanation of how these methods work. While the related work section briefly describes these methods as leveraging low-rank approximations for efficient inference, a more thorough description of their core mechanisms, including how the low-rank decomposition is performed and how the rank is determined during training, is needed. This lack of detail makes it difficult to fully understand the differences and advantages of Maestro compared to these methods. The paper relies on the reader referring to the original papers for these methods, but a brief description in the appendix would be beneficial. Third, the paper lacks a detailed description of the training procedures of the baselines. While the paper mentions that the baseline results are taken from the original works, it does not provide explicit details about the training procedures of the baselines within the paper itself. This lack of detail makes it difficult to assess the fairness of the comparison between Maestro and the baselines. Fourth, the paper's argument that Maestro is superior to SVD because it adapts to the data during training is not entirely convincing. While the method does adapt to the data, so do other regularization techniques. The authors need to provide a more compelling reason why the rank ordering should change throughout training, and why this is superior to a one-time SVD decomposition. The current explanation that the data is transformed by earlier layers is not sufficient. The paper should provide a theoretical analysis or empirical evidence that demonstrates the benefit of dynamically adjusting the rank during training, rather than relying on a static decomposition. For example, they could analyze the singular values of the weight matrices at different stages of training to show how the rank structure evolves and why this is beneficial. Fifth, the paper does not present the Pareto frontier of its method, i.e., results for a range of ranks. Instead, the paper primarily focuses on specific operating points, which makes it difficult to assess the true potential of the method. A more comprehensive visualization of the accuracy-size trade-off would be beneficial. Sixth, the paper is missing some key baselines. Specifically, it does not compare Maestro to a standard network with structured pruning applied, a one-time SVD decomposition followed by unstructured pruning, or a one-time SVD decomposition without any additional pruning. These comparisons are essential to understand if the iterative nature of the proposed method is truly beneficial compared to simpler alternatives. Seventh, the paper lacks a detailed description of the hyperparameter tuning process, especially for the $\lambda_{gl}$ parameter. The current description is vague and does not provide enough information to reproduce the results. The authors should also clarify what they mean by 'relative GMACs' and why the GMACs increase when the hierarchical group lasso penalty is removed. It is also important to clarify the difference between progressive shrinking and unstructured pruning. While both methods reduce the number of parameters, they do so in different ways, and the authors should explain why progressive shrinking is more beneficial in this context. Finally, the paper lacks a detailed analysis of the computational cost of the proposed method, including the overhead of the progressive shrinking and the SVD decomposition. These weaknesses, which I have verified through direct examination of the paper, significantly impact my assessment of the paper's contributions and require further attention.
