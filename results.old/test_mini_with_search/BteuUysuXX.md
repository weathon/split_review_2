## Summary

This paper proposes "verbose images"—imperceptible adversarial perturbations designed to maximize the length of sequences generated by large vision-language models (VLMs), thereby increasing their energy consumption and latency during inference. The method combines three loss objectives (delayed EOS, token-level uncertainty via entropy maximization, and sequence-level token diversity via nuclear norm maximization) with a temporal weight adjustment algorithm. Experiments across four VLM models and two datasets show 7.87× and 8.56× average increases in generated sequence length over original images, substantially outperforming prior methods (sponge samples, NICGSlowDown).

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated and empirically grounded attack target.** The paper establishes an approximately linear relationship between generated sequence length and both energy consumption and latency time using actual NVML measurements (Figure 1). This directly motivates the attack goal (maximizing sequence length) rather than targeting a proxy.

2. **Three complementary loss objectives with clear justification.** Each loss addresses a distinct aspect of the problem: delayed EOS (Eq. 1) directly suppresses termination, uncertainty loss (Eq. 2) breaks token-level output dependency via entropy maximization, and token diversity loss (Eq. 3) breaks sequence-level dependency via nuclear norm maximization of hidden states. The ablation study (Table 4) confirms each loss contributes individually and their combination yields the best result (226.72 vs. 119.46 for L₁ alone on BLIP-2/MS-COCO).

3. **Consistent and large improvements across diverse VLMs and datasets.** Table 1 shows verbose images achieve the highest sequence length, energy, and latency in every setting across four models (BLIP, BLIP-2, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4) and two datasets (MS-COCO, ImageNet). Gains range from ~2.2× (InstructBLIP) to ~31.8× (BLIP) over original images, and the method substantially outperforms both sponge samples and NICGSlowDown baselines.

4. **Systematic ablation and sensitivity analysis.** Tables 4–6 isolate the contributions of each loss function, the temporal weight adjustment components (temporal decay + momentum), and perturbation magnitude ε. The analysis shows that both temporal decay and momentum contribute meaningfully, and documents the expected trade-off between sequence length and perceptual quality (LPIPS).

5. **Interpretability analysis connecting the attack to attention dispersion and hallucination.** GradCAM visualizations (Figure 5) show the attack disperses attention across the entire image, and CHAIR metrics (Table 2) document massive increases in object hallucination (e.g., BLIP CHAIR_i from 11.41% to 79.93%), providing mechanistic insight into *why* the attack succeeds.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Mathematical error in the temporal weight adjustment formula.** Equation (5) defines λ₂(t) = ||L₂||₁ / ||L₂||₁ / 𝒯₂(t). With the stated parameters a₂=0, b₂=0 (Section 5.1), the temporal decay function 𝒯₂(t) = 0·ln(t) + 0 = 0 for all t. Division by 𝒯₂(t) is therefore undefined. The experimental results in Table 5 show that the temporal decay component *does* improve performance (152.49 → 226.72 on BLIP-2/MS-COCO), so the actual implementation must differ from the written formula—either a₂ is not truly 0, the intended formula uses a different operator (e.g., multiplication instead of division by 𝒯), or the "/ /" notation means something other than sequential division. This is a genuine reproducibility gap that must be corrected for the paper to be self-contained.

2. **Evaluation scope does not match the claimed threat model.** The introduction and threat model (Section 3.2) frame verbose images as a general attack against VLMs "including image captioning, visual question answering, and visual reasoning." The problem formulation (Eq. 2) also mentions c_in as "a placeholder ∅ in image captioning or a question in visual question answering and visual reasoning." Yet every experiment evaluates only the captioning task with default prompt templates. No VQA or visual reasoning experiments are reported. This does not invalidate the captioning results, but the paper's claims are broader than the evidence. The authors should either add at least one non-captioning experiment or narrow the claims accordingly.

### Minor

1. **No variance reporting for a stochastic process.** The paper averages results over three runs (line 235) but reports no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or any per-image distribution statistics for the main results. Given that nucleus sampling introduces substantial randomness and the attack effectiveness depends on the specific sampling paths encountered during optimization, mean-only reporting makes it difficult to assess the reliability of the reported multiples (7.87×, 8.56×). The length distribution plots (Figure 4) partially address this across images, but run-level variance is absent.

2. **Gradient flow through autoregressive generation is not discussed.** The token diversity loss (Eq. 4) maximizes the nuclear norm of hidden states from generated tokens, and the uncertainty loss (Eq. 2) involves the output probability distribution. Both depend on tokens generated by the VLM's autoregressive process. The paper does not explain how gradients flow through this discrete sampling process (e.g., whether Gumbel-softmax, straight-through estimators, or another approximation is used). This is standard practice in adversarial attack papers on VLMs, but documenting it would aid reproducibility.

3. **CHAIR evaluation on ImageNet.** The CHAIR metric (Table 2) is designed for captioning tasks with ground-truth object annotations from MS-COCO. The paper also applies it to ImageNet, which is a classification dataset. It is unclear what ground-truth object annotations were used for ImageNet CHAIR computation. This should be clarified.

4. **InstructBLIP's baseline length is an outlier.** InstructBLIP generates 63.79 tokens on clean MS-COCO images—~6× more than BLIP (10.03) and BLIP-2 (8.82). Consequently, its relative improvement (2.2×) is far smaller than for BLIP (31.8×) and BLIP-2 (25.7×). The paper does not discuss this disparity or what it implies about model susceptibility.

### Trivial

- The linear correlation plots (Figure 1) show only two of the four evaluated models (BLIP-2 and MiniGPT-4); adding the other two would be more comprehensive.
- The ablation table column header reads "LIPIS" (likely a typo for "LPIPS").

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation on sampling policy parameters (temperature, top-p/top-k) would be useful, as the attack's effectiveness likely depends on these.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the temporal weight formula.** Clarify whether a₂ is actually 0, whether the division by 𝒯(t) should be multiplication, or whether the notation means something other than sequential division. Provide the exact executable update rule.
2. **Add one non-captioning experiment** (e.g., VQA on a model like InstructBLIP or MiniGPT-4) or explicitly narrow the scope claims to image captioning.
3. **Report standard deviations** for the main results (Table 1) across the three runs, or provide per-image distribution statistics beyond the histograms.
4. **Clarify how gradients are computed** through the autoregressive generation for L₂ and L₃.
5. **Clarify the ground-truth annotations** used for CHAIR on ImageNet.
