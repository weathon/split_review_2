## Summary

ChEF presents a modular evaluation framework for Multimodal Large Language Models, decomposing evaluation into four components (Scenario, Instruction, Inferencer, Metric) that can be composed into *Recipes*. It introduces six additional evaluation dimensions beyond accuracy (calibration, in-context learning, instruction following, language performance, robustness, hallucination) and conducts a large-scale evaluation of 9 MLLMs across 9 scenarios. The framework's key insight is that existing MLLM benchmarks can be expressed as different recipes within ChEF, enabling standardized comparison.

## Strengths

- **Modular framework design that subsumes existing benchmarks.** The decomposition into four composable components (Scenario, Instruction, Inferencer, Metric) is a clean architectural contribution. Section 3.2 provides concrete examples (Flickr30k and VOC2012) showing how the components compose, and Figure 1b demonstrates that existing benchmarks (MME, MMBench, SEEDBench, etc.) can be expressed as ChEF recipes. This provides a genuinely useful organizing principle for the fragmented MLLM evaluation landscape.

- **Six new evaluation desiderata beyond accuracy.** The paper introduces dedicated recipes for calibration (ECE), in-context learning (RIAM), instruction following (MR), language performance (GPT-based), robustness (RRM), and hallucination (POPE-based). Section 3.3 defines each with specific metrics and recipe diagrams (Figure 4). This is a substantive contribution toward holistic MLLM assessment.

- **Reliable inference via PPL-based evaluation is convincingly demonstrated.** Figure 5 shows for CIFAR10 and ScienceQA that PPL inference dramatically reduces accuracy variance across query variations compared to direct free-form output, while Multi-Turn (CoT + PPL) achieves the best accuracy–stability trade-off. The boxplots across three MLLMs make this evidence concrete and actionable.

- **Large-scale systematic evaluation.** 9 MLLMs are evaluated across 9 scenarios and 6 desiderata, producing a standardized comparison (Table 1, Figure 6) that reveals several non-obvious findings (e.g., all models struggle with object counting, detection capability is decoupled from other abilities, Kosmos-2 falters on multi-choice format despite strong detection). This scale of holistic evaluation is beyond what prior individual benchmarks cover.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Default recipe fairness is insufficiently justified.** The paper selects default Recipes by evaluating "stability to Instruction variations" (line 406), but the supporting analysis (Figure 5) is shown for only 2 of 9 Scenarios (CIFAR10 and ScienceQA) and only 3 of 9 MLLMs. The paper then applies these defaults uniformly across all 9 models when drawing its headline comparisons (Table 1). This leaves open the possibility that the chosen defaults systematically favor certain model families (e.g., InstructBLIP) over others. The paper itself notes that Kosmos-2 "struggles to comprehend the meaning of options {A, B, C, D}" (line 412), confirming that the PPL-based multi-choice format is not equally suitable for all models. This is a structural concern because it affects the validity of the central comparative claims (e.g., "InstructBLIP attains superior performance across most Scenarios" may partly reflect evaluation design rather than pure visual competence). The paper should at minimum discuss this limitation explicitly and bound the sensitivity.

- **Correlation analysis is based on too few data points to support the claimed relationships.** Section 4.4 presents a Pearson correlation matrix computed across 9 MLLMs. With n=9, the confidence intervals on correlation estimates are very wide, and a single outlier can dominate the result. The paper treats correlations of ~0.6–0.8 as "strong" without significance testing or effect-size context, and draws specific conclusions such as "hallucination is strongly correlated with the performance on MMBench" (line 470) that are plausible but not demonstrated by the evidence. This analysis should either be removed, accompanied by explicit caveats about the limited sample size, or migrated to a larger set of model variants.

### Minor

- **Detection task is redefined as multi-choice ranking with a constructed answer pool.** For VOC2012, the answer pool is "generated by random scaling and translating the ground-truth bounding boxes" (line 303), and PPL selects among these. This converts detection into a ranking task where the answer pool always includes a near-correct box. The resulting scores (e.g., 55% for Shikra on VOC2012) are not comparable to standard mAP, and it is unclear what capability this actually measures. The paper should be explicit that detection is evaluated as a constrained multi-choice task, not as open-vocabulary detection.

- **ICL evaluation mixes two qualitatively different settings (with and without images) then averages across shot numbers to compute RIAM.** As the paper acknowledges (line 222), "some MLLMs only support single-image input, restricting ICE's image inclusion." Models that can use in-context images have a fundamentally different (and richer) task than those that cannot, yet the RIAM score collapses this distinction. The inconsistency makes the ICL scores difficult to interpret.

- **No variance or significance reporting for the main benchmark results.** Table 1 and the desiderata results (Figure 6) report single numbers per model without standard errors, confidence intervals, or information about the number of runs/seeds. For a paper that aims to establish a standardized evaluation framework, this is a notable gap that would be expected in a mature benchmark.

- **The "first comprehensive evaluation framework" claim is slightly overblown.** The paper acknowledges LAMM (Yin et al. 2023) and LVLM-eHub (Xu et al. 2023) as prior frameworks (line 148), and the modular design and six desiderata are genuine advances. The claim should be softened to reflect incremental but meaningful progress over these existing efforts rather than a claimed first-of-its-kind status.

### Trivial

- The paper has duplicated section content (two Section 3s, two Section 4s, etc.) — this is a parser extraction artifact, not an author error, but it does make the paper harder to follow in this format.

## Nice-to-Haves

- The "instruction following" evaluation tests only verbalizer manipulation (natural/neutral/unnatural output tokens); this is a useful slice but does not capture whether models follow complex task-level instructions. Renaming it to "output-format adherence" would better reflect what is actually measured, or the authors could add a more challenging instruction-following component.
- The language performance evaluation via GPT could be grounded by including a simple reference-based metric like BLEU or ROUGE alongside the GPT scores, even if imperfect, to show that the GPT-based scores are not reflecting a different confound.
- The desiderata evaluation (ICL, calibration, instruction following, robustness, language performance) is reported on only one or two Scenarios (MMBench and ScienceQA). Showing that these properties generalize across more Scenarios would strengthen the framework's diagnostic value.

## Novel Insights

The two reviews converge on the paper's genuine architectural contribution (modular four-component design that subsumes existing benchmarks) while disagreeing on severity. The harsh critic's most interesting observation — that the default Recipe choice may be a lever that systematically shifts rankings — is not merely a weakness but points to a deeper tension in MLLM evaluation: the more you standardize evaluation to enable fair comparison, the more you risk disadvantaging models whose training paradigms don't match the standardized format. The paper's own data implicitly confirms this: Kosmos-2's collapse on discriminative tasks (34.6% on ScienceQA, 32.8% on MMBench) versus its strong detection performance (54.55% on VOC2012) is not fully explained by visual ability but by format compatibility. This tension between standardization and fairness is the most interesting unresolved question the paper surfaces, and acknowledging it more centrally would strengthen rather than weaken the contribution.

## Suggestions

1. **Primary: Bound the sensitivity of rankings to Recipe choice.** Select, for each model, the Recipe that yields its best (or median) accuracy per Scenario, and compare the resulting rank ordering side-by-side with the one-size-fits-all default. If the ranking is stable, the framework's claims are much stronger; if it shifts, the shifts themselves are a valuable finding.

2. **Remove or substantially caveat the correlation analysis.** Either drop the Pearson matrix or present it with explicit confidence intervals and the caveat that n=9 provides insufficient statistical power. Better yet, expand the analysis to include multiple checkpoints or hyperparameter variants of the same models.

3. **Acknowledge the detection task redefinition explicitly.** Add a sentence or footnote noting that detection results are based on a constrained multi-choice formulation and are not directly comparable to open-vocabulary mAP.
