## Summary
This paper proposes APPLe (Adaptive Prompt Prototype Learning), a method that replaces the single prompt template used in zero-shot CLIP with multiple per-class textual prototypes generated by GPT-3. An adaptive attention mechanism weighs the importance of different prototypes, and a decorrelation loss suppresses co-occurrence of multiple prototypes. The training-free variant (APPLe*) uses only the GPT-3 prompts without any fine-tuning. The method is evaluated on 11 datasets across base-to-new generalization, few-shot learning, and domain generalization.

## Strengths
1. **Well-motivated and clean idea**: The paper identifies a genuine limitation of single-prompt CLIP inference — visual variance within a class cannot be captured by one template — and proposes a conceptually straightforward solution: multiple prompt prototypes per class. The motivational example (apple pies varying in appearance) is effective and directly connected to the method design.

2. **Training-free variant demonstrates the core insight cleanly**: The APPLe* variant (no training, no support images) isolates the contribution of multi-prototype representations from the training procedure. This provides a clean ablation of the paper's central claim that diversity in textual prototypes, not context optimization, is what drives generalization. The paper explicitly reports HM of 74.83% for APPLe* across 11 datasets (line 125).

3. **Comprehensive evaluation across three distinct settings**: The method is tested on base-to-new generalization, few-shot learning, and domain generalization across 11 datasets spanning coarse, fine-grained, scene, texture, satellite, and action domains. The ablation study (Table 3, discussed in lines 150-152) validates each component individually.

4. **Prototype number analysis confirms the central motivation**: Figure 4 (discussed in lines 133-138) varies K from 1 to 50 on ImageNet and shows that (a) new-class performance increases with more prototypes, and (b) with ≤3 prototypes, fine-tuning degrades new-class accuracy below zero-shot CLIP — directly supporting the claim that overfitting of single-prompt methods is mitigated by multiple prototypes.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Missing quantitative comparison to directly competing GPT-prompt baselines**: The paper mentions Menon & Vondrick (2022) and Pratt et al. (2023) in the related work (line 32) but includes **no experimental comparison** to any GPT-prompt-based zero-shot method (e.g., WaffleCLIP, DCLIP, or the cited works). This is a critical omission because the training-free APPLe* is itself a GPT-prompt-based zero-shot method. Without these baselines, the claimed significance of the training-free variant cannot be assessed — the reader cannot tell whether the improvement comes from APPLe's specific design (prototypes + attention) or simply from using GPT-3 prompts, which is an already-established line of work. The paper acknowledges the thematic overlap ("While there are similarities in leveraging GPT for prompt generation") but does not provide the quantitative evidence needed to establish differentiation.

2. **Prototype count K not specified for main experiments**: The ablation on ImageNet (Figure 4, lines 133-138) varies K from 1 to 50, but the paper never states which value of K was used for the 11-dataset base-to-new, few-shot, or domain generalization experiments. Without this specification, the main results are not reproducible. Given that Figure 4 shows performance varies significantly with K (especially below 3), and that the optimal K may differ across datasets, this is a substantive missing detail.

3. **No error bars or statistical significance reported**: Not a single experiment reports standard deviations, confidence intervals, or measures of statistical significance. Given that the reported improvements are modest (e.g., 1.55% on ImageNet, some domain gaps under 1%), it is unclear whether these gains are robust or within the noise of single runs.

### Minor
4. **Few-shot results lack tabular data**: The few-shot experiments are only presented as a line plot (Figure 3) with no numeric table in the main text. The paper states "The numeric results are reported in Section A.7" (line 123), but the appendix is not available in the review copy. While this is partly an artifact of review formatting, the few-shot claims cannot be verified from the main paper alone.

5. **Typo in the few-shot reporting**: Line 123 says "PLOT respectively gained 1.86%, 1.0%, 0.50%, 0.51%, and 0.12% performance boost over PLOT" — the method name appears to be incorrectly repeated (should read "APPLe... over PLOT"). This makes the sentence unintelligible without context.

6. **Hyperparameter sensitivity for λ₁ and λ₂ not shown**: The paper states both λ₁ and λ₂ are fixed to 3 for all experiments (line 101) but provides no analysis of how sensitive the results are to these values. Given that the decorrelation loss (ℓ_dec) and max-prototype loss (ℓ_max) are core contributions, their weight sensitivity should be demonstrated.

7. **Training provides marginal new-class gains**: The paper's own description of the ablation (lines 150-152) indicates that training all components on ImageNet produces only a small new-class improvement over the training-free version (e.g., from 73.40 to 73.68, as cited by the reviewer from Table 3; the paper text does not provide these numbers explicitly). This suggests that the training primarily boosts base classes and the main mechanism driving new-class performance is the multi-prototype design itself — which is fine, but the paper's presentation would benefit from stating this trade-off directly rather than framing all gains as uniform improvements.

## Novel Insights
The harsh critic and strength finder together surface an interesting tension: the training-free APPLe* variant is arguably the paper's most striking result (showing that multiple prompts alone, without training, match or exceed training-based methods), yet this is also the variant that lacks the most relevant baselines (existing GPT-prompt methods). This creates a blind spot — the paper cannot distinguish whether the strong training-free results are attributable to its specific design (prototypes + calibration) or simply to using GPT-3 generated descriptions, a known technique. The strength finder correctly highlights the ablation and prototype-number analysis as validating the multi-prototype idea, but these are conducted on ImageNet only, and the paper does not demonstrate that GPT-3's specific descriptions are necessary (vs. simpler template variants). The reviews collectively suggest the paper would benefit from explicitly reframing its contribution: the main finding is that *multiple prototypes mitigate single-prompt overfitting during fine-tuning*, not that the training-free variant is a fundamentally new zero-shot method.
