## Summary

This paper proposes VChangeCodec, which integrates a voice changer directly into a neural speech codec by inserting a lightweight causal projection network (Converter) between the encoder and decoder. The architecture enables seamless switching between original and converted speech with claimed 40 ms latency and fewer than 1M parameters. The paper evaluates both codec quality and voice conversion performance against SOTA methods.

## Strengths

1. **Genuinely novel integration of voice changer into a speech codec as a unified architecture**: Unlike prior work that cascades VC preprocessing with a separate codec, VChangeCodec inserts a lightweight Converter at the token level between encoder and decoder, enabling seamless switching and a single end-to-end pipeline. This is a well-motivated architectural contribution.

2. **Ultra-low end-to-end latency of 40 ms with voice changer active**: Section 3.2 reports that the full pipeline (encoder+converter+decoder) runs in 2 ms per 20 ms chunk on a single CPU core of an iPhone X, yielding total 40+ ms latency. Table 6 confirms RTF <0.01 on a MacBook Pro M1 Pro. These are concrete, impressive engineering results.

3. **Very lightweight architecture (<1M total parameters)**: Table 1 reports 0.77M parameters at 2 kbps — a ~70× reduction versus DAC (55.3M). The use of scalar quantization instead of RVQ is a practical design choice that enables this efficiency.

4. **Speaker similarity outperforms all compared VC methods**: Table 2 shows VChangeCodec achieves the highest speaker similarity (95.45% with Resemblyzer), surpassing FACodec (89.26%) by 6.19%. Subjective S-MOS results (Table 3) also show the highest scores for both male and female target timbres.

5. **Systematic ablation study validates design choices**: Table 5 ablates metadata, Converter dimension, token commitment loss, and encoder freezing. Each component is shown to contribute measurably, with the frozen encoder and token commitment loss being empirically justified.

6. **Retraining experiments provide fairer comparison**: Table 4 retrains VQMIVC, QuickVC, and DDDM-VC on the same target timbre dataset. VChangeCodec maintains the best speaker similarity (90.01% vs. 88.52%) and lowest MCD (5.76 vs. 6.35), strengthening the claim that the integrated approach is intrinsically more effective.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Converter training depends on RVC-generated data, creating an evaluation confound.** The Converter is trained on "near-parallel data" generated by the Retrieval-based-Voice-Conversion (RVC) toolkit (Section 4.1, line 137). This means the network learns to reproduce RVC's output distribution rather than learning timbre adaptation from natural parallel speech. The retraining experiments (Table 4) address this in part by training baselines on the same target-timbre data, but the fundamental concern remains: does the Converter learn timbre adaptation or does it learn to replicate RVC's artifacts? The ablation study does not include a variant trained on naturally paired multi-speaker data (e.g., the same utterance from different speakers), which would disentangle architectural merit from teacher quality. This limits the interpretability of the VC comparison results.

2. **Missing comparison against streaming/low-latency VC baselines.** The paper is motivated by the high latency of existing VC pipelines (Section 1, Figure 1) and claims VChangeCodec's 40 ms as a key advantage. However, all VC baselines in Tables 2–4 are non-streaming (Diff-VC, VQMIVC, QuickVC, DDDM-VC, FACodec). StreamVoice and StreamVC are cited in related work (lines 52–58) but are not included in any experiment. Including at least StreamVC (cited as real-time capable) in both objective and subjective evaluations is necessary to substantiate the claimed latency advantage over competing streaming approaches.

3. **Codec evaluation transparency is insufficient to verify the reported scores.** The paper reports notably strong POLQA/ViSQOL scores for VChangeCodec at low bitrates (e.g., POLQA "beyond 4.0" at 2–6 kbps, outperforming EnCodec at 24 kbps — Section 4.2, line 154). The paper does not explicitly state whether codec baselines (OPUS, EVS, Lyra2, EnCodec, DAC) were evaluated on the exact same 68-utterance test set with properly configured checkpoints, or whether some scores were taken from original publications. The parameter sizes are noted as "from their original papers" (Section 4.4, line 309), creating ambiguity about the quality metric values. Given the unusually strong results, a clear statement of evaluation protocol is needed.

### Minor

1. **Subjective VC evaluation uses only two target timbres** (one male, one female — Section 4.1, line 137). Adding at least one more target speaker would improve generality of the subjective results.

2. **Ablation does not vary the training data source.** The ablation study (Table 5) systematically varies metadata, dimensions, token commitment loss, and encoder freezing, but never varies whether the training data is RVC-generated vs. natural parallel data. This would be the most informative ablation for interpreting the core contribution.

3. **No statistical significance testing** is reported for either codec or VC comparisons, though the paper relies on small test sets (42–68 utterances, 24 listeners for subjective tests).

### Trivial

1. The bitrate derivation from the SQ configuration (R=2, downsampling factor 320×, frame size 20 ms) is not explicitly mapped to the reported bitrates (1.5/3/6/12 kbps). A brief derivation table would aid reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Include StreamVC or another streaming VC method in comparisons to directly validate the latency claim.
- Add a training variant that uses naturally paired multi-speaker data (e.g., same utterance from different LibriTTS speakers) to disentangle architectural merit from RVC teacher quality.
- Provide confidence intervals or standard deviations for key metrics in Tables 1–4.
- Break down the parameter count into encoder, SQ codebook, Converter, and decoder components.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's observation about the RVC training data creating a "distillation-wrapped evaluation artifact" is the most penetrating insight not fully articulated in the paper itself. The paper treats the RVC-generated data as a purely practical choice, but this choice means the Converter is fundamentally learning to approximate RVC's output distribution, not learning voice conversion from first principles. The retraining experiments help but do not resolve this because the teacher signal is always RVC. This suggests that a more informative experiment would be comparing VChangeCodec against a variant where the Converter is trained directly on RVC as a teacher (i.e., the baseline is "what RVC produces, but through the VChangeCodec architecture"). The paper's ablation dimensions are all architectural; the data-source dimension is unexplored.

A second observation: the paper's framing as "operator-oriented" with built-in privacy protections (Section 6) is presented as a contribution, but the privacy argument is entirely about deployment policy (operators manage embeddings, display notification labels), not a technical privacy mechanism. This framing adds rhetorical weight but does not constitute a technical contribution to privacy-preserving speech processing.

## Suggestions

1. Most critically, validate the Converter architecture with a training setup that does not depend on RVC for data generation. This could be done by (a) training on naturally parallel multi-speaker data where the same utterance is spoken by multiple speakers (e.g., picking speakers from LibriTTS who read the same passage), or (b) including a variant that directly uses RVC as a teacher model and comparing the Converter against it as a baseline. This would disentangle architectural merit from teacher quality.

2. Include StreamVC or another streaming VC method in the objective/subjective comparisons. These are the actual competitors for the RTC use case that motivates the paper.

3. Clarify the codec evaluation protocol: state explicitly whether all baselines were run on the same 68-utterance test set using downloaded checkpoints, and report any configuration details needed to reproduce the scores.

4. Expand the subjective VC evaluation to include at least one more target timbre and report confidence intervals.
