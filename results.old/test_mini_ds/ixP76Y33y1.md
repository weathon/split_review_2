## Summary

This paper introduces **intrinsic label sharpness (K<sub>F</sub>)**, a dataset property measuring how visually similar images with different labels can be, and shows that medical image datasets have higher K<sub>F</sub> than natural image datasets. The authors derive a generalization scaling law (log L ≈ −(1/d<sub>d</sub>) log N + log K<sub>F</sub> + const.) that incorporates K<sub>F</sub>, connect K<sub>F</sub> to adversarial robustness via a lower bound on the expected robustness radius, and relate dataset intrinsic dimension d<sub>d</sub> to representation intrinsic dimension d<sub>r</sub>. Experiments span 6 CNN architectures and 11 datasets (7 medical, 4 natural) across multiple training set sizes.

## Strengths

1. **Novel label sharpness metric (K<sub>F</sub>).** The paper proposes a clean, model-independent dataset property that captures how "sharp" the labeling function is on the data manifold. The estimate (Eq. 2) is fast to compute and reveals a systematic difference between medical and natural image domains (Fig. 1, right panel: medical ∼2.5× 10⁻⁴ vs. natural ∼1× 10⁻⁴). This is the first metric to directly quantify this cross-domain gap.

2. **Scaling law that incorporates dataset-specific label sharpness.** Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 together yield Eq. (4): log L ≲ −(1/d<sub>d</sub>) log N + log K<sub>F</sub> + a. This extends prior work (Bahri et al. 2021) by making the previously-ignored K<sub>F</sub> term estimable, and provides a principled explanation for why medical and natural domains exhibit different generalization-error scaling even at the same d<sub>d</sub> and N. The ≈2.5× K<sub>F</sub> gap between domains is consistent with the observed intercept difference in Fig. 2.

3. **Broad and systematic empirical evaluation.** Experiments cover 6 models (ResNet-18/34/50, VGG-13/16/19) × 11 datasets × 6 training sizes (N = 500–1750), producing 454 (model, dataset, size) triplets per figure. Results are averaged over multiple class pairings for natural datasets with error bars. This is substantially more comprehensive than typical scaling-law papers.

4. **Connecting K<sub>F</sub> to adversarial robustness across domains.** Theorem 3 derives a lower bound on the expected robustness radius scaling as Ω(1/K<sub>F</sub>). The empirical correlations between loss penalty from FGSM attacks and K<sub>F</sub> (Table 1) are consistently positive across models and attack strengths, and medical datasets (higher K<sub>F</sub>) do show higher attack susceptibility, consistent with prior observations (Ma et al. 2021).

5. **Linking d<sub>d</sub> and d<sub>r</sub>.** Theorem 5 provides a theoretical connection between dataset intrinsic dimension and representation intrinsic dimension (d<sub>r</sub> ≲ d<sub>d</sub>), empirically supported across all models and datasets in Fig. 5, with the paper honestly noting this is approximate ("usually the case").

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The primary empirical test of the scaling law is cross-sectional, not a direct test of N-dependence.** The central theoretical result (Eq. 4) predicts how test loss scales with training set size N as L ∝ N⁻¹/ᵈ. Yet Fig. 2 plots log loss against d<sub>d</sub> (or 1/d<sub>d</sub>), not against log N. Each point bundles a different N, so the figure tests whether loss increases with d<sub>d</sub> at variable N — which is consistent with the theory but does not validate the functional form of the N⁻¹/ᵈ scaling. The paper mentions that an appendix analysis accounts for K<sub>F</sub> explicitly, but the main text's evidence remains correlational rather than a direct quantitative fit of the predicted N-exponent. This is not fatal — the cross-domain separation in Fig. 2 is still informative — but it leaves the central predictive claim significantly less supported than the paper's narrative suggests.

2. **The K<sub>F</sub> estimator (Eq. 2) is used without convergence guarantees.** For binary labels, the estimator reduces to 1 / (minimum inter-class distance in a sample of M=1000 pairs). This systematically overestimates the minimum distance (i.e., underestimates K<sub>F</sub>) because the true infimum over the manifold could be much smaller. The paper states M=1000 is "more than sufficient" without any convergence analysis or bound. Since quantitative K<sub>F</sub> differences between domains drive the paper's main explanatory claim (medical K<sub>F</sub> ≈ 2.5× natural), an unvalidated estimator weakens the evidence.

3. **The adversarial robustness theorem (Thm. 3) is not tested in the experimental setting.** The theorem provides a lower bound on the expected robustness radius for **on-manifold** points, explicitly restricting to pairs on M<sub>d</sub> (footnote 1). The experiments use FGSM attacks with L<sub>∞</sub> perturbations that produce images **not** on M<sub>d</sub>. The paper does not claim the theorem is validated by these experiments — the correlation is presented as an empirical observation — but the disconnect between theory and evidence is significant. The correlations in Table 1 are moderate (e.g., r=0.18 for ResNet-34 at ε=8/255) and vary substantially across models without confidence intervals or significance tests.

### Minor

4. **The d<sub>r</sub> ≲ d<sub>d</sub> bound is approximate and has systematic violations.** Figure 4 shows points above the d<sub>r</sub>=d<sub>d</sub> line, especially for medical datasets and larger training sets. The paper acknowledges this ("usually the case"), which is appropriate, but Theorem 5 is derived from equating the two scaling laws as estimates — the empirical violations suggest the derivation's assumptions (e.g., treating bounds as approximate equalities) are not equally valid across all settings.

5. **Theorem 4 is presented without proof or derivation sketch in the main text.** The claim L ≈ O(K<sub>L</sub> N⁻¹/ᵈʳ) is central to Section 7, but the loss bound in Theorem 4 drops the max(K<sub>f</sub>, K<sub>F</sub>) term that appears in the input-space analysis without justification. The proof is relegated to a stripped appendix.

6. **The step from Theorem 1 (a bound) to Eq. (4) (an approximate equality) involves several unguarded simplifications.** Replacing max(K<sub>f</sub>, K<sub>F</sub>) with K<sub>F</sub> relies on Theorem 2 which is asymptotic (N → ∞) and has no finite-N convergence rate. Dropping K<sub>L</sub> as a fixed constant is reasonable for cross-dataset comparisons but means the "scaling law" is really a scaling bound. These are common practices in scaling-law papers but should be more transparently flagged.

### Trivial
- The paper uses both "log L ∝ −1/d<sub>d</sub>" (line 150) and "log L ≲ −(1/d<sub>d</sub>) log N" (Eq. 4) to describe Fig. 2, but the x-axis is labeled as d<sub>d</sub> (not 1/d<sub>d</sub>). The description could be more precise.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A controlled isolation of K<sub>F</sub> (e.g., synthetic datasets with matched d<sub>d</sub> but varying K<sub>F</sub>) would strengthen the causal attribution that K<sub>F</sub> drives the domain gap, rather than relying on a single measured difference.
- Formal significance tests for the adversarial robustness correlations (Table 1) would help assess whether the moderate r-values are meaningful.
- On-manifold attacks (e.g., using normalizing flows or the known low-dimensional structure) would bridge the gap between Theorem 3 and the experiments.

## Suggestions

1. **Directly test the N⁻¹/ᵈ scaling.** For each dataset, fit log L ≈ α − β log N and compare the fitted β to 1/d<sub>d</sub> (adjusted for K<sub>F</sub>). This would transform the cross-sectional evidence into a quantitative test of the core prediction.

2. **Provide convergence guarantees for the K<sub>F</sub> estimator.** Show that as M grows, K̂<sub>F</sub> converges to a well-defined quantity (e.g., the essential minimum inter-class distance on the manifold), or alternatively, define K<sub>F</sub> directly in terms of the sampling-based estimate and clarify it measures "empirical label sharpness" rather than the true Lipschitz constant.

3. **Acknowledge the adversarial robustness theory-experiment gap explicitly** and discuss what type of experiments would constitute a proper test of Theorem 3 (e.g., on-manifold attacks, or a bound for off-manifold perturbations).
