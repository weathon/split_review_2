## Summary

This paper proposes MetaMath, a family of LLaMA-2 models finetuned on MetaMathQA — a bootstrapped dataset created by augmenting GSM8K and MATH training questions through: (1) answer augmentation via rejection sampling, (2) LLM-based question rephrasing, and (3) backward reasoning questions using Self-Verification and FOBAR. The core motivation is that each mathematical question is a single view of underlying meta-knowledge, and bootstrapping provides multi-view augmentation that improves diversity and generalization. MetaMath achieves strong results on GSM8K (66.5% for 7B, 82.3% for 70B) and MATH (19.8% for 7B, 26.6% for 70B), substantially outperforming prior open-source models and with the 70B variant slightly exceeding GPT-3.5-Turbo on GSM8K.

## Strengths

1. **Strong, consistent state-of-the-art results across multiple model scales.** Table 1 shows that MetaMath-7B surpasses the previous best open-source 7B model by 11.6% on GSM8K and 9.1% on MATH, and MetaMath-70B (82.3%) slightly exceeds GPT-3.5-Turbo (80.8%). These gains are consistent across 7B, 13B, and 70B scales, providing strong evidence for the effectiveness of the augmentation strategy.

2. **Novel application of backward reasoning questions as training data, not just inference verification.** Section 3.3 explicitly distinguishes the paper from prior work (Self-Verification, FOBAR) by using backward reasoning questions as fine-tuning data rather than inference-time verification. The ablation in Table 2 shows that adding SV+FOBAR on top of AnsAug+Rephrasing improves accuracy from 60.6% to 64.4% on GSM8K (GSM8K-trained model) and from 15.3% to 17.7% on MATH (MATH-trained model), providing controlled evidence for the additive value of backward reasoning augmentations.

3. **Quantified positive correlation between diversity gain and accuracy (Pearson coefficient 0.972).** Section 4.5 and Figure 4 show that adding 20K samples from Rephrasing, FOBAR, or SV yields increasing diversity gains that correlate strongly with accuracy improvements. This provides a principled, data-driven explanation — grounded in a measurable quantity — for why question bootstrapping outperforms simple answer augmentation, and directly supports the paper's diversity-centric framing.

4. **Well-structured ablation isolating the contribution of each augmentation component.** Table 2 progressively adds AnsAug, Rephrasing, and backward reasoning (SV+FOBAR), quantifying the marginal benefit of each. The cross-evaluation (train on GSM8K → test on MATH, and vice versa) further demonstrates that MetaMathQA improves generalization to unseen task distributions.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. No verified weakness invalidates the paper's core claims.

### Major

1. **The GSM8K-Backward test set uses the same generation procedure as the training augmentations, creating a biased measure of backward reasoning ability.** The paper (Section 4.6) generates the GSM8K-Backward test set by applying SV and FOBAR to the original GSM8K test set — the exact same methods used to produce the backward reasoning training data. As the paper states (line 542): "we propose a GSM8K-Backward test set, including 1270 backward questions by using SV and FOBAR to augment the original GSM8K test set." This means the test set and training augmentations share surface patterns and masking conventions. Reporting superior performance on this test set conflates genuine backward reasoning ability with familiarity with the augmentation template. This does **not** affect the primary GSM8K/MATH results (which are on standard, independent benchmarks), but it weakens the paper's specific claims about backward reasoning's role. A proper evaluation would require human-written backward questions or questions generated by a method unseen during training.

2. **The backward question generation procedure underspecifies which number is masked, affecting reproducibility.** Section 3.3 states "a number in the question $q_i$ is masked by 'x'" but provides no detail on how the masked number is selected when a question contains multiple numerical values. As the examples show (lines 159-166), the question "James buys 5 packs of beef that are 4 pounds each at $5.50 per pound" has three numbers; masking different quantities yields qualitatively different backward problems. The selection mechanism is algorithmically nontrivial and significantly affects data quality and difficulty. This is a reproducibility gap.

### Minor

1. **The "More Data is not Always Better" experiment (Section 4.7) is confounded and the conclusion is overstated.** The experiment mixes RFT data (generated by rejection sampling from LLaMA-2, a different pipeline) into MetaMathQA and observes a performance drop. The paper concludes "more augmented data does not always help the generalization." However, the drop may simply reflect distribution mismatch between the two augmentation pipelines rather than a saturation of useful data. A clean within-distribution scaling experiment (adding more MetaMathQA-style data to MetaMathQA) would directly test the stated claim. The current experiment merely shows that mixing two different augmentation distributions can hurt — a less surprising finding.

2. **The perplexity analysis (Section 4.4) is not well-specified and does not provide causal evidence.** The paper reports perplexity of an "under-finetuned LLaMA-2-7B model" without defining what "under-finetuned" means (number of steps, training configuration, data used). The claim that lower perplexity data "may be more conducive to eliciting bolstered problem-solving abilities" is speculative; no causal link is established between lower perplexity and better downstream accuracy. The section acknowledges this ("we speculate") but the analysis adds limited evidentiary value to the paper's core contribution.

3. **No hyperparameter details for fine-tuning.** The paper only states "fully fine-tuning" for 7B/13B and QLoRA for 70B, without reporting learning rate, batch size, number of epochs, learning rate schedule, or warmup steps. These are standard reporting requirements for reproducibility.

4. **No confidence intervals or variance estimates.** All results are reported as point estimates. Given the test set sizes (1,319 for GSM8K, 5,000 for MATH), bootstrap confidence intervals would help readers assess the reliability of comparisons, especially for margins of 0.7–1% (e.g., MetaMath-70B vs. WizardMath-70B on GSM8K: 82.3% vs. 81.6%).

## Novel Insights

The synthesis of the human reviews reveals a pattern that the paper itself does not fully capitalize on: the tension between the paper's strongest evidence (the clean, monotonic results in Table 1 and the diversity-accuracy correlation in Figure 4) and its weakest evidence (the confounded RFT experiment, the underspecified perplexity analysis, and the evaluation loop in GSM8K-Backward). The paper's core claim — that question bootstrapping via rephrasing and backward reasoning produces a diverse, effective training set — is well-supported by the first category. The secondary claims about *why* it works (perplexity-based simplicity, "more data is not always better") are supported by weaker experiments that do not control for confounds. A notable observation is that the ablation in Table 2 separates AnsAug, Rephrasing, and backward reasoning collectively, but not SV and FOBAR individually, leaving open the question of whether one backward method dominates. Additionally, the error analysis by question length (Section 4.9) is a useful diagnostic but stops short of suggesting concrete remediation beyond "further augment the dataset," which is not actionable.

## Suggestions

1. **Replace or supplement the GSM8K-Backward evaluation** with an independent test. Generate backward questions using a method *not* seen during training (e.g., human-written reversals, or questions from math competitions that naturally require solving for an intermediate variable). This would cleanly validate the backward reasoning claim.

2. **Specify the number-masking selection algorithm** in Section 3.3. Describe the rule for choosing which number to mask (first number? last number? only deterministic quantities?) and any filtering criteria beyond answer correctness.

3. **Run a within-distribution scaling experiment** for MetaMathQA: train on increasingly large subsets (100K → 200K → 395K) of the full MetaMathQA dataset and measure accuracy. This would provide a clean test of whether "more data is not always better" within the same augmentation distribution, or whether the observed saturation is an artifact of cross-pipeline mixing.

4. **Report hyperparameters** (learning rate, batch size, epochs, schedule) and add confidence intervals (e.g., bootstrap) for key comparisons, especially those with narrow margins.

5. **Clarify the perplexity analysis**: define the "under-finetuned" model, and ideally show that the correlation between low perplexity and high accuracy is specific to mathematical reasoning rather than a general property of any training data.
