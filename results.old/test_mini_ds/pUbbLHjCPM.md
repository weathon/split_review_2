## Summary

This paper proposes Progressive Thought Refinement (PTR), a two-stage framework that (1) constructs a dataset of "thoughts" (generated by a weak model) and "refined answers" (generated by a strong model via ICL) using a weak-strong model collaborative selection strategy, then (2) fine-tunes an LLM with a weighted thought-mask loss intended to teach the model to iteratively improve its own outputs. Experiments across ten diverse tasks show average accuracy gains from 49.6% to 53.5%, with the method outperforming prompting-only and instruction-fine-tuning baselines.

## Strengths

1. **Broad evaluation across ten diverse tasks without task-specific fine-tuning.** The paper evaluates on knowledge reasoning (MMLU, Winogrande, CommonsenseQA), code (HumanEval), math (GSM8K, MATH), comprehension (DROP), summarization (XSum), and complex reasoning (ARC, GPQA). This breadth directly supports the claim of general refinement ability. The reported average gain from 49.6% to 53.5% and MMLU improvement from 57.1% to 64.1% (Qwen2-7B) are concrete, non-trivial improvements (Abstract, Section 4.1).

2. **Clear distinction from knowledge distillation.** The IFT baseline (standard SFT on the same data) degrades on some tasks (e.g., CommonsenseQA drops from 46.1% to 40.3% at the second iteration) and fails to improve iteratively, whereas PTR consistently improves. This demonstrates that the improvement is not simply memorizing strong-model outputs (Section 4.1).

3. **Analysis of emergence and iteration dynamics.** Section 4.4 shows that refinement ability emerges around ~24,000 training steps, with simpler tasks (MMLU, DROP) improving earlier and complex tasks (ARC, GPQA) showing later gains. Section 4.5 tracks performance over ten iterations, showing sustained improvement without overfitting. These analyses add depth beyond simple benchmark reporting.

4. **Demonstration of robustness across prompts and model architectures.** PTR produces iterative improvements across three different prompt templates and works on both Qwen2-7B and Llama3-8B (Sections 4.1, 4.3), suggesting the method is not brittle to instruction wording or base model choice.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Weak and strong models used for dataset construction are never named.** Section 3.1.2 discusses selection criteria (parameter strength, version, domain fine-tuning) but never identifies the actual models deployed. The paper's claim of "annotation-free" general refinement and the interpretation of whether this is simply distillation depend critically on whether the strong model is GPT-4/Claude-class or a model within the same capability band as Qwen2-7B/Llama3-8B. Without this information, the reader cannot assess how much of the improvement comes from the training method versus teacher quality. This is a structural reproducibility issue.

2. **Key components of the loss function (Eq. 1) are undefined.** The consistency term $\mathcal{F}_{\text{cons}}(y_t, y_{t-1})$ and the confidence weights $\beta_t$ are introduced in the loss equation but never defined. The loss sums over $t=1,\dots,n$ where $y_t$ are elements of the thought sequence — the paper does not specify how $\Pr(y_t \mid q_i, S_{i,\text{thought}}; \theta)$ is computed when the model sees the entire thought sequence as input during training. The thought mask mechanism is described only at a high level ("selectively hides parts of the thought process"). These omissions prevent the reader from understanding or reproducing the training objective precisely.

3. **RL (DPO) baseline is insufficiently specified.** The paper states that DPO is applied using "thoughts and answers of the PRD dataset to construct preference data" (Section 4.1, baseline 3). It is unclear how preference pairs are formed from a thought sequence (a multi-step object) and a final answer (a concise response). The description "one reinforcement learning training iteration" is atypical for DPO, which normally trains for multiple epochs. Without a clear protocol, the claim that PTR outperforms RL is not well-supported — the baseline may be artificially weak.

### Minor

1. **The thought generation process has unspecified parameters.** The number of weak-model samples per query (the value of $t$ in Eq. 3) is never stated. The paper says "Multiple weak models can be used" or a single model "can produce multiple attempts" (Section 3.1.2), but never gives concrete numbers. This matters for data construction cost and for expected behavior at test time.

2. **No error bars or multi-seed results.** Across all ten tasks, results are reported as single-point comparisons. For a method claiming consistent gains, especially on benchmarks with known high variance (HumanEval, GPQA), the absence of variance estimates weakens the evidence.

3. **Consistency filtering is mentioned but not described.** Section 3.1.2 states "we apply consistency filtering to remove inconsistent outputs" but provides no details on what metric or threshold is used. The metric for "accuracy" on tasks like XSum (summarization) is also not clarified.

### Trivial

- Line 87 refers to "Qwen2-8B" while the rest of the paper consistently uses "Qwen2-7B" — a minor inconsistency.
- The refinement instructions ("Please continue thinking and refine your answer") are mentioned but it is unclear whether these are concatenated during training, at test time, or both.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation study separating the three loss terms (final-answer NLL, consistency, confidence weighting) would isolate which components drive the iterative improvement. The current IFT baseline removes the mask but also changes the training structure; a cleaner ablation would fine-tune on the full (thought → answer) sequence without masking.
- Reporting multi-seed results with variance for 3–4 representative tasks would substantially strengthen the empirical grounding.

## Novel Insights

The reviewers' analyses converge on a core tension: the paper proposes an interesting and well-motivated framework with unusually broad evaluation, but the technical reporting is insufficiently precise. The most novel observation from the reviews is that the contrast with IFT (which degrades on some tasks) provides stronger evidence for the method's distinctiveness than any direct comparison to SOTA baselines — yet this contrast is partly undermined by the lack of ablated loss terms. A second insight is that the emergence analysis (Section 4.4) showing delayed onset for complex tasks is actually one of the paper's most compelling pieces of evidence for genuine refinement learning rather than memorization.

## Suggestions

1. **Name the weak and strong models explicitly** used for dataset construction. If the strong model is GPT-4 or similar, state this clearly. If multiple weak models were tested, report the range.
2. **Define $\mathcal{F}_{\text{cons}}$ and $\beta_t$**, and clarify how $\Pr(y_t \mid q_i, S_{i,\text{thought}}; \theta)$ is computed during training under the thought mask.
3. **Specify the DPO baseline more precisely**: how are preference pairs constructed from the thought-answer data, what hyperparameters are used, and how many epochs/steps?
4. **Add multi-seed results with variance** for at least 3–4 representative tasks.
5. **State the number of weak-model samples per query** (the value of $t$) and the consistency filtering metric/threshold.
