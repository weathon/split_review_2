## Summary

This paper proposes LURE, a post-hoc revisor that corrects object hallucination in LVLM outputs. It first analyzes three factors contributing to hallucination (object co-occurrence, token-level uncertainty, and later-position bias), then trains a small LVLM-based revisor on synthetic hallucinatory descriptions generated by modifying correct captions using these three factors. The revisor takes a potentially hallucinatory LVLM output, masks uncertain/late objects with a placeholder token, and reconstructs a corrected description. Experiments across six LVLMs show substantial reductions in CHAIR scores and top rankings in human/GPT evaluations.

## Strengths

1. **Consistent and large improvements across six LVLMs.** Table 1 shows LURE achieves the lowest CHAIR_S and CHAIR_I on every single model, with improvements that are often dramatic (e.g., mPLUG-Owl CHAIR_S: 71.2→18.8, MiniGPT-4: 26.8→19.7). This pattern holds across diverse model architectures and sizes (7B–13B), supporting the method's generality.

2. **Human and GPT ranking validation confirms automated metrics.** Table 2 reports that LURE receives the best average rank in both human and GPT evaluations across all six LVLMs (all rankings ≤2.08), whereas the next-best baseline (GPT-Teacher) has rankings up to 3.56. This is important because CHAIR alone has well-known limitations (limited to COCO's 80 categories).

3. **Controlled experiment rules out "more data" explanation.** Table 4 directly compares LURE against fine-tuning the base LVLM on the same hallucination dataset used to train the revisor. Fine-tuning raises CHAIR_S (26.8→31.0) while LURE lowers it to 19.7, cleanly showing the revisor's rectification mechanism — not extra data — drives the gains.

4. **Ablation confirms all three factors contribute.** Removing any one of co-occurrence, uncertainty, or position degrades CHAIR_S from 19.7 to 21.2–22.6, establishing that each factor provides meaningful (if incremental) benefit.

5. **Robustness across revisor backbones.** Training the revisor with three different backbone LVLMs all yield CHAIR_S at least 4.7 points below the original 26.8, demonstrating the approach is not tied to a single architecture.

While these may appear in the appendix (stripped by the parser), their absence from the main text makes it harder for readers to mentally assess the method's sensitivity or reproduce results from the main paper alone.

These are consistent with the paper's empirical findings but do not specifically justify or predict why masking uncertain/late tokens with a placeholder and training a revisor on GPT-3.5-generated corruptions would work. 5. **The GPT-3.5 prompt used for generating co-occurrence objects in the training data is not described.** The paper says it "leverages GPT-3.5 to deduce and incorporate objects that are most likely to co-occur in the scene" but does not specify the prompt, whether co-occurrence lists are precomputed or generated per caption, or how GPT-3.5 is instructed to avoid introducing its own hallucinations.

6. **No discussion of failure cases or limitations.** The paper does not analyze when/why the revisor might fail — e.g., does it ever over-correct (removing real objects), introduce new hallucinations, or struggle with particular types of images or objects?
