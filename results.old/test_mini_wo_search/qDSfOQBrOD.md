## Summary

This paper presents VChangeCodec, a neural speech codec that integrates a voice changer directly into the encoding pipeline via a lightweight causal projection network operating on quantized tokens. The key contributions are: (1) an integrated codec+VC architecture achieving ~40ms end-to-end latency, (2) a lightweight converter (<1M total parameters, 70× reduction vs DAC) that adapts timbre at the token level using openSMILE metadata, and (3) operator-oriented deployment with privacy safeguards. Experiments show competitive codec quality (POLQA >4.0) and VC speaker similarity (83.75% objective, S-MOS 3.70–3.80).

## Strengths

- **Seamless integration of voice changer into codec with negligible overhead**: The converter adds only ~2ms per 20ms chunk on iPhone X (Section 3.2), achieving ~40ms total latency vs. the ~107.5ms cumulative delay of cascaded systems (Figure 1). This is a concrete architectural innovation.

- **Competitive VC quality vs. non-streaming SOTA methods**: In Table 2, VChangeCodec achieves the best speaker similarity (83.75%) and lowest MCD (5.76) among compared methods (Diff-VC, VQMIVC, QuickVC, DDDM-VC, FACodec). Table 3 shows S-MOS of 3.70 (male) / 3.80 (female) — higher than all baselines.

- **Lightweight codec with 70× parameter reduction vs DAC while maintaining quality**: Table 1 shows 0.879M parameters vs. DAC's 59.22M, with POLQA >4.0, highest ViSQOL, and subjective MOS (Table 9) comparable to non-streaming DAC. This combination of efficiency and quality supports the RTC suitability claim.

- **Token commitment loss and systematic ablation**: Section 3.3 introduces a token commitment loss between predicted and ground-truth tokens. Ablation Table 5 validates its contribution (~2% speaker similarity improvement) and shows that encoder freezing outperforms retraining — a useful design guideline.

- **Real-device latency profiling**: Latency measured on iPhone X (2ms per chunk) and RTF on MacBook Pro (encoder/decoder 0.028, converter 0.001), going beyond typical GPU-only evaluations.

- **Operator-oriented privacy design**: Section 6 explicitly limits target timbres to operator-controlled voices and recommends notification labeling, with the design constraint that the encoder/decoder are immutable by users.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Voice changer training data dependency on RVC limits the strength of the timbre adaptation claim.** The converter is trained on "approximately parallel data" generated by the open-source RVC toolkit (Section 4.1). This means the model learns to mimic RVC's conversion outputs rather than learning a direct source→target mapping from natural speech. While this is a pragmatic training strategy (teacher-student) and does not invalidate the measured results, it weakens the claim of "superior timbre adaptation" — the model's ceiling is effectively RVC's quality, and any artifacts or biases from RVC are inherited. The paper should more explicitly acknowledge this limitation and discuss what the converter contributes beyond distilling RVC. Additionally, the ablation study (Table 5) and comparisons to other VC methods (Tables 2, 4) are less informative without an analysis of how much performance comes from the proposed architecture vs. the teacher model. The paper's headline claim of "excels in timbre adaptation compared to SOTA VC models" is overstated given this dependency.

### Minor

- **Bitrate not explicitly reported for VChangeCodec.** The paper never states the operating bitrate. From the given parameters (R=2, N=84, 20ms frames at 16kHz), the bitrate can be calculated as ~9.75 kbps, but this should be explicitly reported. The claim of "high-efficiency" and comparisons with OPUS/Encodec at "similar bitrates" are weakened by this omission. A rate-distortion curve across multiple bitrate operating points would strengthen the codec analysis.

- **No experimental comparison with StreamVC, the most directly comparable prior work.** StreamVC (Yang et al., 2024b) also integrates a speaker embedding into a streaming codec (SoundStream) for real-time VC. The paper mentions StreamVC in related work (lines 54–63) and notes design differences, but provides no experimental comparison. Including StreamVC in Tables 2–4 would significantly strengthen the positioning.

- **Voice changer test set is small.** The VC evaluation uses 42 test utterances (one per speaker) for objective metrics (Section 4.1) and 10 conversion pairs for subjective evaluation. This limits the statistical reliability of the VC results, particularly for speaker similarity.

- **Female timbre subjective results are mentioned but not tabulated.** The paper states "in the case of female timbre, our approach has significantly narrowed this gap" (Section 4.3) but Table 3 only reports N-MOS and S-MOS for male timbre. Full results for both target timbres should be presented.

### Trivial

- **Ambiguous phrasing about "no further training is necessary."** Line 108–109 states "no further training is necessary to achieve high-quality timbre adaptation" — this refers to the encoder/decoder being frozen, but could be misread to mean the converter requires no training, which contradicts Section 3.3. Clarify the wording.

- **Latency characterization inconsistency.** The abstract claims "just 40 ms" while Section 3.2 says "40+ ms." A clearer breakdown of the algorithmic delay components (frame size, encoder lookahead, converter processing, decoder processing) would improve precision.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation comparing scalar quantization (SQ) to RVQ directly at matched bitrates, to empirically demonstrate the claimed complexity benefit for the speech codec itself (not just the image codec literature).
- A larger VC test set with multiple utterances per source speaker for more robust evaluation.
- Rate-distortion curves (POLQA vs. bitrate) across multiple R values.
