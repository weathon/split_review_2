The paper proposes Adversarial Perturbation Dropout (APD), a plug-in module for iterative gradient-based adversarial attacks that, at each step, uses Grad-CAM++ on the current adversarial example to find the top n=3 local attention maxima and generates nm=15 dropped versions (different-sized square regions around each midpoint), averaging gradients over them. Integrated with MI/DIM/TIM/SIM/AAM/AA-TI-DIM, APD reports consistent black-box gains on ImageNet under single-model, ensemble-model, defense-model, and diverse-architecture settings.

## Strengths
- **Consistent empirical gains across baselines and targets.** Integrated with six attacks (MI, DIM, TIM, SIM, AAM, AA-TI-DIM), APD raises the average black-box ASR by 12.7/12.7/12.3/10.3/11.0/6.8% (Table 1) and 15.62% on average in the ensemble-model setting (Table 2). These are broad and not isolated to a single baseline.
- **Plug-in compatibility.** APD is formulated as a module that fits any iterative gradient-based attack without altering its base update, and the experiments demonstrate the integration on six distinct base methods (Section 4.2, Tables 1–2).
- **CAM-vs-random ablation supports the attention-guidance choice.** Figure 4 shows that, holding the rest of the method constant, CAM-guided region selection consistently beats random region selection across four source models, validating the specific design choice that distinguishes APD from a generic random-masking augmentation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The headline "synergy of perturbations" motivation is not isolated by the supporting experiment (Section 1, Fig. 1(b)).** The paper argues that extending-style attacks fail because attention-aligned perturbations rely on "synergy" from neglected perturbations. The supporting evidence — "Selective Noise Removal" (remove noise in source-attention but not target-attention regions) drops ASR more than equal-area Random Noise Removal — is confounded with perturbation density: iterative L∞ attacks deposit most of their effective magnitude in source-attention regions, so removing those regions removes higher-magnitude perturbation per pixel. This shows that high-attention perturbations matter more per pixel — not that there is a synergy mechanism. The paper's principal conceptual contribution beyond "another input transformation" therefore rests on a test that does not actually isolate the claim.
- **No compute-matched comparison to baselines (Sections 3.4, 4.1).** APD's per-iteration cost is one Grad-CAM++ pass plus n·m = 15 additional forward+backward passes through the source model — roughly 15–16× the per-step cost of MI/DIM/TIM and 3–5× that of SIM (m1=5) and AAM (m2=3). Baselines are run at their standard settings, so the reported 6.8–12.7% margins blend the proposed mechanism with a larger gradient-sample budget per step. A budget-matched run (e.g., scaling SIM scale copies or AAM samples to 15) is the natural fair comparison and would let readers attribute the gains to APD's specific design rather than to ensembling more inputs per iteration.
- **Configured hyperparameters disagree with the paper's own ablation (Sections 3.4 vs. 4.4).** Section 3.4 fixes n=3 centers and the main experiments use m=5 scales, but Section 4.4 / Figure 6 reports that ASR improves with more centers up to 4 and with more scales up to 7. The headline tables are therefore generated with a sub-optimal-by-the-paper's-own-ablation setting, without justification for the lower choice or a rerun at (n=4, m=7).

### Minor
- **The mechanism is closer to input-augmentation gradient averaging than to neuron dropout, but the conceptual contrast with that family is thin (Sections 2.1, 3.4).** Generating a batch of 15 augmented inputs (here, perturbation-masked variants) and averaging their gradients is structurally similar to SIM/Admix/AAM, yet the related-work treatment lists these as citations without contrasting APD's mechanism against them. The CAM-vs-random ablation (Fig. 4) is informative but does not establish that the gains come specifically from CAM-guided masking rather than from any informative input-perturbation policy with comparable diversity.
- **Operational definition of "drop perturbation" is described in words rather than equations (Eq. 3, Algorithm 1 reference).** The paper alternates between language that suggests setting adversarial pixels back to clean and language that suggests masking only for the gradient computation; "x^drop_{tjk} is the image generated by dropping the perturbations located at the j-th center point with scale kβ from x^adv_t" is the most explicit definition but does not pin down the operator formally. Iterating these two interpretations produces different attacks, so this should be tightened.
- **The 1.8% gap on MnasNet (Section 4.3, Table 3) is not discussed.** Within the "diverse architectures" experiment, gains of 13.3% (Seq2d-l) and 11.3% (ViT-B/16) sit alongside 1.8% on MnasNet, but the narrative averages over them. Given that the strongest transferability story would predict relatively uniform cross-architecture gains, the outlier deserves a sentence.
- **The empirical claim that attention regions are "composed of a limited number of blocks" (Section 3.3) is illustrated rather than measured.** It is supported in-body by qualitative CAMs on a single image (Fig. 2) and an appendix pointer; the in-body case for the claim is thin.

### Trivial
- The introductory framing ("Existing literature ... focus on spreading the adversarial perturbations towards the whole image") is broader than the cited works; methods like SIM and momentum-based attacks are not spatial-spreading methods, and a more precise framing would sharpen the contribution.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A direct test of the synergy hypothesis: e.g., compare transferability of a perturbation jointly optimized over two attention hotspots vs. the union of perturbations independently optimized at each hotspot, at matched budget.
- An ablation that replaces Grad-CAM++ with (a) Grad-CAM, (b) a cheaper saliency map, or (c) the magnitude of the current accumulated perturbation, to check how much of the gain depends on CAM quality vs. simply masking regions of high gradient mass.
- Quantify how much the attention region expands across iterations, since recomputing CAM at every step is the more expensive design choice (Section 3.4).

## Evaluation Axes
- **Originality:** Modest. CAM-guided masking + averaged gradients is a reasonable variation but lies within the established input-augmentation-ensemble family (SIM/AAM/Admix).
- **Importance of question:** Transferability is a well-motivated black-box-attack question with sustained community interest.
- **Are claims well supported:** Partially. The empirical claim (broad ASR improvement) is supported by multiple tables. The conceptual claim ("synergy of perturbations") is not isolated by the offered experiment.
- **Soundness of experiments:** Reasonable breadth across baselines, target models, defenses, and architectures, but lacking compute-matched comparison and using a configuration the paper's own ablation marks as sub-optimal.
- **Clarity of writing:** Acceptable; method intent is clear. The drop operator and the relation to neuron dropout vs. input-augmentation could be tightened.
- **Value to the community:** A practical plug-in that improves ASR with multiple base attacks; useful as an empirical data point, but the principled story it advertises is not yet demonstrated.
