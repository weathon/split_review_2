This is a reasonable structure for a position paper. However, I think it's fair to note that the bridge from "model says low risk instead of negligible" to "this is hurting vulnerable users" is an inferential step that could be stronger.

2. **Metric operationalization**: OCR (whether model's final risk exceeds GP label) is a reasonable but limited operationalization. The paper acknowledges this is one metric and also includes tag-based analysis of response patterns. The harsh critic's point about tone vs. risk-label mismatch is partially valid - the paper defines over-cautiousness as both objective risk overstatement AND subjective alarming tone, but the headline metric only measures the former. However, the tag-based analysis does address tone aspects (e.g., reassurance undermining, symptom checking).

3. **Counterposition engagement**: The paper does engage with the main counterargument in Sec. 6 (lines 323-406), including the tradeoff argument and the reassurance-seeking argument. The harsh critic argues this engagement is insufficient, particularly regarding the deployment-wide optimization question. I think the paper provides a reasonable but not exhaustive engagement.

4. **Split between two theses**: This is a valid observation - the paper oscillates between (a) a broad critique of current alignment ("always erring on the safe side") and (b) a narrower claim about calibration for vulnerable users. These are different scopes, and the paper would be sharper if it committed to one.

5. **GP labels as normative anchor**: The paper explicitly says they chose NOT to have GPs judge response appropriateness for OCD users (lines 207-210), acknowledging this limitation. The use of GP scenario risk as a baseline is pragmatic, not ideal.

Overall, this is a solid position paper with a clear, discussable stance, genuine empirical evidence, and thoughtful engagement with counterarguments. Its main weaknesses are: (1) the evidential bridge from OCR to real harm is inferential rather than demonstrated, (2) the paper oscillates between a broad alignment critique and a narrower calibration claim, and (3) the OCR metric captures risk-label mismatch but not the full construct (tone, triggering language) the paper claims to target. None of these are fatal for a position paper.

The paper argues that LLMs are over-cautious in health-related responses, and that this over-cautiousness actively harms vulnerable populations—particularly individuals with OCD and health anxiety—by reinforcing anxious thought patterns and driving unnecessary medical escalation. It introduces OCD-Eval (225 OCD-themed health anxiety queries), measures Over-Cautious Rate (OCR) across 22 LLMs (averaging 43.9% on low-risk scenarios), demonstrates that models with low OCR maintain high emergency recognition (debunking the assumed safety tradeoff), and shows that simply providing the user's OCD context to the model is not a reliable solution. The position calls for model-level recalibration toward actual risk levels.

## Strengths

- **Novel and important reframing**: The paper reframes over-cautiousness as a safety problem, not merely a helpfulness problem. This is a genuinely underexplored failure mode—prior work on over-refusal frames the drawback as "reduced helpfulness," whereas this paper argues it causes active harm to vulnerable users. This is a clear, discussion-worthy position.

- **Empirical evidence directly supports the core claim**: The OCD-Eval evaluation demonstrates over-escalation is widespread (43.9% average OCR across 22 models), and crucially, Figure 2 / Section 6 shows that models achieving low OCR (e.g., claude-sonnet-4.6 at ~20%) still maintain ~98% emergency recognition—directly rebutting the natural counterargument that reducing caution endangers users. This is substantive evidence, not merely philosophical assertion.

- **Practically valuable finding in Section 4.3**: Showing that providing the model with OCD context fails reliably (Gemma 3 27B dropped to 68% emergency recognition without consistently reducing OCR) is a concrete, actionable finding that rules out a superficially obvious solution and strengthens the case for model-side recalibration.

- **Thoughtful engagement with the reassurance-seeking counterargument (Sec. 6)**: The response to "OCD patients shouldn't use LLMs for reassurance" is nuanced—the paper separates the design question from the behavioral one, arguing that once a query is submitted, the interaction has already occurred; an over-cautious response redirects the reassurance loop toward more costly compulsive actions rather than preventing it. This is a genuine contribution to the debate.

- **Rigorous GP annotation with noise bound**: Using 7 GPs, computing inter-rater reliability (Gwet's AC2 of 0.93), and deriving an upper bound of 0.16 on annotation noise affecting OCR metrics (lines 205–224) ensures the reported rates reflect genuine model behavior rather than labeling artifacts.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The paper oscillates between a broad alignment critique and a narrower calibration claim—undermining positional sharpness.** Section 3 challenges "aligning AI with human values/preferences" and general over-alignment, while the empirical evidence specifically supports the narrower claim that models over-escalate low-risk, OCD-shaped queries. These are different theses at different scales. The broader philosophical attack on alignment orthodoxy (lines 127–159 with virtue ethics analogies) is more sweeping than what OCR data on health queries can sustain. The paper would be stronger if it foregrounded the specific, well-supported claim (risk calibration for vulnerable users) rather than interleaving it with a general alignment critique. As written, readers may find the position unclear at times.

- **The primary metric (OCR) does not fully capture the paper's own definition of over-cautiousness, creating a gap between what is measured and what is claimed.** The paper defines over-cautiousness as both objective risk overstatement AND subjective alarming tone (lines 29–32). The headline OCR metric captures only the former—whether the model's self-reported risk category exceeds the GP label. The tag-based analysis (symptom checking, reassurance undermining) partially addresses tone, but the central quantitative results and conclusions are driven by OCR. This matters because a model could assign the correct risk category while using triggering language, or vice versa, and OCR alone would miss both misalignments. This is not fatal—the evidence is still relevant and probative—but it means the empirical case does not fully substantiate the harm pathway that specifically involves alarming tone and reassurance undermining.

### Minor

- **The inferential step from "model overstates risk" to "this harms vulnerable users" relies on plausible but undemonstrated causal pathways.** Section 3 lists concrete harm pathways (chronic stress, skin damage, unnecessary doctor visits, dietary restriction), but these are extrapolated from OCD clinical literature and analogy to symptom checkers, not from direct evidence about LLM interaction outcomes. For a position paper, this reasoning is acceptable, but the conclusion in Section 7 states the problem "poses real harm" more definitively than the evidence warrants—given that no user outcome data is presented.

- **The paper explicitly declines to have GPs judge response appropriateness for OCD users (lines 207–210), leaving the normative anchor partially underspecified.** The authors acknowledge this is because "it is difficult for [a] GP to judge what is suitable for someone with an OCD background." This is understandable, but it means the paper lacks a clinically grounded standard for what an ideal response would look like—what language, tone, and information structure constitutes appropriate calibration for a user with health anxiety. Without this, "recalibration" remains somewhat underspecified as a prescription.

- **Section 6's claim that current models have "knowledge and expertise on par with clinical professionals" (lines 396–404) is stronger than needed and somewhat tangential.** The core argument only requires that models are competent enough to assess risk levels accurately—a much weaker threshold. Claiming parity with clinicians invites contestation on grounds irrelevant to the main position, without strengthening the essential case.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Direct evidence from OCD/anxious users about how model responses affect their anxiety or behavior (even small-scale qualitative evidence) would substantially strengthen the harm claim.
- A specification of what an "ideally calibrated" response template looks like for low-risk anxiety-triggered queries would make the recalibration prescription more actionable.
- Separation of three distinct phenomena currently conflated: triage over-escalation, hedged legalistic language, and psychologically triggering framing—each may require different interventions.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most novel and productive insight is the asymmetry argument embedded in the reassurance-seeking counterargument: once a user has submitted a health anxiety query to an LLM, the reassurance-seeking episode has already occurred—so the design question is not whether to provide reassurance, but which form causes less marginal harm. An over-cautious response doesn't prevent the reassurance loop; it redirects it toward more costly compulsive actions. This reframes the clinical dilemma productively and invites disagreement about the optimal response design.

## Suggestions

- Refine the scope: foreground the specific, well-supported claim (risk miscalibration harms anxious users) and de-emphasize the broad alignment critique. Either sharpen the alignment argument to specifically address health-safety tuning or treat it as context rather than a parallel thesis.
- In the discussion of OCR results, explicitly acknowledge what OCR does and does not measure relative to the paper's two-part definition, and state that the tag analysis is a partial but incomplete proxy for the tone/emotional impact dimension.
- Add a brief specification of what a well-calibrated response template would look like for a low-risk OCD-related query—this would make the recalibration prescription concrete and invite more productive disagreement.
