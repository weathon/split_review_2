## Summary

This position paper argues that LLMs are over-cautious in responding to health queries, and this over-cautiousness actively harms vulnerable populations—particularly individuals with OCD and health anxiety. The paper introduces OCD-Eval, a 225-query dataset measuring risk overstatement across 22 LLMs, finds an average Over-Cautious Rate (OCR) of 43.9% on low-risk scenarios, and empirically shows that giving models explicit OCD context degrades emergency recognition without reliably reducing over-cautiousness. It calls for recalibrating LLM alignment to match actual risk levels rather than defaulting to maximum caution.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and important distinction between over-refusal and over-cautiousness.** The paper identifies that LLMs can cause harm not just by refusing to answer (over-refusal, well-studied) but by answering with inflated risk levels that reinforce maladaptive thinking patterns. This conceptual reframing of over-cautiousness as active harm rather than mere unhelpfulness shifts the problem from a quality issue to a safety issue—a genuine and under-discussed contribution.

2. **Empirical demonstration that the most natural alternative solution (disclosing OCD context) fails.** Section 4.4 provides the paper's strongest empirical finding: providing models with the user's OCD background degraded emergency recognition (Gemma 3 27B dropping to 68.0%) without consistently reducing over-cautiousness (GPT-5.5 Thinking still over-cautious 44.2% of the time). This rules out the most obvious user-side workaround and strengthens the position that recalibration must be model-side.

3. **Thoughtful engagement with the reassurance-seeking counterargument (Section 6).** The paper honestly acknowledges that even well-calibrated responses reinforce reassurance-seeking in OCD, but argues correctly that the relevant comparison is between over-cautious and calibrated responses—not between any response and no interaction. This is a nuanced and persuasive argument: once a user has asked the question, an over-cautious response redirects the reassurance loop toward more costly compulsive actions (e.g., unnecessary ER visits), making calibrated responses the less harmful option.

4. **Equity dimension.** Lines 174–181 identify that over-cautiousness disproportionately harms remote, low-income, and underserved populations who face greater costs from unnecessary medical visits and are less likely to have mental health professionals who can identify their reassurance-seeking as an OCD symptom. This elevates the argument beyond a niche mental health concern to a justice issue.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The rebuttal to the trade-off counterargument relies on cross-sectional model comparisons that do not establish that alignment recalibration preserves emergency recognition.** The paper's strongest objection is that reducing over-cautiousness might come at the cost of worse emergency detection. The rebuttal (Section 6) cites models like Claude Sonnet 4.6 achieving ~20% OCR with ~98% emergency recognition versus Gemini 3 Flash's ~30% OCR with similar recognition. However, these are different models with different architectures and training. The within-family comparisons (Gemma 3→4, Gemini 2→3, Claude 3.7→4.6) better support the claim, but the paper itself attributes these improvements to "better general abilities and knowledge"—which is a capability confound, not evidence that alignment recalibration specifically can reduce over-cautiousness while maintaining safety. Since the policy recommendation is about recalibrating alignment, not about building more capable models, the evidence does not directly speak to the key question. This matters because the trade-off objection is the most natural and strongest counterargument.

- **The GP ground truth may not adequately capture OCD-specific over-cautiousness.** The paper acknowledges (lines 268–272) that GPs' labels for OCD-appropriate versus general-population-appropriate responses "are almost identical, because they may not fully recognize the nuances specific to OCD patients." This means OCR is measured against a general-population standard. For the paper's strongest claims about harm to OCD patients specifically, the metric may underestimate over-cautiousness relative to what an OCD-informed assessment would deem appropriate. The paper acknowledges this limitation but does not explain how the results remain valid under this condition—it merely abandons the OCD-specific labels. A brief discussion of whether the general-standard OCR is a lower bound, approximate, or potentially misleading for the OCD-specific claim would significantly strengthen the paper.

### Minor

- **The causal pathway from measured over-cautiousness to actual harm is inferred rather than demonstrated.** The paper lists physical harms (skin damage, infections, unbalanced diets, lines 161–173) as downstream effects of over-cautious LLM responses via reinforced anxiety. The links are plausible but rely on extrapolation from general OCD and health anxiety literature (Dorison et al., Doherty-Torstrick et al.) to LLM interactions specifically. The Anxiety Index (Section 4.3) measures predicted anxiety via a single mental health professional on 42 items, not behavioral or health outcomes. For a position paper, this level of analogical reasoning is acceptable—especially since the paper acknowledges the chain is speculative (line 229)—but the gap between "model assigns low risk instead of negligible" and "user develops skin infections" is bridged by inference rather than evidence.

- **The solutions section (Section 5) is thin relative to the position's ambition.** "Industry standards and metrics" and "user and public education" are generic recommendations that do not address the fundamental challenge the paper identifies: a general-purpose LLM cannot detect whether a user has OCD, and Section 4.4 shows explicit disclosure doesn't reliably help. Acknowledging this gap more explicitly would make the position more honest and invite productive discussion about how to serve heterogeneous user populations.

- **The paternalism objection from the opposite direction is not addressed.** The paper argues current alignment is paternalistically safety-oriented, but recalibrating toward "accurate risk assessment" for OCD patients could itself be seen as paternalistic—assuming we know what response pattern is best for them. Engaging with whether this simply replaces one set of values with another would strengthen the argument.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A user study, even small-scale, showing that OCD-diagnosed or high-anxiety participants report greater distress after over-cautious vs. calibrated responses would substantively strengthen the causal claim at the paper's core.
- Deeper psychological justification (beyond Krohne 1989 on general threat estimation) for the clinically critical distinction the OCR metric relies on—between "negligible" and "low" risk for OCD patients specifically.
- More specificity in the solution proposals about what alignment recalibration means in practice for models that cannot detect user vulnerability.

## Novel Insights

The most novel insight is that over-cautiousness and over-refusal are fundamentally different failure modes with different downstream consequences for vulnerable users. Over-refusal prevents the user from getting any information; over-cautiousness provides information but with inflated risk, which specifically reinforces the maladaptive thought patterns of health anxiety and OCD. This distinction matters because solutions for one (e.g., reducing over-refusal) do not address the other, and over-cautiousness may be more harmful precisely because it appears informative while amplifying the very distress it claims to help with. The empirical finding that giving models OCD context fails (Section 4.4) is also a genuinely novel and important result: it suggests that naive user-side disclosure is not a reliable solution, which has practical implications for how alignment training should proceed.

## Suggestions

- In the trade-off rebuttal, explicitly acknowledge the confound between model capability and alignment recalibration, and discuss what specific evidence would be needed to show that reducing over-cautiousness via alignment training (as opposed to capability gains) preserves emergency recognition. This would make the counterargument engagement more honest and productive.

- In Section 4, add a brief discussion of whether the general-population OCR should be interpreted as a lower bound for OCD-specific over-cautiousness, or whether it might be approximately correct, given the acknowledged GP limitation.

- In Section 5, explicitly acknowledge the open problem of serving heterogeneous users who cannot be identified in context, and frame this as a key open question for the community rather than eliding it with generic solutions.
