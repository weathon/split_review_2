## Summary

This position paper argues that LLMs' over-cautious responses to health queries constitute a safety harm—not merely a helpfulness deficit—particularly for vulnerable users with OCD and health anxiety. It defines over-cautiousness (objective risk overstatement + alarming tone), introduces OCD-Eval (225 queries sourced from authors with OCD experience), measures Over-Cautious Rate (OCR) across 22 LLMs (finding 43.9% average on low-risk scenarios), and argues for recalibration, contending that reducing over-cautiousness and maintaining emergency recognition are compatible goals.

## Strengths

- **Reframes over-cautiousness as a safety harm, not just reduced helpfulness**: The paper's central conceptual move—arguing that over-cautiousness is not merely "less helpful" (as OR-Bench frames over-refusal) but actively dangerous for vulnerable users—shifts the register of the debate and is crucial for the position's force. This reframing has genuine implications for how alignment should be evaluated and prioritized.

- **Choice of OCD as a focal population gives the argument specificity and measurable contours**: Rather than a generic "LLMs are too cautious" claim, grounding the argument in a specific, well-characterized clinical population (reassurance-seeking, intolerance of uncertainty, compulsive behavior patterns) makes the problem concrete and the harms identifiable. The dataset construction (Section 4) and its differentiation from HealthBench (authentic OCD-tone queries vs. neutral general-audience prompts) are well-justified.

- **The rebuttal to "OCD patients shouldn't seek reassurance from LLMs" (Section 6, lines 362–391) is the paper's strongest argumentative contribution**: The distinction between "what is therapeutically optimal" and "what causes least harm once interaction has occurred" is a genuine and productive reframing. The paper properly scopes this as harm reduction—"reducing the marginal harm introduced by over-cautiousness, not resolving the broader clinical challenge of reassurance-seeking itself" (lines 386–388). This is a carefully scoped, hard-to-dismiss argument.

- **Section 4.3's negative result on user-side OCD disclosure is valuable**: Providing models with the user's OCD background did not reliably reduce over-cautiousness and substantially degraded emergency recognition for some models (e.g., Gemma 3 27B dropped to 68%). This meaningfully strengthens the position by blocking the most obvious alternative solution and reinforcing that model-level recalibration—not user accommodation—is necessary.

- **Qualitative analysis of harmful response patterns (Section 4.2)**: The identification of "symptom checking" and "reassurance undermining" patterns (lines 265–272) provides concrete, observable behaviors that are more directly connected to the harm mechanism than the OCR metric. These patterns—"telling the user to 'keep an eye on' symptoms simultaneously communicates that something might go wrong"—are vivid and clinically plausible.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The 43.9% OCR headline metric predominantly reflects one-level risk elevations whose clinical significance for OCD patients is reasoned rather than grounded**: The paper acknowledges that SOCR (two+ level elevations) is low (lines 260–261: "the AI is unlikely to greatly exaggerate the risk, but will still tend to elevate the stated level of risk"), meaning the 43.9% figure primarily captures cases where "negligible" becomes "low." The paper argues this matters for OCD patients due to "intolerance of uncertainty, perfectionism, and overestimation of threat" (lines 237–238), but no OCD treatment literature is cited to establish that a model saying "low risk" rather than "negligible risk" materially worsens OCD symptoms or compulsive behaviors. Meanwhile, the qualitative findings that more directly implicate harm—symptom checking and reassurance undermining—receive much less emphasis than the OCR figure. The paper would be considerably stronger if it led with the qualitative harm mechanisms rather than the OCR metric, since those patterns are harder to dismiss as clinically insignificant. This gap means the headline measurement does not fully carry the weight of the severity claim the paper builds on it.

- **The trade-off rebuttal uses a cross-sectional observation to support a causal conclusion**: Section 6 (lines 331–350) argues that reducing over-cautiousness and maintaining emergency recognition are "not in tension" by observing that models with lower OCR also maintain high emergency recognition. The paper partially acknowledges the confound ("likely due to better general abilities and knowledge," line 347), but this acknowledgment undermines the conclusion: better-resourced, newer models may perform better on everything due to improved capabilities, which does not establish that intentionally reducing over-cautiousness through alignment changes would preserve emergency recognition. The data is compatible with both "no trade-off exists" and "there is a trade-off, masked by model quality." The paper's own partial acknowledgment of this confound makes the strong conclusion ("the trade-off this objection presupposes does not appear to be zero-sum," line 342–343) overstate what the evidence supports.

### Minor

- **The solutions section (Section 5) is generic and aspirational**: Industry standards, more professionals in alignment, and user education are reasonable but do not engage with the hard engineering question of how to recalibrate without introducing under-caution. The paper's own data shows some models achieve both low OCR and high emergency recognition, but doesn't explain what makes them different, missing an opportunity to transform the cross-sectional observation into an actionable claim.

- **The physical harms causal chain (lines 160–171) involves multiple asserted steps without specific evidence**: The chain from "LLM says 'see a doctor'" → "OCD patient validates fear" → "engages in compulsive behavior" → "skin damage / infection" involves multiple causal links, each asserted without evidence. The paper acknowledges this is secondary harm ("LLMs likely will not directly suggest these behaviors; however, the reinforced anxiety might lead users to them," line 169–170), but the speculative chain weakens the physical harm dimension of the position.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most distinctive contribution is identifying that over-cautiousness operates through two distinct harm channels that current evaluations conflate: (1) risk label inflation (negligible→low), which may be clinically mild, and (2) response-style harm (symptom checking, reassurance undermining, alarming hedging), which more directly triggers OCD maladaptive patterns. The fact that most of the paper's severity weight rests on channel (1) via the OCR metric, while the more compelling harm evidence comes from channel (2) via the qualitative analysis, suggests that future work on this topic should develop metrics that capture response-style harm directly, rather than relying on risk label mismatch as a proxy. This distinction between "what is measured" and "what matters for harm" is the paper's implicit but underexplored insight.

## Suggestions

- Reorder the presentation to lead with the qualitative harm patterns (symptom checking, reassurance undermining) as the primary evidence for harm, and present the OCR metric as supplementary quantitative corroboration. The qualitative findings are far more compelling and harder to dismiss than "negligible→low" risk elevations.
- Strengthen the trade-off rebuttal by acknowledging the cross-sectional confound more honestly and replacing the strong causal claim ("not in tension") with the more defensible position that the existence of models achieving both demonstrates it as an achievable engineering goal (which the paper already partially does at lines 348–350).
- Add 1–2 concrete example pairs showing current vs. ideal recalibrated responses to OCD-type queries, to make the solution vision tangible and debatable.
