## Summary

This position paper argues that strict alignment of image generation models to generalized aesthetic preferences undermines user autonomy and narrows artistic expression. It supports this position by constructing a "wide-spectrum aesthetics" benchmark of prompts requesting unconventional/anti-aesthetic outputs, then showing that aesthetic-aligned reward models (especially HPSv3, AUROC 0.385) fail to identify images matching those prompts even when explicitly cued, while unaligned base models (BLIP-L, AUROC 0.888) succeed. The paper further validates this bias by showing that historically significant real artworks receive low reward-model scores, and briefly tests for emotional bias against negative affect.

## Strengths

- **Empirically demonstrates that aesthetic alignment directly undermines instruction following**: The core result in Table 4 is compelling—aligned reward models like HPSv3 (AUROC 0.385) perform worse than random when selecting images matching anti-aesthetic prompts, while their unaligned base models BLIP-L (0.888) and CLIP-L (0.810) succeed. This cleanly isolates alignment as the cause rather than prompt comprehension, making a precise causal case for the position.

- **Real artwork validation grounds the argument concretely**: Testing reward models on canonical artworks (e.g., *The Scream* scoring only 5.23 on HPSv3 vs. typical AI images scoring 10–15) extends the finding beyond synthetic benchmarks and demonstrates the bias has cultural significance. The 10K-artwork LAPIS dataset validation with scores below 2 standard deviations from AI-image means strengthens this further.

- **Identifies a genuine, under-discussed tension in generative model alignment**: The paper articulates a real and specific conflict between instruction-following optimization and aesthetic preference optimization—this is not a generic anti-alignment argument but a precisely scoped technical tension that the ML community needs to address.

- **Productive distinction between safety filtering and aesthetic filtering**: Section 5's separation of "truly unsafe content" from "ideologically or aesthetically filtered content" provides a useful analytical framework that avoids opposing all alignment and enables productive disagreement.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper takes a clear position, is not merely a literature review, and its core argument is logically coherent even if overclaimed.

### Major

- **Normative claims outrun the empirical evidence**: The paper establishes that several current reward models and generation models fail to follow anti-aesthetic prompts. It does not establish that this is inherent to "universal aesthetic alignment" as such rather than a contingent artifact of specific datasets, reward formulations, or deployment choices. The conclusion's language—"aesthetic authoritarianism," "instrument of cultural assimilation," "systematically suppresses legitimate creative expression"—represents a normative escalation that the technical findings do not sustain at the same level of confidence. The paper itself provides counter-evidence: Flux Krea is preference-aligned yet preserves wide-spectrum flexibility, suggesting the problem is *how* alignment is done, not that alignment as a category is inherently authoritarian.

- **Failure to cleanly engage the strongest opposing position**: Section 5 rebuts a caricature ("clean and positive as morally superior") rather than the most defensible alternative: that default aesthetic priors are reasonable product design for general-use systems, provided reliable override mechanisms exist for explicit user requests. The paper's own text acknowledges this possibility (Section 2.4: "While a system may reasonably prioritize general preferences by default, it must maintain the flexibility to respect and execute a user's personalized style") but this nuance disappears in the conclusion. Since the paper's proposed remedy (Section 7, points a–d) primarily concerns user control and adjustable alignment—consistent with the "default + override" model—the paper should explicitly confront whether its evidence supports abolishing generalized aesthetic alignment, weakening it, or merely making it optional and transparent.

### Minor

- **Construct validity ambiguity in the benchmark**: Many anti-aesthetic prompts request blur, noise, fragmentation, poor lighting, and obscuration—traits that could manifest as simple technical degradation rather than artistically successful anti-aesthetic execution. The paper partially addresses this in Section 5 ("only 'clarity' could be argued as a technical flaw"), but this does not fully resolve the concern since generated images that *fail* to produce coherent anti-aesthetic art may still be scored as "successfully following the anti-aesthetic prompt." The paper would be stronger if it acknowledged this ambiguity more directly and discussed how to distinguish deliberate deviation from model failure.

- **Emotional bias evidence is underdeveloped in the main text**: Section 4.4 provides only one paragraph on emotional bias despite "toxic positivity" being a major plank of the paper's argument. Since the appendix is not accessible to all readers, this key supporting evidence is too thin in the main paper.

- **The real-artwork validation needs stronger linkage to generation-time suppression**: Low reward-model scores on canonical artworks suggest narrow evaluative regimes, but the paper does not establish that generation systems will actually suppress those styles in practice. The paper anticipates this objection and argues it constitutes "predictable structural harm" analogous to facial recognition failures, but this analogy is too quick for a central argumentative move.

### Trivial
- The Table 4 caption says "pick the better image from I_o and I_a with prompt p_o" but the text discusses evaluation under the wide-spectrum aesthetics prompt p_a; this appears to be a caption error rather than a substantive issue.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Deeper analysis of exceptions like Flux Krea and Nano Banana, which preserve wide-spectrum flexibility despite being aligned—these cases could clarify what kinds of alignment preserve pluralism and would strengthen the prescriptive claims.
- A more explicit bridge from the empirical results to the philosophical language, even a brief discussion of what level of evidence would be sufficient to justify terms like "authoritarianism" and "cultural assimilation."
- A cleaner statement of whether the paper opposes aesthetic alignment in principle, only when it overrides explicit user requests, or only when it lacks transparency/override mechanisms.

## Novel Insights

The paper makes an important observation that the alignment process itself—when it targets generalized aesthetic preference—creates a measurable conflict with instruction following that is directly demonstrable through reward model performance inversions. The finding that aligned reward models perform *worse* than their unaligned base models on anti-aesthetic prompts (HPSv3: 0.385 vs. BLIP-L: 0.888) is a clean inversion that suggests alignment is not merely additive but can actively impair capabilities. This pattern—that optimizing for general preference systematically degrades performance on the distribution tail—parallels concerns in RLHF about sycophancy and reward hacking, but is demonstrated here in the aesthetic domain for the first time with direct base-vs-aligned comparisons.

## Suggestions

- Narrow the conclusion to match what the evidence demonstrates: "Non-overrideable aesthetic alignment in current systems undermines prompt fidelity for unconventional aesthetics" rather than "aesthetic alignment is cultural assimilation." The strong empirical results will speak louder if the normative claims are calibrated to them.
- Explicitly address the "default + override" position in Section 5 as a distinct alternative from both full paternalism and no alignment. The paper's own recommendations are consistent with this position; acknowledging this would sharpen the debate.
- Develop the Flux Krea/Nano Banana exceptions into a positive argument: they show that "what kind of alignment" matters more than "whether alignment," which could transform the paper's contribution from "alignment is authoritarian" into the more constructive "current alignment approaches are mis-specified—here's what works."
