## Summary

This position paper argues that universal aesthetic alignment in image generation models constitutes "aesthetic authoritarianism": it systematically overrides user intent for anti-aesthetic outputs, narrows artistic expression, and imposes developer-centric values. It supports this claim with empirical evidence showing that reward models (especially HPSv3, which achieves below-random 0.381 AUROC on anti-aesthetic prompts) penalize images that faithfully follow wide-spectrum aesthetic instructions, and that real canonical artworks receive reward scores far below AI-generated images.

## Strengths

- **Striking empirical finding that alignment actively degrades prompt-following**: The below-random AUROC of HPSv3 (0.385) compared to unaligned BLIP-L (0.888) on wide-spectrum aesthetic prompts (Table 4) directly demonstrates that the *alignment process itself* causes degradation, not prompt complexity. The pairwise Wilcoxon and McNemar's tests with p-values below 10^-5 provide strong statistical support (Table 1).

- **Clear, non-trivial position identifying a specific architectural tension**: The paper identifies a concrete conflict between instruction-following and generalized aesthetic preference as competing alignment objectives. Its conclusion — that "instruction fidelity must take precedence over generalized aesthetic preferences" — is crisply stated and takes a definite stance.

- **Systematic evaluation across multiple model families and reward models**: Testing across Flux variants, SDXL/Playground, SD3.5M variants, and Nano Banana, combined with seven reward models plus unaligned baselines, demonstrates that the bias is systemic rather than an artifact of any single architecture (Tables 1–4).

- **Real artwork validation strengthens external validity**: Evaluating reward models on ~10K real artworks from LAPIS, showing that canonical works (e.g., Munch's *The Scream* scoring 5.23 on HPSv3 vs. typical AI scores of 10–15) receive systematically low scores, anchors the finding beyond synthetic prompts (Section 4.3, Figure 4).

- **Well-drawn distinction between moral safety and aesthetic conformity**: Section 5 cleanly separates "truly unsafe content" from "aesthetically filtered content," identifying a genuine conflation in current alignment practice that obscures the scope creep from safety to taste.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The anti-aesthetic prompts conflate artistic subversion with descriptions of generation failure, and this conflation is load-bearing for the "authoritarianism" claim.** Many generated prompts describe features indistinguishable from failed generations: "extremely blurry," "proportions warp chaotically," "lacking background detail and suffering from poor lighting," "noise-filled visual" (Figure 2). The paper's rebuttal that "only 'clarity' could be argued as a technical flaw" (Section 5) understates the problem: dimensions like "brightness," "detail," and "realism" are also standard quality metrics, and prompts combining multiple "bad" descriptors read as descriptions of model failures. This matters because the normative claim of "aesthetic authoritarianism" requires showing that reward models suppress *legitimate artistic expression*, but the prompts don't cleanly distinguish artistic anti-aesthetics from requests for degraded outputs. A prompt set requesting clearly intentional artistic styles (expressionism, surrealism, noir, horror) separate from requests for "bad" generation quality would isolate whether reward models suppress *style* or merely reward *visual fidelity* — and this distinction directly affects whether the phenomenon is "authoritarianism" or reasonable quality filtering.

- **Insufficient engagement with the "user preference vs. developer imposition" counterargument.** The paper frames aesthetic alignment as "developer-centered values" and "pre-emptive governance" (Section 2.4), but reward models like HPSv3, PickScore, and ImageReward are trained on *human preference data* — actual users choosing between images. If most users prefer conventionally attractive images, the alignment reflects aggregate user preference, not developer imposition. The paper's rebuttal in Section 5 (two sentences: "the experience of a minority should not be sacrificed for the majority") is insufficient for a position paper whose central normative claim depends on resolving this tension. A more developed argument — distinguishing between the *design choice* to optimize for aggregate preference (which is developer-imposed) from the *content* of that preference (which is user-derived), or proposing default-override architectures — would significantly strengthen the position.

### Minor

- **BLIP/CLIP comparison doesn't fully isolate aesthetic preference from intentionality detection.** The paper argues that "complex prompt understanding is not the underlying issue" because unaligned BLIP/CLIP perform well (Section 4.1). But BLIP/CLIP perform image-text similarity matching — a different task from preference ranking that doesn't require judging whether visual quality is intentional. This doesn't invalidate the finding (the aligned-vs-unaligned gap is real and striking), but the inference that reward models *choose* to override user intent vs. *cannot detect intentionality* is not definitively resolved by the current experiments. This is an addressable concern that could be clarified with additional experimental conditions.

- **The harm pathway from low aesthetic preference scores is underspecified.** The paper draws an analogy to facial recognition bias (Buolamwini & Gebru, 2018) in Section 4.3, but the comparison is incomplete: facial recognition failures cause direct material harm (misidentification), while low aesthetic preference scores suppress certain styles in generation. The paper should articulate more precisely how aesthetic suppression causes harm — e.g., by homogenizing creative output, limiting artistic tools for marginalized aesthetics, or reducing the expressive range available to users.

- **The mitigation section (Section 6) is underdeveloped.** A single figure showing LoRA results with no quantitative evaluation leaves practical implications vague for a paper that calls for "user-controllable mechanisms" and design reforms. This is a minor issue since mitigation is not the paper's primary contribution, but given the strong calls to action in the conclusion, more concrete demonstration would help.

### Trivial
None.

## Novel Insights

The paper identifies a specific, measurable conflict between two alignment objectives — instruction-following and aesthetic preference — and demonstrates that current systems resolve this conflict by silently overriding user instructions in favor of visual conformity. The below-chance performance of HPSv3 (AUROC 0.385) is a genuinely surprising finding: alignment doesn't just add noise to instruction-following; it actively inverts it for anti-aesthetic prompts. This is more than a preference gap — it's a systematic inversion of user intent, which strengthens the "authoritarianism" framing beyond what a mere accuracy drop would suggest.

## Suggestions

- Separate the anti-aesthetic prompt set into two categories: (a) prompts requesting clearly intentional artistic styles (e.g., "painted in an expressionist style with distorted proportions and clashing colors") and (b) prompts requesting degraded technical quality (e.g., "blurry, poorly lit, low detail"). Run both through the same reward model evaluation. If both categories show the same inversion, the "authoritarianism" claim is strongly supported. If only (b) does, the concern is narrower but still significant.

- Engage more substantively with the user-preference counterargument: distinguish between the *choice to optimize for aggregate preference* (which is a developer design decision) and the *content of that preference* (which reflects user data). This distinction could strengthen the paper's position by showing that even if preferences are data-driven, the decision to make them non-overridable is developer-imposed.

- Add quantitative evaluation of the LoRA mitigation (Section 6) — even basic metrics comparing anti-aesthetic generation success rates and conventional image quality before and after LoRA application would make the calls to action more concrete.
