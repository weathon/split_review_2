## Summary

Hi-Light is a training-free, backbone-agnostic video relighting framework that addresses two known failure modes of existing methods — light flickering and detail degradation — through three plug-and-play modules: a lightness-prior-anchored progressive diffusion scheme, a Hybrid Motion-Adaptive Lighting Smoothing Filter (HMA-LSF), and a LAB-based Detail-Preserving Fusion module (LAB-DF). The paper additionally proposes the Light Stability Score (S_LS), a novel quantitative metric for temporal lighting consistency. Evaluated on 100 diverse video clips, Hi-Light substantially outperforms open-source baselines on SSIM and S_LS.

---

## Strengths

- **Clean ablation isolates causal component roles (Table 2):** All 2³ combinations are tested. HMA-LSF is the primary driver of S_LS improvement (0.285 → 0.462), LAB-DF is the primary driver of SSIM (0.607 → 0.943), and combining all three achieves the best of both. The component attribution is clear and methodologically sound.

- **Frequency-domain evidence is independent and difficult to game (Figure 7, left):** The Fourier magnitude spectra show Hi-Light's output is visually indistinguishable from the input's spectral profile, while competing methods exhibit pronounced high-frequency attenuation. This corroborates the SSIM findings through a completely independent signal-processing lens.

- **HMA-LSF's motion-adaptive design explicitly addresses the motion-blur/flicker tradeoff:** The adaptive α in Eq. 7 reduces smoothing when motion magnitude is high, preventing ghosting artifacts. This is a principled, well-motivated engineering choice.

- **S_LS is validated against human perception (§5):** A double-blind study with 30 participants yields 95.6% agreement between S_LS rankings and human rankings for light stability — providing meaningful perceptual grounding for a novel metric.

- **Practical compute footprint:** The method operates on a single L40 GPU using CPU-only Farneback optical flow, making it genuinely accessible compared to GPU-intensive baselines.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None that strictly invalidate the method's technical operation.

### Major

- **The SSIM headline claim is circular by design.** Eq. 9 reads: $L' = L_i + \beta(G_\sigma * L_r)$. This replaces most of the output's L-channel with the original input's L-channel, adding only the low-frequency illumination from the relit video. By construction, the output structurally mimics the original input — high SSIM relative to the input is therefore guaranteed, not independently earned. Table 2 makes this explicit: without LAB-DF, Hi-Light's SSIM (0.607–0.623) is comparable to baselines (0.552–0.604); LAB-DF alone drives the jump to 0.943. The paper's central quantitative claim — "56% improvement in detail preservation" — describes a designed outcome, not a demonstration that relighting occurred with better quality. Comparing this to purely generative baselines that produce different pixels is not an apples-to-apples test of relighting fidelity.

- **No metric in the evaluation assesses whether the relighting was actually effective.** SSIM, LPIPS, FID, S_LS, and VBench all measure fidelity-to-input, perceptual quality, or temporal stability. None measures whether the text-specified lighting prompt was realized in the output. A method that returns the unmodified original video would score ~1.0 SSIM and ~0.68 on S_LS (higher than Hi-Light's 0.509, as Figure 6 shows). The human evaluation (§5) asks participants to rank temporal light stability and spatial detail quality — not whether the relighting matched the prompt. For a paper proposing a "refined evaluation paradigm" for video relighting, the complete absence of any lighting-accuracy metric is a fundamental gap that prevents the paper's core claim from being quantitatively supported.

- **The paper incorrectly characterizes RAFT as "transformer-based" (§3.2.2).** The cited paper [12] is titled "Recurrent All-Pairs Field Transforms" — RAFT is a recurrent CNN (GRU-based), not a transformer. The stated 80GB VRAM requirement for RAFT on 81 frames is also unfounded; RAFT operates on sequential frame pairs and requires well under 4GB per pair on 480p. While choosing Farneback for CPU accessibility is a reasonable engineering decision, the stated motivation is factually incorrect and should not be published as written.

### Minor

- **S_LS comparison with TC-Light is confounded by sequence length.** TC-Light processes 30 frames; Hi-Light processes 81 (acknowledged in Figure 7 caption but not addressed in Table 1). S_LS temporal smoothness is sensitive to sequence length — shorter sequences have fewer inter-frame transitions. The claim that Hi-Light "nearly doubles" TC-Light's S_LS cannot be cleanly attributed to better stability alone.

- **S_LS's fixed threshold τ = 125 is arbitrary and not validated.** The metric segments "bright pixels" above a fixed grayscale threshold of 125 regardless of the video's overall exposure. Dark-scene videos would yield very few suprathreshold pixels, making S_LS computed from sparse counts unstable. No sensitivity analysis for τ is provided.

- **FID reference distribution is not specified.** It is unclear whether FID (Table 1) is computed against the original input frames, a held-out natural video distribution, or something else. This matters because FID against the input is again biased toward methods that do not modify the video significantly.

- **No variance or content-type breakdown reported.** Results are aggregated across 100 clips (70 portraits, 30 non-human). LAB-DF's performance likely varies by scene type — particularly on dynamic scenes with occlusions and complex motion where optical flow is noisy. A breakdown by content category would substantially strengthen or sharpen the claims.

- **β hyperparameter (Eq. 9) tuning is undisclosed.** It is not stated how β was determined or whether it was set before or after seeing the 100-clip evaluation set.

### Trivial

- The normalization by peak-to-peak range $R$ in S_LS can be numerically unstable for low-variance clips where $R \approx 0$.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Including a CLIP-based or lighting-estimation metric to measure how well the output's illumination matches the text prompt would directly address the main evaluation gap. Even a simple directional correlation between CLIP(output) and CLIP(prompt) versus CLIP(input) and CLIP(prompt) would disambiguate "detail-preserving relighting" from "barely changed original."

- Presenting intermediate outputs (I_smooth before LAB-DF fusion) compared to baselines on the same SSIM axis would demonstrate that the relighting quality of the intermediate video is comparable to baselines, anchoring the claim that LAB-DF preserves detail without sacrificing relighting efficacy.

- A synthetic validation for S_LS using videos with artificially introduced flicker of known frequency and magnitude would demonstrate that the metric is sensitive to flicker specifically rather than general temporal smoothness.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation is the inversion of the detail-transfer direction in LAB-DF (Eq. 9): rather than transplanting input high-frequency detail into the relit frame (which causes ghosting due to geometric misalignment), the method transplants only the low-frequency illumination from the relit frame onto the intact input. This is an elegant insight that sidesteps the alignment problem entirely. However, this same inversion is also why the SSIM evaluation is circular — the two are inseparable design consequences. A reviewer contribution not in the paper: the evaluation gap and the architectural design are in tension; the paper cannot simultaneously claim LAB-DF as a "relighting component" and claim SSIM as an independent measure of relighting quality.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a relighting-faithfulness metric.** Compute CLIP similarity between the output video and the text lighting prompt, and compare this against the same score for the unmodified input. This will demonstrate that Hi-Light actually applies the lighting, not merely preserves the original.

2. **Correct the RAFT characterization.** Replace "transformer-based optical flow models like RAFT" with "flow models such as RAFT" and either provide an accurate VRAM figure or replace the 80GB claim with a citation to a specific configuration's profile.

3. **Acknowledge the SSIM circularity explicitly.** A sentence in §3.1.2 noting that SSIM against the original input rewards structural similarity by design, and that the LAB-DF architecture is partly motivated to achieve this, would make the evaluation honest and actually strengthen the paper's framing — the paper's claim is *not* that relighting is perceptually similar to the original, but that relighting is applied *while* preserving original detail.

4. **Control for TC-Light sequence length in S_LS comparisons.** Either run Hi-Light on 30 frames for that comparison, or note the confound explicitly in Table 1.

## Score and Decision

Hi-Light presents a practical and well-engineered training-free relighting framework with genuine technical contributions (HMA-LSF, LAB-DF, S_LS). The ablation is clean and the qualitative results are compelling. However, the two major issues are directly verifiable from the paper: (1) the headline SSIM figure is inflated by an architectural design that explicitly re-injects the original structure, making it a circular measure rather than an independent assessment of relighting quality; and (2) no metric in the paper — including the proposed S_LS — measures whether the target lighting was actually achieved. For ICLR, where quantitative evaluation is foundational to the contribution, these gaps are substantial. The factual error about RAFT adds further concern about the precision of the technical claims. The paper is not in a state suitable for publication at a top venue without major evaluation redesign.
