## Summary

This paper identifies a fundamental blind spot in how frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems are evaluated. It shows that current pixel-level metrics (PSNR, SSIM) are inadequate for measuring identity-level privacy, and presents FaceLinkGen — a simple distillation-based attack that extracts identity information from protected templates without recovering original pixels. The attack achieves high linkage accuracy (e.g., 0.99/0.98/0.92 1-to-1 verification across three PPFR methods on LFW) and regeneration success (≥97.9% Success@5 across all settings), works with oracle access or a constrained high-pass proxy, costs under $2 to run, and generalizes to non-frequency methods (CanFG, TIP-IM). The paper's core argument — that the vulnerability lies in the representation, not the attack sophistication — is well-supported.

## Strengths

1. **Exposes a fundamental evaluation blind spot.** The paper provides concrete evidence (Table 1) that pixel-level similarity and identity-level similarity are not correlated in PPFR contexts. This directly challenges the prevailing evaluation paradigm and motivates a shift toward identity-centric metrics. This is the paper's most important contribution.

2. **Simple, highly effective attack that proves the point.** FaceLinkGen uses only cosine similarity distillation with a standard ArcFace teacher, yet it extracts identity information near the theoretical upper bound set by dataset noise (e.g., linkage accuracy 0.8572 vs. original-to-original 0.8823 in Table 4). The simplicity is intentional and persuasive: if a generic distillation pipeline suffices, the vulnerability is structural, not an artifact of sophisticated attack design.

3. **Extremely low barrier to attack.** The distillation completes in under 2 hours on a single A6000 GPU (~$0.80–$1.60), with the constrained proxy attack requiring only 30 paired samples. Even with just 256 training images, the attack achieves 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration success. This provides a realistic lower bound on attack feasibility.

4. **Thorough and multi-faceted evaluation.** The paper evaluates three frequency-domain PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) plus two non-frequency methods (TIP-IM, CanFG), across three datasets (CASIA hold-out, LFW, TPDNE), with cross-verification by two commercial APIs (Face++, Amazon), under both oracle-access and constrained threat models. Results are internally consistent.

5. **Soft biometric leakage analysis.** The demonstration that protected templates leak age, gender, and race at near-original accuracy (Table 9: FracFace gender 0.925 vs. original 0.949) adds an independent privacy risk dimension and strengthens the argument for identity-centric evaluation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Abstract and conclusion numerically overstate results.** The abstract claims "over 98.5% matching accuracy and above 96% regeneration success." The regeneration claim holds for Success@5 (all ≥97.9%), but the matching accuracy claim is unsupported for the weakest method: FracFace achieves only 0.92 (92%) 1-to-1 verification in Table 3. The conclusion and Section 1 repeat similar language. While the best-case or averaged numbers could approach 98.5%, the paper states them as unconditional bounds covering "three... systems." This is a factual inaccuracy that undermines trust in the reporting. The authors should report the range (92–99%) or the per-method breakdown in the abstract. This does not invalidate the paper's central contribution but must be corrected.

### Minor

2. **The regeneration attack's added value beyond the linkage attack is not precisely delineated.** The paper correctly notes in Section 7 that identity extraction is established without any generative model (via cosine similarity distributions and linkage accuracy). However, the regeneration attack is billed prominently alongside the linkage attack, and the paper does not explicitly state that linkage is the core result while regeneration is an illustrative downstream capability. Arc2Face's generative quality could inflate perceived attack success — the paper partially addresses this (Section 7 similarity analysis), but a clear hierarchical statement would sharpen the argument.

3. **Missing architecture ablation for the student model.** The student model prepends a 3×3 Conv2D layer to Antelopev2 to handle variable template channel counts. The sensitivity of the attack to this architectural choice is not explored. A brief ablation (e.g., replacing the conv with a linear projection, or using a different backbone) would strengthen the claim that the attack is not brittle.

4. **Constrained attack evaluated only on LFW.** The constrained high-pass proxy attack (Section 6) is tested on LFW only (Table 7). Reporting results on TPDNE and CASIA hold-out for this setting would strengthen the claim that the proxy works under distribution shift.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Cross-embedding generalization.** Training the student with a different teacher model (e.g., FaceNet instead of ArcFace) and a different public dataset would further rule out dataset/architecture-specific leakage. The paper claims this is possible (Section 3) but does not demonstrate it.
- **Testing the high-frequency hypothesis.** The paper suggests that identity information resides in high-frequency components. Directly low-pass filtering templates at inference time and measuring extraction degradation would confirm or refute this mechanism.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews collectively surface an important observation: the paper's demonstration that a generic distillation pipeline works across multiple PPFR methods suggests that *any representation that preserves recognition utility will necessarily preserve recoverable identity information*. This has structural implications beyond the specific PPFR methods tested — it questions whether frequency-domain transformation-based PPFR can ever achieve identity-level privacy without cryptographic guarantees, since the very property that makes the representation useful for recognition (identity-discriminative features) is what makes it attackable. The constrained high-pass proxy attack reinforces this: if even a generic blur-based filter suffices as a proxy, the vulnerability is not in the conversion algorithm but in the decision to convert to a manifold that retains identity information at all.

## Suggestions

1. **Correct the abstract and conclusion claims.** Report the full range of results (e.g., "matching accuracy of 92–99% and regeneration success of 90–100% across methods") rather than best-case numbers. Alternatively, report method-specific results in the abstract.
2. **Add a one-sentence clarification** that the linkage attack constitutes the core identity-extraction result, with regeneration being an illustrative downstream application that additionally depends on Arc2Face.
3. **Add an architecture ablation** (even a brief one) showing the attack works with different student model adaptations.
4. **Report constrained-attack results** on at least one additional dataset (TPDNE or CASIA hold-out) to strengthen distribution-shift claims.
