## Summary

FaceLinkGen argues that the dominant evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) — measuring attack resistance via PSNR and SSIM — fundamentally mischaracterizes identity-level privacy. The paper presents FaceLinkGen, a distillation-based attack that trains a student model to extract ArcFace embeddings from protected templates and then regenerates identity-consistent faces via a diffusion model (Arc2Face), without recovering original pixels. Evaluated on three PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), the attack achieves >98.5% matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success validated by two independent commercial APIs (Face++ and Amazon), at minimal cost (~$1.20 and under 2 hours on a single GPU).

---

## Strengths

- **Pixel-metric vs. identity-metric decoupling is concretely demonstrated.** Table 1 and Figure 2 provide a clean proof-of-concept: a CanFG-protected face achieves SSIM=0.841/PSNR=26.81 against its original yet has face similarity FS=0.008, while two arbitrary photos of the same person can share SSIM=0.36 but FS=0.72. This directly grounds the paper's main premise in observable data rather than abstraction.

- **Near-total identity recovery across three systems and three datasets.** Table 2 shows regeneration Success@5 of 97.9–100% and per-image Pass@1e-5 of 90.4–99.3% across all (system, dataset) combinations, leaving essentially no PPFR system able to credibly claim identity-level protection under the stated threat model.

- **Independent dual-commercial-API cross-validation.** Oracle-access results are verified by both Face++ ("financial-grade security") and Amazon (Table 5: 92–99%), which are independent of ArcFace/Arc2Face and of each other. This directly addresses any circularity concern arising from using the same model family for both training and evaluation.

- **Constrained-attacker extension is conceptually elegant.** The high-pass-filter proxy attack (Section 6) — trained on one model and tested on all three PPFR methods without system-specific knowledge — achieves 92–96% matching accuracy and 94–96% Face++ Success@5 using only 30 validation pairs. This shows the vulnerability is structural, not an artifact of oracle access.

- **Soft biometric leakage adds meaningful practical scope.** Table 9 demonstrates that race (0.66–0.67 vs. 0.70 original), gender (0.89–0.93 vs. 0.95), and age (MAE 4.7–5.9 vs. 4.6) can be recovered from protected templates via a lightweight MLP head trained on FairFace for one epoch — exposing an independently sensitive privacy risk not visible in pixel-level metrics.

- **Minimal-resource attack (Section 5.1) rules out data-abundance as an explanation.** Training on 800 images in under 50 seconds still achieves 97.0% Pass@1e-5 and 99.5% linkage accuracy, establishing that the vulnerability is in the representation itself, not a consequence of a well-resourced attacker.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The Amazon API gap in the constrained-attacker scenario is dismissed without analysis.** Table 7 reports Face++ Success@5 of 94–96% alongside Amazon Success@5 of only 44–57% — a 40–50 percentage-point gap between two independent commercial verifiers evaluating the same generated images. The paper's sole explanation is "the Amazon API is likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images" (Section 6). This is speculation: it is not tested, and the gap is large enough that a competing explanation — that Arc2Face-generated images genuinely fail Amazon's liveness or AI-image detection layer — would substantially qualify the headline constrained-attacker claim. The oracle-access Amazon results (Table 5: 92–99%) do not have this gap, which suggests the discrepancy is specific to Arc2Face's generated images in the constrained setting, not to Amazon's general strictness. The authors should either test Arc2Face-generated images against Amazon for known identities (to isolate the cause) or lower the constrained-attacker success claim accordingly.

### Minor

- **The claim that linkage accuracy "essentially reaches the theoretical maximum" is overstated.** Section 5.2 attributes the entire gap between MinusFace-to-MinusFace top-1 recall (0.72) and original-to-original recall (0.88) to 9.3–13% label noise in CASIA-WebFace. However, the noise estimate was established for standard face-to-face recognition; in the template domain, representation quality also degrades, introducing additional error that the paper does not account for. The 16-point gap is not trivially explained by noise alone. The claim should be softened (e.g., "suggesting near-ceiling performance given dataset noise") and ideally supported by an independent noise correction estimate.

- **The structural impossibility argument is under-formalized for its implied scope.** Section 3 states "as long as $z_I$ persists in $T$, an attacker can recover $z'_I$" and concludes that any utility-preserving PPFR system is implicitly vulnerable — but this is not stated as a formal claim. The paper's empirical results fully support a general impossibility argument (that any oracle-access adversary can succeed against any utility-preserving PPFR system via a method of this form), yet the paper frames most findings as exposing specific failures in the three evaluated systems. Adding even an informal statement of this general consequence — rather than leaving it implicit — would significantly strengthen the paper's conceptual contribution and make clear the findings generalize beyond the three tested methods.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most significant conceptual insight — made implicitly but not fully formalized — is that *utility-preservation and privacy are fundamentally in tension for any transformation-based PPFR system under oracle access*: because any system that enables recognition necessarily preserves learnable identity-discriminative structure in the template, and because a service-provider adversary can trivially construct paired (original, template) data, a standard distillation pipeline will always succeed. The paper's empirical demonstration that this works with only 800 images and $0.80 in compute turns this into a practical lower bound: future PPFR systems cannot claim identity privacy on the basis of pixel-level metrics alone, and any non-cryptographic transformation that preserves recognition utility should be treated as identity-leaking by default. The soft biometric recovery results (Table 9) extend this insight: the same distilled embeddings that enable face regeneration also enable profiling — the privacy failure is not limited to hard identity but encompasses the full attribute space encoded in facial embeddings.

## Suggestions

1. Investigate and explain the Amazon API gap in the constrained scenario: generate Arc2Face images for known identities (not from protected templates), test against both Face++ and Amazon, and report the differential pass rate. This will cleanly isolate whether the 44–57% Amazon rate is a generator-quality artifact or a real limitation of the constrained extraction step.
2. Add a two-sentence formal statement of the structural consequence: "Any PPFR system that preserves recognition utility necessarily retains learnable identity information; any oracle-access adversary can recover it via a distillation method of this form." Place it in the conclusion to frame the findings as a community-level evaluation standard, not a specific attack on three systems.
3. Qualify the "essentially reaching theoretical maximum" claim in Section 5.2 with either a noise-corrected estimate or more conservative language.
