## Summary

This position paper argues that LLM safety alignment, when applied with excessive caution to health-related queries, produces harmful "over-aligned" responses that can exacerbate anxiety and OCD—making over-cautious behavior unethical and dangerous for vulnerable populations. The position is supported by drawing on cyberchondria and OCD literature, virtue theory and Kantian ethics, illustrative case studies across three state-of-the-art LLMs, quantitative labeling results, and rebuttals to three counterarguments.

## Strengths

- **Identifies a genuinely under-discussed and important problem.** The observation that LLM safety alignment can cause specific harms to OCD/anxiety populations—by treating "see a doctor immediately" as a one-size-fits-all response—is novel and timely, especially given that 30% of US consumers already use generative AI for health information (Choy et al., 2024).

- **Exceptional counterargument engagement.** Section 6's rebuttal to "people with anxiety/OCD should not use LLMs" is the paper's strongest moment: it concedes the objection is "technically correct" but then dismantles it with specific clinical statistics—a 5–12 year diagnosis gap (Bey et al., 2025; Ziegler et al., 2021), only 17.5% service utilization in 12 months (Mack et al., 2014), and less than 4% awareness that reassurance-seeking is counterproductive (Mohammed et al., 2019). This transforms the strongest objection into support for the position.

- **Effective case studies that concretize the argument.** The rabies case (Case Study 2) is particularly vivid—showing how Gemini's response to "a dog passed by me" triggers immediate medical escalation, while Qwen correctly identifies near-zero risk. These examples ground what would otherwise be an abstract philosophical argument in recognizable, high-stakes scenarios.

- **Productive conceptual distinction from sycophancy.** The appendix (lines 686–690) clearly differentiates over-alignment on harmlessness from the already-discussed phenomenon of over-alignment on helpfulness/sycophancy, identifying an underexamined dimension of alignment failure.

- **Honest limitations section.** The authors explicitly acknowledge the small dataset, low inter-rater reliability, lack of multi-turn evaluation, and that queries are based on patients' experiences rather than professional opinions (lines 377–402). This strengthens credibility.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Gap in the causal mechanism linking over-cautious LLM responses to OCD/anxiety exacerbation.** The paper's evidence base is drawn from cyberchondria and symptom checker literature, where the mechanism of harm involves exposure to *alarming diagnostic possibilities* (e.g., "your symptoms could indicate cancer"). But the over-cautious LLM responses the paper identifies include both genuinely alarming responses (Gemini's rabies escalation) *and* generic medical disclaimers ("I am not a medical professional," "monitor for symptoms"). The paper acknowledges this gap at lines 211–214 ("LLMs likely will not directly suggest these behaviors; however, the reinforced anxiety might lead users to them as a form of secondary harm"), but this is assertion rather than argument. It matters because the core claim—that over-cautious responses are "dangerous"—requires establishing that the same anxiety-amplification pathway applies when the response is simply a conservative disclaimer rather than a catastrophic possibility list. Some of the case studies do show alarming content (Gemini's rabies advice), which partially bridges this gap, but the paper's broader claim extends to generic cautious disclaimers where the mechanism is less clear. This doesn't invalidate the position but reduces how far the argument can carry "dangerous" as a characterization of all over-cautious responses.

- **Underdeveloped normative alternative.** The paper identifies what over-cautious responses look like and why they're harmful, but provides only thin guidance on what appropriately calibrated responses should look like. The case studies show that Qwen's responses are "better," but no general principle is articulated for where the line should be drawn. The Appendix solutions—"domain-specific model development," "professionals in alignment," "user education"—are standard recommendations that could apply to any alignment concern. This matters because a position paper advocating change benefits from at least sketching what the changed landscape looks like, so readers can evaluate feasibility. Without this, the reader cannot assess whether a feasible alternative avoids both over-caution and under-caution, which is the paper's own stated requirement (Section 6/Ethical Considerations, lines 407–428).

### Minor

- **Unresolved tension between context-awareness and anti-personalization.** The paper argues LLMs should be "context aware" and recognize anxiety patterns (e.g., identifying that "a dog passed by me" with rabies worry is "a classic symptom of OCD"—Case Study 2, line 262), but also explicitly rejects personalization on privacy grounds, arguing that AI "should be context aware and avoid being overly cautious in any situations, regardless [of] user's mental health history" (Appendix A, lines 718–720). The paper partially addresses this by advocating general risk-calibration, but if the recommendation is universal calibration rather than OCD-specific response adaptation, then the OCD-specific framing becomes primarily motivational rather than argumentatively essential. The paper should be clearer about whether it is advocating for a general improvement (which would benefit everyone) or a specific accommodation (which raises the privacy issues it flags).

- **Brief treatment of liability as a structural driver.** The paper mentions corporate liability at lines 164–166 but treats it as an ethical failing rather than engaging with it as a genuine design constraint. In many jurisdictions, providing specific medical advice without qualification could expose developers to legal liability. This is not just corporate self-protection—it constrains what responses are feasible. A stronger position would engage with this as a real trade-off rather than primarily an ethical critique.

### Trivial
None worth listing.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A worked example of an "appropriately calibrated" response with explicit criteria (beyond "Qwen's response was better") would help readers evaluate the feasibility of the alternative.

- Deeper engagement with the asymmetric risk problem: an under-cautious "you're probably fine" response could delay care for a genuine medical issue, which is a real design trade-off the paper could address more fully.

- The term "over-alignment" invites comparison to overfitting, but the phenomena are structurally different. The paper defines its usage clearly enough, but a brief acknowledgment of the disanalogy (rather than leaning into the analogy) would prevent reader confusion.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most novel contribution is the inversion of the standard safety framing: rather than arguing that LLMs are too dangerous, it argues that LLM safety measures themselves can be dangerous to specific populations. The strongest insight is the counterargument response—using clinical statistics on OCD diagnosis delays and low treatment-seeking rates to establish that placing responsibility on users to avoid problematic interactions is unrealistic. This transforms what seems like an obvious objection ("just don't use LLMs if you have OCD") into evidence for why the problem demands systemic attention.

## Suggestions

- Articulate at least a preliminary framework for what appropriately calibrated health responses look like—for instance, criteria like: (1) acknowledge the actual probability level, (2) avoid triggering symptom-checking, (3) provide context-appropriate reassurance while maintaining responsible disclaimers. Even 2–3 principles would help the reader evaluate the feasibility of the alternative.

- Resolve the context-awareness vs. anti-personalization tension explicitly: clarify whether the argument is that all users benefit from better risk-calibration (making it a general alignment problem) or that OCD/anxiety users need specific accommodations (making it a targeted intervention that requires detection).
