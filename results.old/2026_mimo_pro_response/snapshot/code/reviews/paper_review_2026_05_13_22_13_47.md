## Summary

This paper challenges the prevailing pixel-level evaluation paradigm for frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems. The authors present FaceLinkGen, a distillation-based attack that extracts identity embeddings directly from protected templates and uses them for identity linkage and face regeneration — without attempting pixel-level reconstruction. Across three recent frequency-domain PPFR methods (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace) and three datasets, FaceLinkGen achieves >98.5% matching accuracy and >96% regeneration success verified by commercial APIs, demonstrating a substantial gap between pixel-distortion metrics and actual identity-level privacy.

## Strengths

- **Clear, well-motivated problem identification with strong empirical support.** The paper demonstrates that pixel-level distortion metrics (PSNR, SSIM) are not a reliable proxy for identity-level privacy. The side-by-side comparison in Table 6 — where FracFace's own high "protection" metrics correspond to near-total identity recovery under FaceLinkGen — directly and convincingly refutes the implicit assumption that preventing pixel reconstruction implies identity protection.

- **The attack is simple, reproducible, and intentionally generic.** FaceLinkGen uses a standard distillation pipeline from a frozen ArcFace teacher, requiring no knowledge of the target PPFR system's architecture or parameters. Training completes in under two hours on a single GPU. The simplicity is a deliberate design choice that strengthens the argument: if a standard procedure suffices, the vulnerability is in the representation itself, not in a clever attack.

- **Thorough threat model analysis that returns to PPFR first principles.** Section 2 carefully argues that the service provider — not a network eavesdropper — is the intended primary adversary, and that oracle access to the conversion process is a realistic assumption. The paper also explores a more constrained attacker setting (Section 6) with only 30 paired samples and no knowledge of internals, achieving 92–96% matching accuracy, showing the vulnerability is not an artifact of a strong attacker model.

- **Comprehensive empirical evidence across multiple attack vectors.** The paper evaluates linkage, regeneration, soft-biometric leakage, and constrained-attack scenarios across three datasets (CASIA-WebFace hold-out, TPDNE, LFW) and two independent commercial verification APIs (Face++, Amazon). The minimal-resource experiments (256–800 images, Section 5.1) demonstrate the leakage is intrinsic and not data-hungry.

- **Extension beyond the core PPFR family broadens relevance.** Transfer experiments to adversarial de-identification (TIP-IM, Section 8) and soft-biometric inference (race, gender, age, Section 9) show the vulnerability pattern extends beyond frequency-domain PPFR, suggesting a structural limitation in current privacy-preserving approaches that retain recognition utility.

### Minor

- **No baseline for false identity match rates in the regeneration experiment.** The paper reports regeneration success using Face++ thresholds (Success@5, Pass@1e-5) but does not measure how often Arc2Face-generated faces from *random or unrelated* embeddings accidentally pass Face++ verification against a given original image. While the stringent FAR thresholds (1e-5) and the independent linkage attack results (Table 4, Section 5.2) provide strong orthogonal evidence that the recovered embeddings are genuinely identity-discriminative, a false-match baseline would strengthen the direct demonstration of regeneration leakage and eliminate any concern about Face++ or Arc2Face biases inflating success rates.

- **Regeneration evaluation compares against the enrollment image, potentially conflating identity-level and image-level recovery.** The regeneration attack (Section 5.3) evaluates by comparing the generated face against the *same* original image that produced the protected template. The linkage attack already demonstrates cross-image identity matching (Table 4), and Section 7 shows the extracted embedding has higher cosine similarity to its source image than to other images of the same person — which the paper honestly reports rather than hides. However, the per-image regeneration success numbers in Table 2 may overstate how recognizable the generated face is to *other* photos of the same person. A cross-image regeneration benchmark would make the identity-level claim airtight, though the current evidence is already strong.

### Trivial

- **Table 6 layout and the definition of FracFace's protection metric could be clearer.** The parsed version of the paper makes Table 6 difficult to parse, and the text explanation of the "Protection (%)" metric and its relationship to FaceLinkGen success rates could benefit from more explicit exposition for readers unfamiliar with FracFace's internal evaluation.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A false-match baseline for regeneration (generate faces from random ArcFace embeddings and measure Face++ pass rates) would eliminate any residual concern about verification API bias.
- Cross-image regeneration evaluation (measuring whether the generated face matches a *different* photo of the same person) would further isolate identity-level from image-level leakage.
- Extending the attack to target a different teacher embedding model (e.g., FaceNet) would demonstrate that the leakage is not specific to ArcFace's representational properties, though the paper already argues this model-agnosticism theoretically.
- A concrete proposal for an identity-centric PPFR benchmark (dataset, protocols, metrics) would convert the paper's motivating critique into actionable infrastructure for the community.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the review process highlights an interesting meta-observation: the paper's most compelling evidence is not its strongest-looking numbers (the >96% regeneration success) but rather the *contrast* in Table 6 between FracFace's own protection metric and FaceLinkGen's identity recovery rate. This contrast exposes a structural problem in how the PPFR community evaluates privacy — not merely that one metric is imperfect, but that the current evaluation paradigm is measuring the wrong thing entirely. The fact that channel disruption ratios can reach 100% while identity recovery remains near-total suggests the frequency-domain operations these methods perform are essentially orthogonal to identity suppression. This is a deeper and more damaging finding than a simple "attack works" paper would deliver.

## Suggestions

- Add a regeneration false-match baseline (random embeddings → Arc2Face → Face++) to Table 2 or as an appendix figure. This is a straightforward experiment that would preempt any concern about verification API bias.
- Clarify Table 6 by explicitly defining each column and the relationship between FracFace's protection metric and FaceLinkGen's success rates, ensuring readers unfamiliar with FracFace can follow the argument without cross-referencing.
- Consider adding a cross-image regeneration result on a subset (e.g., 200 identities with multiple images each) to strengthen the identity-level claim for the regeneration attack specifically.
