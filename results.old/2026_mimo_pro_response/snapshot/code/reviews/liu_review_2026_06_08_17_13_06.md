## Summary
Hi-Light is a training-free, plug-and-play video relighting framework that decouples relighting from detail preservation. It introduces three components: a lightness-prior-anchored progressive fusion for diffusion guidance, a Hybrid Motion-Adaptive Lighting Smoothing Filter (HMA-LSF) for temporal flicker suppression, and a LAB-based Detail Fusion (LAB-DF) module that transfers only low-frequency illumination from the relit video onto the original high-resolution input while preserving high-frequency structure. The paper also proposes a Light Stability Score (S_LS) as the first quantitative metric for temporal lighting consistency.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated pipeline decomposition with clean ablation**: Table 2 systematically isolates each component's role — HMA-LSF drives temporal stability (S_LS: 0.285 → 0.462), LAB-DF drives structural fidelity (SSIM: 0.623 → 0.943), and the Lighting Prior damps frame-to-frame luminance oscillations. The decomposition into separate flicker-suppression and detail-preservation stages is principled.

- **Practical computational accessibility**: The deliberate choice of Farneback optical flow over RAFT (citing >80GB VRAM for 81 frames, Section 3.2.2) and operating at 480p during diffusion enables single-L40-GPU inference, making the framework genuinely accessible. This is a real engineering contribution.

- **LAB-DF elegantly avoids the afterimage problem**: The paper correctly identifies that naively copying high-frequency detail from input to relit frames causes ghosting due to geometric misalignment (Section 3.2.3, lines 158-168). Inverting the transfer direction — extracting only low-frequency illumination from the relit output — is a clean solution to this specific failure mode.

- **Light Stability Score fills a genuine evaluation gap**: No prior metric quantified temporal light flickering. The decomposition into three interpretable signals (I_t, C_t, İ_t) with a bounded smoothness score is reasonable, and the 30-participant human evaluation (95.6% top rank for light stability) provides validation that S_LS tracks human perception.

- **Frequency-domain analysis provides direct evidence**: Figure 7 (left) showing Hi-Light's Fourier magnitude spectrum nearly identical to the input's, while competing methods show pronounced high-frequency attenuation, is a rare and concrete form of evidence in this domain.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Circular evaluation paradigm: metrics reward not-changing-the-input rather than relighting quality.** The LAB-DF module (Eq. 9-10) preserves the input's high-frequency L channel and transfers only low-frequency illumination from the relit video. SSIM, the paper's headline metric, measures structural similarity *to the input*. A method designed to maximally preserve the input will inherently dominate SSIM over baselines that perform more substantive generative transformation. The 0.943 SSIM is not evidence of superior relighting — it is evidence of minimal structural modification. LPIPS suffers from the same issue (perceptual distance to input). Figure 6 confirms this: the input video itself sits at the top-right corner, and Hi-Light is the nearest method — the paper frames this proximity as superiority, but it equally reads as "least modification." The paper would be substantially strengthened by including an identity baseline (input video passed through unchanged) in Table 1 to calibrate how much metric advantage comes from preserving the input vs. actual relighting.

- **No metric for relighting faithfulness.** The paper evaluates detail preservation (SSIM), distributional realism (FID), generic video quality (VBench), and temporal smoothness (S_LS), but none of these answer the central question: "Given the prompt 'sunset lighting,' does the output actually look like sunset lighting?" The human evaluation (Section 5) assesses light stability and detail quality but not relighting faithfulness either. Without this, the paper cannot fully support its core claim of being a superior *relighting* method as opposed to a superior *detail-preserving tonal adjustment* method. Even a simple CLIP-based text-image alignment score would partially address this.

- **Disconnect between motivation and mechanism.** The introduction explicitly contrasts Hi-Light against CapCut filters, arguing filters "cannot synthesize the complex, physically grounded effects of true illumination" including "directionality, highlights and shadows" (Section 1, lines 21-29). However, the LAB-DF module's mechanism — adding low-frequency brightness via Gaussian blur and transferring chrominance channels — cannot reposition shadows, create new specular highlights, or alter directional illumination characteristics, because these are high-frequency phenomena preserved from the input. The method's actual capability (stable, high-quality tonal and chromatic adjustment guided by a diffusion model) is genuinely useful, but the paper overclaims by framing it as physical relighting. The color transfer via A_r, B_r channels (Eq. 10) does carry over the relit scene's color cast, which is real but partial.

### Minor

- **S_LS inherently favors minimal-change methods.** A method that barely modifies the input inherits the input's temporal stability, trivially achieving high S_LS. The metric also penalizes legitimate dynamic lighting (e.g., flickering torch light — one of the test prompts). While this doesn't invalidate the metric, the paper should discuss this bias and potentially normalize for it.

- **Key hyperparameters unspecified in the main text.** The β parameter controlling transfer strength in Eq. 9, the Gaussian σ for extracting low-frequency illumination in LAB-DF, and the brightness threshold τ in S_LS are not specified. These are critical parameters — varying σ fundamentally changes what "low-frequency illumination" means (very large σ ≈ global color grading; small σ retains structural information). The amplification factors (20, 20, 5) for S_LS sub-scores are described as "empirically yielded" without sensitivity analysis.

- **No discussion of failure cases or limitations.** The paper does not address what happens with extreme lighting changes (bright daylight → dim torch), dynamic lighting prompts, or scenes requiring shadow geometry alteration. Acknowledging these bounds would strengthen the contribution by scoping it honestly.

- **Dataset skew toward portraits (70/30 split).** The 70 portrait / 30 non-human split means evaluation is heavily weighted toward faces, where structural preservation is visually most salient. Generalization to more complex scenes (outdoor environments with complex shadow geometry) is less demonstrated.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Include an identity baseline (unmodified input video) in all quantitative tables to transparently calibrate metric advantages.
- Add a relighting faithfulness metric (e.g., directional CLIP score measuring alignment between output and target lighting prompt).
- Provide sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters (β, σ, τ, amplification factors).
- Show failure cases where the low-frequency-only transfer breaks down (e.g., dramatic shadow redistribution scenarios).
- Discuss the lightness prior's behavior when target lighting legitimately differs from input lightness structure (Section 3.2.1, Eq. 4 anchors to a time-invariant reference that may fight against correct relighting for extreme changes).

- **"The evaluation framework is circular" framed as fatal**: The harsh critic frames this as a structural invalidation. While the circularity concern is real and significant (retained as Major), it does not *invalidate* the paper's contribution. The method does perform relighting — it transfers low-frequency illumination and relit chrominance — and the SSIM/LPIPS metrics, while biased, are not meaningless (they correctly capture that Hi-Light preserves details better). The issue is one of *incomplete* evaluation, not fraudulent evaluation. Demoted from fatal to major.

- **"The method is just a color filter"**: The harsh critic claims LAB-DF is "functionally closer to a color filter operating in LAB space than to physical relighting." While partially valid (retained in the motivation/mechanism disconnect weakness), this overstates the case. The diffusion model (IC-Light) does generate physically-informed relighting in the intermediate stage; LAB-DF then transfers the illumination information from that stage. The method is not simply applying a filter — it uses a diffusion-based relighting backbone and then carefully transfers the result. The A_r, B_r channels carry real color information from the relit video.

- **"Farneback flow accuracy concerns"**: The critic notes Farneback is less accurate than learned methods but the paper already justifies this tradeoff on computational grounds (80GB VRAM for RAFT). No evidence is presented that flow errors actually cause visible artifacts.

- **Missing hyperparameters as reproducibility concern**: Partially retained as minor weakness about unspecified parameters, but the hard rule removes pure reproducibility nitpicks. The concern about σ's effect on the method's behavior is substantive and retained.

## Novel Insights
The paper's key insight — inverting the detail transfer direction (extracting low-frequency illumination from the relit output rather than injecting high-frequency detail into it) to avoid geometric misalignment artifacts — is a genuinely clever design choice that addresses a real failure mode of naive approaches. The meta-observation from this review is that the resulting method occupies an interesting middle ground between "filter" and "relighting": it uses a physically-informed diffusion model to generate illumination guidance but transfers only the aspects that can be stably composed with the input. This trade-off between relighting fidelity and output quality is underexplored in the paper but represents a meaningful design axis for future work.

## Suggestions
- Add a CLIP-based relighting faithfulness metric to demonstrate that Hi-Light achieves comparable lighting accuracy to baselines while excelling in stability and detail.
- Include the identity baseline in Table 1 — this single addition would address the circularity concern by showing how much of the metric gap is due to actual relighting vs. input preservation.
- Reframe the contribution more honestly as high-fidelity tonal and chromatic relighting transfer rather than physical relighting, acknowledging that shadow geometry and specular highlights are not modified.
- Add a brief limitations section discussing the method's scope — particularly the constraint that only low-frequency illumination changes are transferred.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a clean, practical engineering pipeline that solves real problems (flickering, detail loss) in video relighting. The individual components are well-motivated, the ablation is informative, and the human evaluation supports the claims of improved stability and detail. However, the evaluation paradigm has a significant gap: the primary metrics reward preservation of the input rather than relighting quality, and no metric assesses whether the target lighting was achieved. The disconnect between the paper's ambitious framing (physical relighting beyond filters) and its actual mechanism (low-frequency tonal/chromatic transfer) weakens the narrative. These are addressable issues — adding a faithfulness metric and an identity baseline would substantially strengthen the paper — but as submitted, the overclaiming and incomplete evaluation bring it below the acceptance threshold for a top venue.
