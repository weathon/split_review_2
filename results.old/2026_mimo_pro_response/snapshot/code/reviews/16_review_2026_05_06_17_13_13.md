# Review of 16.md

## Summary
The paper argues that health-related LLM responses can be too cautious in ways that are not merely unhelpful but potentially harmful for vulnerable users, especially people with OCD or health anxiety. It supports this position with a conceptual account of over-caution/“over-alignment,” prior literature on cyberchondria and reassurance-seeking, and OCD-Eval, a 225-query benchmark comparing GP-labeled scenario risk against risk levels stated by 22 LLMs.

## Strengths
- **Clear and discussion-worthy position.** The central claim is easy to summarize: “erring on the safe side” in health advice can itself be unsafe for users with health anxiety/OCD. This is explicitly stated in the introduction and position section, e.g. lines 52–60 and 184–189.
- **Distinctive reframing of AI safety.** The paper usefully shifts attention from under-cautious medical advice and over-refusal-as-helpfulness-loss to over-caution as a possible safety harm in its own right. This is a meaningful position-paper contribution for the NeurIPS community.
- **Concrete vulnerable-user focus.** Rather than making a generic claim about all health users, the paper focuses on OCD and health anxiety, connecting LLM health responses to reassurance-seeking, intolerance of uncertainty, symptom checking, and cyberchondria literature.
- **OCD-Eval makes the position more refutable and concrete.** The dataset of 225 OCD-triggered health-anxiety queries, GP risk labels, and evaluation across 22 models provides a useful first measurement apparatus for the phenomenon, even if the construct validity is incomplete.
- **Good comparison against existing health evaluation.** The paper explains why HealthBench’s emergency/non-emergency categories do not fully capture emotionally loaded reassurance-seeking queries, motivating a separate evaluation setting.
- **The paper engages real counterarguments.** Section 6 addresses under-cautiousness, whether OCD users should seek reassurance from LLMs, traditional symptom-checker analogies, and medical-knowledge limitations. This helps the paper invite productive disagreement rather than merely asserting a one-sided thesis.
- **The disclosure experiment is a useful design insight.** Section 4.4 tests whether giving models the user’s OCD/health-anxiety context solves the problem, and finds inconsistent effects, including degraded emergency recognition for some models and persistent over-caution for others.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The main OCR metric only partially measures the paper’s own definition of over-cautiousness.** The paper defines over-cautiousness as both objective risk overstatement and fearful/alarming tone, but OCR primarily counts whether the model’s final self-assigned risk category exceeds the GP scenario label. This means a one-level shift from “negligible” to “low” is treated as over-cautious even if the response is otherwise calm, while an anxiety-provoking response with a matching risk label may not be captured. This matters because the empirical claim that there is widespread over-cautiousness rests heavily on the reported 43.9% OCR.
- **The OCD-specific clinical appropriateness construct is under-supported.** The paper explicitly states that GPs were not used to judge whether LLM responses were suitable for OCD users because their OCD-specific and non-OCD labels were nearly identical, suggesting they “may not fully recognize the nuances specific to OCD patients” (lines 267–272). That is a significant limitation for a paper whose central concern is OCD-specific harm. GP risk labels are useful for medical baseline risk, but they do not establish whether a whole response is clinically appropriate or harmful for reassurance-seeking/OCD users.
- **The recommended response strategy is not yet sufficiently grounded.** Section 6 argues that once a reassurance-seeking query has occurred, a calibrated low-risk answer is less harmful than an over-cautious one. This is plausible and the authors acknowledge the ERP objection, but the design recommendation still needs a clearer clinical account of alternatives: calibrated reassurance, uncertainty-tolerance coaching, limits on repeated reassurance, symptom-threshold guidance, and referral to OCD resources may have different effects. Since the paper is partly about what LLMs should do, this unresolved clinical tradeoff weakens the actionability of the position.
- **The “over-alignment” causal framing is asserted more strongly than demonstrated.** The paper attributes over-cautious health responses to broad alignment with universalized safety values and to legal/public-relations pressure, using analogies such as wet-floor signs and Proposition 65 warnings. These are plausible explanations, but the paper does not distinguish alignment effects from other causes such as medical uncertainty, missing patient context, training-data norms, triage conventions, or benchmark incentives. The core position can survive this, but the “over-alignment” diagnosis is currently less well supported than the observed risk-label inflation.

### Minor
- **The emergency-safety tradeoff evidence supports “not inevitably in tension,” but not the stronger claim that the goals are simply “not in tension.”** The paper compares emergency recognition on HealthBench with over-caution on OCD-Eval and observes that some models achieve both high emergency recognition and lower OCR. This is useful evidence against a strict zero-sum tradeoff, but it does not show that interventions designed to reduce over-caution would preserve emergency recognition, nor does it rule out confounding by model capability, model family, or prompting.
- **The dataset construction limits the breadth of claims.** The 225 queries are sourced from two authors with past/current OCD experience and then filtered/validated. This is a reasonable starting point for a position paper, but it may not capture the diversity of OCD presentations, health-anxiety styles, cultural contexts, severity levels, or comorbidities. Claims should be framed around the evaluated query distribution rather than all OCD or all health use.
- **The anxiety-index validation is preliminary.** Section 4.3 uses one mental-health professional rating 42 sampled questions to calibrate an LLM judge. This is a useful early signal, but it cannot by itself validate anxiety induction across models, query types, or vulnerable users.
- **The paper sometimes moves too quickly from measured risk-label inflation to actual user harm.** The prior literature and examples make harm plausible, and a position paper need not empirically prove clinical outcomes. However, the paper’s strongest formulations—e.g. that the evaluation “demonstrates” widespread harmful over-cautiousness and “poses real harm”—would be more convincing if separated into: measured over-caution, plausible mechanisms of harm, and still-needed clinical validation.
- **The proposed standards rely heavily on medical risk baselines while the paper’s own argument requires mental-health communication expertise.** Section 5 calls for healthcare professionals to provide ground-truth risk assessments and third-party escalation criteria. That is useful, but OCD/health-anxiety response calibration also needs clinicians familiar with OCD, ERP, reassurance-seeking, and anxiety-sensitive communication.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A small expert panel including OCD clinicians, clinical psychologists, or ERP specialists judging full responses for OCD-specific appropriateness would substantially strengthen the paper.
- A clearer taxonomy separating medical risk calibration, triage/escalation, tone, reassurance dynamics, and mental-health support would make the evaluation and recommendations cleaner.
- A few concrete examples of “ideal” responses for different query types would help operationalize the design recommendation.
- A controlled prompt or policy intervention study could test whether lowering OCR preserves emergency recognition, but this is not required for a position paper.

## Removed Points
These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution.

- **Removed: “The paper should directly prove that LLM responses worsen anxiety or cause physical harm in OCD users.”** This would evaluate the paper as a clinical outcomes study rather than a position paper. The absence of direct user-outcome evidence is a limitation to acknowledge, but not a fatal or standalone major flaw because the paper also uses conceptual reasoning, prior literature, examples, and preliminary evaluation.
- **Removed/softened: Generic “overclaiming” criticism.** Position papers are allowed to use forceful claims. I retained only the specific concern that the paper sometimes conflates measured risk-label inflation with demonstrated clinical harm.
- **Removed: Complaints that tag frequencies are in the appendix.** Appendix placement is not a substantive weakness, and parser extraction may omit relevant material.
- **Removed: Missing related work concerns.** I do not have a basis to verify absent references, and the paper already engages several relevant literatures.
- **Removed/softened: “No novel experiments/baselines/ablations.”** The paper is a position paper and should not be judged as a standard benchmark paper. Its empirical component should be assessed for whether it supports the argument, not for standard experimental completeness.

## Novel Insights
The strongest novel observation is that “safe” medical caution is not uniformly safe across user populations: for reassurance-seeking users, a marginally elevated risk label, symptom-monitoring caveat, or “see a doctor if worried” hedge may function differently than it does for a typical user. The paper’s most valuable contribution is therefore not the exact OCR number, but the argument that health-response safety should be evaluated interactionally: medical risk calibration, emotional tone, and user vulnerability jointly determine whether a response is safe.

## Suggestions
- Redefine OCR as one component of over-cautiousness, not the full construct; explicitly distinguish risk-label inflation from anxiety-inducing communication.
- Add OCD/ERP-informed expert judgments of complete responses, even on a small subset, to validate whether OCR and LLM-judge tags correspond to clinically concerning response styles.
- Reframe the strongest causal statements: “may plausibly harm,” “risks harming,” or “can reinforce harmful patterns” is better supported than “is hurting” or “demonstrates real harm.”
- Clarify the target response policy: e.g. calibrated medical risk statement + avoidance of unnecessary symptom checking + explicit uncertainty-tolerance language + suggestion of OCD/health-anxiety support when repeated reassurance-seeking is detected.
- Treat the emergency-recognition result as evidence that the tradeoff is not inevitable, rather than proof that reducing over-caution and preserving emergency safety are generally not in tension.
- Narrow claims from “LLMs are too cautious about health” to the evaluated setting: low-risk, emotionally loaded health-anxiety/OCD-related queries.
