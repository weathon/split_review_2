## Summary

This paper presents FaceLinkGen, an identity-centric attack against frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems. Rather than attempting pixel-level reconstruction, the attack uses a lightweight distillation pipeline to align protected templates with a public face embedding space (ArcFace/Antelopev2), then performs identity linkage and face regeneration via Arc2Face. The core argument — that pixel-level metrics (PSNR, SSIM) are insufficient for measuring identity privacy — is convincingly supported by experiments showing >90% regeneration success at Face++'s strictest threshold and >98.5% matching accuracy across PartialFace, MinusFace, and FracFace. The attack is remarkably simple (training in under 2 hours, ~$0.80–1.60 per method, and as little as 50 seconds with 800 images), which the paper correctly argues demonstrates the vulnerability lies in the representation itself rather than in sophisticated attack engineering.

## Strengths

1. **Fundamental reframing of PPFR evaluation.** Table 1 provides concrete, hard-to-refute evidence that pixel similarity and identity similarity are orthogonal: a CanFG-generated face achieves SSIM 0.841 but identity similarity 0.008, while two different photos of the same person have SSIM 0.235 but identity similarity 0.586. This directly challenges a decade of PPFR evaluation practice.

2. **Strikingly high attack success across all evaluated systems.** Table 2 shows regeneration pass rates at the strictest Face++ threshold (1e-5) of 0.993 (PartialFace), 0.936 (MinusFace), and 0.904 (FracFace) on TPDNE. Table 6 drives the point home by contrasting FracFace's claimed protection rates of 0.68–1.00 (under their channel-disruption metric) against 0.002–0.015 actual protection under identity-centric evaluation — a devastating comparison.

3. **Attack simplicity as a feature, not a bug.** Section 5.1 shows distillation completes in under 50 seconds using 800 images, achieving 97.0% regeneration success. With 256 images, linkage accuracy remains 98.7%. The paper intentionally keeps the pipeline standard to argue that the vulnerability is structural, not an artifact of clever attack design.

4. **Constrained-attacker experiment broadens impact.** Section 6 shows that even with only 30 paired samples and a generic Gaussian-blur high-pass filter proxy (no knowledge of PPFR internals), >92% matching accuracy and >94% regeneration Success@5 are achieved. This elegantly reveals a shared structural weakness in frequency-domain designs.

5. **Cross-verification and generalization.** Results are validated against both Face++ and Amazon face comparison API (Table 5), plus cosine similarity analysis (Section 7). Extension to TIP-IM (adversarial de-identification) and CanFG (Table 8) shows the vulnerability is not frequency-domain-specific.

6. **Soft biometric leakage with policy relevance.** Table 9 shows race, gender, and age can be inferred from FracFace templates at near-original accuracy, directly contradicting the original authors' claims of successful obfuscation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The paper's core claims are well-supported and its limitations are addressable.

### Minor

1. **Missing baseline: regeneration from original embeddings.** The attack's regeneration success is evaluated against Face++ verification, but the paper does not report the success rate of generating faces from *original* (unprotected) ArcFace embeddings through the same Arc2Face + Face++ pipeline. Without this baseline, it is difficult to attribute how much of the reported Pass@1e-5 rates reflect student accuracy vs. the inherent tolerance of Arc2Face and Face++. If the original-embedding baseline yields 99% Pass@1e-5, the student's ~90–99% numbers are essentially at ceiling. If it yields substantially higher numbers, the student degrades quality more than the paper suggests. This experiment is easy to run and would meaningfully contextualize the results.

2. **Reliance on a specific public embedding space.** The attack uses ArcFace/Antelopev2 as the teacher model, and the paper argues that any PPFR system preserving recognition utility must be alignable with *some* publicly accessible embedding space. This is a reasonable theoretical argument but is not experimentally validated — for instance, using FaceNet or a different embedding backbone as the teacher. While the paper notes that "other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used," no such experiment is run. Showing the attack works with an alternative teacher would strengthen the generality claim.

3. **Amazon API discrepancy under-analyzed.** In the constrained-attack experiment (Section 6, Table 7), the Amazon API pass rates (44–57%) are roughly half the Face++ rates (94–96%), yet the paper only briefly notes that "the Amazon API is likely more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images." This large gap deserves deeper analysis: does it reflect threshold differences, a systematic inability of Amazon's API to verify synthetic faces, or a genuine attenuation of identity signal under the high-pass proxy? The current explanation is insufficient and somewhat weakens the constrained-attack results.

4. **Failure case analysis absent.** The paper does not show or analyze cases where regeneration fails. Understanding what causes failures (extreme pose, low-quality templates, etc.) would help the community understand the attack's limitations and inform future defenses. Even 2–3 illustrative failure examples per PPFR method would be valuable.

5. **Hyperparameter disclosure is sparse.** The paper reports the dataset size (~90K images, ~10K identities) and training duration, but does not specify batch size, learning rate, optimizer, convergence criteria, or architecture details of the student model beyond "Antelopev2 with one additional 3x3 Conv2D layer." While the attack's simplicity makes exact reproduction less critical, full disclosure would aid reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Running prior reconstruction attacks on the same data for direct comparison.** The paper cites prior attack failures qualitatively (Figure 3), and Table 6 contrasts FracFace's reported metrics with FaceLinkGen's. A direct head-to-head quantitative comparison on identical data (e.g., applying FracFace's own reported U-Net attack and FaceLinkGen on the same templates) would make the "pixel metrics overestimate privacy" thesis even stronger, though the current evidence is already sufficient.

- **t-SNE visualization of template vs. original embeddings.** A visual embedding-space comparison would help readers intuitively grasp the alignment achieved by distillation.

- **Histogram of regeneration similarity scores.** Instead of only pass rates at discrete thresholds, showing the full distribution of Face++ or cosine similarity scores would reveal confidence margins.

- **Testing with a non-ArcFace teacher (FaceNet) or a self-supervised setup.** Would further strengthen the claim that vulnerability is representation-level.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation emerging from the reviews — beyond the paper's own contributions — is the constrained-attack result's implication that all three frequency-domain PPFR methods share a common structural vulnerability: their output templates remain strongly coupled with simple high-pass filtering operations. This suggests the fundamental reason frequency-domain PPFR fails is not implementation-specific but arises from the design choice of retaining high-frequency identity information while discarding low-frequency visual content. This insight, if confirmed by further analysis (e.g., frequency spectrum comparison between templates and high-pass images), could inform a principled redesign of PPFR: perhaps future systems should explore low-frequency-preserving schemes or learnable frequency masking that disrupts the simple high-pass proxy exploited here.

## Suggestions

1. **Add the regeneration-from-original-embeddings baseline** (Section 5.3). This single experiment would significantly strengthen the paper by disentangling student accuracy from generator/verifier effects. Report Pass@1e-5 and Success@5 for original ArcFace embeddings → Arc2Face → Face++, and compare directly with the student-derived numbers in Table 2.

2. **Run the attack with a different teacher embedding model** (e.g., FaceNet + a compatible generator). This would experimentally validate the paper's claim that "other embedding models like FaceNet can also be used" and strengthen the generality argument. Even a small-scale demonstration on one PPFR method would suffice.

3. **Provide a deeper analysis of the Amazon API discrepancy.** Examine whether the 44–57% pass rate reflects (a) a higher internal threshold at Amazon, (b) sensitivity to AI-generated imagery, or (c) genuine signal loss in the constrained high-pass proxy setting. This is important because the constrained-attack is the paper's most practical scenario.

4. **Include a failure case analysis.** Show 2–3 failure examples per PPFR method with the cosine similarity between extracted and original embeddings for those cases, to help the community understand the attack's limitations.
