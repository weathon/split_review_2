## Summary
This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT) using disentangled content and style representations, trained on both paired and unpaired data. It also introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The framework formulates EPR as a Seq2Seq task without requiring note-level alignment, enabling scalable training.

## Strengths
- **Multi-faceted disentanglement validation with converging evidence**: The paper provides three complementary lines of evidence for content-style disentanglement: (1) quantitative performer/composer identification (Table 4: style→composer accuracy 77.46% vs. content→composer 29.99%, with the gap confirming that style captures performance-related information better), (2) 2D visualizations showing clear composer/performer clusters (Figures 3–4), and (3) subjective style transfer tests demonstrating successful transfer without quality loss (Figure 5). No single test is conclusive, but the combination is compelling.

- **Alignment-free EPR achieves competitive performance against alignment-dependent baselines**: Table 3 shows Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) compared to alignment-dependent VirtuosoNet (91.88%, 4.23%) and DExter (91.27%, 5.11%). Table 2 shows Ours (Target) achieves best MAE(D)=0.37 and best KL(V)=1.76. This validates the third contribution that note-level alignment is not necessary for competitive EPR.

- **Practical PSR module with empirical support**: The diffusion-based PSR generates style embeddings from score content alone. Figure 4 shows PSR-generated styles closely mirror the era-based clustering of styles extracted from actual performances, and PSR outputs receive competitive subjective ratings (Figure 2). This enables automated style-aware rendering without performer/composer labels.

- **Comprehensive experimental evaluation across multiple dimensions**: The paper evaluates across APT metrics (Table 1), EPR objective metrics (Tables 2–3), subjective listening tests (Figure 2), disentanglement analysis (Table 4, Figures 3–5), and PSR effectiveness (Figure 4). This breadth of evaluation significantly exceeds what most comparable papers provide.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Confounded ablation prevents isolating the benefit of joint modeling**: The EPR-Only baseline is "trained solely on the ASAP dataset" (line 209), while the full model additionally uses 75,913 unpaired score files and YouTube-derived performances. Comparing EPR-Only vs. Ours (Target) in Table 2 changes two variables simultaneously: joint APT modeling and ~76K additional training sequences. The paper acknowledges this ("joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance," line 235–236) but presents the comparison as validating the joint framework. Without an ablation that trains EPR-Only on the full unpaired dataset (or vice versa), the relative contribution of joint modeling versus additional data cannot be determined. This matters because the first stated contribution is "leveraging the duality between the two tasks for mutual supervision" (line 29).

- **Subjective evaluation lacks inter-rater reliability and is small**: The listening test uses 11 participants "trained in music performance" (lines 149, 200–201), but no inter-rater agreement metric is reported (verified: no mention of Fleiss' kappa, Krippendorff's alpha, or standard deviation anywhere in the paper). With only 5 pieces per composer rated by 11 participants, the subjective evaluation—which is positioned as key evidence for EPR quality claims—has limited statistical robustness.

### Minor
- **Pitch edit distance regression insufficiently analyzed**: In Table 1, Ours achieves Ep=3.08, significantly worse than the end-to-end baseline (2.73, p<0.01, marked with ‡). The paper frames the overall APT result as "comparable to the state-of-the-art" (line 207) and briefly attributes the gap to the alignment-free formulation, but pitch accuracy is arguably the most fundamental APT metric. A more prominent analysis of its practical implications would strengthen the paper.

- **Disentanglement mechanism is lightweight**: Disentanglement relies on architectural separation (note-level content vs. CLS-token global style) plus KL regularization on the style posterior (Eqs. 5–6). There is no adversarial loss or information bottleneck explicitly enforcing style-invariant content representations, which is standard in stronger disentanglement frameworks. The empirical results are reasonable, but the theoretical guarantees are limited.

- **Weight sharing between content encoders unclear**: The paper states content encoder and score encoder "share the same architecture" (line 127) but does not clarify whether they share weights, which matters for reproducibility.

- **Visualizations use bottleneck classifier projection**: Figures 3–4 use a 2D bottleneck layer before a classification head for projection. Clustering quality therefore reflects the classifier's inductive bias rather than raw embedding structure. Comparing with unsupervised projections (e.g., t-SNE on raw embeddings) would distinguish embedding quality from classifier structure.

### Trivial
- Ours (PSR) achieves better alignment rate and lower insertion rate than Ours (Target) in Table 3, which is counterintuitive since target styles are ground-truth—worth a brief discussion.

- Analysis of noise robustness from the external transcription model used to generate YouTube performance MIDI.

## Novel Insights
The paper's key insight is that EPR and APT are naturally dual tasks sharing a disentangled latent space of score content and performance style, enabling mutual supervision without note-level alignment. The demonstration that a Seq2Seq formulation of EPR can match alignment-dependent methods while being more scalable is a genuine contribution. The PSR module—generating stylistically appropriate performance styles from score content alone—is a practical and novel component that mimics a pianist's interpretive ability, supported by era-based clustering analysis showing that PSR outputs mirror the temporal progression of actual performance styles.

## Suggestions
1. **Add one ablation**: Train EPR-Only on the full unpaired dataset (without joint APT) to isolate the contribution of joint modeling vs. additional data. This single experiment would either validate or qualify the central claim.
2. **Report inter-rater reliability**: Add Fleiss' kappa or Krippendorff's alpha and confidence intervals for the subjective evaluation.
3. **Discuss the Ep regression more prominently**: Analyze the practical implications of the significant pitch edit distance degradation (3.08 vs. 2.73, p<0.01) for downstream applications.
4. **Clarify weight sharing**: State whether content encoders share weights. Compare 2D bottleneck projections with unsupervised projections of raw embeddings.
