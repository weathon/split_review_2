## Summary

This paper proposes Ego-Foresight (EF), a self-supervised method that disentangles agent from environment by predicting future visual frames from proprioceptive commands, without requiring any segmentation mask supervision. The learned representation is fed into a model-free RL algorithm (TD3/DDPG) to improve sample efficiency. The method is evaluated on DMC, Distracting DMC, and Meta-world, showing competitive performance against supervised (SEAR) and unsupervised (DrQ-v2, Dreamer-v3, TD-MPC2) baselines.

## Strengths

1. **Self-supervised agent-environment separation without mask supervision** — The method achieves agent-environment separation using only motion and prediction (Section 3.2), without the segmentation masks required by prior work like SEAR (Gmelin et al., 2023). The qualitative results in Figure 7 demonstrate this clearly: the Door Open task shows the arm predicted while the static door is not, whereas in the Hammer task, the grasped hammer is correctly predicted as part of the agent. The Distracting DMC results (Figure 5) further confirm robustness to changing visual backgrounds, which would be very difficult without some form of agent-environment separation.

2. **Performance advantage scales with agent complexity** — On the Quadruped task (16 degrees of freedom), EF achieves its best relative performance among all baselines. The paper explicitly notes (Section 4.2): "the greater the complexity of the agent in terms of degrees of freedom, the more advantage our approach offers." This provides strong evidence that self-prediction of the agent's own body is particularly beneficial for high-DOF embodiments, a setting where prior methods struggle.

3. **Emergent tool integration into body schema** — The qualitative results on the Hammer task (Figure 7) show that once the hammer is grasped, the model predicts its motion as part of the agent. This body-schema adaptation — integrating grasped objects into the agent model — emerges purely from the self-supervised prediction objective, without any explicit mechanism for tool detection. This capability is absent from supervised methods like SEAR, which rely on a fixed mask.

4. **Systematic ablation of key components** — The ablations on prediction horizon \(H\) (Figure 8) and similarity loss weight \(\alpha\) (Figure 9) provide empirical guidance for these design choices. The \(\alpha\) ablation is particularly informative, showing that the similarity loss matters more when the scene undergoes large variations (Door Open) than when visual changes are mostly due to agent motion (Box Close, Hammer).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The SEAR baseline comparison is compromised, weakening the primary comparison to the supervised oracle.** The paper states (Section 4.1): "For SEAR, despite using the publicly available code, we failed to match the results reported in the original paper. We note however, some inconsistencies in the original plots such as success rates above 1." Since SEAR is the most relevant baseline — the supervised method that demonstrates the cost of supervision — the inability to reproduce its published results means the SEAR curves shown may be weaker than they should be. The paper's central claim of "removing the need for supervision while preserving sample-efficiency" depends critically on comparing well against this oracle. Without a correct SEAR reproduction (or a compelling explanation of why the shown SEAR results are still fair), it is unclear whether EF closes the gap to supervision or merely outperforms a degraded baseline. The authors' transparency about the issue is appreciated, but the problem remains.

2. **Disentanglement is claimed as the core mechanism but never directly probed.** The paper's motivation and name ("Ego-Foresight") revolve around learning to separate agent and environment. However, the only quantitative evaluation is downstream RL task performance. Performance gains could arise from the auxiliary prediction loss acting as a general regularizer, without achieving actual agent-environment separation. The similarity loss ablation (\(\alpha=0\)) provides indirect support but does not isolate disentanglement. Direct tests are needed — for example: training a classifier on ground-truth agent masks from scene features to measure leakage, showing invariance of the scene encoder to agent pose, or demonstrating that the proprioception encoder captures only body state. Without such evidence, the claimed mechanism remains a hypothesis, not a demonstrated finding.

### Minor

1. **Limited statistical evidence for claimed improvements.** The paper acknowledges "greater variability in our results is due to a lower number of repetitions with different random seeds" and that "not all baselines and experiments use the same number of random seeds." On Meta-world (Figure 6), several tasks show overlapping confidence intervals between EF and baselines (e.g., Door Open, Drawer Open, Reach). While the overall trends are informative, the number of seeds limits the reliability of finer-grained claims about which method is "best" on individual tasks. The paper's broader message (EF is competitive and often matches or improves) is supported, but the statistical base is thin.

2. **No quantitative assessment of prediction quality.** The qualitative predictions in Figure 7 are compelling, but the paper does not report any quantitative measure of prediction fidelity (e.g., MSE, FVD, or perceptual similarity) against ground-truth frames. This makes it difficult to assess whether the prediction task is being solved adequately, whether prediction quality varies across tasks, or whether improvements in prediction correlate with downstream RL gains.

3. **Ablation scope is limited to two tasks each.** The ablations on prediction horizon \(H\) (Figure 8) and similarity loss weight \(\alpha\) (Figure 9) are informative but restricted to two Meta-world tasks each. The effect of \(\alpha\) in particular may vary substantially across environments with different amounts of environmental change.

## Nice-to-Haves

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface a useful tension that the paper itself does not fully address: the qualitative evidence for disentanglement (Figure 7 showing the model correctly ignoring static objects while predicting the agent and grasped tools) is actually quite strong, but the paper pairs this only with downstream RL metrics rather than with direct representation analysis. A genuinely novel insight is that the strength of evidence for disentanglement is asymmetric across tasks — the Door Open case (where the object is static and NOT predicted) is more informative than the Hammer case (where the tool is predicted because it moves with the arm), yet the paper does not distinguish the evidential value of these two scenarios. A useful future direction would be to design tasks where the agent-environment separation can be ground-truthed (e.g., known object positions vs. known joint configurations) to directly measure representation quality.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix or replace the SEAR baseline.** Either (a) work with the original authors to resolve the reproduction issue, (b) use the original published SEAR numbers as a reference line even if reproduced locally, or (c) replace SEAR with another supervised disentanglement method that can be reliably reproduced. A frank discussion of what differs between the reported and published SEAR results is essential.

2. **Add direct disentanglement tests.** Train a linear probe on scene-encoder features to predict agent mask pixels; compute mutual information between scene features and proprioceptive state; or show that the scene representation is invariant to agent pose changes. Even a simple quantitative comparison of predicted vs. actual agent masks on held-out data would greatly strengthen the core claim.

3. **Report prediction quality metrics** (MSE, LPIPS, or FVD) on held-out trajectories, ideally separated into agent vs. environment regions, to quantify whether the model is indeed learning the visuomotor mapping.

4. **Run at least 5 seeds** for the main experiments and report per-task performance with effect sizes or rank-based summaries (e.g., mean rank across tasks) to provide more robust statistical evidence.
