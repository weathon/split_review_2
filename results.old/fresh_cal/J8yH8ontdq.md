## Summary
Audio-Agent proposes a framework that uses GPT-4 as a planning module to decompose complex text prompts into atomic sub-tasks for a pre-trained TTA diffusion model (Auffusion), and fine-tunes a lightweight LLM (Gemma2-2B-it) to produce HuBERT semantic tokens from video for VTA generation. The core idea—leveraging an LLM to decompose long multi-event descriptions into time-stamped generation steps—is timely and practically motivated.

## Strengths

- **LLM-based decomposition for complex TTA is novel and effective.** The paper demonstrates that GPT-4 can break long, multi-event text descriptions into atomic generation steps with start/end times. Table 1 shows Audio-Agent uniquely supports long complex generation among all compared methods, and Table 2 reports meaningful CLAP improvements on complex captions (24.06% vs. 14.40% for Auffusion and 23.98% for AudioGen). Figure 2 provides visual evidence that only Audio-Agent's outputs preserve distinct event structure (e.g., river stream followed by typing) while baselines mix sounds.

- **VTA temporal alignment without a separate timestamp detector.** The paper's VTA design—fine-tuning Gemma2-2B-it with LoRA to predict HuBERT semantic tokens, then conditioning the diffusion model via cross-attention—achieves competitive synchronization without the explicit timestamp detector required by methods like FoleyCrafter. Table 3 reports Onset ACC 29.01 and Onset AP 69.38, marginally exceeding FoleyCrafter (28.48, 68.14) on onset metrics.

- **Unified TTA and VTA in a single lightweight pipeline.** The same pre-trained Auffusion backbone serves both tasks. The VTA side adds only a 2B LLM fine-tuned with LoRA, avoiding training from scratch. The ablation (Tables 5, 6) shows cross-attention converges within 20,000 steps, and meaningful synchronization is achievable even with low-rank (R=16) fine-tuning.

- **Transparent ablation on LoRA rank.** The paper provides a full ablation study (Tables 4–5 in the ablation section) across rank values 16, 32, 64, allowing readers to assess the trade-off between quality and synchronization themselves.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The "on-par" VTA quality claim is misleading.** The paper's abstract states Audio-Agent achieves "on-par results compared to the state-of-the-art task-specific models." However, Table 2 shows large gaps: FoleyCrafter achieves MKL 1.497 vs. 2.623, CLIP 11.94 vs. 8.55, and FID 36.80 vs. 52.93. These are not "on-par" differences—they represent substantial quality degradation. The method is competitive on synchronization (Table 3), but the overall claim conflates the two evaluation axes. The paper later acknowledges that baselines trained on larger datasets, but the initial framing remains overstated.

- **TTA evaluation is too small to be conclusive.** Following prior work, only 20 captions are evaluated per condition (single caption, two captions, complex captions). No confidence intervals, error bars, or statistical significance tests are reported. For the "complex captions" condition, the test prompts are generated by GPT-4—the same model family used as the planner. While all baselines receive the same prompts and the comparison is not circular, the small sample size makes it impossible to assess whether the reported improvements are reliable. A 20-sample evaluation on a single metric (CLAP score) is insufficient for the paper's central claim about handling complex text.

- **Inconsistency between metric names (FID vs. FAD).** The evaluation metrics section (Section 4.2) states the paper uses "Frechet audio distance (FAD)" for VTA quality, but Table 2 and the ablation tables label the same column "FID" and cite Heusel et al. 2017 (the original image FID paper). The paper does not clarify whether FID is computed on mel-spectrograms with a pre-trained classifier, or whether FAD is the intended metric. This inconsistency undermines the credibility of the experimental section and makes the results difficult to interpret.

### Minor

- **Missing architectural detail on temporal resolution alignment.** The LLM produces HuBERT semantic tokens at 50 Hz (500 tokens for 10 seconds of audio). The diffusion model operates on latent representations with a different temporal resolution (approximately 86 time steps for 10 seconds at the VAE hop length used by Auffusion). The paper does not explain how the two temporal axes are aligned—whether interpolation, positional encoding, or length-matching is applied—before cross-attention. Without this information, the architecture cannot be reproduced or fully understood.

- **LoRA rank selection is not well justified.** The ablation (Table 5, sync metrics) shows LoRA rank=16 yields the best Onset ACC (29.74) and Onset AP (70.63), yet the main results use rank=64. The paper states "for a fair comparison, we use the rank value of 60 across all metrics" (presumably a typo for 64), without explaining why quality metrics are prioritized over synchronization—especially since the paper's VTA claim centers on synchronization.

- **Loss curve does not correlate with performance.** The paper notes that "the loss curve is not a reliable indicator of the model's performance" and "the model can achieve a good performance even when the loss curve remains flat." This is unexplained: if loss does not correlate with performance, what early stopping criterion was used? This raises questions about training procedure reliability.

### Trivial
- The paper writes "rank value of 60" in the ablation discussion (line 253) but the table shows ranks 16, 32, 64. This appears to be a typo.
- The temporal connector is described only as "a 1D convolution block and a projection layer" without kernel size, stride, or layer count.

## Nice-to-Haves
- **Controlled TTA ablation:** Feed the same GPT-4 decomposition directly to Auffusion (generate each sub-clip and concatenate) and compare against Audio-Agent's full pipeline. This would isolate whether the benefit comes from decomposition or from waveform-level fusion.
- **Human evaluation for TTA:** A listening test with paired comparisons would directly demonstrate perceptual advantages claimed for complex captions.
- **Larger-scale TTA evaluation:** Use 100+ prompts from held-out human-written scripts rather than GPT-generated complex captions.
- **VTA dataset expansion:** Training on a larger dataset (e.g., VGGSound) would enable a fairer comparison with FoleyCrafter and clarify whether the quality gap is primarily due to data size.
- **Statistical significance reporting:** Confidence intervals or error bars would strengthen confidence in the reported improvements.

## Suggestions

1. **Revise the VTA "on-par" claim** to precisely distinguish between axes: acknowledge the quality gap while separately claiming synchronization competitiveness. This reframing would be more honest and less likely to attract criticism.
2. **Fix the FID/FAD inconsistency** by clarifying which metric is used, how it is computed (e.g., on mel-spectrograms with a pre-trained audio classifier vs. the image Inception network), and citing the appropriate reference.
3. **Expand the TTA evaluation** to at least 100 prompts per condition with confidence intervals or bootstrapped error bars. Use human-written multi-event descriptions (or draw from movie scripts) for the complex condition.
4. **Explain the temporal resolution alignment** between 50 Hz HuBERT tokens and the diffusion latent timeline, even in a brief note.
5. **Justify the rank=64 choice** for main results more explicitly, or present the main results with rank=16 which gives better sync and note rank=64 provides a quality-sync trade-off.
6. **Clarify the early stopping criterion** given the admitted disconnect between loss and performance.
