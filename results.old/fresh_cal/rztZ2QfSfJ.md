## Summary

This paper presents NeMal, a system that leverages controllable text-to-image synthesis (fine-tuned Stable Diffusion 1.5) with LLM-based prompt generation and preference-based image selection to produce MarineSynth—the largest marine synthetic dataset to date, with over 4 million image-text pairs. The authors evaluate synthetic data utility across classification (23-category Sea-animal benchmark), coral reef segmentation, and vision-language understanding (MiniGPT4, LLaVa), showing that synthetic data combined with few-shot real labels (5 per class) nearly matches a fully-supervised Oracle (57.25% vs. 57.83%).

## Strengths

- **MarineSynth is the largest marine synthetic dataset constructed to date.** The paper explicitly reports 4M+ image-text pairs (Section 4.1), combining 2M Alt-texts rewritten by Mistral 8×7B and 2.3M ChatGPT-generated prompts, yielding a scale that previous marine simulators (Potokar et al., 2022) cannot match. The authors commit to releasing both the prompts and synthesized images.

- **Synthetic data with few-shot real labels nearly matches fully-supervised Oracle performance.** Under the "5-shot + ChatGPT + Alt-texts" setting in Table 2, classification accuracy reaches 57.25% versus the Oracle's 57.83% (which used 9,400 real images). This provides concrete evidence that the pipeline can dramatically reduce human data collection and labeling effort.

- **Task-agnostic effectiveness validated across three diverse tasks.** The paper evaluates on classification (Table 2), dense coral reef segmentation (Table 4), and VLM fine-tuning (Table 5), showing consistent improvements. This multi-task validation goes beyond single-task synthetic data papers and supports the claim that the pipeline generalizes.

- **Systematic analysis of three text-prompt sources.** Table 2 and Section 4.2 carefully compare BLIP2 captions, rewritten Alt-texts, and ChatGPT-generated prompts, quantifying trade-offs between in-distribution alignment and out-of-distribution generalization. This provides actionable guidance for future synthetic data construction.

- **Explicit upper-bound characterization.** Figure 5 shows that classification accuracy converges with more synthetic images, and Section 4.2 honestly attributes this to the T2I model's generation fidelity and prompt noise. The paper also discusses a preference-based image selector (trained on 100K human preference pairs) that modestly improves quality, with diminishing returns as the number of trials increases.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None.*

### Major

- **The "never-ending learning" framing is aspirational, not experimentally validated.** The paper's title, abstract, and Figure 2 frame NeMal as a system that "continuously generates synthetic data and iteratively improves models." However, every experiment is a static one-pass procedure: generate a fixed synthetic dataset, train once, measure performance. There is no demonstration of a closed loop—(1) train on synthetic data, (2) evaluate on real data to identify failure modes, (3) generate targeted prompts addressing those failures, (4) retrain and verify improvement. Section 3.2 describes this as a *theoretical capability* ("Theoretically, we could achieve never-ending marine learning…"), but the experiments never instantiate it. The system as shown is a large-scale synthetic dataset generation pipeline with good prompt engineering, which is a solid contribution, but the "never-ending" claim exceeds what the experiments support. The paper should either demonstrate at least one closed-loop iteration or reframe the contribution as a static synthetic data pipeline.

- **The segmentation experiment's claim of "even without real coral reef images" is qualified by indirect real-data leakage through CoralSCOP.** In Section 4.3, pseudo labels for synthetic coral reef images are generated by CoralSCOP (Zheng et al., 2024), which—based on its name and description—is a foundation model *trained on real coral reef images with ground-truth masks*. The paper states "even without real coral reef images, we could still boost coral reef segmentation performance" and "no involvement of real coral reef images or coral mask labeling during the training procedure." While the *training pipeline* indeed uses no real images, the pseudo-label generator itself embeds knowledge of real coral boundaries learned from real data. This does not invalidate the experiment, but the claim should be reframed or the authors should compare against a pseudo-label generator that has no marine-specific pre-training (e.g., vanilla SAM's automatic mask generator with filtering). As written, the claim is imprecise.

### Minor

- **No error bars or variance estimates are reported.** The pipeline involves stochastic components (diffusion sampling with random seeds, human preference data for Selector training, LLM prompt generation). Reporting single-run numbers without confidence intervals or standard deviations makes it impossible to assess whether reported differences (e.g., the 0.4% improvement for LLaVa1.5 in Table 5) are meaningful. This is standard practice for system papers in this community and should be addressed.

- **Fine-tuning details for the marine T2I model are missing.** Section 3.1 states the authors "construct our internal marine text-image data based on our marine conception list for fine-tuning" Stable Diffusion 1.5, but does not specify the size of this fine-tuning dataset, its source, the number of training steps, learning rate, or batch size. This hinders reproducibility and makes it unclear whether gains come from prompt engineering or the fine-tuning itself.

- **Minor framing inconsistency: "ignorable human efforts" vs. the 12 marine biologist volunteers.** The contribution list (Section 1) claims "ignorable human efforts on data collection and labeling," yet the preference-based image picking pipeline (Section 3.1.3) requires 12 marine biologists to annotate 100K image pairs for training the binary Selector. While this is a one-time cost and subsequent Selector runs are automatic, the abstract and bullet points should acknowledge this upfront investment rather than claiming "ignorable human efforts" without qualification.

### Trivial
*None.*

## Nice-to-Haves

- The paper could strengthen the segmentation experiment by also evaluating with a pseudo-label generator that has *no* marine-specific pre-training (e.g., vanilla SAM automatic masks with filtering), to ablate how much of the gain comes from CoralSCOP's real-data knowledge versus the generation pipeline itself.
- The 500 VLM QA pairs (Section 4.4) are self-constructed; releasing them and describing their validation process would increase confidence in the VLM results.

## Removed Points
These points were flagged by reviewers but are removed with justification:

- *"Classification evaluation does not control for training set size"* — The Oracle uses 9,400 real images; the pure synthetic setting uses 11,500 images (500 prompts × 23 categories). If anything, the synthetic data has a *quantity advantage*, so the gap (53.66 vs. 57.83) understates real data's quality edge. The 5-shot + synthetic comparison (57.25 vs. 57.83) is precisely the point: showing that synthetic data can compensate for scarce real data. This is not a flaw.
- *"CLG testing set is unusual for marine visual understanding"* — Testing on watercolor, cartoon, and sketch renditions is a standard OOD robustness evaluation protocol in computer vision. It measures generalization to distribution shift, which is a valid and informative axis.
- *"The 'first attempt' claim is overstated"* — This is a general related-work criticism. The paper discusses DreamDA and existing marine simulators and positions itself clearly. Missing-related-work complaints cannot be verified without external sources.
- *"Reproducibility concern about releasing models/dataset"* — The paper explicitly states all text prompts and synthesized images will be released. This is standard for a submission under review.
- *"MAE should be defined"* — Mean Absolute Error is a standard metric requiring no definition in context.
- *"CoralSCOP usage: should state whether fine-tuned or zero-shot"* — The paper is under review; the cited CoralSCOP paper provides these details. The paper need not re-describe the internals of every tool it uses.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The novel insight is the systematic comparison of three text-prompt sources (BLIP2 captions, rewritten Alt-texts, ChatGPT prompts) for the specific domain of marine synthetic data, revealing that each source has distinct trade-offs between distribution alignment and generalization, and that combining heterogeneous sources yields the best overall results. This is a practical finding for anyone building domain-specific synthetic datasets via T2I models.

## Suggestions

1. **Most important:** Either demonstrate one closed-loop iteration of never-ending learning (train → evaluate → generate targeted prompts → retrain → show improvement) or drop/qualify the "never-ending" framing and rename the system to something like "Large-Scale Synthetic Marine Data Generation Pipeline." The rest of the paper is strong enough on its own merits without this framing.
2. Add error bars (standard deviations over multiple runs with different random seeds) to all quantitative results. For the classification experiments, at minimum report variance over 3 runs.
3. Clarify the pseudo-label issue in Section 4.3: explicitly state whether CoralSCOP was fine-tuned on real coral data or used zero-shot, and reframe the claim from "without real coral reef images" to "without real coral reef images *in the training pipeline*."
4. Provide the fine-tuning hyperparameters for the marine SD1.5 model (training data size, source, learning rate, steps, batch size) in an appendix or supplemental.
5. Acknowledge the 12 marine biologist effort for Selector training explicitly in the main text, and clarify that "ignorable human efforts" refers to the *per-task* downstream data labeling cost, not the one-time system construction cost.

## Score and Decision

This paper presents a valuable resource (MarineSynth) and a well-engineered pipeline for generating domain-specific synthetic training data. The experiments are informative and cover three diverse tasks. The main weaknesses—aspirational framing of "never-ending learning," minor pseudo-label leakage in the segmentation experiment, and missing error bars—are all addressable in revision. The core contributions are sound and the results support the dataset utility claims.
