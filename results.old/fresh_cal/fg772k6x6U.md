## Summary

This paper proposes CariNet, a framework for both detecting deepfakes and generating "Deepfake Caricatures" — amplified versions of artifacts designed to make fakes more detectable by humans. The model uses human-annotated artifact maps to guide a self-attention classifier and to drive a caricature generation module that magnifies motion differences between frames. The paper reports competitive detection performance across multiple benchmarks and a user study showing large improvements in human detection accuracy with caricatures.

## Strengths

- **Human-guided attention demonstrably improves machine detection.** The ablation study (Table 3) provides clear, controlled evidence: removing modulation from the Artifact Attention module drops overall AUC from 86.04% to 83.72%, and replacing human-informed maps with a fixed Gaussian causes a further drop to 77.91%. This isolates the contribution of human supervision beyond standard self-attention.

- **Strong generalization to unseen datasets.** CariNet34 achieves the highest overall AUC (87.4%) across four unseen benchmarks (Celeb-DF v2, DFDCp, FaceShifter, DeeperForensics — Table 1) and top or near-top scores under four types of perturbation (Table 4), outperforming prior methods including LipForensics on overall AUC.

- **Comprehensive three-way ablation design.** The ablation in Table 3 (no attention, no human modulation, fixed Gaussian) cleanly disentangles the contributions of self-attention and human-informed modulation, giving readers a precise understanding of what each component contributes.

- **The Artifact Attention module reproduces human annotations well.** The module achieves 0.745 Pearson correlation with human maps versus 0.423 for a 2D Gaussian baseline, confirming it learns meaningful artifact locations rather than a trivial spatial prior.

- **User study includes engagement analysis.** The finding that the caricature advantage persists across participant engagement levels (Fig. 3, bottom) strengthens practical relevance — the benefit is not limited to fully attentive users.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Ambiguity in the user study design creates a potential confound.** The paper states that it "compared the detectability of standard (i.e. unmodified) deepfakes with deepfakes caricatures" using 400 videos (200 real, 200 fake) from DFDCp. It does **not** specify whether real videos were shown with any comparable transformation. If caricatures were applied only to fake videos, participants could learn the heuristic "distorted → fake" and respond accordingly, without actually attending to the *specific artifacts* the caricature was designed to amplify. The reported metric is the detection rate of fake videos (hit rate); false alarm rates on real videos are not reported, making it impossible to distinguish a genuine improvement in sensitivity from a simple criterion shift. Since the human experiment is positioned as a primary contribution (contributions list: "increase human deepfake detection accuracy by up to 40%"), this ambiguity is consequential. *Mitigation: clarify whether real videos received any transformation; if caricatures were applied only to fakes, add a control condition or report false alarm rates / d' to rule out the distortion heuristic.*

### Minor

- **The "5% accuracy boost" claim conflates self-attention with human supervision.** The claim at line 29 states that leveraging human artifact information "boosts its accuracy by more than 5%." The ablation table shows the full CariNet18 (86.04%) vs. CariNet18 *without any attention mechanism* (80.78%) yields a ~5.3% absolute improvement. However, the improvement specifically attributable to human-informed modulation — i.e., CariNet18 full vs. CariNet18 with self-attention but without human modulation — is ~2.3% (86.04% vs. 83.72%). The larger 5% figure includes the benefit of self-attention blocks generally. The claim should be stated more precisely.

- **Cross-dataset behavior of the Artifact Attention module is underspecified.** Human annotations were collected only on DFDCp (1000 videos). The classifier is trained on FF++ and evaluated on Celeb-DF v2, FaceShifter, DeeperForensics, etc. The paper does not clarify whether the Artifact Attention module is frozen when applied to these unseen datasets (in which case its heatmap quality on those domains is unexamined) or retrained (which would require new human annotations not described). The training description on line 115-118 says the classification pipeline is trained on each dataset separately, but the Artifact Attention module's loss (requiring human maps) is part of that pipeline. This ambiguity affects how interpretable the detection results are and whether the module generalizes across datasets.

- **Exact baseline detection rates in the user study are not reported.** The paper states standard deepfakes were "detected no better than chance" but does not report the actual detection rates per presentation duration. Since participants' baseline performance varies with presentation time, the absolute improvement (14–43 percentage points) would be more interpretable with per-duration baseline values.

### Trivial

- The Artifact Attention module is labeled "semi-supervised" in the abstract, but it is trained with direct supervision from human heatmaps. The overall framework uses two forms of supervision (classification labels + human maps), which could be called semi-supervised, but the module itself is fully supervised; the terminology is imprecise.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting observation from the reviews is that the two reviewer perspectives converge on a core tension: the user study's dramatic headline results (14–43 percentage point gains) and the detection ablation results are evaluated with different standards of evidence. The detection side is thoroughly controlled (three ablation conditions, multiple datasets, perturbation robustness), while the human experiment — which is the paper's primary novel contribution — has a design ambiguity that the detection experiments do not. This asymmetry weakens the paper's central claim even though the individual components are well-executed. A secondary insight is that the paper's detection contribution is carefully ablated but numerically modest (~2.3% AUC gain from human modulation), which is at odds with the "5%" framing; the paper's main distinctiveness lies in the caricature concept, not in detection performance alone.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the user study design immediately.** State explicitly whether real videos received any transformation. If they did not, report false alarm rates alongside hit rates, or compute d' (sensitivity index) to disentangle sensitivity from response bias. Alternatively, add a control condition where real videos receive a comparable non-informative distortion.
2. **Rephrase the "5% accuracy boost" claim** to distinguish the benefit of the full attention framework from the benefit specifically attributable to human-informed modulation. The latter (~2.3% AUC) is the more precise claim and is still a meaningful contribution.
3. **Clarify the training pipeline** for the Artifact Attention module: when the classifier is trained on FF++ (without DFDCp human annotations), is the AA module frozen or fine-tuned? If frozen, provide evidence that its heatmaps are meaningful on non-DFDCp inputs.
4. **Report exact baseline detection rates per duration** in the user study results (not just "no better than chance").

## Score and Decision

The paper tackles an important problem with a creative approach, and the machine detection results are well-supported by ablation and generalization experiments. However, the primary novel contribution — the human experiment showing caricature effectiveness — has an unresolved design ambiguity that could affect interpretation of the headline result. Combined with the imprecise "5%" claim and underspecified cross-dataset behavior of the Artifact Attention module, these issues warrant a major revision.
