## Summary

The paper introduces **3D Intention Grounding (3D-IG)**, a new task that detects objects in 3D point cloud scenes from free-form human intention text (e.g., "I need a place to sit down and relax") without requiring explicit referential object descriptions — shifting the reasoning burden from the human to the AI. The authors construct the **Intent3D dataset** (44,990 intention texts over 209 fine-grained classes from 1,042 ScanNet scenes) using ChatGPT with careful prompt engineering and manual cleaning, establish baselines spanning expert models (BUTD-DETR, EDA), a foundation model (3D-VisTA), and an LLM-based model (Chat-3D-v2), and propose **IntentNet** which incorporates verb-object alignment, candidate box matching, and cascaded adaptive learning. IntentNet outperforms the next-best baseline by +11.06% Top1-Acc@0.25 and +5.6% AP@0.5 on the test set.

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-motivated task formulation.** The paper clearly distinguishes 3D-IG from 3D visual grounding (Fig. 1): 3D-VG requires a human to observe, reason, and produce a referential description; 3D-IG tasks the AI with performing that reasoning itself from raw intention language. This reframing opens a meaningful new direction for language-grounded 3D perception.

- **Large-scale dataset with careful construction.** Intent3D provides 44,990 intention texts across 209 fine-grained classes from 1,042 ScanNet scenes. The construction pipeline (object selection with three criteria → ChatGPT generation with constraints to avoid referential leakage → manual cleaning) is clearly documented. The dataset exhibits genuine linguistic diversity — 1,568 distinct verbs and 2,894 nouns — going well beyond simple rephrasings of object labels (Fig. 3, Section 3.3).

- **Comprehensive baselines across model families.** The paper evaluates four baseline methods covering expert models (BUTD-DETR, EDA), a foundation model (3D-VisTA), and an LLM-based model (Chat-3D-v2), under training-from-scratch, fine-tuning, and zero-shot protocols (Tables 1–2). This provides a thorough picture of where existing language-based 3D detectors fall short on intention reasoning.

- **Strong quantitative gains with component-level validation.** IntentNet outperforms all baselines by large margins (+11% Top1-Acc@0.25, +5.6% AP@0.5 on the test set). The ablation study (Table 4) systematically removes each proposed component and confirms that every one contributes positively — the verb alignment component alone accounts for a 5.25-point drop when removed — establishing that the gains are not from a single monolithic change.

- **Detailed dataset analysis and informative figures.** The paper provides text length distributions, verb/noun word clouds, and per-category verb/noun counts (Fig. 3), along with clear qualitative visualizations of ablation effects (Fig. 6) that make the method's behavior concrete.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Dataset ground-truth validity is unverified against real human intentions.** The intention texts are generated by ChatGPT (GPT-4) with prompt engineering to simulate human daily needs. Manual cleaning filters out gibberish and duplicates, but there is **no human validation study** (e.g., asking humans to write intentions for the same objects and comparing distributions, or checking whether ChatGPT's generated intentions correspond to plausible real-world human goals in those scenes). This leaves open the possibility that the benchmark measures how well models reproduce ChatGPT's linguistic patterns rather than how well they reason about genuine human intention. The contribution (task framing, dataset pipeline, and method) remains useful, but the criterion-referenced interpretation of the results is weakened. The authors should provide, at minimum, a human evaluation comparing ChatGPT-generated and human-written intention distributions on a sample of the data.

2. **Cascaded Adaptive Learning lacks a principled explanation.** The scheme multiplies lower-priority losses by `Loss_A.sigmoid() + 0.5`, which up-weights lower-priority objectives when the higher-priority loss is *large* (i.e., when the model is worse at the prerequisite task). This is the opposite of what a curriculum or typical balancing scheme would do (normally, a large loss on a prerequisite would down-weight downstream tasks until the prerequisite is learned). The ablation shows it helps empirically (+3.90 points on Top1-Acc@0.5), but the paper provides **no analysis** — no loss trajectory plots, no gradient scale visualizations, no convergence diagnostics — to justify why this counter-intuitive design works. The mechanism remains a heuristic that happens to work on this setup.

### Minor

1. **Val/test split from ScanNet's validation split needs clarification.** The paper states that the train set comes from ScanNet's training split, while val and test both come from ScanNet's 312-scene validation split and "each [has] disjoint scenes" (line 130). However, the exact method of partitioning these 312 scenes into val and test (e.g., random 50/50? scene-based? stratified?) is not specified. Since test performance is reported as a central result, the split procedure should be transparently documented.

2. **No statistical significance reporting.** All main results (Tables 1–3) are reported as single runs without error bars or significance tests. Given typical variance in 3D detection training, it is unclear whether the reported margins (e.g., the ~0.5–2 point differences in ablation Table 4) are stable. The paper should report mean and std over at least 3 runs for the top configurations.

3. **Contrastive learning hyperparameters omitted.** Equation (2) presents a contrastive loss but the paper explicitly omits temperature and normalization (line 220), deferring to future code release. Temperature critically affects the behavior of contrastive losses; omitting it makes exact replication impossible without the code.

4. **Ablation improvements for non-verb components are modest.** Removing Verb2Obj drops Top1-Acc@0.25 by only 0.47 points, and removing Adapt drops it by 0.95 points (Table 4, rows (b) and (d) vs. (e)). While these components are individually beneficial, the dominant gain clearly comes from the verb alignment component (5.25 points). The paper could more honestly characterize the secondary components as incremental refinements rather than essential contributions.

5. **No failure case analysis.** The qualitative results (Fig. 6) show cherry-picked successes. The paper would benefit from including failure cases or a per-category breakdown of accuracy to reveal where the method still struggles (e.g., categories with few training samples, multi-instance scenarios with high ambiguity).

### Trivial

- The split sizes (35,850 train / 2,285 val / 6,855 test) in samples do not obviously follow from the scene-level partitioning described; a brief explanation would preempt confusion.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A human evaluation comparing ChatGPT-generated and human-written intention distributions on a sample of objects (as described in Major weakness 1).
- A controlled experiment that takes a baseline architecture (e.g., BUTD-DETR) and incrementally adds IntentNet's components, rather than only removing them from the full model. This would more cleanly isolate the marginal benefit of each component when starting from a standard 3D-VG architecture.
- Analysis of Cascaded Adaptive Learning: loss trajectory plots, gradient scale visualizations, or a comparison against standard weighting schemes (e.g., uncertainty weighting, fixed weighting).
- Soft assignment for candidate box matching (e.g., IoU-weighted) as an alternative to the hard 0.25 threshold, to test robustness.
- Per-category accuracy breakdown and failure case visualization.

## Novel Insights

The reviews do surface one insight that goes beyond the paper's own framing: the cascaded adaptive learning mechanism, as currently specified, is **counter-intuitive relative to its stated motivation**. The paper claims that higher-priority losses should be "addressed before" lower-priority ones, yet the implemented scheme up-weights lower-priority losses precisely when the higher-priority loss is large (i.e., not yet addressed). This tension between the stated rationale and the actual computation is worth noting — the mechanism may work for a different reason than the authors think (e.g., it could act as a form of gradient normalization or implicit balancing, rather than curriculum-style prioritization). A deeper analysis could reveal a more general design principle.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a human validation study** for the dataset: ask 100–200 participants to write intentions for a subset of objects, then compare the verb/noun distributions and semantic similarity between human-written and ChatGPT-generated texts. This would substantially strengthen the benchmark's credibility.
2. **Analyze the Cascaded Adaptive Learning** mechanism: plot the adaptive factors over training, compare against fixed weighting and uncertainty weighting, and provide a clear explanation (or revised rationale) for why the `Loss_A.sigmoid() + 0.5` schedule helps.
3. **Document the val/test split procedure** precisely (exact number of scenes per split, sampling method).
4. **Add error bars** (mean/std over 3 runs) for the main tables.
5. **Include failure cases** or a per-category breakdown in the qualitative analysis.

## Score and Decision

The paper defines a genuinely new task, contributes a large and carefully-constructed dataset, evaluates a comprehensive set of baselines, and proposes a method with well-validated components and large empirical gains. The two main concerns — dataset validity (no human verification of ChatGPT intentions) and the insufficiently analyzed cascaded learning mechanism — are significant but not fatal; they weaken the confidence in interpreting the results without invalidating the core contribution. Overall, the paper makes a solid contribution that will be of interest to the 3D vision and embodied AI communities.
