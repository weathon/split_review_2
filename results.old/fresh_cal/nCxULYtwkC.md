## Summary

The paper proposes a framework for improving hand pose estimation in the wild by combining two techniques: (1) **Stylize** — applying AdaIN-based style transfer from unlabeled in-the-wild images (e.g., ImageNet) to early feature maps of training images, and (2) **Align** — a continuous consistency regularization (CCR) loss that enforces fine-grained metric relationships among 3D pose predictions. Evaluated on 3D hand pose estimation (on an MSCOCO test set with NeuralAnnot-generated ground truth) and transfer learning for 2D hand pose estimation, the method demonstrates improved generalization compared to baselines trained on lab datasets, including outperforming a model trained on the much larger InterHand2.6M dataset while using substantially less labeled data.

## Strengths

1. **Empirically grounded motivation with clear evidence.** Figure 1 provides a t-SNE visualization of style statistics from ResNet feature maps, showing a clear separation between lab/synthetic datasets and in-the-wild images. This directly motivates the paper's core thesis that the style domain gap is a key obstacle for real-world hand pose estimation.

2. **Ablation validates both components independently and jointly.** Table 3 (Section 4.5, as described) shows that stylization alone substantially reduces error, and CCR adds further improvement, with the combination outperforming either component individually. This provides clean evidence that both *Stylize* and *Align* contribute positively and complement each other.

3. **Transfer learning evaluation shows representation quality beyond 3D pose.** Table 2 shows that frozen representations learned with the proposed method (even from the smallest dataset) achieve substantially higher PCK@0.1 on MSCOCO validation compared to models pretrained on InterHand2.6M or ImageNet, supporting the claim that the framework improves generalization beyond the specific 3D pose estimation task.

4. **Robustness to the choice of unlabeled dataset.** Table 4 shows that different unlabeled sources (ImageNet, Places, Flickr) all improve performance relative to no stylization, demonstrating that the method does not depend on a specific external dataset and that the benefits of style transfer from unlabeled images are broadly applicable.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Test set validity concerns for the core evaluation.** The entire in-the-wild evaluation rests on an MSCOCO-derived test set whose 3D ground truth is generated by NeuralAnnot (Moon et al., 2022b) — an automated neural network annotator, not human-verified ground truth. The paper acknowledges this (line 137: "Although these MANO GTs were generated for training in Moon (2023), we utilized this dataset exclusively as a test set") but provides no analysis of the annotation accuracy or potential systematic bias. Since NeuralAnnot's own biases could produce phantom "improvements" that align with the method's predictions, and the reported gains are modest (e.g., the paper's best PA-MPJPE of 29.28mm vs. baseline 31.61mm), this undermines confidence in the central claim of improved in-the-wild generalization. The paper would benefit from validation on a real-captured in-the-wild test set or at least a careful analysis of NeuralAnnot's accuracy on the specific test samples used. This is the most significant weakness.

2. **Missing comparisons to related style augmentation baselines.** The paper cites MixStyle (Zhou et al., 2021) and UniStyle (Lee et al., 2022) in the related work (Section 2) as relevant style-based regularization techniques but never compares against them experimentally. Since the core technical contribution involves a specific application of AdaIN-based stylization on early feature maps using unlabeled external images, it is essential to show that this particular design outperforms or complements simpler alternatives like MixStyle (which operates on feature statistics within-batch). Without these comparisons, it is unclear whether the observed improvements stem from style augmentation broadly or from the paper's specific design choices (e.g., using unlabeled images as style references).

### Minor

3. **Results reported without variance or statistical significance.** All results in Tables 1–4 are reported as single numbers without standard deviations or confidence intervals. Given the stochastic nature of the method (random sampling of style images, random triplet sampling for CCR), and the fact that some improvements are small (e.g., 2D transfer learning in Table 2 with PCK@0.05: 0.507→0.517 and EPE: 16.88→16.70 per the reviewer's reading), single-run reporting makes it difficult to assess whether the gains are statistically robust. While single-run evaluation is common practice in some parts of the field, reporting variance would substantively strengthen the paper.

4. **Data efficiency claim could be misinterpreted.** The abstract states that improvements are achieved "using less than 5% of the data size compared to a large-scale dataset, InterHand2.6M." This refers only to labeled data (FreiHAND ~130K images vs. InterHand2.6M ~2.6M images), but the method additionally leverages millions of unlabeled images (ImageNet). The claim is technically correct in context (comparing labeled training set sizes), but the phrasing could mislead readers into thinking the method uses very little data overall. Clarifying this as "less than 5% of the labeled data size" would improve transparency.

5. **Potential inconsistency between Table 1 and Table 4 baselines.** The reviewer notes that the FreiHAND (all data) PA-MPJPE in Table 1 (12.46mm) differs from the "Baseline (without stylization)" in Table 4 (12.60mm), while both should represent SHNet trained on full FreiHAND. The table descriptions suggest these may evaluate on different datasets (Table 1 on MSCOCO, Table 4's "on FreiHAND" possibly referring to a different evaluation set), but this is not explicitly clarified. The authors should explain the discrepancy or clarify the evaluation protocol for each table.

6. **No sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters.** The paper does not analyze sensitivity to the CCR weight λ or to which layers stylization is applied (stated as "1st and 2nd residual blocks" by default, line 81). These design choices affect reproducibility and would benefit from ablation.
