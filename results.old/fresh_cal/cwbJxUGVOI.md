## Summary
The paper proposes OCN, a two-stage pipeline for unsupervised multi-object segmentation. Stage 1 trains an objectness network on ImageNet (using VoteCut-derived pseudo masks) to learn three explicit object-centric representations: a binary object existence score, an object center field (unit direction vectors pointing to object centers), and an object boundary distance field (normalized signed distances). Stage 2 uses these frozen representations in a network-free, heuristic multi-object reasoning algorithm to discover objects on scene images. Experiments on COCO* and 6 other datasets show consistent improvements over prior methods (CutLER, CuVLER, unSAM, etc.), with the largest gains attributed to the boundary distance field.

## Strengths

- **Three clearly defined, mathematically precise object-centric representations.** Section 3.2 defines each representation with explicit equations: object existence score (binary), center field (Eq. 1: unit vectors pointing to object center), and boundary distance field (Eqs. 2-3: normalized signed distances). The gradient property (Eq. 4) linking the boundary field to object size is a nice theoretical touch that grounds the downstream reasoning. The representations are directly motivated by three fundamental questions about objectness and are empirically shown (Table 4) to be more informative than a plain binary mask.

- **Strong and consistent empirical performance across benchmarks.** The paper demonstrates substantial margins over prior state-of-the-art methods. Under Setting #2 (COCO + ImageNet pseudo labels), OCN surpasses CuVLER Setting #2 by a reported 6.1 AP on COCO*. Zero-shot evaluation across 7 diverse datasets (Table 3) shows OCN achieving the highest AP on 6 of 7 datasets, including challenging domains like KITTI autonomous driving and GlaS medical images. These results are supported by re-evaluating all baselines on the same custom COCO* benchmark, ensuring fair intra-paper comparison.

- **Well-designed ablation study isolating each representation's contribution.** Table 4 systematically ablates the three representations. The key finding — removing the boundary distance field drops AP from 45.3 to 15.2, while using only the boundary field achieves 44.0 AP — cleanly demonstrates that the boundary distance field is the primary driver of performance. This is a concrete, falsifiable claim backed by evidence.

- **Generalization to zero-shot settings.** The paper evaluates a single trained detector (trained on COCO + ImageNet pseudo labels) on 7 unseen datasets spanning natural, driving, medical, and large-scale detection scenarios, without any fine-tuning. Consistent improvements in this setting provide credible evidence that the learned representations capture generalizable object priors, not dataset-specific artifacts.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. No weakness found that invalidates the paper's core claims or results.

### Major

- **Dependency on VoteCut pseudo masks is not analyzed.** The objectness network (Stage 1) is trained entirely on single-object masks generated by VoteCut (CuVLER) applied to ImageNet. The paper states it "exactly follow[s] the VoteCut method" but provides no analysis of the quality, failure modes, or bias of these upstream masks. If VoteCut produces coarse or inaccurate masks, the learned representations inherit those errors. The method's contribution is partly a refinement of VoteCut's outputs, and without ablating the pseudo-mask source (e.g., comparing VoteCut vs. MaskCut vs. FreeSOLO vs. ground-truth masks), it is unclear how much of the improvement comes from the learned representations vs. the quality of the specific initialization. The paper's claim of being "unsupervised" is technically true but downstream of VoteCut's own assumptions, which warrants direct analysis.

- **Evaluation on custom COCO* limits external comparability.** All main results are reported on COCO*, a manually augmented version of COCO val with 197 additional object categories labeled by the authors. While the paper re-evaluates all baselines on the same benchmark (so intra-paper comparisons are fair), the results cannot be directly compared with published numbers on standard COCO val. Reporting results on standard COCO val alongside COCO* would give readers a familiar reference point to calibrate the magnitude of improvement.

The direct discovery and detector-training pipelines involve stochastic elements (random crops, training seeds), and the heuristic reasoning module may have non-negligible variance across runs. At minimum, the main result (Table 2, Setting #2) should report variance across multiple runs.

This is common for heuristic components and is noted only as a completeness concern since the paper explicitly acknowledges the module is heuristic.
