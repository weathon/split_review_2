## Summary

The paper addresses the important problem of hyperparameter optimization for unsupervised GNN node embeddings without relying on labels. It proposes a unified framework for developing "internal strategies" based on two steps: building prior beliefs about what constitutes high-quality embeddings, and quantifying the extent to which those beliefs are satisfied. The framework is instantiated from two complementary perspectives: CSOR (spatial, using pairwise Manhattan distances between embedding matrices to measure "spatial distinctness") and SSOR (spectral, using the area of a radar chart formed by normalized singular values to measure "spectral distinctness"). Experiments across 7 GNN models, 4 datasets, and 1280 hyperparameter configurations per setting show strong Spearman correlations with downstream performance (avg 0.906 for CSOR, 0.969 for SSOR) and competitive hyperparameter selection against existing internal strategies.

## Strengths

- **Strong Spearman correlations with downstream performance**: SSOR achieves an average Spearman coefficient of 0.969 and CSOR 0.906 across all experimental settings (Section 6.2, line 145). These high correlations demonstrate that both methods reliably rank hyperparameter configurations without labels, which is the paper's core claim.

- **Competitive hyperparameter selection among internal strategies**: SSOR obtains the best hyperparameter selection in 15 out of 28 node classification experiments (average rank 1.75) and 18 out of 28 link prediction experiments (average rank 1.39), outperforming or matching strong baselines like RankMe and StableRank (Section 6.2, line 149). CSOR also performs competitively (best in 8/28 node classification settings).

- **Unified framework with two complementary perspectives**: The paper formalizes internal strategies as a two-step process (building prior beliefs, quantifying prior beliefs) and instantiates it from both spatial (CSOR) and spectral (SSOR) angles. This provides a conceptual organization for a previously scattered set of methods and demonstrates that the framework can be realized in distinct but mutually-reinforcing ways (Sections 4, 5).

- **Large-scale systematic evaluation**: Experiments span 7 diverse GNN models (GAE, VGAE, ARGA, ARGVA, GraphSAGE, GIN, GAT), 4 benchmark datasets (Cora, Citeseer, Pubmed, DBLP), and 1280 hyperparameter configurations per model-dataset pair, evaluated on both node classification and link prediction (Section 6.1). This is a thorough empirical assessment.

### Minor

1. **SSOR formula has a missing closing term (typo-level error)**. The formula on line 96-97 writes the sum from i=1 to r-1 without wrapping back to vertex 1:
   $$SSOR = 0.5 \left| \sum_{i=1}^{r-1} \tilde{\sigma}_i \tilde{\sigma}_{i+1} \sin(\theta_{i+1} - \theta_i) \right|$$

   The standard polygon area formula requires summing to r (with σ̃_{r+1}=σ̃_1 and θ_{r+1}=θ_1+2π), which adds the closing term σ̃_r σ̃_1 sin(2π/r). As a result, the formula as printed does not compute the full radar-chart area described in the text. However, this is clearly a typographical error — the concept (radar-chart area of normalized singular values) is unambiguous from the surrounding description, the empirical visualizations confirm the intended behavior, and fixing the summation bound trivially corrects it without affecting any experimental conclusion. The harsh critic's characterization of this as "fatal" or "structural" is overblown: the method's geometric interpretation is sound, and the ranking results would be largely unchanged since the missing term's relative contribution is small (σ̃_r is typically near zero after normalization).

2. **CSOR's "consensus baseline" assumption could be better supported.** The paper's prior belief — that well-performing embeddings are more spatially distinct from a consensus of all embeddings — is motivated by visualizations and a small experiment comparing distance-to-worst vs. consensus-based distance (Figure 42). The evidence is reasonable for an empirical heuristic, but the paper would benefit from a more systematic analysis across models and datasets showing that poor-configuration embeddings indeed cluster together while good ones diverge. Without this, the reader cannot rule out cases where the assumption degrades (e.g., if many mediocre embeddings dominate the "consensus"). The current evidence is sufficient to motivate the method but not to fully validate the claimed geometric principle.

3. **Hyperparameter search space is underspecified.** The paper states (line 116) that 1280 configurations are generated by "varying the number of layers, hidden dimensions per layer, and the number of maximal training epochs," but does not provide the specific ranges or discrete values for these hyperparameters, nor whether other hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate, optimizer, dropout) are held constant. These details are needed for reproducibility and for assessing the difficulty of the optimization task.

4. **No statistical significance tests for ranking comparisons.** The paper reports average ranks for CSOR (2.79 node classification, 3.36 link prediction) and SSOR (1.75, 1.39) but does not provide confidence intervals or paired significance tests (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank) across the 28 model-dataset pairs. Without these, it is unclear whether SSOR's apparent superiority over RankMe is statistically meaningful.

5. **No discussion of computational cost.** CSOR requires O(H²) pairwise Manhattan distance computations (H=1280 → ~800K comparisons), and SSOR requires an SVD per configuration. For larger H or larger graphs, these costs may be prohibitive. The paper does not acknowledge this or provide runtime measurements.

Similarly, SSOR assumes equally-spaced angular distribution of singular values — the impact of this assumption is not explored. Brief ablations would strengthen the case that these choices are not arbitrary.
