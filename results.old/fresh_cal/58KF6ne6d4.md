## Summary

This paper proposes KIRL, a Reinforcement Learning-based method that jointly optimizes toolpath smoothing and feedrate planning for CNC machining. It formulates the integrated optimization as an MDP where an RL agent (PPO/SAC) predicts boundary kinematic states for each segment, quintic polynomials generate the segment trajectories (proven optimal for jerk minimization under boundary conditions in Theorem 1), and Brent's method optimizes segment durations. Experiments on four toolpaths compare KIRL against two traditional decoupled methods (ICR, CCR), showing consistent improvements in smoothness metrics (max curvature, RMS acceleration/jerk) with competitive machining times.

## Strengths

- **First RL-based integrated treatment of a practically important problem.** The paper identifies a genuinely underexplored gap—CNC toolpath smoothing and feedrate planning are traditionally decoupled, leading to suboptimal results. Formulating this as an MDP and applying RL is a novel contribution (Section 3.3). This problem framing has clear industrial relevance.

- **Theoretically grounded trajectory parameterization.** Theorem 1 (Section 3.1) proves that the optimal solution to the segment-level jerk-minimization problem under boundary conditions is a quintic polynomial. This provides a principled foundation for the trajectory representation and means that, given boundary conditions and segment duration, the within-segment smoothness is provably optimal.

- **Consistent empirical smoothness improvements.** The results (Table 1, Sections 4.3) show that both KIRL-PPO and KIRL-SAC consistently achieve lower maximum curvature, maximum turning angle, RMS acceleration, and RMS jerk than the ICR and CCR baselines across all four test toolpaths. The trajectory visualizations (Fig. 5) qualitatively support these quantitative findings.

- **Elegant hybrid optimization design.** The method decomposes the problem so the RL agent focuses on predicting boundary states, while segment duration is efficiently optimized via Brent's method (Section 3.3). The use of a local coordinate system (Section 3.3, Fig. 4) for state representation aids generalization across toolpath geometries.

The paper never specifies the signs or values of \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3\). If these weights are negative (which would align the reward with the objective), this must be stated explicitly. ## Novel Insights

The reviews surface an interesting tension that the paper itself does not fully resolve: the RL agent predicts boundary states, but the within-segment smoothness is guaranteed by the quintic polynomial parameterization. This means the RL agent's effective role is to predict boundary states that (a) satisfy constraints, (b) produce low total jerk across segment boundaries (not within segments), and (c) enable short segment durations. This is a nuanced optimization problem — the RL agent is not directly optimizing jerk (that's handled by the polynomial), but rather predicting transitions that make the overall chain of quintic segments efficient and smooth. Clarifying this decomposition more sharply — "the polynomial guarantees segment-level optimality; the RL agent optimizes inter-segment coordination" — would strengthen the paper's framing and help readers assess what the RL component actually contributes.
