## Summary

This paper proposes a *reward-based policy* that conditions only on the history of rewards and actions (ignoring observations), enabling zero-shot transfer between environments that share transition dynamics and reward function but have different observation spaces. The authors train such policies using an LSTM + PPO + expert guidance loss across three environments (Pointmass, Cartpole, Car Racing), achieving 60–95% of the observation-based upper bound. They demonstrate zero-shot transfer under color palette swaps and a 2D→3D (AirSim) transfer (after matching dynamics), and show that a learned 1D reward estimator outperforms a 5D state estimator for image-based transfer.

---

## Strengths

1. **Demonstrates the feasibility of a genuinely novel concept.** The paper is the first to show that a policy operating purely on reward/action history can be trained to perform reasonably across multiple environments (Pointmass 95%, Cartpole 66%, Car Racing 70% of observation-based upper bound). This is non-trivial given the extreme partial observability.

2. **Zero-shot transfer under large observation shifts is empirically verified.** Table 1 shows that after color palette swaps, the reward-based policy achieves nearly identical mean reward to the original environment (e.g., Cartpole: 830.1 vs 830.2) across 50 trials with low variance — without any fine-tuning or target-domain data. This cleanly demonstrates the core benefit of ignoring observations.

3. **Clear articulation of the method's difficulties and limitations.** Section 3.2 provides an honest discussion of the challenges (exploration difficulty, need for dense rewards, inherently sub-optimal behaviors requiring information-gathering actions) and the requirement that dynamics be unchanged. The ablation in Figure 7 further isolates when expert guidance matters most (Car Racing vs. Pointmass/Cartpole), giving practical insight.

4. **The expert guidance ablation is well-targeted.** Figure 7 provides a controlled comparison showing where the distillation loss matters (critical for the harder Car Racing task, modest otherwise), which supports the design rationale in Section 3.3.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **No baseline comparison for the transfer setting.** The paper's central claim — that reward-based policies enable rapid/zero-shot adaptation — is never compared against any alternative approach. How does the reward-based policy compare to an observation-based policy fine-tuned on a single target image, or to a domain-adapted representation, or to a policy distilled into a latent vector of the same dimensionality as the reward? Table 1 shows the reward-based policy transfers, but the reader cannot evaluate whether this is *better* (in sample efficiency, robustness, or final performance) than even simple alternatives. This is the most significant evidential gap.

2. **The reward estimation experiment (Section 4.4) stacks the deck.** Table 2 compares a 1-D reward estimator against a 5-D state estimator. The finding that estimating a scalar is easier than a vector is unsurprising. The paper explicitly acknowledges that "we could perhaps get a similar result with a trained 1D latent space" (line 111) but dismisses this without testing. To support the claim that reward is a *particularly* good signal for transfer, the paper needs a comparison to a low-dimensional task-relevant latent representation of the same dimensionality (1-D). As-is, the experiment simply confirms a well-known fact about regression difficulty.

3. **The AirSim transfer (Section 4.3) requires engineering the target environment to match the source.** The paper states: "we match the transition function of 3D AirSim environment to that of 2D Car Racing environment by reimplementing its dynamics" (line 102). While the paper is transparent about this, the result undermines the "rapid adaptation" framing. The core difficulty of realistic transfer — dynamics mismatch — is deliberately eliminated. Combined with the absence of baselines, this experiment provides weak evidence for practical utility.

### Minor

4. **Novelty is incremental.** The *concept* of a reward-based policy is novel, but the *practical method* used to train it (LSTM over reward/action history + PPO + behavior cloning loss from an observation-based expert) is a standard combination of existing techniques. The expert guidance loss (Section 3.3) is standard policy distillation. The ablation (Figure 7) shows this guidance is crucial for the harder task, which raises the question: how much of the reported performance is attributable to the reward input vs. imitating an oracle? The paper does not isolate this.

5. **Variance is missing from learning curves and ablation.** Figure 5 and Figure 7 show single learning curves (three seeds averaged into one curve, or individual seeds? unclear) without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or error bands. Only Tables 1 and 2 report variance. This makes it difficult to assess whether the reported differences between reward-based and observation-based policies (Figure 5) or with/without guidance (Figure 7) are significant.

6. **No comparison to simpler architectural alternatives.** The paper asserts that "a recurrent network is essential" (line 58) but does not compare to a feedforward network with a fixed-length history buffer — a simpler, cheaper alternative. Given that reward is a 1D signal, a short fixed window might suffice for some tasks, and the computational cost of the LSTM warrants justification.

7. **The feasibility argument in Section 3.2 is hand-wavy.** The "analytic algorithm similar to a triangulation method" analogy for ND Pointmass is vague and does not constitute a rigorous argument for the general feasibility of reward-based policies.

### Trivial
None.

## Novel Insights

The two reviews disagree sharply on the significance of the contribution: the harsh critic sees the transfer as a trivial consequence of the assumptions, while the strength finder treats the feasibility demonstration as the primary contribution. The calibration that resolves this tension lies in recognizing that the paper has two distinct claims that require separate evidence: (1) *it is possible to train a reward-based policy*, which the paper supports with reasonable evidence across three environments; and (2) *reward-based policies are advantageous for transfer*, which the paper does not adequately support due to missing baselines and an unfairly favorable comparison. The paper's most interesting insight — that reward, as a 1D signal inherent to the MDP, may serve as a natural bottleneck for transfer — is compelling as a conceptual direction but is not yet backed by experimental evidence that distinguishes it from any low-dimensional latent representation.

## Suggestions

1. Add a direct comparison in the transfer setting: train an observation-based policy on the source, freeze it, and evaluate on the target with color swap. Show that it fails. This would quantify the *need* for a reward-based approach.
2. For the reward estimation experiment (Section 4.4), include a 1-D latent representation baseline (e.g., a VAE bottleneck or a CURL-style encoder with dimension 1) to test whether reward is genuinely special or simply low-dimensional.
3. Report confidence intervals or per-seed variability for Figures 5 and 7.
4. Consider an experiment where dynamics are slightly perturbed (e.g., friction or mass changes in Cartpole). Even showing graceful degradation would strengthen the paper far more than the AirSim experiment (which required engineering the target dynamics to match).
5. Clarify the method's reliance on the expert policy: compare a version trained with reward-input + PPO only (no distillation) to the full method, and report the performance delta attributable to distillation vs. reward input.
