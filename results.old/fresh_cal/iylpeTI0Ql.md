## Summary

This paper introduces Zero-Shot Noisy TTA (ZS-NTTA), a practical setting where a frozen VLM must handle test-time data streams containing both in-distribution and out-of-distribution (noisy) samples without any source data. Through careful analysis (three pipelines, score distributions, gradient analysis), the authors show that existing TTA methods fail because (1) noisy samples produce much larger gradients than clean ones, causing models to overfit to noise, and (2) the adapting classifier's dual role in both classification and detection becomes conflicted. They propose AdaND, which decouples these tasks by keeping the CLIP backbone frozen, using ZS-CLIP's outputs as pseudo-labels to train a lightweight linear noise detector, and injecting Gaussian noise to handle clean data streams. Extensive experiments across 11 ID datasets and multiple OOD datasets show consistent SOTA results on both ZS-NTTA and ZS-OOD detection, with computational cost nearly matching the frozen baseline.

## Strengths

- **Gradient analysis directly explains why TTA fails under noise**: Section 3.2 (Observation 3.3, Figure 4) empirically demonstrates that even a few misclassified noisy samples produce gradients an order of magnitude larger than clean ones, causing Tent and other TTA methods to overfit to noise. This concrete evidence directly motivates the frozen-classifier design and is one of the paper's most impactful findings.

- **Decoupled classifier-detector architecture is simple, motivated, and effective**: AdaND (Section 4) keeps the CLIP backbone frozen and trains a single linear layer as a noise detector using ZS-CLIP's own zero-shot outputs as pseudo-labels. This design is directly motivated by the failure analysis (Figure 3 shows Tent's MCM scores shift, making detection harder; AdaND avoids this entirely). The approach achieves consistent SOTA across all 11 ID datasets in Tables 2 and 3.

- **Gaussian noise injection elegantly handles unknown data streams**: Section 4.2 introduces injecting synthetic Gaussian noise to prevent the detector from misclassifying clean samples when no real noisy samples are present. The ablation (Table 6) shows that without injection, clean-stream performance drops sharply; with injection, performance is preserved. This removes the need to know the stream composition a priori.

- **Computational efficiency nearly matches the frozen baseline**: Table 4 shows AdaND's runtime and memory are nearly identical to zero-shot CLIP and substantially lower than Tent, SoTTA, and TPT. This is a practical strength for deployment.

- **Incidental benefit for ZS-OOD detection**: Table 5 shows AdaND improves FPR95 by 9.40% over dedicated OOD detection methods (CLIPN, NegLabel) without requiring their external text data or auxiliary training.

### Minor

1. **Clean-stream metric computation is not clarified.** The paper defines Acc_N as detection accuracy on noisy samples (Eq. in Section 2). In a clean data stream with 0% noise ratio, there are zero noisy samples, making Acc_N's denominator zero and the metric undefined. Table 6 reports Acc_H (harmonic mean of Acc_S and Acc_N) for clean data streams without explaining how this is handled. The *method's* validity on clean streams is supported by Acc_S results and the Gaussian injection rationale, but the metric reporting needs clarification. This is a clarity issue, not a fatal flaw — the claim that AdaND works on clean streams is independently backed by the ablation and by the "simulating real-world adaptation" experiments (Table 17, referenced in Section 5.3).

2. **Pseudo-label quality from ZS-CLIP is not analyzed.** AdaND trains its detector on pseudo-labels generated by frozen ZS-CLIP (Section 4.1). The paper acknowledges that ZS-CLIP "can effectively distinguish most ID and noisy samples" but does not quantify how many are wrong, how label noise in the pseudo-labels propagates to the detector's training, or whether the method would be robust to varying ZS-CLIP accuracy. Strong empirical results across many datasets mitigate this concern, but an oracle-pseudo-label comparison or sensitivity analysis would strengthen the methodological justification.

3. **Gradient analysis is demonstrated on one dataset pair.** Figure 4 and the three-stage gradient description (Observation 3.3) are shown only for CIFAR-10 → SVHN. While the phenomenon is well-reasoned and consistent with the broader results (Figure 2 ranks, Tables 1-3), showing gradient dynamics for at least one more diverse pair (e.g., ImageNet → iNaturalist) would confirm this is a general property of noisy samples rather than specific to small-scale datasets or particular OOD types. This is a minor limitation of the analysis.

## Novel Insights

The Harsh Critic and Strength Finder both converge on the same key observation: the paper's gradient analysis (Section 3.2) is its most novel intellectual contribution, not just a motivation for the method. The finding that noisy samples produce disharmoniously large gradients *and* that TTA methods like Tent gradually lose the ability to distinguish clean from noisy (as their MCM score distributions shift) provides a dual explanation — it's not just that noise dominates gradients, but that the detector itself degrades over time. The Strength Finder insight that AdaND's linear detector trained during ZS-NTTA naturally transfers to ZS-OOD detection without any extra supervision is a genuine added benefit not anticipated by the paper's framing.

## Suggestions

1. Clarify how Acc_H is computed for clean data streams (0% noise). Either report only Acc_S for that case, or state that Acc_H is only reported for mixed streams and note why.
2. Add a brief analysis of ZS-CLIP pseudo-label accuracy: compute agreement between ZS-CLIP detection and ground-truth labels over the first few hundred steps for a representative pair, and show whether detector performance correlates with pseudo-label quality.
3. Extend the gradient visualization to at least one more diverse pair (e.g., ImageNet → iNaturalist) to confirm the three-stage pattern generalizes, or add a brief theoretical justification for why high-entropy OOD samples should produce large gradients generally.
