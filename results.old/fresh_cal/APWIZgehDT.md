## Summary

This paper presents a three-stage framework for studying human perceptual variability: (1) generating ambiguous digit stimuli by sampling along ANN decision boundaries using classifier-guided diffusion with uncertainty/controversial guidance, (2) fine-tuning models with individual human behavioral data (varMNIST dataset, 246 participants, 116k+ trials) to align model and human perceptual variability, and (3) using individually fine-tuned models to generate personalized controversial stimuli that can selectively influence different participants' digit recognition. The key results include an entropy correlation improvement from ρ=0.08 to ρ=0.74 (VGG) after fine-tuning and a demonstration that personalized stimuli modestly improve directional guidance.

## Strengths

- **Novel three-stage pipeline combining generation, alignment, and manipulation.** The paper introduces a coherent framework that progresses from counterfactual stimulus generation through model-human alignment to personalized manipulation. The uncertainty guidance and controversial guidance methods (Section 3.1) are well-motivated, and Figure 3b shows that the average sum of success+bias rates approaches 80% across 19,943 stimuli, demonstrating that ANN-boundary sampling genuinely evokes human perceptual variability in a structured way.

- **Large-scale behavioral dataset with quantitative evidence for the alignment claim.** The varMNIST dataset (19,943 valid stimuli, 246 participants, 116,715 trials) is a substantial contribution in its own right. The subject-specific fine-tuning produces clear, measurable gains: Spearman rank correlation between model entropy and human entropy rises from ρ=0.08 to ρ=0.74 for VGG (Figure 4c), GroupNet and IndivNet improve accuracy on varMNIST by ~20% over BaseNet, and IndivNet further outperforms GroupNet by ~5% on individual-level validation sets (Figure 4a). These results convincingly demonstrate that fine-tuning with human behavioral data bridges a significant gap in capturing perceptual variability.

- **Preliminary evidence for personalized perceptual manipulation.** The manipulation experiment (Section 5) provides initial support for the claim that individually fine-tuned models can generate stimuli that modestly influence targeted decision-making outcomes: a ~3% improvement in success rate and ~12% improvement in targeted ratio (p<0.001, Figure 5c). While the experiment has design limitations (see Weaknesses), the existence of any directional effect from personalized vs. generic stimuli is noteworthy.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The manipulation experiment's comparison confounds personalization with stimulus change.** The experiment compares first-round (varMNIST) stimuli — which were generated for generic digit pairs, not for specific participants — against second-round stimuli generated by individually fine-tuned models. The 12% improvement in targeted ratio may partially reflect the trivial fact that stimuli generated for a specific participant pair are better matched to that pair than stimuli drawn from a general pool, rather than demonstrating that the *fine-tuned models per se* cause the improvement. The baseline targeted ratio for varMNIST is near 50% by construction (targets were not personalized), so the improvement does not necessarily isolate the effect of individualization. The paper should include a control condition where the same participants see non-personalized controversial stimuli in the second round, or alternatively, substantially temper the causal language around manipulation. With only 18 participants, these modest absolute improvements (3% in success rate) are also sensitive to individual variation, and no effect sizes or confidence intervals are reported.

### Minor

- **High participant exclusion rates without transparency about sentinel criteria.** The digit judgment experiment excluded 124/400 (31%) participants and the recognition experiment excluded 154/400 (38.5%), both attributed to "Sentinel trials" with no further description of the criteria, number of sentinel trials, or accuracy threshold used. While online experiments commonly apply attention-check-based exclusions, rates this high warrant justification. Without transparency, the reader cannot assess whether the remaining data are representative or whether the exclusion procedure inadvertently selected a non-random subset of participants (e.g., those most likely to agree with the guidance targets). The paper should report the sentinel design and show robustness to alternative exclusion thresholds.

- **Reproducibility gaps in methodological details.** The paper does not specify the diffusion model architecture, training data, or number of sampling steps used for generation (Section 3.1 mentions "a classifier-guided diffusion model" without further detail). The digit judgment surrogate (Section 3.2) — a critical component that determines whether generated images appear as recognizable digits — is described only at a high level; its architecture, training hyperparameters, and accuracy on held-out judgments are not reported. These omissions make the generation pipeline difficult to reproduce or build upon.

- **Statistical tests not fully specified.** The manipulation results report p < 0.001 (Figure 5c) without stating the test statistic, the type of test used (paired t-test? permutation test?), or providing confidence intervals or effect sizes. This makes it hard to assess the reliability and magnitude of the reported improvements.

### Trivial

- **The 2:1:1 mixing ratio for individual-level training (Section 4.1: varMNIST-i : varMNIST : MNIST) is stated without justification.** While the approach is sensible, a brief rationale or ablation would strengthen methodological clarity.
- **Minor formatting artifacts from PDF parsing** (e.g., stray characters, broken inline math) are present in the extracted text but are not author errors.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation of the digit judgment surrogate.** Generating a set of images *without* surrogate guidance and comparing their human success/failure rates would directly quantify the surrogate's contribution to naturalness and guidance effectiveness. This would strengthen the claim that the surrogate improves image quality.
- **Calibration analysis for the entropy correlation.** The Spearman rank correlation (ρ from 0.08 to 0.74) is appropriate for rank-order alignment and does not require calibrated probabilities. However, showing reliability diagrams or expected calibration error would provide additional insight into whether the fine-tuned models' entropy values are quantitatively accurate or merely rank-order consistent.
- **Baseline comparison to simpler ambiguous stimuli.** Comparing against linearly interpolated digits or controlled-noise additions would clarify whether ANN-boundary sampling is genuinely more effective than trivial alternatives. This is outside the paper's stated scope but would strengthen the method's case.

## Suggestions

1. **For the manipulation experiment:** Add a within-subject control condition where participants see non-personalized controversial stimuli (generated from the group-level model, not from their individually fine-tuned model) in the second round. This would isolate the effect of personalization from the effect of stimulus renewal. If such an experiment is impractical, substantially reduce the causal claims and reframe the manipulation section as a proof-of-concept pilot.
2. **Disclose sentinel trial design:** Report the sentinel criterion, number of sentinel trials per session, accuracy threshold, and demonstrate that the main results (e.g., success rates across digit pairs) are robust to more or less liberal exclusion thresholds.
3. **Provide reproducibility details:** Specify the diffusion model architecture, training data, sampling steps, and the surrogate model's architecture, hyperparameters, and held-out accuracy.
4. **Report complete statistics:** Provide the test statistic and effect size (e.g., Cohen's d) for the manipulation experiment's p-values. Show individual-level data points in Figure 5 (e.g., a scatter plot or connected lines per participant).
5. **Release the varMNIST dataset:** The dataset of 19,943 human-annotated ambiguous digit images is a valuable resource for the community and would significantly increase the paper's impact.
