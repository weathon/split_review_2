## Summary

This paper proposes DPMC (Diffusion Posterior MCMC), which replaces the single-step posterior approximation used in DPS (Chung et al., 2023a) with multiple annealed MCMC sampling steps per noise level when solving inverse problems with pretrained diffusion models. The core idea is to define a sequence of intermediate distributions inspired by DPS's approximation and run multiple MCMC steps at each noise level to reduce accumulated error. Experiments on FFHQ and ImageNet across linear (super-resolution, deblurring, inpainting) and nonlinear (phase retrieval) inverse problems show consistent improvements over DPS, with competitive performance against other baselines.

## Strengths

- **Strong empirical gains over DPS with fewer NFEs**: Across nearly all linear inverse problems on both FFHQ and ImageNet, DPMC achieves better LPIPS and FID while using ~700 NFE compared to DPS's 1000 NFE (Tables 1, 2). The improvement is consistent and the efficiency gain is clearly documented.

- **Ablation studies confirm the core mechanism**: Table 4a shows monotonically improving LPIPS (0.325 → 0.308) and FID (33.42 → 28.81) as MCMC steps K increases from 1 to 8 on Gaussian deblur, directly validating that multi-step MCMC exploration reduces accumulated error. Tables 4b and 4c further ablate the number of intermediate distributions and the weighting schedule, providing empirical justification for key design choices.

- **Demonstrated effectiveness on nonlinear inverse problems**: On phase retrieval (a nonlinear problem), DPMC achieves LPIPS 0.461 vs DPS 0.489 with comparable FID (Table 3), showing the method is not limited to linear settings.

- **Well-motivated and clearly positioned**: The paper explicitly identifies a concrete limitation of DPS (inaccurate posterior approximation at high noise levels, citing Theorem 1 of Chung et al., 2023a) and proposes annealed MCMC as a principled remedy connecting to a known body of sampling methods.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Missing NFE-matched comparison with DPS**. The paper compares DPMC at ~700 NFE against DPS at 1000 NFE and reports better results. While this is an apples-to-oranges comparison that still favors DPMC, a controlled experiment matching NFE (e.g., running DPS at 700 steps) would isolate the benefit of the MCMC mechanism from the effect of different step counts. Without this control, the headline claim "outperforms DPS with less number of evaluations" is presented as a finding when a proper ablation of the factor being varied (number of evaluations) against the baseline is absent.

- **Per-task hyperparameters (η, ξ) not reported**. The paper states that η and ξ are "task-related constants" but never lists their values for any of the five inverse problems tested. Given that Section 5 acknowledges manual tuning as a limitation, listing the chosen values is essential for reproducibility and for assessing the ease of tuning. This goes beyond a minor omission — without these values, another researcher cannot replicate the reported results.

### Minor

- **The "middle 60%" heuristic is not ablated**. The paper applies the proposal-exploration step only during the middle 60% of diffusion steps (using original DPS for the first 30% and last 10%). This is a nontrivial design choice that could significantly affect results, yet no sensitivity analysis is provided. A brief ablation showing the impact of different proportions (e.g., 40%, 60%, 80%) would strengthen the paper.

- **No wall-clock time comparison**. While NFE is a reasonable proxy for computational cost given that each MCMC step requires a score-network evaluation similar to a diffusion step, a brief comparison of actual runtime versus DPS would be useful for practitioners, especially given the ~700 vs ~1000 NFE difference.
