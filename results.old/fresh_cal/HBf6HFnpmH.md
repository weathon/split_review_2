## Summary
This paper introduces CausalMan Small and Medium, two realistic large-scale causal benchmarks from the manufacturing domain, generated by a physics-based simulator validated by domain experts. The authors evaluate seven causal inference models and seven causal discovery algorithms across ATE, CATE, and discovery tasks, revealing that most current methods fail at even simple interventional queries at scale, that deep models stagnate with more data while accurately modeling treated but not control distributions, and that both classic and learning-based discovery methods are computationally prohibitive at larger scales.

## Strengths

- **Realistic large-scale benchmarks with known ground truth**: The paper introduces CausalMan Small (120 nodes) and Medium (361 nodes), generated by a physics-based simulator built and validated by domain experts, with hybrid data types, batching, conditional dependencies, causal insufficiency via latent projection, and monitoring/tolerance mechanisms (Section 4, Table 1). This provides a significantly more realistic and complex testbed than synthetic Erdos-Renyi graphs or small toy examples, directly supporting the paper's goal of bridging the gap between controlled benchmarks and real-world applications.

- **Reveals nontrivial failure modes of current causal models**: The experiments concretely document that (a) simple linear regression outperforms all deep causal models on the simplest ATE task (Table 2), (b) deep causal model performance stagnates with increasing dataset size (Figure 2a), and (c) deep models accurately reconstruct the treated interventional distribution while nearly randomly guessing the control distribution, causing poor ATE estimates (Figure 2b). These are specific, well-evidenced findings that the field should take seriously.

- **Documents severe scalability limitations across method classes**: The paper quantifies memory explosion for CBNs with discretized continuous variables (Section 6.0.1), 20–40× runtime increases for classic discovery methods (PC, LiNGAM) when tripling graph size from Small to Medium (Figure 3), and high computational demands for NCMs (Figure 8 in appendix). This provides concrete grounding for the claim that many methods become computationally prohibitive at larger scales.

- **Comprehensive evaluation across diverse models and tasks**: The paper tests seven causal models (CBN, NCM, CAREFL, CNF, VACA, linear/logistic regression) and seven discovery algorithms (PC, PC-Stable, LiNGAM, NOTEARS, GOLEM, DAG-GNN, GranDAG) across three ATE tasks, CATE, and discovery, with a random DAG baseline for discovery — a broader scope than typical benchmarks.

- **Open release of all artefacts**: The paper commits to releasing the simulator, all datasets with complete causal graphs and marginalized ADMGs, and detailed preprocessing/hyperparameter specifications (Section 8, Appendix F), enabling independent reproduction and future research.

### Minor

- **The claimed advantage of causal models (robustness to confounding) is discussed but not experimentally demonstrated.** In Section 6.0.1, after showing that all models fail on Tasks 2 and 3 (including regression on the confounded Task 3), the paper asks "what are the advantages of using [causal models]?" and answers "robustness against confounders." The paper points to the third ATE task where regression is worst, and while this does show regression's vulnerability to confounding, it does *not* show a scenario where causal models succeed where regression fails — the paper honestly states that "all causal models indeed fail to reproduce simple conditional interventional distributions." The paper then pivots to discussing theoretical advantages (counterfactuals, out-of-distribution generalization) that are never tested. This creates a reading tension between the negative experimental results and the motivation narrative. The paper would be stronger if it explicitly acknowledged that the experiments did not find the expected advantage and reframed this as an open problem for the field, rather than presenting it as though the advantage was demonstrated.

- **The "Is prior knowledge necessary?" discussion (Section 7) is unsupported by any experiment in the paper.** This paragraph speculates about neuro-symbolic AI as a path forward without any evidence from the paper's evaluations. It would be better placed as a future work point.

- **Some discussion points about counterfactual reasoning and out-of-distribution generalization (Sections 6.0.1, 7) go beyond what the paper's experiments test.** While these are reasonable directions for future work, presenting them as advantages of current causal models in a paper that shows these models mostly fail is somewhat misleading. The Limitations paragraph in Section 7 is appropriately honest about this tension.

- In the sentence "a simple linear regression is outperforms all other causal models" (line 190), there is a grammatical issue ("is outperforms").

## Novel Insights

The merged reviews offer one genuinely novel insight beyond the paper's own contributions: the observation that the paper's experimental program and its narrative framing pull in opposite directions. The paper is structured as "here is why causal models are important; they fail at scale" but the discussion sections lean into "here are the advantages of causal models." The tension is that a negative-result paper is strongest when it leans fully into the negative result rather than hedging with untested theoretical advantages. A clear-eyed framing — "current causal models fail at scale even on simple tasks, and the theoretical advantages remain unrealized — this is the gap the field must address" — would both be more honest about what was found and more impactful.
