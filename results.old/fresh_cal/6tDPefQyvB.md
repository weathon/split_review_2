## Summary

This paper proposes an end-to-end framework for rotation-equivariant keypoint detection and description that fuses locally rotation-equivariant features (from group-equivariant convolutions, E2CNN) with globally encoded positional information (via a Transformer encoder). It introduces a multi-scale rotation-equivariant feature fusion strategy that preserves equivariance during multi-level fusion, and a directional uncertainty weighted descriptor loss that accounts for the bias inherent in discrete rotation group sampling. Experiments on rotated-HPatches, MegaDepth (original and rotated variants), and YFCC100M (original and rotated variants) show that the method outperforms prior learning-based rotation-equivariant approaches (ReF, RELF) and non-equivariant baselines (AWDesc) on pose estimation, while competitive with handcrafted methods on planar matching benchmarks.

## Strengths

1. **Multi-scale rotation-equivariant feature fusion that preserves group structure (Section 3.1.2, Fig. 3):** The paper designs a principled fusion strategy that isolates the rotation group dimensions before concatenating multi-scale feature maps, preserving equivariance during multi-level fusion. The ablation study (Table 4) shows this design consistently outperforms two alternative fusion schemes across all three MegaDepth variants (original, Rot90, Rot-Rand), providing concrete evidence that the design choice matters.

2. **Directional uncertainty weighted descriptor loss (Section 3.3, Equations 1–5):** The loss explicitly addresses the misalignment bias caused by discrete rotation group sampling—a real technical challenge that prior rotation-equivariant descriptor methods (ReF, RELF) do not directly handle. It incorporates a confidence measure β for principal direction clarity and a directional clarity loss L_DC, then weights the triplet loss by the product of confidences from both images. The resulting method achieves MMA of 0.472 on rotated-HPatches (versus 0.376 for RELF, per paper text), outperforming all compared learning-based baselines.

3. **State-of-the-art pose estimation on real-world rotation benchmarks:** On MegaDepth, MegaDepth-Rot90, and MegaDepth-Rot-Rand (line 166), the method achieves the best AUC@5°/10°/20° among all compared methods, including prior rotation-equivariant approaches. The same holds for YFCC100M, YFCC100M-Rot90, and YFCC100M-Rot-Rand (line 175). These results are on real datasets with depth information and genuine 3D scene variability, which is a more challenging and realistic evaluation than planar benchmarks. The method maintains strong performance on the *original* non-rotated datasets as well, showing no degradation when positional information is added to equivariant features.

4. **Honest reporting of limitations:** The paper acknowledges (line 151) that traditional handcrafted keypoint methods still "perform slightly better" on rotated-HPatches (planar scenes), and that due to the introduction of global positional information, "perfect rotational equivariance cannot be achieved" (line 166). This transparency strengthens the credibility of the claims that are supported.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The paper's core claims are supported by evidence. The rotated-HPatches result is not a fatal contradiction—it is an honestly reported finding where the method outperforms all learning-based approaches and trails handcrafted methods by a small margin on a planar benchmark, while significantly outperforming all baselines on real 3D datasets.

### Minor

1. **Inference-time PCA-based orientation estimation is not validated against ground-truth rotation:** The loss function (Equation 5) uses ground-truth relative rotation O_gt for the cross-entropy term `CE(Shift(D_A, O_gt, D_B))`. At inference time, PCA-based principal direction estimation replaces this (line 106). The paper does not ablate or compare results when training with O_gt versus training with PCA-estimated directions, nor does it measure how often PCA correctly identifies the principal direction. The reader cannot assess whether the method's test-time performance relies on privileged information that may not transfer. This is the most significant evidence gap in the paper.

2. **Detection supervision from a non-equivariant teacher (SuperPoint) without analysis:** Detection ground truth is generated by SuperPoint (line 138), which is not rotation-equivariant. The detector is trained via weighted binary cross-entropy on these labels. If the ground-truth keypoint locations are not themselves rotation-equivariant, the detector may learn to detect keypoints in a non-equivariant way. The paper reports no detection-specific metrics (repeatability, detection score) to isolate whether the detector's rotation robustness lags behind the descriptor's. The overall matching and pose results could be driven by the descriptor alone.

3. **No analysis of the equivariance-accuracy trade-off introduced by the Transformer branch:** The paper acknowledges (lines 60–66) that "local rotation-equivariance and global position information are often incompatible" but does not directly measure the equivariance error introduced by the Transformer encoder branch. The final descriptor is constructed via a heuristic fusion (Equation 6: `DFE(F+(TE(C4)*Conv(C4)))+F`) with no theoretical guarantee of approximate equivariance. Measuring equivariance error (e.g., average descriptor change under known rotations, broken down by feature component) would substantially strengthen the paper's claims about the fusion design.

4. **Loss function notation is unclear in several places:** In Equation (1), the notation `D(c, argmax_k Σ_c D(k,c))` is ambiguous—the indexing of D appears to switch between 2D and 3D, and the channel vs. group dimension usage is inconsistent. The `argmax` in Equation (2) for L_DC uses a different form (`argmax D(k,c)`) from the `argmax` in Equation (1). While the intent is discernible, this makes the loss description difficult to follow and harms reproducibility.

5. **Group-equivariant interpolation is not specified:** The paper states that multi-scale feature maps are "interpolated" to a common resolution while preserving group structure (Section 3.1.2). Standard bilinear interpolation on a regular spatial grid is not group-equivariant. Whether the authors use group-equivariant interpolation or simply rely on the subsequent operations to compensate is not addressed.

### Trivial
- The runtime comparison (Section 4.5) is reported only against AWDesc (0.4785s vs 0.3106s). Including ReF, RELF, and a traditional method would provide more context but does not affect the paper's validity.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a clear architectural contribution (principled fusion of rotation-equivariant features with positional information under a discrete group framework, plus a loss function that handles group-sampling misalignment). The core claims are supported by experiments on both original and rotated versions of two major real-world datasets (MegaDepth, YFCC100M), where the method achieves the best reported pose estimation AUC among all compared baselines. The weaknesses are incremental—missing validation of PCA-based inference, lack of detection-specific metrics, and unclear notation—none of which are fatal or threaten the paper's validity. The paper is a solid, well-motivated contribution that advances rotation-equivariant local feature learning.
