## Summary

This paper presents a large-scale empirical study investigating whether soft prompt embeddings (learned word vectors for visual concepts) transfer across multimodal models performing different tasks: text-to-image generation (Stable Diffusion), open-set object detection (OWL-v2), and zero-shot classification (DFN/CLIP). The authors train 4,800 embeddings across 40 visual concepts and 4 standard datasets, finding that (1) these embeddings are largely model- and task-specific rather than transferable, (2) performant solutions exist in the neighborhood of *any* anchor word (a phenomenon they term "fracturing"), and (3) these perturbative solutions primarily affect the final layers of the text encoder — offering a mechanistic explanation for why transfer fails.

## Strengths

1. **Large-scale systematic benchmark across tasks and models.** The paper optimizes 1,200 unconstrained and 3,600 constrained embeddings across 40 diverse visual concepts, 4 datasets (ImageNet, DreamBooth, COCO, PASCAL VOC), and 3 state-of-the-art models spanning generation, detection, and classification (Section 4, Figure 2). Results are reported with 95% confidence intervals over 100 randomized trials with varying initialization words (Section 4.3). This scale provides robust quantitative evidence that soft prompts are largely model-specific.

2. **Discovery and characterization of "fracturing" in the embedding space.** The paper demonstrates that for every model, dataset, and concept tested, performant solutions exist within a small radius of *any* unrelated anchor word, and performance saturates at δ=0.5 (Figure 5). Near-identical images can be generated from perturbations near different anchors (Figure 4). This is a novel and non-obvious empirical finding that goes significantly beyond prior prompt-tuning work and has implications for interpretability and interoperability.

3. **Mechanistic explanation via activation truncation.** The paper shows that learned perturbations primarily affect the final layers of the text encoder: when the encoder is truncated to early layers, activations cluster around the anchor word and generations depict the anchor concept rather than the target concept (Figure 6, Section 5.2). This provides a concrete, testable reason why transferred embeddings fail — the "concept" representation is a shallow, layer-specific adversarial perturbation rather than a meaningful semantic encoding.

4. **Principled and reproducible transfer methodology.** The paper defines a clean linear least-squares transfer function (Equation 2) between embedding spaces, fit on >40,000 shared vocabulary words, and evaluates all six ordered model-pair transfer directions. This provides a reproducible framework for future work on model interoperability.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The central claim of non-transferability is tested only through a single linear transfer function, which constrains the conclusion.** The paper restricts to linear least-squares maps (Equation 2, line 59: "to simplify the investigation, we restrict our focus to the class of linear Transfer Functions") and draws the broad conclusion that "soft prompts are model-specific and non-transferable." If a non-linear stitching method (e.g., a small MLP with the same training data) recovered meaningful performance, the conclusion would weaken from "prompts are inherently incompatible" to "linear stitching is insufficient." This concern is partially mitigated because (a) the fracturing and activation analyses (Section 5) provide evidence for non-transferability independent of the stitching method, and (b) the paper's claim about "fracturing" — that solutions live near arbitrary words — is independent of the transfer function. However, the paper's title, abstract, and discussion emphasize non-transferability as the central finding, and the evidence for it is conditional on the linearity assumption. Testing even one non-linear baseline would substantially strengthen the paper.

### Minor

1. **The "fracturing" result is descriptive rather than causal as an explanation for transfer failure.** The paper shows that performant solutions exist near arbitrary anchor words (Section 5.1) and that these solutions target final layers (Section 5.2). It then suggests that fracturing *causes* non-transferability. However, no direct test connects the *degree* of fracturing (e.g., number of distinct solutions per concept) with the *degree* of transfer failure across concepts. The activation truncation experiment (Section 5.2) provides a plausible mechanism, but a correlational analysis (e.g., showing that concepts with higher fracturing transfer worse) would strengthen the explanatory link. This is a common limitation of empirical studies — the paper's contribution as an observational finding remains solid.

2. **Generation quality is evaluated entirely through a CLIP-based metric without human validation.** The generation metric is "probability that generation contains the concept measured by CLIP L-14" (Table 1, line 94). While this is a standard automated metric for generation quality, relying solely on CLIP (a model with known biases and vulnerabilities to adversarial perturbations) is a limitation — especially since the paper's core finding is that these embeddings *are* adversarial-like perturbations. A small human evaluation subsample (e.g., 100 images rated by Mechanical Turk) would substantially strengthen confidence that the automated metric reflects genuine visual concept presence rather than CLIP-specific features.

3. **The concept-level breakdown by granularity is missing.** The paper notes that common concepts (PASCAL) transfer better than fine-grained ones (DreamBooth), but does not systematically analyze this dimension. Given that 4 of the 10 "common concepts" results differ notably from the fine-grained ones (Section 4.3, Figure 2), plotting concept-level results (fine vs. coarse) alongside aggregate trends would help readers understand when transfer might work and why.

### Trivial

1. **Abstract phrasing is slightly imprecise.** The abstract states "perturbations within an ε-ball to any prior embedding that generate, detect, and classify an arbitrary concept." The phrase "any prior embedding" is ambiguous — the paper later clarifies (Section 5) that this means the ball around the anchor/initialization word, not any arbitrary prior embedding. Clarifying this in the abstract would prevent reader confusion.

2. **Figure 2 caption does not explicitly name the y-axis metric.** While the metric is clearly defined in Table 1 and the actual rendered figure likely has axis labels, the caption for Figure 2 only says "Visual word embeddings trained for one task... perform well" without restating the metric. This could be ambiguous in the text format.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Test a non-linear transfer baseline** (e.g., a 2-layer MLP with the same shared vocabulary training data). Even a negative result would strengthen the claim that the embeddings are truly incompatible rather than just linearly misaligned.
- **Evaluate whether fine-tuning the transferred embedding** for a small number of steps on the target task recovers performance. This would speak to practical transferability rather than zero-shot only.
- **Report the condition number or residual error** of the linear least-squares fit to address whether the map is ill-conditioned due to high dimensionality.
- **Include a random embedding baseline** (e.g., a randomly initialized token) on all tasks to contextualize transferred performance.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews did not surface an insight about the paper that the paper itself does not already present. However, one observation worth noting: the combination of the fracturing finding (performant solutions near any word) with the activation truncation result (perturbations target only final layers) together imply that these embeddings are essentially "adversarial suffixes" attached to arbitrary base words — they do not encode semantic content about the concept in any distributed or compositional way. This is stronger than either result alone, and the paper makes this connection explicitly.

## Suggestions

1. Add one non-linear transfer baseline (e.g., 2-layer MLP with 100 hidden units trained on the same shared vocabulary). This is the single highest-value addition: if it also fails, the non-transferability claim is robust; if it succeeds, the paper's conclusions become more nuanced but also more informative.
2. Add a human evaluation subsample for generation quality (e.g., 100 generated images rated by crowd workers on whether they depict the target concept).
3. Add a concept-granularity breakdown to the main figures (fine-grained DreamBooth vs. common PASCAL/COCO/ImageNet concepts).
4. Clarify the abstract's phrasing about "any prior embedding" to specify the ball around the anchor word.

## Score and Decision

This is a solid empirical study with genuine contributions: the first large-scale benchmark of soft prompt transferability across multimodal models and tasks, the discovery of embedding space fracturing, and a mechanistic explanation via activation truncation. The main limitation is that non-transferability is tested through a single linear stitching method. This is a real constraint on the conclusion but not a fatal flaw, because the fracturing and activation analyses provide independent evidence. With reasonable revisions (particularly a non-linear baseline), the paper would be a clear accept.
