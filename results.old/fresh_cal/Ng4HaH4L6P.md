## Summary

This paper presents SlideChat, the first vision-language assistant designed to process and understand gigapixel whole-slide pathology images end-to-end, along with the SlideInstruction dataset (4.2K captions + 176K VQA pairs) and the SlideBench multi-task benchmark. The architecture uses a frozen patch-level encoder (CONCH) → trainable slide-level encoder (LongNet with sparse attention) → projector → LLM, enabling whole-slide context. Results show SOTA on 18 of 22 tasks across multiple benchmarks, with substantial margins over patch-based and thumbnail-based MLLMs (e.g., +13.47% on SlideBench-VQA TCGA).

## Strengths

- **First MLLM designed for gigapixel whole-slide pathology images**: Prior pathology MLLMs (GPT-4o, MedDr, LLaVA-Med, PathChat) operate only on patches or thumbnails, losing either global context or fine detail. SlideChat's architecture—CONCH + LongNet with sparse attention + projector + LLM—is a principled solution to the gigapixel problem, described clearly in Section 3.1. The empirical gap (e.g., 81.17% vs. 67.70% overall accuracy on SlideBench-VQA TCGA) directly demonstrates the value of whole-slide processing.

- **State-of-the-art performance with large margins across multiple benchmarks**: SlideChat achieves SOTA on 18 of 22 tasks. On SlideBench-VQA (TCGA) it scores 81.17% (+13.47% over MedDr), on SlideBench-VQA (BCNB) 54.14% (+12.71%, zero-shot), and on WSI-VQA 60.18% (+5.82%) as shown in Table 2. The cross-domain (BCNB) results are particularly strong and demonstrate genuine generalization.

- **Largest open-source WSI instruction dataset and benchmark**: SlideInstruction contains 4,181 WSI-caption pairs and 175,753 VQA pairs from 3,294 patients across 10 cancer types (Section 3.2). SlideBench provides three test sets covering captioning and 20+ clinical tasks, with VQA test questions filtered by four LLMs and validated by five pathologists. This is a significant community resource.

- **Ablation studies validate key design choices**: Table 4 shows removing the slide-level encoder causes a >10% drop on cross-domain BCNB VQA (45.49% vs. 54.14%) while in-distribution performance is comparable, revealing that the encoder's primary benefit is cross-domain generalization — an interesting and well-supported finding. The systematic comparison of six LLMs and three model scales (3B/7B/14B) confirms Qwen2.5-7B as the best performance-efficiency trade-off.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Training data (SlideInstruction) generated by GPT-4 without pathologist verification**: The entire training dataset — 4.2K captions and 176K VQA pairs — is produced by prompting GPT-4 on TCGA pathology reports (Section 3.2, lines 128–139). No pathologist validation is applied to the training data. Only the test set (SlideBench-VQA) receives LLM filtering + five-pathologist review (lines 158–162). While the data derives from real reports (mitigating hallucination risk vs. GPT generating medical facts from scratch), the lack of quality characterization on the training data means the model may learn systematic biases or inaccuracies introduced by GPT-4's interpretation of reports. The authors should at minimum report a pathologist-evaluated random sample with error rates. This is a significant gap for a model framed as a clinical assistant.

### Minor

- **Baselines are reasonable but could be strengthened**: The paper compares against patch-based methods using 30 random patches + majority voting and 1024×1024 thumbnails (lines 218–220). These are the standard adaptation strategies for MLLMs not designed for WSIs, and the paper acknowledges this limitation. However, a stronger baseline — using the same patch encoder (CONCH) with a learned slide-level aggregator (e.g., attention-based MIL or a small transformer) feeding into the same LLM — would isolate whether the advantage comes from SlideChat's specific LongNet-based design or simply from processing more patches with a learned aggregation. The paper would be stronger with such an ablation; its absence is a missed opportunity rather than a fatal flaw.

- **Computational cost reporting is ambiguous**: The paper states the pipeline "includes the patch-level encoder, slide-level encoder, multimodal projector module, and large language model" and reports "average response time within 1 second" and "~27 GB GPU memory" (line 383). However, the phrasing "After extracting the local and global features of WSIs, the average response time was within 1 second" suggests the 1-second figure may exclude feature extraction time. This needs clarification. Total end-to-end latency (including patch extraction and slide-level encoding) and peak memory should be clearly separated and reported.

- **Interpretability analysis is qualitative and thin**: The attention analysis (Section 4, Figure 5) shows only two example WSIs with qualitative alignment between top attention patches and output text. There is no quantitative evaluation (e.g., overlap with pathologist-annotated regions of interest, or comparison against random patch selection). For a model targeting clinical use, this does not sufficiently support claims about reliability or trustworthiness. A quantitative analysis using an annotated dataset would substantially strengthen this section.

- **Table anomaly: Vicuna-7B shows identical scores on two different benchmarks**: In Table 4 (line 337), Vicuna-7B-v1.5 reports 41.43 for both VQA (TCGA) and VQA (BCNB) — two different test sets. This is suspicious and likely a copy-paste error that should be corrected.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the reviews surface one genuinely interesting angle: the ablation reveals that the slide-level encoder's primary benefit is cross-domain generalization rather than in-distribution performance (BCNB drops >10% when removed, while TCGA VQA is essentially unchanged at 81.21% vs. 81.17%). This suggests that for within-distribution tasks, a simpler aggregation might suffice, while the LongNet-based encoder is specifically valuable for handling distribution shift — a finding with practical implications for model deployment that the paper could explore more deeply.

## Suggestions

1. **Validate a random sample of training data with pathologists**: Report error rates on ~200 captions and ~200 VQA pairs from SlideInstruction. This is the single most impactful improvement for credibility.
2. **Build a stronger patch-based baseline**: Use CONCH + learned aggregator (e.g., attention-based MIL) + Qwen2.5-7B to isolate whether SlideChat's advantage comes from LongNet specifically or from learned aggregation more generally.
3. **Clarify computational cost reporting**: Separate feature extraction time from LLM inference time, and report peak memory for the full pipeline including patch extraction.
4. **Quantify interpretability**: Use a WSI dataset with pathologist-annotated ROIs to compute overlap (e.g., mean IoU) between attention-highlighted patches and ground-truth regions, compared to a random baseline.
5. **Correct the Vicuna row in Table 4**: Verify whether the two 41.43 values for VQA (TCGA) and VQA (BCNB) are correct or a copy-paste error.
