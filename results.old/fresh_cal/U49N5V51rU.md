## Summary

This paper introduces a theoretical framework for analyzing length generalization in causal transformers with absolute positional encodings. It defines **LimitTransformers** (a limiting object that encapsulates the computations of a sequence of transformers across growing input lengths) and an **idealized inference procedure** with a norm-based regularizer. Theorem 1 shows that functions expressible by LimitTransformers satisfying *Periodic* and *Local* properties are guaranteed to length-generalize under this procedure. The paper connects this to **CRASP** programs (providing lower bounds on expressiveness) and uses communication complexity (providing upper bounds). Experiments on 8 algorithmic tasks and 17 formal languages show a strong correlation between CRASP expressiveness and empirical length generalization.

## Strengths

1. **Theorem 1 (main guarantee) is the first formal equivalence result linking expressibility in a restricted transformer formalism to guaranteed length generalization.** The paper shows that a function is expressible by a LimitTransformer satisfying *Periodic* and *Local* if and only if the Inference Procedure eventually yields transformers that generalize from length n/2 to n. This formalizes a direction only conjectured in prior work (the RASP-L Conjecture of Zhou et al., 2023). The product reparameterization that eliminates dependence on transformer width is a clever technical contribution.

2. **The communication complexity upper bound (Theorem 4) provides a clean necessary condition for length generalization under the framework.** It proves that any LimitTransformer satisfying *Periodic* and *Local* has logarithmic communication complexity, from which the paper derives non-expressibility of copying with repetition and addition (Corollary 1). This goes beyond prior single-layer communication bounds (Sanford et al., 2023; Bhattamishra et al., 2024) by applying to multilayer transformers.

3. **The classification of regular languages into CRASPpl/CRASPempty (Lemma 4) provides concrete, testable predictions.** The paper shows PARITY ∉ CRASPpl, (aa)* ∈ CRASPpl \ CRASPempty, Σ*be* ∉ CRASPpl, and that piecewise testable languages are in CRASPempty. These predictions align well with the experimental results (Figure 1), explaining previously puzzling variation in transformer length generalization across formal languages.

4. **The experimental results show a strong correlation between CRASP expressiveness and empirical length generalization across 25 tasks.** The experiments use a uniform framework with systematic control for offset invariance and random offsets. The alignment between CRASP predictions and results (e.g., NoPE failing on (aa)* but APE succeeding, matching Lemma 4(2)) provides genuine empirical support for the claim that CRASP expressiveness is a useful predictor.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The core theoretical contribution is sound, and the weaknesses below are addressable through reframing and additional exposition.

### Major

1. **Overclaiming: the paper conflates a formal result about an idealized procedure with "explaining" empirical behavior.** The Inference Procedure (Definition 6) differs from real training in several significant ways: it requires exact matching on *all* inputs up to length n/2 (not a sample), minimizes a specific composite regularizer (not SGD with cross-entropy), assumes translation invariance of internal positional products (Equation 11, which real APE transformers do not satisfy), and uses log-scaled attention with precision truncation. The abstract claims the theory "explains a broad set of empirical observations" and "predicts the success and failure of length generalization," but the experiments test CRASP expressiveness as a *correlate* of generalization — they do not test whether the Inference Procedure's mechanisms (regularizer, translation invariance, log-scaling) are necessary for that correlation. The paper does acknowledge this gap in a brief "Limitations" paragraph (line 467-471) and the Discussion, but the framing throughout the abstract, introduction, and conclusion claims substantially more. This gap between the theory's assumptions and the claims made about empirical observations is the paper's most significant weakness.

2. **The translation invariance hypothesis (Equation 11) is extremely strong and unverified in practice.** The paper requires that *all* positional products involving parameter matrices are offset-independent (not just the input-output behavior). This is described as "natural" but is actually a strong inductive bias that real APE transformers do not have. It does most of the work in the finiteness argument for Theorem 1. The paper states no bridging argument or empirical evidence that SGD-trained transformers approximately satisfy this condition. Without such a bridge, the theory's relevance to practical behavior remains indirect — the experimental correlation may be driven by CRASP as a complexity measure rather than by the specific theoretical mechanism.

### Minor

1. **The finiteness argument in Theorem 1 is insufficiently justified in the main text.** Line 211 states "The regularizer bounds width, depth, and precision of the LimitTransformers," yet the regularizer (Definition 5) does not bound the width d or MLP dimension d_MLP — it bounds ranks, norms, depth, and precision. The paper correctly notes (line 181) that "the width d does not explicitly enter R," implying infinitely many transformers can have the same regularizer value. The product reparameterization is the intended resolution, but the sketch does not fully explain why this prevents infinitely many distinct LimitTransformers from emerging. This gap is likely addressable with the full proof but the main text's reasoning is incomplete.

2. **The CRASP-to-LimitTransformer construction (Theorem 3) is stated without a general construction sketch.** The paper asserts that "Any CRASP[periodic, local] program can be translated to a LimitTransformer" but provides only one concrete example (induction head, two layers). CRASP includes counting, conditionals, Boolean combinations, and arbitrary periodic/local relations — implementing all of these in softmax attention with bounded precision and the restrictions on attention patterns is nontrivial. While full proofs may reside in the appendix, a high-level sketch of the general translation scheme in the main text would be needed to assess the plausibility of the claim.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation emerging from these reviews is about the **tension between the paper's theoretical framing and its empirical validation**. The paper presents a cohesive formal framework (LimitTransformers + Inference Procedure) that yields a clean theoretical guarantee, then separately demonstrates that CRASP expressiveness correlates with experimental results. The reviews collectively surface that these two contributions are not as tightly linked as the paper suggests — the theoretical guarantee depends on assumptions (translation invariance of internal products, the specific regularizer, log-scaling of attention) that the experiments do not test, and the experiments succeed based on CRASP expressiveness which could be a valid predictor for reasons independent of the specific theoretical mechanism. Reframing the contribution as "a formal framework for length generalization in an idealized setting, plus independent evidence that CRASP expressiveness is an empirically useful predictor" would make the paper stronger than its current framing, which attempts to claim the theory *explains* the experiments. None of this insight is beyond what the paper could state itself with more careful framing.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the contribution honestly.** Separate (a) the formal theorem about the Inference Procedure from (b) the empirical observation that CRASP expressiveness correlates with generalization. The paper currently presents (a) *as if* it entails (b), but it does not. A candid statement that the theory provides necessary conditions for length generalization under an idealized procedure, while CRASP expressiveness serves as a useful empirical predictor, would better match what the paper actually demonstrates.

2. **Add a bridging argument or clearer discussion of the gap** between the Inference Procedure and practical training. Even a brief argument that SGD with weight decay approximately minimizes a regularizer of the considered type, or that the translation-invariance condition captures a natural symmetry, would substantially strengthen the paper.

3. **Provide a sketch of the CRASP-to-LimitTransformer construction** in the main text, showing how counting operations, conditionals, and positional relations map to specific transformer components. This is central to the paper's claims about which functions length-generalize.

4. **Fix the imprecise statement** in line 211 that "the regularizer bounds width" of LimitTransformers, since the regularizer does not bound d or d_MLP. Clarify how the product reparameterization resolves this.
