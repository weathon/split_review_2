## Summary

This paper introduces the Path-Driven Independence Testing (PIT) algorithm, a constraint-based Bayesian network structure learning method that reduces the number of conditional independence (CI) tests required by PC-style algorithms. The key idea is to restrict conditioning sets for each pair of variables (X,Y) to only those neighbors of X (or Y) that are connected to the other variable by a path, and to identify "blindly blockable" paths whose nodes can always be included in the conditioning set without breaking d-separation. Three algorithm variants (PIT, BPIT, Opt. BPIT) are presented. Empirical results on 11 benchmark networks show PIT is up to 7× faster than PC and significantly reduces CI tests, while being competitive in accuracy.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated approach to reducing CI tests.** The paper clearly identifies the conservatism in PC's conditioning set search (conditioning on *all* neighbor subsets when only some neighbors are connected to the other variable) and exploits this via the concept of essential parents and covering paths. Example 1 (Section 3.3) concretely demonstrates the reduction from O(2ⁿ) to O(n³) CI tests on a naive Bayes structure.

2. **Strong empirical evidence of speedup and CI test reduction.** Experiments on 11 established benchmark networks across multiple sample sizes (Tables 2–4) show PIT is up to 7× faster than PC with significantly fewer CI tests (Wilcoxon p < .001). The comparison also includes PC-stable, Hill Climbing, and Tabu, with efforts to match runtime for the latter two. These results are directly relevant to the paper's central claim.

3. **Three algorithm variants with different trade-offs.** Presenting PIT, BPIT, and Opt. BPIT as a family allows readers to understand the progression from the basic path-driven idea to the more aggressive blind-blocking version and its optimized variant. The empirical comparison between them provides insight into when each variant is preferred.

4. **Complexity analysis for special graph structures.** Table 1 and Propositions 1–2 provide concrete CI test counts for naive Bayes, star, and forest skeleton graphs, helping readers understand when the method yields the largest gains.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Lemma 4 is stated without a proof or proof sketch in the main text, making the theoretical guarantees unverifiable from the submission.** Lemma 4 claims that non-adjacent nodes X and Y are independent conditioned on *every* separator M*_{XY} (or M*_{YX}), where separators are constructed from essential parents plus arbitrary connected blockable neighborhoods and off-path blockable sets. This is a strong universal claim — far stronger than Lemma 2 (which guarantees existence of at least one separating set). The paper does not provide even a sketch of why adding arbitrary blockable neighborhoods and off-path nodes preserves d-separation in all cases. The soundness and completeness of all three algorithms (Theorem 1) rests on this lemma; without being able to evaluate its proof, the paper's core theoretical contribution is incompletely supported in the main text. *(Relevant lines: Lemma 4 at line 109, Definition 4 at lines 99–101, Theorem 1 at line 142.)*

2. **The experimental setup is described imprecisely, specifically the "16 datasets" claim.** Eleven benchmark networks are listed, and the text says datasets were generated by sampling "10,000 (resp. 1,000 and 10) times… resulting in 16 datasets." With 11 networks × 3 sample sizes = 33 possible combinations, the number 16 is not accounted for. The paper does not clarify which networks were used at which sample sizes, or whether only a subset was used. This makes it difficult to assess the completeness of the evaluation. *(Relevant lines: 157–159.)*

3. **The claimed accuracy advantage is not supported by the reported statistics.** The paper states PIT was "often more accurate or no worse compared to PC and PC-stable, although there was no significance difference (p=.61, p=.78)" (line 160). Despite this, the conclusion (Section 5) and abstract assert "more accurate" without qualification. The Wilcoxon p-values indicate the accuracy differences are indistinguishable from noise. The claim should be caveated to reflect the actual statistical evidence. *(Relevant lines: 160, Conclusions Section 5.)*

### Minor

1. **No variance or confidence intervals are reported for any experimental metric.** Runtime, CI test counts, and SHD are reported as point estimates. Given that CI test outcomes are stochastic and the results are based on single generated datasets per condition, some measure of variability (standard deviations across multiple runs or data realizations) would strengthen the reliability of the conclusions.

2. **The computational overhead of computing the restricted conditioning sets is not analyzed or reported separately.** The paper acknowledges the algorithms require computing reachable sets, identifying potentially trigger nodes, and updating W and B sets as the graph changes. End-to-end runtime includes this overhead, and the overall results are positive. But without any analysis of how this overhead scales with graph size or density, it is unclear whether there exist regimes where the overhead could erode the CI test savings. *(Relevant lines: Section 3.1 definitions, Algorithm 4 description in lines 120–121.)*

3. **The algorithm descriptions in the main text are high-level and would be difficult to reimplement from prose alone.** The paper defines many interrelated concepts (covering paths, potentially trigger nodes, maximal blockable sets, connected blockable neighborhoods, off-path blockable sets, separators) and describes Algorithms 1, 2, 4, and 5 in dense paragraphs. While pseudocode images are included in the original submission (the PDF parser strips them), the textual descriptions are ambiguous in several places (e.g., the handling of the iterative nature of B-set computation as edges are removed). *(Relevant lines: 116–124.)*

1. **Naming inconsistency:** The introduction (line 18) refers to "Path-Driven Independence Testing (PDIT) Algorithm" while the title and the rest of the paper use "PIT." The abbreviation should be consistent.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's suggestion that the "every separator" quantifier in Lemma 4 may be unnecessarily strong points to a genuine tension in the paper's theoretical framing: the algorithms as described likely only need *some* separator (one that includes the essential parents plus some blockable nodes), not *every* possible combination, to be correct. If Lemma 4 indeed requires the universal quantifier for the proof but the algorithms only use existential separators in practice, there is a gap between theory and algorithm that the paper does not address. Conversely, if Lemma 4 is provable as stated (the definitions of potentially trigger nodes and blockable neighborhoods are carefully constructed to guarantee that any subset of the maximal blockable set preserves d-separation), this would be a noteworthy theoretical contribution. The paper's current presentation leaves this ambiguity unresolved.
