## Summary

AgentQuest aggregates six existing RL game environments (BabyAI, Crafter, TextWorld, Baba Is AI, MiniHack, NetHack) into a unified benchmark for evaluating LLM/VLM agents on long-horizon interactive tasks. It provides fine-grained scoring, decouples models from prompting strategies via an open-source framework, and presents zero-shot baseline evaluations of 13+ model variants in both language-only and vision-language modalities. The paper identifies that models perform worse when image observations are added, and documents qualitative failure modes including a "knowing-doing gap."

## Strengths

- **Diverse suite spanning a wide difficulty range, preventing saturation.** The six environments range from BabyAI (solvable in seconds) to NetHack (years to master), with Table 1 mapping required skills (long-term planning, spatial reasoning, exploration) to each game. This contrasts with prior game-based benchmarks (e.g., Liu et al., 2023a; Todd et al., 2024) where the hardest environment (Crafter) is already seeing strong performance. Evidence: Section 2.1, Table 1.

- **Systematic dual-modality evaluation revealing a non-obvious finding.** The paper evaluates every environment (except TextWorld) in both language-only and vision-language formats and finds that adding images consistently degrades performance. This is a non-trivial result that points to a real bottleneck in current multimodal agents, going beyond reporting aggregate scores. Evidence: Section 4, Tables 2 and 3; Section 6 (Limitations of Current Vision-Language Models).

- **Comprehensive baseline evaluation across a broad range of SOTA models.** The zero-shot evaluation covers 18 model variants (GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Gemini-1.5-Flash/Pro, Llama 3.1 8B/70B, Llama 3.2 1B/3B/11B/90B, o1-mini, o1-preview) across both modalities, providing a reproducible starting point for the community. Evidence: Section 3.3, Figure 2.

- **Open-source toolkit with decoupled architecture.** The framework separates the underlying model (client.py) from the prompting strategy (agent.py) and supports parallelized, asynchronous evaluation across multiple APIs (OpenAI, Gemini, Claude, vLLM), lowering the barrier for researchers to test new models or inference-time strategies. Evidence: Figure 1, Section 2.2, Section 3.1.

- **Procedurally generated environments prevent test-set leakage.** The paper notes that all environments use procedurally generated instances, making verbatim memorization of solutions infeasible — a known weakness of static benchmarks. Evidence: Section 1, paragraph on memorization.

- **Qualitative analysis identifying specific failure modes.** The analysis in Section 4.1 documents concrete failure patterns (spatial reasoning, systematic exploration, long-term planning, discovering environment dynamics) and identifies a "knowing-doing gap" where models know the right action but fail to execute it (e.g., dying from rotten food in NetHack despite correctly identifying it as dangerous when queried separately). Evidence: Section 4.1.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

None.

### Minor

- **The vision-language comparison is not fully disentangled.** The VL condition provides both an image and the full text description of the environment state (Section 3.2). The comparison is text-only vs. text+image, not text vs. image. The observed performance drop could arise from redundant/noisy information rather than a failure of vision-based reasoning per se. An image-only ablation (or testing text and image independently) would cleanly separate the effect of visual input from the effect of adding a second source of information. The paper's claim that "vision-based decision-making is far outside our reach" (abstract, conclusion) is likely correct in spirit, but the current experimental design cannot fully rule out alternative explanations. Evidence: Section 3.2 ("Vision-Language Format"), Section 4, abstract line 5.

- **The "knowing-doing gap" is described qualitatively but not systematically quantified.** While the examples (exiting the dungeon despite knowing it causes death; eating rotten food) are concrete and suggestive, the paper provides no systematic quantification — how often this occurs across models, across how many episodes, or whether the knowledge probe was conducted under consistent conditions. This reduces a potentially significant finding to an anecdotal observation. Evidence: Section 4.1, "Knowing-Doing Gap" paragraph.

- **No variance or confidence intervals reported for main results despite using multiple seeds.** The paper asserts that "multiple seeds" are used (Section 3.1) but reports all results as point estimates without standard errors, interquartile ranges, or any measure of dispersion. For a benchmark paper, this omission makes it impossible for readers to assess whether differences between models are statistically meaningful. Evidence: Section 3.1 (line 60: "We use multiple seeds..."), Tables 2 and 3 (no variance shown).

## Suggestions

1. **Add an image-only ablation for a subset of models.** Run GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Llama 3.2 90B on a subset of environments (e.g., Crafter and BabyAI) with only the image (no text description) as the observation. This would cleanly separate the question "does the model struggle with visual input?" from "does redundant information confuse the model?" and would substantially strengthen the paper's central claim about VLMs.

2. **Quantify the knowing-doing gap.** After each episode, systematically probe the model's knowledge of the relevant game mechanic (e.g., "Is eating rotten food dangerous?") and report the proportion of episodes where knowledge is correct but action contradicts it, broken down by model and environment. This would turn a suggestive anecdote into a measurable phenomenon.

3. **Report variance/confidence intervals.** Since multiple seeds are already used, presenting standard errors or interquartile ranges for the main results in Tables 2 and 3 would significantly improve the credibility of cross-model comparisons.

## Score and Decision

The paper introduces a useful and well-motivated benchmark that fills a genuine gap — existing game-based benchmarks lack the difficulty headroom that NetHack provides, and the unified framework with a decoupled architecture is a practical contribution. The baseline evaluations are thorough in their breadth (18 model variants), and the qualitative analysis identifies failure modes that the community can act on. The weaknesses are real but minor: the VL finding would be strengthened by an ablation, the knowing-doing gap needs quantification, and variance is missing from the reported results. None of these threaten the core contribution of the benchmark itself. With the suggested fixes, this would be a solid contribution to the agent evaluation literature.
