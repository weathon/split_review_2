## Summary

This paper introduces *preference discerning*, a paradigm that conditions generative sequential recommendation on textual user preferences (generated by LLMs from user reviews). It proposes a five-axis benchmark (preference-based recommendation, fine/coarse-grained steering, sentiment following, history consolidation) and a new model, Mender, that fuses semantic IDs with pretrained language encoders via cross-attention. MenderTok significantly outperforms existing generative retrieval baselines (TIGER, LC-REC, VocabExt variants) across most benchmark axes, and the paper demonstrates that certain steering capabilities can either emerge from recommendation-only training or be injected via targeted data augmentation.

---

## Strengths

1. **Five-axis benchmark with formal definitions enables holistic evaluation of preference discerning.** Section 3.2 provides clear equations for each axis (Eqs. 1–3 for preference matching and steering, combined hit-rate for sentiment following, and the multi-preference setup for history consolidation). No prior sequential recommendation benchmark tests these distinct capabilities together.

2. **MenderTok achieves strong and consistent improvements over baselines.** Table 1 reports up to ~45% relative improvement on the recommendation axis (e.g., Beauty Recall@10: MenderTok 0.132 vs. TIGER 0.091) and up to ~70% relative improvement on fine-grained steering. The gains hold across all four datasets, indicating the benefit of explicit preference conditioning is robust.

3. **Demonstrates that multiple preference-discerning capabilities can be learned via appropriate training data.** The paper shows that fine-grained steering emerges from recommendation-only training on Amazon subsets (attributed to data distribution, with a contrastive failure on Steam), and that coarse-grained steering and sentiment following can be explicitly injected via data augmentation without degrading recommendation performance (Section 4.3, MenderTok-All results). These findings are informative for future work.

4. **Ablation study (Figure 5) confirms that combining both user preferences and item language representations outperforms using either alone.** This directly validates the key design choice of MenderTok — encoding both modalities in one token sequence — and the result is quantified.

5. **MenderEmb provides a documented speed–accuracy trade-off.** Training ~5× faster than MenderTok while achieving second-best performance on Amazon datasets (Section 4.2). This practical contribution is noted and contextualized.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Preference quality validation is anecdotal and insufficiently documented.** The paper's entire data generation pipeline rests on a single sentence (line 53): *"The participans found that usually around 75% of the generated preferences correctly approximate the user's preferences."* No details are provided about who the participants were, how many, what annotation protocol was used, what "correctly approximate" means operationally, or if inter-annotator agreement was measured. This is a significant methodological gap: the quality of the generated preferences directly affects the benchmark's validity and informs how much trust to place in conclusions that depend on preference accuracy. However, this weakness does **not** invalidate the paper's core contributions — the relative comparison between methods is controlled (all methods use the same preferences), so the Mender improvements are meaningful. But the absolute claims about preference-discerning capability are weakened, and the benchmark's construct validity is partially opaque without proper preference validation.

### Minor

2. **Potential structural correlation between benchmark construction and model design.** The benchmark matches preferences to items via cosine similarity in Sentence-T5 space (Eq. 1), and the RQ-VAE semantic IDs are also derived from Sentence-T5 embeddings. Mender additionally uses pretrained Sentence-T5 encodings. This creates a potential path where the benchmark may be structurally easier for methods using Sentence-T5 representations. While this doesn't invalidate the results (all semantic-ID-based baselines share the same embedding space), the paper would benefit from discussing whether this correlation could inflate reported performance on the preference-based recommendation axis.

3. **The claim that generated preferences are "mostly orthogonal" is unquantified.** Line 53 states that *"the information contained in the different user preferences are mostly orthogonal"* without any evidence, metric, or even a definition of what "orthogonal" means in this context (semantically distinct? non-redundant for prediction?). This claim matters for the history consolidation axis, which assumes preferences are sufficiently varied to test the model's ability to ignore irrelevant ones.

4. **LC-REC baseline is evaluated without its published auxiliary tasks.** The paper acknowledges this (line 118: *"do not add the auxiliary tasks"*) and later notes that LC-REC *"usually requires auxiliary tasks to align the two spaces properly."* This is an honest limitation, but including LC-REC with its full training recipe as an additional comparison point would strengthen the claim that Mender's fusion mechanism is superior.

5. **Aspirational language in the introduction overstates what was evaluated.** Line 20 states *"users can specify in natural language what item properties they wish to avoid or prefer"* — but all experiments use LLM-generated preferences, not user-written ones. This should be qualified as a future capability enabled by the paradigm, not a demonstrated property.

6. **Preference generation details are underspecified for replication.** The exact prompt template used for LLM preference generation, the number of preferences generated per user per timestep (the paper says "five" at line 53 but this appears only in the running text), and the filtering criteria are not provided with sufficient precision for independent reproduction.

7. Typo on line 53: *"participans"* should be *"participants"*. ("each each" on the same line is also a minor repetition.)

## Novel Insights

The Harsh Critic identifies a genuinely useful methodological point that goes beyond what the paper itself emphasizes: the structural entanglement between the preference matching procedure (cosine similarity in Sentence-T5 space) and Mender's own representation space creates a potential evaluation confound that the paper does not discuss. This is not a fatal flaw — all semantic-ID baselines share this space — but it surfaces a subtle design consideration for future benchmark construction in this area: the choice of embedding model for preference-item matching should be carefully disentangled from the representation spaces used by the evaluated models, or at minimum acknowledged as a limitation.

## Suggestions

1. **Strengthen the preference quality validation.** Run a proper human evaluation with ≥3 annotators, report inter-annotator agreement, define what constitutes a "correct" preference approximation, and provide examples of successes and failures. This is the single highest-leverage improvement.
2. **Discuss the Sentence-T5 correlation explicitly** in the limitations or benchmark design section.
3. **Add LC-REC with auxiliary tasks** as an additional baseline, or clearly justify why it was omitted beyond the stated reason.
4. **Provide the exact prompt template** for preference generation in the main text or appendix.
