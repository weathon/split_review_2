## Summary

This paper introduces FB-CPR (Forward-Backward representations with Conditional Policy Regularization), an unsupervised RL algorithm that grounds zero-shot policy learning by regularizing FB representations toward imitating unlabeled motion-capture trajectories. The key technical idea is to use the FB representation's ER_FB trajectory embedding to condition a discriminator on latent codes, enabling per-policy distribution matching rather than marginal state-only matching. The authors train a whole-body humanoid behavioral foundation model on SMPL with AMASS mocap data and evaluate across 45 reward tasks, 50 goal-reaching tasks, and 990 motion-tracking tasks. FB-CPR achieves 73.4% of per-task top-line performance without any task-specific training, outperforming the closest zero-shot baseline (ASE) by >1.4× in each category, while requiring only 12 seconds per inference episode.

## Strengths

1. **Novel technical contribution with clear empirical validation.** The latent-conditional discriminator regularized by ER_FB trajectory embeddings (Eq. 9) is a well-motivated extension of FB representations. The ablation in Figure 4 (top left) directly verifies that the latent-conditioned discriminator outperforms a state-only marginal variant, confirming that the ER_FB embedding is doing useful work beyond what simpler distribution-matching would provide.

2. **Strong, multi-faceted empirical evaluation.** The paper evaluates across 45 reward tasks, 50 goal-reaching tasks, and 990 tracking motions — a scale that meaningfully supports the claim of a "behavioral foundation model." FB-CPR achieves 73.4% of per-task top-line performance and outperforms ASE by >1.4× across all categories (Table 1). The human evaluation (Figure 3) further shows that FB-CPR produces behaviors judged comparably successful to TD3 while being rated more human-like.

3. **Systematic ablations isolating each component.** Figure 4 separates the contributions of (a) the latent-conditioned vs. state-only discriminator, (b) the unsupervised FB term \(F^\top z\), (c) online vs. offline training (FB-AW), and (d) scaling with model capacity and dataset size. Each ablation cleanly targets a specific design choice and supports the paper's claims.

4. **Practical computational advantage over planning-based approaches.** FB-CPR completes a 300-step episode in ~12 seconds, vs. ~30 minutes for OracleMPPI and ~5 hours for DIFFUSER. This makes the zero-shot policy approach viable for real-time applications.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper's core claims are supported by the evidence presented.

### Major

1. **Non-stationarity in the distribution-matching objective is not analyzed.** The ER_FB trajectory embedding used to label the dataset (\(z = \frac{1}{n}\sum B(s_i)\)) depends on the backward embedding \(B\), which is trained concurrently via the FB loss (Eq. 5). This means the target distribution \(p_\mathcal{M}(s,z)\) that the discriminator attempts to match shifts as \(B\) changes during training. The paper does not discuss whether this non-stationarity causes training instability, nor does it provide convergence guarantees for the resulting fixed point. The ablation showing the conditional discriminator helps (Figure 4, top left) does not disentangle conditioning from encoding stability. While the empirical results suggest the method works in practice, this is a significant methodological gap. The paper should at minimum report training curves showing that both the FB loss and discriminator loss converge stably. *(The authors acknowledge in the Limitations that "a more formal and theoretical understanding of these components is still missing," but this specific coupled-dynamics issue is not addressed.)*

### Minor

1. **Planning baselines are trained on data that includes FB-CPR's replay buffer.** The action-labeled dataset used for DIFFUSER, H-GAP, and learned-model MPPI is constructed by combining the FB-CPR replay buffer with Goal-GAIL trajectories (line 167). This creates a confound: the planning methods' training data is partly generated by the very algorithm they are compared against. The paper does not control for this by using a strictly independent dataset. This weakness is partially mitigated by the fact that (a) the main comparison is to OracleMPPI (which uses direct dynamics access, not the dataset), and (b) the paper is transparent about the data construction. However, it weakens the DIFFUSER comparison.

2. **Mixture proportions for \(\nu\) (latent distribution) are not reported or ablated.** The paper introduces \(\nu\) as a mixture of three components (ER_FB-encoded trajectories, goal-reaching states, and uniform) to balance exploration and exploitation, but neither the mixture weights nor any ablation on this choice is reported. This is a potentially important design parameter that is left unspecified.

3. **Best scaling configuration not included in main results.** The scaling experiments (Figure 4) show that the 2× model with 100% data significantly outperforms the default 1× model reported in Table 1. The paper mentions this qualitatively but does not report the scaled model's performance in the headline comparison. This makes the scaling results feel like an afterthought rather than a core finding.

4. **Training computational cost not reported.** The paper provides inference time (12 seconds per episode) but does not report total training time or GPU-hours for FB-CPR vs. baselines. This information is important for practitioners assessing the method's practicality.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic highlights an important structural issue: the circular dependency between the ER_FB embedding (computed from the concurrently learned \(B\)) and the discriminator's target distribution. This is a genuinely novel observation about FB-CPR's training dynamics that the paper does not discuss. The strength finder's observation about the ablations being systematic is well-taken, but the key insight is that the most important ablation — setting \(\alpha = 0\) to isolate the contribution of the FB unsupervised term from the regularization — is partially addressed (the top-right ablation removes \(F^\top z\)) but not fully (removing the discriminator entirely). This is a small gap in an otherwise thorough evaluation. None beyond the paper's own contributions — the synthesised observation about the non-stationarity issue is worth noting but the reviewer flagged it, not a novel synthesis from the reviews.

## Suggestions

2. Include an ablation removing the discriminator regularization entirely (\(\alpha = 0\)) to measure the standalone contribution of FB unsupervised learning vs. the conditional policy regularization.
3. Report the scaling study's 2× model results in the main comparison table, or explain why the 1× model was chosen for the headline results.
4. Report the mixture proportions used for \(\nu\) and ideally ablate this choice.
