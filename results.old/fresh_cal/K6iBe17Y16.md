## Summary

This paper studies supervised heuristic learning for forward search from a statistical perspective. It argues that (1) admissible heuristics (e.g., lmcut) should *not* be used as training targets (since a learned copy of a poly-time heuristic adds no value), but instead as **lower bounds** of a **Truncated Gaussian** (TG) distribution modeling the optimal cost \(h^*\); (2) the commonly used MSE loss implicitly assumes an untruncated Gaussian, which ignores known bounds; and (3) the resulting TG negative log-likelihood loss yields faster convergence and more accurate heuristics than MSE. Experiments on four classical planning domains with Neural Logic Machines (NLM) show that TG-based heuristics achieve lower test MSE and better GBFS planning coverage than Gaussian/MSE counterparts.

## Strengths

1. **Principled theoretical grounding.** The paper derives the TG loss from first principles (maximum entropy), showing that when a finite mean, finite variance, and bounded support \((l,u)\) are the known constraints, the max-ent distribution is the Truncated Gaussian (Section 3, citing Dowson & Wragg 1973). This provides a formal justification for moving beyond the ad-hoc MSE loss — a step absent from most prior heuristic learning work.

2. **Empirically demonstrated accuracy gains.** Table 1 shows TG models consistently achieve lower test MSE than Gaussian models. The gain is dramatic in several configurations (e.g., ferry/learn/none: Gaussian MSE 141.05 vs. TG MSE 8.63). The "clip" post-processing trick on Gaussian predictions partially closes the gap but rarely matches TG, confirming that principled modeling — not just bound enforcement — drives the improvement.

3. **Faster convergence.** Figure 2 plots validation MSE over training steps (5 seeds), showing that TG models converge substantially faster than Gaussian models. This is a concrete, visible advantage that directly supports the claim that the TG loss is more effective for learning.

4. **Insightful ablation on learned vs. fixed σ.** Table 1 and the accompanying analysis (lines 635–649) show that learning σ improves TG in 7/8 comparisons while it hurts Gaussian in 5/8. This is a non-trivial finding: the TG distribution genuinely leverages the additional parameter because σ affects the truncated mean \(\mathbb{E}[x]\), whereas for the Gaussian, \(\mathbb{E}[x] = \mu\) is independent of σ.

5. **Novel use of formally derived admissible bounds.** The paper distinguishes its contribution from prior work where TG was used with *arbitrary* constraints (deadlines, signal ranges) by being the first to combine TG with *automatically proved* admissible heuristics from symbolic planning (lmcut). This is a clean connection between the planning and ML-statistics literatures.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The paper's core claims are supported by sound theoretical reasoning and consistent experimental evidence. The weaknesses below are substantive but do not threaten the central thesis.

### Minor

1. **Narrow domain coverage limits generalizability.** Experiments span only four classical planning domains (blocksworld-4ops, ferry, gripper, visitall). These are among the simpler IPC benchmarks, and the claim that TG "consistently" outperforms Gaussian rests on this limited set. While the results are consistent across configurations within these domains, the absence of more challenging domains (e.g., logistics, miconic, or domains with dead-ends or continuous features) makes it unclear whether the advantage persists when the lower bound (lmcut) is weaker or plan costs are harder to learn. The paper's contribution is *evidentially bounded* rather than structurally flawed, but broader validation is needed to support the claim of general effectiveness.

2. **Only NLM results appear in the main text.** The paper states that conclusions for HGN and linear regression models were "not substantially different" (lines 601–604), but the actual numbers are not shown in the main body. The main narrative relies on the reader accepting this claim on trust or consulting the appendix. For a core empirical claim (TG advantage generalizes across architectures), reporting all results — even in a compact form — would strengthen confidence. *(Note: this does not imply the appendix is missing; it means the main text should be self-contained on this point.)*

3. **Model selection via validation MSE may systematically disadvantage TG.** Both Gaussian and TG models are saved by minimizing validation MSE (line 521). For the Gaussian with fixed σ, the NLL reduces to MSE up to a constant, so validation MSE is well-aligned with training. For the TG, the training objective (NLL) differs from the selection metric (MSE), which may select checkpoints with lower MSE at the expense of better search guidance. The paper does not discuss this potential bias. (That TG still outperforms despite this possible disadvantage is *suggestive* of robustness, but the issue should be acknowledged.)

4. **No statistical significance testing for planning coverage.** Table 2 reports means and standard deviations across 5 seeds for coverage proportions, but coverage is a binomial outcome and the paper offers no paired significance test (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank or McNemar). The consistent trends (TG ≥ G in learn/ff across all four domains) are visually convincing, but a proper test would strengthen the claim that the advantage is systematic rather than driven by one or two seeds.

### Trivial

1. **Equation (8) numerical stability.** The paper correctly warns that a naive implementation of the TG mean (Eq. 8) suffers from rounding errors (line 399), but does not provide or reference a numerically stable implementation (e.g., log-space or the scaled complementary log-log approach). This is a minor practical omission for implementers.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation on lower bound quality.** All experiments use lmcut as the lower bound. Varying the bound tightness (e.g., blind vs. hmax vs. lmcut) would clarify how much of the TG advantage depends on the quality of the admissible bound itself, versus the distributional modeling.
- **Robust implementation note for Eq. (8).** Providing a numerically stable formula (or citation) for the TG mean would improve reproducibility.
- **Earlier domains.** Adding 2–3 more varied IPC domains would substantiate the generality claim — a natural direction for future work as the paper notes.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's observation about **validation MSE selection potentially biasing against TG** is the most insightful point that does not appear in the paper itself. It identifies a subtle experimental-design asymmetry: the Gaussian's training loss (fixed-σ NLL) is equivalent to MSE, making the validation metric perfectly aligned, whereas the TG's training loss is different, so using MSE as the selection criterion may systematically pick suboptimal TG checkpoints. The fact that TG still outperforms despite this possible handicap *underscores* the method's strength, but the authors should address this in a rebuttal. The strength finder's observation that the learning-σ ablation (7/8 improve for TG vs. 5/8 degrade for G) is a genuinely non-trivial insight that goes beyond the paper's headline claim.

## Suggestions

- Add a supplementary table or compact summary (even one sentence with the trend) showing HGN and linear results in the main body, rather than relying on the claim of "not substantially different."
- Acknowledge and briefly discuss the potential bias from using validation MSE as the selection criterion for TG models, and consider whether using validation NLL would change the conclusions (even a brief ablation would suffice).
- Include a statistically robust test (e.g., Wilcoxon signed-rank) for the planning coverage results in Table 2.
- Provide a numerically stable implementation reference for the TG mean formula (Eq. 8).

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a genuine contribution: it provides a theoretically grounded loss function for heuristic learning that demonstrably outperforms the standard MSE approach. The weaknesses are bounded (domain scope, reporting choices) and do not undermine the core claims. The theoretical derivation is sound, the experiments are well-designed within their stated scope, and the writing is clear. This is a solid paper that raises the bar for how the community should think about training targets and loss functions in learned planning heuristics.
