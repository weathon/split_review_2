## Summary

The paper presents Musketeer, a vision-language model with fully-shared parameters trained jointly on multiple heterogeneous tasks (visual grounding, visual entailment, VQA, captioning, classification, detection, summarization). The key innovation is the Task Explanation Prompt (TEP), a structured textual prompt with five components (Data Description, I/O Format, Output Description, Instance Prompt) that reduces task interference by explicitly describing each task's input/output spaces in natural language. Experiments across three data scales and two model sizes show that TEP consistently outperforms one-hot and base prompts, and that the resulting multi-task model achieves results competitive with or better than OFA specialist models on most tasks, while substantially outperforming prior unified multi-task models (Unified-IO, UniTAB, Uni-Perceiver) with far fewer parameters.

## Strengths

- **Single jointly-trained model matches or surpasses OFA specialists on most tasks while supporting multi-tasking (Table 1).** Musketeer Large on Subset$_{\text{caption}}$ outperforms OFA Large on all three visual grounding splits (90.8/93.1/87.6 vs. 90.1/92.9/85.3), matches on VE test (90.2 vs. 90.2), and slightly trails on VE dev (89.9 vs. 90.3) and caption CIDEr (140.2 vs. 142.2). Crucially, Musketeer achieves this with a single model that performs all tasks without separate heads or task-specific fine-tuning.

- **TEP enables positive transfer as task count increases, unlike baseline prompts (Table 7).** Adding more tasks (1→3→5→7) under TEP consistently improves visual grounding val (86.4→86.0→86.3→87.5), while BaseP degrades sharply (88.6→86.7→86.3→85.8). This provides direct evidence that TEP's structured descriptions allow the model to benefit from additional tasks rather than suffer from increased interference.

- **Musketeer substantially outperforms existing unified multi-task models with fewer parameters (Table 4).** Musketeer Base (182M) achieves 40.9 B@4 and 137.2 CIDEr on caption vs. Unified-IO Base (241M) at 126.3 CIDEr (XLarge, 2925M), and 72.0 VQA (using only 44% of VQA data) vs. UniTAB's 69.1. This demonstrates that TEP-based training yields better task integration than prior fully-shared architectures.

- **Ablation confirms every TEP subprompt contributes (Table 6).** Removing any component degrades performance — e.g., removing I/O Format drops VG val from 87.5 to 86.7, and removing the Instance Prompt drops caption CIDEr from 130.3 to 126.7. This confirms the structured multi-component design is responsible for the gains, not just prompt length.

- **Consistent TEP advantage across diverse settings.** TEP outperforms baselines across 7 tasks, 3 data scales, 2 model sizes, and even in few-shot (32/100 samples) and zero-shot transfer to unseen tasks/datasets — demonstrating robustness beyond the main comparison.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

### Major
None. The paper's core claims are well-supported by the evidence. The issues below are bounded and addressable.

### Minor

- **The "almost uniformly" claim slightly overstates the caption gap.** The paper claims "comparable to or better... almost uniformly across multiple tasks." In Table 1, the caption CIDEr gap on Musketeer Large vs. OFA Large is 140.2 vs. 142.2 (1.4% lower). The paper partially acknowledges this (noting the absence of a separate CIDEr optimization stage), but does not similarly discuss the VE dev gap (89.9 vs. 90.3). The claim would be more precise as "competitive with specialists, outperforming on some tasks and falling slightly short on others." This is a framing issue, not an evidential one.

- **Single-run results without variance estimates.** No standard deviations or multi-run averages are reported. For large-scale benchmarks this is common practice (OFA itself does not report error bars), but it limits the reader's ability to assess whether smaller reported improvements (e.g., 0.1–0.5 points on certain metrics in Table 5) are meaningful. The paper would benefit from at least a statement confirming trends are stable across runs.

- **No comparison to learned soft prompts or prefix-tuning baselines.** The related work discusses soft prompts (Li & Liang, 2021; Zhong et al., 2022), but the experiments compare TEP only to one-hot vectors and BaseP (a single sentence). A soft-prompt baseline would clarify whether TEP's benefit comes from its *semantic content* or simply from having more tokens / a structured format. This is the most impactful missing experiment.

- **The TEP wording sensitivity is not explored.** TEP subprompts are generated by ChatGPT and human-verified, but there is no analysis of how much the specific phrasing matters. A simple variant with synonyms or restructured descriptions would strengthen the claim that the *structural information* (the five-component schema) drives gains rather than the exact ChatGPT-chosen words.

- **BaseP-1 vs. TEP-1 performance difference is not discussed.** Table 7 shows BaseP with a single task (88.6 VG val) substantially outperforms TEP with a single task (86.4 VG val). This implies TEP's extra structure hurts when there is no interference to manage. The paper highlights the beneficial trend as tasks increase but does not acknowledge this starting-point disadvantage, which would be informative for understanding TEP's trade-offs.

- **Zero-shot results on unseen tasks are framed as a success but lack absolute performance context.** TEP achieves 49.1% on zero-shot VE (vs. BaseP's 38.6%), which is a 27% relative improvement. However, 49.1% is only modestly above chance for a 3-way classification task (33.3%), and far below the fully-supervised 89+%. The paper correctly focuses on the relative comparison but could add a caveat about absolute capability to prevent over-interpretation.

### Trivial

- **Latency claim is unsupported.** The conclusion mentions "around 10%-15% extra latency compared to specialist OFA" without citing a measurement or experiment. This should be quantified or moved to a limitations paragraph with appropriate hedging.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Direct analysis of interference reduction.** The paper relies on downstream metrics to argue that TEP reduces task interference. A more direct analysis — e.g., measuring gradient cosine similarity between tasks with and without TEP, or showing reduced cross-task variance in feature activations — would strengthen the mechanistic claim.
- **A brief report of detection performance.** The paper states OFA-based models have low detection baselines and thus omits detection from main results. Even a footnote or supplementary entry with numbers would improve transparency.
