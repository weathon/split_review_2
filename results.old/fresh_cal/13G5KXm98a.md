## Summary

This paper introduces a Voronoi tessellation-based confidence decision boundary visualization for active learning. The method decomposes decision boundaries into predicted ridges, each evaluated with a novel ridge confidence metric ($C_{\text{pred}} = 1 - \sum_k P(\hat{y}_i=k)P(\hat{y}_j=k)$) that differentiates informative from uniform boundary sections. Three complementary visualization types (Confidence Decision Boundary, Cumulative Sampling, Error Detection) are designed to capture cumulative AL dynamics across rounds. The approach is demonstrated on MNIST and CIFAR-10, analyzing eight AL query strategies.

## Strengths

- **Novel ridge confidence metric (Eq.~2) with quantitative validation (Figure~4).** The metric explicitly quantifies boundary-section informativeness, addressing the prior limitation of treating all boundary sections uniformly (Section~2.2). Figure~4 provides direct evidence: predicted ridge accuracy ($A_{\text{ridge}} = |\mathcal{B}_{\text{pred}} \cap \mathcal{B}_{\text{gt}}| / |\mathcal{B}_{\text{pred}}|$) improves monotonically with higher confidence intervals and across training rounds, demonstrating that the metric meaningfully captures boundary certainty and tracks model progress.

- **Three complementary visualization types for cumulative AL dynamics.** The Confidence Decision Boundary, Cumulative Sampling over 5 Rounds, and Error Detection views (Figure~5) capture multi-round effects rather than single-stage snapshots. The Error Detection visualization in particular (blue/green/purple regions tracking persistent, corrected, and new errors across rounds) addresses a genuine gap in prior AL visualization work, which mostly shows one round at a time.

- **Multi-class Voronoi decomposition.** The method handles 10-class tasks (MNIST, CIFAR-10) directly, overcoming the limitation of prior Voronoi-based work (Migut et al., 2015) that reduced multi-class to binary classifiers. This is a concrete technical advance that makes the method applicable to realistic AL scenarios.

- **Quantitative validation of the core metric grounds the qualitative analysis.** Unlike purely descriptive visualization papers, this work validates its confidence metric objectively (Figure~4), providing a foundation for the subsequent qualitative observations about strategy behavior.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Single-run experiments without statistical grounding for behavioral claims.** The AL experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 appear to be single runs with no seeds, confidence intervals, or variance reported. AL is stochastic (model training, dropout, and initial sample selection all introduce randomness). The paper's qualitative claims in Section~4 — "entropy sampling frequently engages in high-risk boundary-crossing sampling," "BALD dropout distributes sampling more broadly," "the proportion of noise data points with high uncertainty tends to increase" — are presented as general findings about strategy behavior but could be artifacts of a single run. This is the most significant limitation because the paper's title and conclusion position the visualization as a tool for *understanding* AL strategies, yet the behavioral observations lack the experimental replication needed to be reliable.

2. **Visualization model uses full labeled data while AL models use sparse labels.** Section~3.3 trains a separate visualization model (same architecture) on the *entire pool dataset with ground-truth labels* to obtain a "fixed 2D feature distribution" for cross-strategy comparison. The AL models being analyzed only see 1.22% of the pool. The paper acknowledges this gap and mentions two validation tests (Correlation Test, Local Structure Preservation) but **reports no results** from either. Without these numbers, it is unclear whether the 2D Voronoi boundaries shown reflect the AL model's actual decision regions or an idealized projection. This undermines the central claim that the visualization reveals the AL model's behavior.

3. **No comparison against existing AL visualizations.** The paper criticizes prior visualizations (scatter plots, iso-contours) as inadequate but provides no side-by-side comparison, user study, or quantitative metric demonstrating that the proposed method is more informative. Without evidence that the Voronoi confidence coloring surfaces insights that simpler methods miss, the practical value of the contribution is asserted rather than demonstrated. This is particularly important because many of the "insights" in Section~4 (e.g., "entropy sampling targets points near the decision boundary") are already well-established in the AL literature — the paper should show what *new* understanding the visualization uniquely enables.

### Minor

1. **Initial labeled set overlap across strategies unspecified.** The paper states AL begins with 10 labeled samples (Section~3.1) but does not specify whether the same 10 samples initialize all strategies. If each strategy draws an independent initial set, early-round visual differences could stem from initialization rather than strategy behavior, making comparisons like "entropy does more boundary-crossing than BALD" in early rounds (Section~4.1) uninterpretable.

2. **Qualitative observations lack quantitative anchors.** Several key claims in Section~4 are based on visual inspection without quantification: "nine ambiguous regions" (line~152), "high-risk boundary-crossing" (line~154), "proportion of these two types of uncertainty changes" (line~182). For a paper whose central contribution is a visualization tool, these observational claims would be strengthened by even simple quantitative measures (e.g., fraction of selected points near high-confidence ridges vs. interior).

3. **Color description inconsistency.** Figure~3 caption states "red lines represent high confidence values" (line~100) while Figure~5's description says "blue ridges are considered high-confidence predicted ridges" (line~152). Both refer to confidence decision boundaries. If these use different colormaps, this should be explained; if both are the same visualization type, the inconsistency is confusing.

4. **Missing validation results for dimensionality reduction evaluations.** The paper mentions conducting "Correlation Test" and "Local Structure Preservation" to validate t-SNE for Voronoi diagrams (Section~3.3) but never reports the outcomes. Even a brief summary or pointer to an appendix would be needed to support the claim that spatial distortion is acceptable.

## Suggestions

1. **Run multiple seeds (≥5) for each AL strategy** and present the key observed patterns (boundary-crossing density, ridge alignment, error correction patterns) as aggregate statistics rather than single-instance examples. Even a qualitative statement like "this pattern appeared in 4/5 runs" would significantly strengthen the claims.
2. **Report the Correlation Test and Local Structure Preservation results** that are already mentioned in Section~3.3. These are the paper's own validity checks and leaving the results out is an obvious gap.
3. **Add a controlled-initialization experiment** where all strategies share the same 10 initial labeled samples for at least one comparison set, to factor out initialization effects.
4. **Provide a direct comparison** between the proposed Voronoi confidence view and a standard scatter plot (color-coded by uncertainty or predicted label) on the same data, highlighting at least one insight visible only in the Voronoi view.
5. **Quantify key observations** from Section~4 (e.g., "X% of entropy-sampled points land within 1 Voronoi cell of a high-confidence ridge vs. Y% for BALD") to convert visual claims into testable findings.

## Score and Decision

**Originality:** Moderate-high. The ridge confidence metric and multi-class Voronoi visualization for AL are novel, building on prior Voronoi-based work (Migut et al., 2015) while addressing its multi-class limitation.

**Importance of research question:** High. Understanding why AL strategies behave differently is an important open problem, and better visual diagnostics would benefit the community.

**Claims supported:** Partially. The core metric is well-validated (Figure~4). The behavioral insights (Section~4) are plausible but based on single-run qualitative observations without controlled initialization.

**Soundness of experiments:** Moderate. The technical validation of the confidence metric is sound, but the AL strategy analysis lacks statistical rigor and missing validation results weaken the overall experimental narrative.

**Clarity of writing:** Good. The method and visualizations are clearly described. The color inconsistency (red vs. blue for high confidence) is a minor presentation issue.

**Value to community:** Moderate. The visualization method and metric could be useful diagnostic tools, but the paper would benefit from stronger experimental validation to demonstrate practical value.

The paper presents a genuinely novel visualization approach with a well-motivated quantitative metric. The technical core is solid. However, the qualitative AL behavioral analysis — which forms much of the paper's experiential content — rests on single-run experiments with uncontrolled initialization and no comparison against existing methods. These are addressable issues that do not invalidate the method itself.
