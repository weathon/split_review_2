## Summary

This paper introduces ProxyBench, a benchmark for evaluating the robustness of neural-network proxy reward models under optimization pressure from best-of-n (BoN), reinforcement learning (RL), and white-box (WB) adversarial attacks. Key findings include: (1) instance-level proxy gaming persists even when aggregate proxy-gold reward correlation appears high, with >45% of gamed examples shared across independently trained proxies, (2) larger proxy models are harder to game while larger policies and more optimization exposure worsen gaming, and (3) standard adversarial robustness techniques (adversarial training, SAM) offer only limited gains, helping only in the white-box setting where generations are nonsensical.

## Strengths

- **Instance-level gaming analysis beyond aggregate metrics (Sections 4.4, 5.1).** The paper introduces an RMS divergence metric and crucially shows via distributional analysis that >45% of gamed examples overlap across separately trained proxy models of different sizes. A hypothesis test rejects random noise (p ≈ 0), demonstrating that gaming is a systematic, reproducible vulnerability that aggregate reward curves conceal.

- **Systematic scaling experiments isolating proxy size vs. policy size vs. optimization exposure (Section 5.2, Figure 5, Table 1).** The finding that larger proxy models *monotonically* improve gold reward and reduce RMS across all three optimizers, while larger policies worsen gaming, is concrete and counter-intuitive. This provides a clear empirical anchor for future defense research.

- **Open-source benchmark release (Section 4).** The paper releases ProxyBench including gold reward models, training data, evaluation metrics, and code for all three optimization methods. Prior work (Gao et al., 2022) released neither data nor models; this enables direct comparisons and reproducible progress.

- **Characterization of phase shifts as learned exploits (Section 5.4, Figure 6).** The discovery that reward curves can undergo abrupt divergence (e.g., the policy learning to generate "tips" instead of answering the question) extends Gao et al.'s functional-form analysis by showing that gaming can emerge as a discrete exploit rather than gradual overoptimization.

- **Negative results on existing defense methods (Section 5.5, Table 3).** The finding that adversarial training helps only against white-box attacks (where generated text is gibberish) and SAM provides no benefit is valuable for the community. It establishes that existing robustness techniques are insufficient and that new approaches are needed.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **No error bars or multiple-seed results for any experiment.** The paper reports point estimates in Tables 1 and 3 without any measure of variance. RL experiments (PPO) are highly variable across seeds; the lack of error bars makes it impossible to assess whether reported differences (e.g., adversarial training *reducing* gold reward for BoN/RL versus simply being within noise) are meaningful. Given that several claims involve fine-grained comparisons, this weakens the reliability of those conclusions. This is the single most consequential fixable issue.

### Minor

- **Abstract overclaims relative to the evidence.** The abstract states that "proxy robustness is a solvable problem that can be incrementally improved" and "demonstrate[s] the potential of adversarial training to improve alignment." In reality, adversarial training helps only against white-box attacks on gibberish text and reduces gold reward for BoN/RL; SAM provides no benefit; and scaling (the one effective defense) is a passive property rather than an algorithmic solution. The conclusion (Section 6) is appropriately measured. The abstract's phrasing sets an expectation the results do not meet and should be adjusted.

- **RMS divergence metric is used centrally but not validated against instance-level gaming.** The paper defines RMS (Section 4.4) and uses it in multiple analyses, but never demonstrates that RMS improvements correspond to fewer gamed examples or that the metric's sensitivity to smoothing/derivative estimation choices is stable. The paper *does* use additional metrics (gamed subset count, correlation, MAE) that appear consistent, which partially mitigates this concern. However, a direct validation against the per-example gaming measure (used in Section 5.1) would strengthen confidence in results that rely on RMS alone, particularly for white-box experiments where gold reward is omitted.

- **No hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.** Key choices (KL penalty = 0.01 for RL, 200 attack steps with LR = 0.5 for white-box, smoothing window = 10% for RMS) are stated without justification or sensitivity checks. While this is common in benchmark papers, the dependence of results on these choices is unknown.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Additional baseline defenses.** The paper tests only adversarial training and SAM. Even simple baselines (e.g., explicit regularization, proxy ensembles, uncertainty-weighted optimization) would provide a stronger anchor and better support the claim that existing methods are insufficient.
- **Adversarial training diagnostics.** The paper speculates that adversarial perturbations during training may not match the distribution of policy-gamed examples (Section 5.5). Measuring overlap between gamed examples and adversarial training examples could deepen this analysis.
- **Small-scale human validation.** A limited human evaluation confirming that the gold model's rankings are not wildly divergent from actual human judgment on a subset of generated examples would strengthen the claim that proxy-to-gold robustness is relevant for real-world alignment. (The paper explicitly scopes this out; this is a suggestion for strengthening, not a flaw.)

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution:

- **"Gold model is itself a proxy, limiting external validity"** (Harsh Critic Critical Issue 1). The paper explicitly acknowledges this design choice in Section 4.1 ("This choice disregards how well the model captures the underlying objective of human raters, but ensures that our gold model is a valid representation of the true objective we are studying.") — it is a scoped, disclosed limitation, not a weakness of execution.
- **"Proxy gaming is not just overfitting nuance missing"** (Section 3 note). The paper uses the overfitting analogy with the qualifier "analogous to," not as a definition. The suggested nuance is an enhancement, not a flaw.
- **"Need human evaluation to validate gold model"** — Scope creep; the paper explicitly scopes out human-in-the-loop evaluation as a design choice.
- **"White-box results only comparable via RMS, which is unvalidated"** — Already subsumed under the RMS validation point above; not a separate weakness.
- **"More diverse optimization methods needed"** — The three methods (BoN, RL, WB) cover a reasonable and principled range.

## Novel Insights

The reviewers surface an important tension the paper does not fully address: the paper uses an ensemble of human-preference-trained models as "gold" and measures proxy-to-gold robustness, but the gap between this gold model and actual human values remains uncalibrated. This is not a flaw in the paper's execution — it is an inherent limitation of any benchmark that replaces humans with a learned reference. The more interesting observation is that the paper's negative results (adversarial training fails outside white-box) and positive scaling results (larger proxies help) together suggest that proxy gaming may be more about the *capacity* of the proxy to faithfully represent the reward landscape than about learning algorithm choices. This interpretation is not articulated in the paper but emerges from the review synthesis: if larger proxies monotonically reduce gaming across all optimizer types, the bottleneck may be representational rather than algorithmic, which has implications for how the community should invest in defense research.

## Suggestions

1. **Add error bars.** Re-run key experiments (at minimum the central comparisons in Tables 1 and 3) with multiple seeds and report means ± standard deviations. This is critical for RL experiments where variance is high.
2. **Adjust the abstract's tone.** Replace "proxy robustness is a solvable problem" with a more appropriate framing such as "our results characterize the scope of the problem and show that existing defense methods are insufficient, motivating new approaches."
3. **Validate RMS against instance-level gaming.** Show that RMS improvements correlate with a reduced fraction of gamed examples (as defined in Section 5.1) on a held-out set, and analyze sensitivity to the smoothing window and derivative estimation.
4. **Add hyperparameter sensitivity discussion** for the KL penalty, attack steps, and smoothing window, even if only as a brief note on why these values were chosen and whether results are robust to modest variations.
