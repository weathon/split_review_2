## Summary

ORSO frames the selection of shaping reward functions in RL as an online model selection problem. It uses bandit algorithms (especially D³RB) to allocate training iterations across candidate rewards generated by an LLM, provides a refined regret bound under a monotonicity assumption, and evaluates on six Isaac Gym continuous control tasks. The central claim is that ORSO reaches human-level performance roughly 2× faster than a naive uniform-allocation baseline and can match or surpass human-engineered rewards.

---

## Strengths

1. **Refined regret bound under monotonicity (Lemma 4.4).** Under Assumption 4.2, the paper proves a tighter regret guarantee for ORSO with D³RB than the general D³RB analysis of Dann et al. (2024). The bound depends on the true regret coefficient \(d_T^{i_\star}\) rather than the worst-so-far \(\bar{d}_T^{i_\star}\), meaning that even if the optimal learner has a slow start, ORSO can recover and achieve regret comparable to running that learner alone. This is a concrete, specific theoretical improvement — lines 117–127.

2. **Empirical speedup over the naive baseline at equal budget.** Figure 2 (left) aggregates 162 runs (6 tasks × 3 budgets × 3 reward sets × 3 seeds) and shows ORSO (D³RB) reaching human-level reward-normalized performance in roughly half the normalized iterations required by the Naive Selection baseline. Critically, **this comparison uses the same total budget \(B \times n_{\text{iters}}\) for both methods** (line 171), so the speedup is not an artifact of unequal compute. The result directly supports the paper's core claim that online selection improves allocation efficiency.

3. **Effectiveness with large candidate sets.** Figure 6 evaluates ORSO on the ANT task with \(K=48\) and \(K=96\) reward candidates. D³RB consistently finds an effective reward function while greedier methods (ε-greedy, UCB, ETC) and more exploratory methods (Exp3, uniform) on average fail to match the human-designed baseline. This demonstrates scalability to practically relevant sizes where exhaustive evaluation is prohibitive.

4. **GPU-time reduction via algorithmic efficiency.** Figure 3 shows that ORSO on a single GPU achieves the same wall-clock time to human-level performance as the Naive Selection strategy running on 16 parallel GPUs. While the time estimates are approximate (acknowledged in line 179), the trend demonstrates that the algorithmic savings translate into meaningful hardware cost reductions.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Unequal training budgets undermine the "surpasses human" claim.**  
   The Human baseline is trained for \(n_{\text{iters}}\) iterations on one engineered reward. ORSO is run for \(B \times n_{\text{iters}}\) total iterations (\(B \in \{5,10,15\}\), line 165), allocating these across \(K\) candidates. When Figure 2 (middle) shows ORSO "surpassing human-designed reward functions," this averages settings where ORSO has 5–15× the *total* training iterations. Because more training often yields better policies regardless of reward quality, this comparison conflates the benefit of the selection algorithm with the benefit of additional compute. The paper should either (a) compare at equal total iterations (select a reward, then train from scratch for \(n_{\text{iters}}\)), or (b) clearly separate the compute spent on selection from the compute used to produce the final policy. The primary speedup claim (Figure 2 left) is not affected by this issue because it compares ORSO and Naive Selection at the same budget — but the "surpasses human" framing in Figure 2 middle and the conclusion is misleading as presented.

2. **Strong, unverified theoretical assumption (Assumption 4.2).**  
   Assumption 4.2 requires a learner \(i_\star\) whose *cumulative* expected reward dominates all other learners at *every* time step, and whose average performance is non-decreasing. This rules out natural scenarios where the best reward function in hindsight performs poorly early in training but overtakes rivals later — a common pattern in RL (initial policies on complex shaping rewards may be random). The paper provides no justification that this assumption holds in the reward-design setting, nor does it discuss the regret behavior when it fails. The theoretical improvement in Lemma 4.4 is conditional on this assumption, and the paper's theoretical contribution rests on it. A discussion of when the assumption approximately holds, or an empirical analysis of regret under violations, would substantially strengthen the work.

3. **The Naive Selection baseline is not a state-of-the-art competitor.**  
   The paper claims to use EUREKA (Ma et al., 2024) but describes it as performing "uniform exploration" (line 155–156), which is a simplification — actual EUREKA uses LLM-based iterative evolution. More importantly, even if implemented faithfully, this baseline simply allocates compute uniformly across candidates without any intelligent selection. The paper does not compare against standard resource-allocation methods such as Successive Halving, Hyperband, or Bayesian optimization over reward weights, which are natural competitors for this problem. While the simple baseline cleanly isolates the benefit of online selection, the paper's framing overstates the significance of a 2× speedup against it. The contribution would be better contextualized by including at least one competitive resource-allocation baseline.

### Minor

1. **No per-task results in the main paper.** All results are heavily aggregated across 6 tasks, 3 budgets, and 3 reward sets (Figure 2). The reader cannot determine whether ORSO works well on all tasks or whether the aggregate is driven by a subset (e.g., simpler tasks like CartPole and BallBalance). Per-task tables or plots should be included.

2. **Low number of seeds.** Experiments use 3 seeds per configuration (line 171). For continuous control tasks with known training variance, 3 seeds is at the low end of standard practice. While confidence intervals are shown, the small seed count limits confidence in the aggregate conclusions.

3. **No comparison to successive halving or Hyperband.** Given that the problem is allocating a budget across candidates, these standard resource-allocation methods are natural baselines. Their absence limits the ability to assess whether the more sophisticated D³RB machinery is needed or whether simpler adaptive allocation would suffice.

4. **The GPU time comparison (Figure 3) is approximate.** The paper acknowledges this (line 179: "the plotted time is an approximation based on the time needed to complete one iteration of PPO"). The 16× claim should be interpreted with this caveat in mind.

### Trivial

None.
