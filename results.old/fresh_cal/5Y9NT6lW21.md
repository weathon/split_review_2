## Summary

This paper proposes Adversarial Preference-based Policy Optimization (APPO), a computationally tractable offline PbRL algorithm with provable sample complexity guarantees under general function approximation. The core technical novelty is a reparameterization (Definition 1, Equation 7) that transforms the intractable confidence-set optimization of prior offline PbRL methods into a two-player game amenable to standard optimization. Theorem 4.1 provides a sample complexity bound, and Section 5 describes a practical neural-network implementation.

## Strengths

- **First computationally tractable offline PbRL algorithm with provable sample complexity under general function approximation.** Theorem 4.1 gives a formal bound, and the algorithm (Algorithm 2) avoids the intractable confidence-set constructions required by prior work (FREEHAND-transition, Sim-OPRL). This is a genuine theoretical advance — see Section 4 and the remark on computational complexity in Section 3.3.

- **Novel reparameterization enabling offline-only optimization.** Definition 1 (induced reward model) and Equation (7) allow value-function optimization directly from offline data, eliminating the need for transition rollouts or a separate policy-evaluation oracle. The derivation from Equation (6) to Equation (7) via the performance difference lemma is the paper's central conceptual contribution.

- **Concrete practical loss functions bridging theory and neural-network implementation.** Section 5 provides specific training objectives (Equations 10–13) for Q-functions, V-functions, and the policy, incorporating clipped double Q-learning and target networks. This makes the theoretical framework implementable in modern deep RL pipelines.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Narrow experimental evaluation fails to support the claim of "practical applicability."** The paper tests on only one dataset (Meta-world medium-replay) from a single prior work (Choi et al., 2024), reporting results for six tasks. Baselines (MR, PT, DPPO, IPL) are not re-implemented — their numbers are taken verbatim from Choi et al. (2024), inheriting another paper's hyperparameter tuning, training setup, and random seeds. There is no experiment on any other domain (e.g., D4RL, Atari) and no ablation isolating the effect of the reparameterization. A method claiming to be "the first offline PbRL algorithm to offer both statistical efficiency and practical applicability" (Abstract) should demonstrate its effectiveness more broadly. The theoretical contribution is solid, but the experimental section as presented provides only thin support for the practical half of the claim.

- **Unexplained gap between theory and practice.** The theoretical analysis (Section 4, Theorem 4.1) is based on the reparameterized optimization in Equation (7), which uses a learned transition model \(\hat{P}\) to compute the induced reward. However, the practical implementation (Section 5, line 251) bypasses the transition model entirely by approximating \(P^*(Q_\phi \circ \pi_\theta)(s_l,a_l) \approx (Q_\phi \circ \pi_\theta)(s_{l+1})\). The paper does not analyze how errors from this approximation affect the theoretical guarantees, nor does it provide any empirical comparison between the practical approximation and a version that explicitly learns \(\hat{P}\). This disconnect between what is analyzed and what is implemented weakens the paper's claims.

### Minor

- **Surprising Oracle outperformance not discussed.** APPO is reported (Table 1, lines 291–295) to outperform the Oracle — a policy trained via IQL with ground-truth rewards — on dial-turn and button-press-topdown-wall tasks. It is unusual for a method using only preference feedback to beat a method with access to true rewards, yet the paper offers no analysis of why this occurs (e.g., whether the Oracle baseline is well-tuned for these tasks, or whether the success rate metric is noisy). This anomaly undermines confidence in the comparison.

- **Sample complexity bound has unfavorable scaling.** The required number of unlabeled trajectories \(N\) scales as \(R^4 H^5\) (line 212), which is substantially worse in \(H\) and \(R\) than prior bounds (e.g., Zhan et al., 2024a). The paper correctly notes that its bound avoids dependence on the potentially-exponential \(C_P\), but this trade-off deserves more careful discussion — particularly whether the \(H^5\) dependence makes the bound vacuous for problems of practical horizon.

- **Baseline numbers are borrowed, not re-implemented.** While using numbers from a prior paper is acceptable as a rough comparison, the evaluation lacks independent verification of baselines, and any differences in training conditions, random seeds, or environment specifics between the two papers could affect the comparison.

### Trivial

- The paper should more explicitly qualify its "first" claim (Abstract, line 4) to read "with general function approximation," since Zhu et al. (2023) already provides a tractable algorithm for the linear model case.
- The approximation \(P^*(Q \circ \pi) \approx (Q \circ \pi)(s_{l+1})\) (line 251) is stated without justification of why it is reasonable.
