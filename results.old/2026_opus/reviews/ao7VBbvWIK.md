## Summary
HASTE proposes a code-context retrieval/compression pipeline that combines AST-aware chunking, hybrid lexical+semantic retrieval (BM25 + embeddings fused via RRF), call-graph expansion, and token-budget pruning to produce compact, structurally coherent context for LLM-driven code edits. The paper claims up to 85% compression while maintaining high Judge Scores on a curated 6-file benchmark plus 12 SWE-PolyBench instances, with a single LLM (Gemini 1.5 Flash) as the underlying generator.

## Strengths
- **Concrete, modular pipeline specification.** Section 3 describes the Scanner, Chunker, Identifier Extraction, Payload Builder, Hybrid Ranker (RRF with k=60), call-graph expansion, and Exporter as distinct stages, giving a usable architectural template (Sections 3.1–3.4).
- **A concrete qualitative success on `test3.py`.** The judge justification (Section 5.1) attributes the LLM's correct complex-type-hint generation to HASTE's call-graph expansion pulling in a dependent class definition — a specific, mechanism-grounded illustration that AST-aware expansion can supply missing structural context.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The baselines defined in Section 4.1.3 are never actually run.** Section 4.1.3 explicitly enumerates IR-only retrieval, AST-only retrieval, and naïve truncation as comparison conditions, and RQ1 (Section 4) is framed as a comparison "compared to baseline methods." Yet Section 5 (Table 2, Figures 2–3) reports *only* HASTE's own Judge Scores. There is no head-to-head number against any of the three baselines anywhere in the body. Consequently, the central claims that (a) the hybrid design beats relevance-only or structure-only methods, (b) AST-bounded pruning preserves quality where token-level pruning would not (Section 2.2), and (c) "structure-agnostic pruning would likely lead to a catastrophic drop in performance at similar compression levels" (Section 5.2) are *asserted* rather than demonstrated. This is the exact experiment the paper says it is doing; its absence undermines the paper's case.

- **Two of three promised evaluation metrics are never reported.** Section 4.2 defines (1) LLM-as-Judge, (2) AST Fidelity, and (3) Hallucination Rate. Only the Judge Score appears in Section 5. The abstract explicitly claims HASTE "maintain[s] high structural fidelity" and is "reducing model-generated hallucinations" — but the body contains no AST Fidelity number and no Hallucination Rate number. Two of the three explicit contribution claims have no empirical support in the body of the paper.

### Major

- **The headline "85% compression" depends on a single file.** Of the six curated files in Table 2, five sit between 1.2× and 2.7× (17–63% reduction); only `test3.py` reaches 6.8× (~85%). That same file is also the lowest-scoring one (90 vs. 98–100). Stating "up to 85% code compression" in the abstract overstates a single-point extreme as average behavior, and the only datum supporting it is also the strongest quality regression observed.

- **RQ2's compression-vs-quality analysis (Pearson r = −0.97) rests on n=6 and is dominated by one outlier.** With six points and one clear extreme (`test3.py` at 6.8×, score 90), the correlation is mechanically anchored by that single point. More importantly, the result actually *undercuts* the paper's thesis: quality monotonically degrades with compression even within HASTE's outputs (Section 5.2). The paper's gloss — "HASTE successfully navigates the frontier of this trade-off" — needs a comparison curve to be meaningful, and no baseline curve exists.

- **The curated benchmark is generated by a HASTE component and graded on uniformly easy tasks.** Section 4.1.2 states that the curated tasks are produced by HASTE's own *Suggestion Generator*. The tasks Table 2 lists are all single-line cosmetic edits (add try/except, add type hints, add return type hints). Tasks the system itself proposes are exactly the tasks the system is best positioned to support, so the curated evaluation cannot establish external validity for RQ2 — it is partially circular and trivially scoped.

- **SWE-PolyBench evaluation is small and dominated by trivially-passable NOOP tasks.** Section 5.3 / Figure 3 report 12 instances; 7 are POLYBENCH-NOOP where the success criterion is producing a non-empty patch that does not alter functionality (e.g., adding a comment). Of the 5 non-NOOP instances, the scores are 95, 10, 10, 5, 0 — a 1/5 success rate on real edits. The paper's framing "a majority of the tested instances achieved perfect or near-perfect scores" is technically true only because NOOPs count. Twelve instances is also far below the standard scale of SWE-PolyBench evaluations.

- **The LLM judge is unspecified and uncalibrated.** Section 4.2.1 says only that "a general-purpose LLM" assigns 0–100 scores across correctness, readability, and instruction alignment. Identity of the judge, prompt template, anchoring of the score scale, inter-run agreement, and human-judge agreement are all unreported. Because the judge is the *only* metric actually reported, this is a serious evidential gap.

### Minor

- **Only one underlying LLM (Gemini 1.5 Flash) is evaluated** (Section 4.1.4). Even one additional generator would substantially sharpen the generality claims. Variance/standard error across the "three runs averaged" is not reported.

- **Several algorithmic details in Section 3 are left at "configurable" granularity.** The fusion weights between BM25 and semantic retrieval (only k=60 for RRF is given), the call-graph expansion depth, the encoder choice, and the precise AST-bounded pruning rule under aggressive compression are not specified. These are the points where HASTE's distinctness from a generic hybrid retriever would live.

- **Section 5.3 deflects PolyBench failures onto "initial suggestion" or "downstream LLM reasoning."** If HASTE is providing context to support correct edits, degraded outputs on harder tasks are part of HASTE's evaluation, not an external nuisance.

- **The entire evaluation is intra-file, while the "context bottleneck" the paper opens with bites hardest cross-file.** Cross-file analysis is named as future work (Section 6), but it is the motivating problem.

### Trivial
- The "Data Availability" note states the package "is currently available on the Python Package Index (PyPI) under the name 'HasteContext'." Naming a deployed package by its unique name plausibly compromises double-anonymous review.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Run the three baselines defined in Section 4.1.3 and present Pareto curves of compression vs. Judge Score rather than a single correlation. This is the experiment the paper already promises.
- Actually compute and report AST Fidelity and Hallucination Rate, since both are defined in Section 4.2 and directly map to the paper's framing claims.
- Expand SWE-PolyBench beyond 12 instances, and break results out by NOOP vs. functional-edit tasks.
- Ablate the components distinguishing HASTE from a generic hybrid retriever: call-graph expansion on/off, AST-bounded pruning on/off, RRF vs. naïve interleaving.
- Specify the judge LLM, the judge prompt, and ideally a human-agreement spot-check.
- Replace or augment the self-generated *Suggestion Generator* tasks with externally sourced tasks.
