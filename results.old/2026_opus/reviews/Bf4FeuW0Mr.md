## Summary
DemoGrasp reformulates universal dexterous grasping as single-step demonstration editing: an RL policy outputs an SE(3) wrist transform and delta hand-grasp pose at t=0, the edited demonstration is replayed open-loop in simulation, and the episode returns a single scalar reward (binary success × no-table-collision). A flow-matching vision policy is then distilled from rendered rollouts for zero-shot sim-to-real. The method achieves SOTA on DexGraspNet (95.2%/94.4% train/unseen with Shadow Hand), generalizes across six embodiments trained on only 175 objects, and grasps 110 unseen real-world objects (86.5%, including 71.1% on small/thin items).

## Strengths
- **Novel and compact problem reformulation.** Reducing universal dexterous grasping to single-step editing of a known demonstration trajectory (Section 2.3, Eqs. 1–2) genuinely changes the RL exploration problem: the action space is small and the reward becomes a simple binary success × no-collision product (Eq. 3), removing the need for the dense reward shaping and curricula used by UniDexGrasp/ResDex/UniGraspTransformer.
- **Strong, head-to-head simulation results.** Table 1 shows DemoGrasp surpasses UniGraspTransformer by ~4–6 pp across train/test seen/unseen on DexGraspNet, *while also* training under a 50 cm × 50 cm spatial randomization regime that baselines do not use (Section 3.2).
- **Cross-embodiment generality with no per-hand tuning.** Trained on 175 objects, the same recipe yields >90% training success on all five-fingered hands and 84.6% average across six unseen test datasets (Section 3.3, Table 10), and head-to-head beats RobustDexGrasp on 4/5 unseen datasets (Table 2).
- **Sim-to-real evaluation on a meaningful object set.** Real-world success of 95.3% on normal objects and 71.1% on small/flat/thin objects (Table 3) — a regime that prior tabletop dexterous methods explicitly struggle with — substantiates the practical reach of the method.
- **Demonstration-quality ablation is unusually informative.** Table 9 shows raw demo replay ranges from 3.9% to 75.3% depending on which demonstration is used, yet the trained policy lands at 95–96% training and 81–83% test for *every* demonstration. This is concrete evidence that the framework is genuinely learning to edit, not memorizing.
- **The collision-half-randomization trick (Section 2.3) is clever and reusable.** Disabling robot-table collision in half of parallel envs gives a graded expected reward (1 / 0.5 / 0) that lets the policy use minimal contact for flat objects without otherwise abandoning collision-free behavior.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The plain-replay baseline does a substantial share of the work, and the framing under-weights this.** Table 8 row 1 shows that simply replaying the original demonstration — no RL at all — already achieves 75.3% training / 73.4% test. Full RL editing raises this to 96.2% / 82.7%. So on the test set, RL contributes ~9 pp on top of the demonstration-editing parameterization itself. The abstract and contributions attribute the gains to "RL with a simple reward," but Table 8 suggests the right parameterization is doing much of the lifting, and RL is the right optimizer over it. This does not invalidate the contribution, but the framing should better acknowledge it.
- **The "closed-loop" claim of the deployed vision policy is asserted, not demonstrated.** Section 3.4 states the deployed vision policy "exhibits regrasp behaviors to recover from failures in a closed-loop manner," but the imitation dataset is generated by open-loop replays of *successful* RL rollouts. The mechanism by which reactive recovery emerges in distillation deserves an explicit experiment (e.g., perturbing objects mid-trial, or measuring how often the deployed policy's actions deviate from the open-loop replay). As written, the claim rests on qualitative video evidence.

### Minor
- **The DexGraspNet comparison protocol is not strictly apples-to-apples.** DemoGrasp trains/tests with 50 cm × 50 cm randomization while baselines do not (Section 3.2). The paper argues this is the *harder* setting for DemoGrasp because of replay translation invariance — which is plausible — but reporting a non-randomized number for DemoGrasp would close the loop and tighten the comparison.
- **The RobustDexGrasp head-to-head (Table 2) mixes training-set differences with test-set differences.** The paper argues this is fair since both target universal grasping over arbitrary objects, but reporting a controlled-training-set comparison (or noting the training-set sizes/contents side-by-side) would be more convincing.
- **No real-world side-by-side against another contemporary sim-to-real dexterous grasping system on the same object set.** Real-world numbers are strong in absolute terms but anchored only to the paper's own claims. Sim-to-real object sets are notoriously hard to standardize, but a single comparison would have addressed the otherwise self-referential nature of these results.

### Trivial
- **The "where vs how to grasp" interpretation (Section 2.2) is intuitive but never quantitatively validated.** A small correlation analysis showing learned $T^{ee}$ tracks object-centroid offset and $\Delta q^G$ tracks size/aspect ratio would convert narrative motivation into a confirmed mechanism.

## Novel Insights
The single most novel observation from cross-reading the paper and reviews is what Table 9 implies about the framework: success rates of the *learned* policy are essentially invariant to the demonstration used, even when raw replay success of those same demonstrations ranges from 3.9% to 75.3%. That is a strong claim — the demonstration provides structure (decomposition of approach/grasp/lift, finger-closure timing), and RL recovers nearly the same universal policy regardless of the structural prior's quality. If this generalizes beyond grasping, single-demonstration editing as an RL exploration scaffold could be reusable for other long-horizon manipulation tasks where dense reward shaping currently dominates.

## Suggestions
- Reframe the contribution paragraph (Section 1 and abstract) to credit the *demonstration-editing parameterization* as the central insight, with RL as the right optimizer over it. Table 8 row 1 supports this framing directly.
- Add a perturbation experiment (e.g., shift the object mid-trial) comparing open-loop replay of the RL policy to the deployed vision policy. This would substantiate the closed-loop claim in Section 3.4.
- Report DemoGrasp on DexGraspNet under the baselines' non-randomized setting alongside the 50×50 cm number, so the reader can directly compare without taking the translation-invariance argument on faith.
- For RobustDexGrasp (Table 2), report the training-set composition for both methods explicitly and, if possible, a controlled training-set variant.
- Add a small quantitative validation of the "where vs how" decomposition: correlate $T^{ee}$ with centroid offset and $\Delta q^G$ with object size/aspect across the test sets.
