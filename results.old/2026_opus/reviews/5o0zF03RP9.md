## Summary
The paper proposes **IncentRL**, which augments RL with a KL term penalizing divergence between a predicted outcome distribution $p(o|s,a)$ and a preferred outcome distribution $q(o|s)$, weighted by $\beta$. The paper claims as its "central novelty" a Bayesian scheme that adapts $\beta$ online without manual tuning, plus theoretical results and experiments on a 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid DoorKey 8x8.

## Strengths
- **Concrete MiniGrid improvement (Fig. 2).** With $\beta=0.01$, success rate rises from 90.5% to 98% and mean episode length drops from ~75 to ~29 steps over 2M timesteps, against an identically-configured $\beta=0$ baseline. This is a real, specific gain.
- **MountainCar shows a tunable shaping signal helps when β is well-chosen.** Table 2 reports an 18% improvement in mean goal reaches at $\beta=0.1$ over the $\beta=0$ DQN baseline.
- **The toy-MDP analysis is self-contained and explicit** (Section 5.2). Setup, $p$, $q$, $\beta$ sweep, seeds, and per-$\beta$ outcomes are all reported.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The "central novelty" — Bayesian adaptation of $\beta$ — is never algorithmically specified, and its primary supporting experiment is "not shown."** Sections 1 and 7 explicitly foreground Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation as the contribution distinguishing this work from intrinsic-motivation literature ("Bayesian adaptation (novelty)..."). But Section 3 contains no prior, no likelihood, no update rule for $\beta$; Section 5.1's five-step algorithm has no Bayesian update step; Section 4 has no analysis of the Bayesian procedure. Section 5.4 then concedes: *"In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of $\beta$ achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value"*. The headline contribution is therefore unspecified on the page and supported by an experiment the paper says it omitted. This is not a missing-ablation issue; it is the central claim of the paper.

- **Figures 3–4 and Table 3 contradict the text describing them.** The text states the posterior "quickly concentrates near the effective region ($\beta \approx 0.1$)" and Figs. 3–4 mark $\beta=0.1$ as the "target effective." Table 3 reports posterior means of 0.1057 → 0.0454 → 0.0194 → 0.0173 across rounds — i.e., concentration around ~0.02, an order of magnitude away from the labelled target. Additionally, MiniGrid's best fixed $\beta$ is $0.01$, not $0.1$. The claim "Bayesian adaptation recovers the same effective values discovered by fixed sweeps" is not supported by the numbers the paper reports.

### Major

- **The KL term is mathematically ill-defined under the experimental specification.** In Section 5.2, $q(o|s_0) = \{s_1:1.0, s_0:0.0\}$ (a delta on the goal); in Section 5.3, "$q(o|s)$ assigns all probability to the goal." For any non-terminal state, $p(o|s,a)$ places mass off-goal (e.g., the explicit 0.3/0.7 mixture in Section 5.2), so $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$ is infinite. Yet Table 1 reports finite numbers without specifying any smoothing, clipping, or surrogate. What is actually being optimized is therefore unspecified, which weakens both the propositions and the interpretation of the experiments.

- **Baselines are too thin to support the headline claim.** The Related Work positions IncentRL against ICM, RND, VIME, and empowerment, but no experiment compares against any of them, and the natural minimal comparator — potential-based shaping toward the goal (Ng et al., 1999, which is cited) — is also absent. The only comparator on every benchmark is "same algorithm, $\beta=0$." With this comparator set, the paper cannot distinguish KL-to-goal shaping from a classical goal-distance potential, and cannot back up the framing against intrinsic-motivation methods.

- **MountainCar undermines the central message about robustness.** Table 2 shows $\beta=0.3$ at -53% and $\beta=1.0$ at -75%; the $\beta$ choice swings results by ~4×. The paper claims Bayesian adaptation removes the need to tune $\beta$, yet does not run Bayesian adaptation on MountainCar — the very environment where adaptation would matter most. The benchmark where the claim is most stress-tested is left untested.

### Minor

- **Propositions 1 and 2 do not engage with the actual contribution.** Prop. 1 is a standard small-perturbation/continuity argument; Prop. 2 follows by inspection from $\beta\to\infty$. Neither addresses convergence behavior of the Bayesian update on $\beta$, or whether the KL shaping accelerates learning beyond trivial cases. As written, Section 4 does not theoretically support the empirical claims.

- **$p(o|s,a)$ in experiments is the environment's true dynamics, not a learned forward model.** Section 3.1 frames $p$ as "what the agent expects... obtained from a forward model or from environment dynamics", but the experiments use environment dynamics. This makes the implementation closer to a static goal-distance shaping term than to the "preference–prediction gap" the introduction motivates; the cognitive-science framing (FEP, RPE) is decorative under the implementation reported.

- **Sample sizes for the deep-RL experiments are small** (3 seeds on MountainCar and on MiniGrid DoorKey). The MiniGrid gap (98% vs 90.5%) is plausibly within seed noise; no significance test is reported. For the toy MDP (7 seeds), Table 1 shows std comparable to the gap (e.g., 16.57 ± 11.30 vs 28.00 ± 6.00).

- **Section 4.3 is aspirational, not used.** It describes modeling $p,q$ as Gaussians via VAEs or diffusion priors — none of which is instantiated in any experiment.

### Trivial
- The $\beta=0.3$ and $\beta=1.0$ rows in Table 1 are identical to two decimal places, suggesting saturation; the table would be more informative with a finer sweep near the regime of interest.
