## Final Consolidated Review

## Summary

The paper applies an existing algorithm — Goal-Oriented Environment Inference (GOEI; Takahashi et al., 2024) — to the two-player, five-card variant of the card game "Hol's der Geier." Trained on 300,000 Rand-vs-NE games and then evaluated against a Nash-equilibrium opponent, the authors report that GOEI achieves a median reward rate of −0.010 (versus 0 optimal) while compressing the 15,542 possible observations down to 452 representative states (~2.9%). The contribution is empirical: extending GOEI's prior abstract-environment demonstration to a competitive game with a known Nash equilibrium.

## Strengths

- **Concrete near-NE performance with substantial state reduction.** Table 1 shows the best GOEI configuration (β=0.2, α=25) achieves a median reward rate of −0.010 against the NE opponent while using only 452 representative states across rounds (5 + 8 + 31 + 408), versus the 15,542 possible observations and the NE strategy's analytic state count of 247/945/69 at t=2/3/4.
- **Clear separation from the Q-learning baseline at the chosen scale.** Figure 2A shows GOEI converging toward reward rate ≈ 0 within ~500 epochs while tabular Q-learning at its best learning rate (η=0.2) stays near −0.08 even after 2,000 epochs — so the state-abstraction approach beats a model-free tabular baseline at the given observation scale.
- **Useful, honest hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.** Table 1 and Figure 4 systematically vary β ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.3} and α ∈ {11, 25, 50}, exposing genuine instability at β=0.1 and underperformance at α=11, which provides practical guidance.
- **Information-content analysis is reported transparently.** Figure 3 decomposes per-feature entropy into mutual information with the reduced state versus information loss, surfacing the (somewhat counterintuitive) finding that AH/OH information is "almost completely reduced" — the paper is forthright about this rather than concealing it.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The structural concern that training on Rand-vs-NE conflates "environment inference" with "fitting the NE opponent's empirical distribution" (Section 3.3) is real, but the authors themselves explicitly scope evaluation to non-interactive inference and acknowledge it in Section 5 ("the effectiveness of the GOEI function in interactive learning should be further confirmed"). This is a framing/scope issue, not a fabrication or proof error.

### Major

- **Single-domain, narrow validation undercuts the paper's claim of extending GOEI to "more realistic environments."** The entire empirical case rests on one small game (15,542 observations, well within tabular range — §3.3) with a fixed Rand-vs-NE training distribution. There is no controlled comparison against the original abstract environment from Takahashi et al. (2024), no scaled-up variant, and no opponent diversity (πₖ strategies are introduced as motivation in §2.2 but never used as test opponents). For a paper whose claimed contribution is empirical generalization, the single point of validation is thin.
- **Evaluation design conflates environment inference with opponent fitting.** Section 3.3 trains GOEI on a fixed dataset of Rand-vs-NE games, then uses the inferred model to plan against the NE opponent. Because the training distribution is generated by the NE opponent, the inferred observation-transition model is largely a fit to NE's empirical behavior. The abstract's claim of "a nearly optimal strategy equivalent to the Nash equilibrium" should be read in this light: it is best-responding to an NE opponent given a large supervised sample of that opponent. The paper does acknowledge this scope in Section 5, but the abstract and introduction frame the result more strongly.
- **The "core extraction" framing compares against the wrong baseline at the most informative round.** Table 1 reports NE uses 69 states at t=4 while GOEI's best configuration uses 408 — roughly 6× more — in the round whose structure is clearest (the outcome is determined by the t=4 action). The headline "2.9% of 15,542 observations" is true but largely driven by reducing against the raw observation space rather than the analytic NE core (247+945+69 at t=2/3/4). A round-by-round comparison against the NE core would give a sharper, more honest picture of where GOEI compresses well (t=2, t=3) and where it does not (t=4).
- **No game-theoretic baseline.** For an imperfect-information zero-sum game with a known NE target, the natural reference is CFR / MCCFR / DCFR (the introduction even cites Brown et al. 2019), yet only tabular Q-learning and simple πₖ heuristics serve as baselines. Without a model-based or game-theoretic comparator, "GOEI beats Q-learning" does not isolate whether state reduction is doing the work, or whether any reasonable Bayesian model-based method would match it.

### Minor

- **Explainability motivation is not realized, and the paper concedes this.** Section 1 frames the work around interpretability, but Section 5 admits "we could not give a verbal explanation of the reduced state representation more concretely than Figure 3," and Figure 3 itself shows that AH and OH — the features humans actually reason about — are "almost completely reduced." If explainability cannot be cashed out, the introduction's framing is misleading and should be softened.
- **"Equivalent to Nash equilibrium" is slightly overclaimed.** The best median reward rate is −0.010, not 0.000 (Table 1, β=0.2, α=25). "Close to" or "indistinguishable in practice from" would be more honest than "equivalent to" used in the abstract.
- **The §4.2 conclusion overreaches given the analysis performed.** Stating that "the required information is maintained in complex combinations of all the features" follows from low marginal mutual information, but the paper does not measure joint or higher-order MI — so the claim is a plausible interpretation rather than a demonstrated result.
- **Interactive-learning experiment is missing.** The paper itself flags this as an open question (§5), and it materially restricts the strength of the "useful for RL" claim, but it is at least named as a limitation rather than ignored.

### Trivial
- The π₀, π₁, … chain motivation in §2.2 is never used as a test opponent; this is a minor coherence issue between motivation and evaluation.
- Figure 2A's y-axis spans only [−0.2, 0.0], which visually amplifies the GOEI–QL gap; including Rand (−0.527) on a wider scale would calibrate the reader.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Run GOEI on the original abstract environment from Takahashi et al. (2024) and on Hol's der Geier in a single table — a head-to-head comparison would directly support the "extends to a realistic environment" claim.
- Train on Rand-vs-Rand games and evaluate inference quality against held-out NE play, separating "inferred core dynamics" from "fit NE's empirical distribution."
- Round-by-round comparison of GOEI's recovered state set against the NE analytical core, quantifying which compressions are loss-free.
- Add a CFR/MCCFR baseline to anchor the comparison in the standard game-theoretic toolkit.
- Either deliver an explainability result or reposition GOEI as a sample-efficient Bayesian state-inference method.

None beyond the paper's own contributions.
