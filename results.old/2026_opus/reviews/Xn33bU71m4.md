## Summary
The paper presents an empirical evaluation of nine open-source mid-sized LLMs (12–16B parameters) — CodeLlama, Llama2, Deepseek-V2/R1, Qwen2.5/3, WizardCoder, Phi4, Gemma3 — on two reverse-engineering tasks (name recovery and type inference) over a 40k-function dataset built from GNU binaries across four architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS) and four optimization levels (O0–O3), with both zero-shot and LoRA-fine-tuned settings, plus a real-world firmware test using Ghidra and IDA Pro. The headline finding is that, with a single fixed prompt and uniform pipeline, all evaluated models achieve very low F1 scores (mostly <0.10) and fine-tuning yields inconsistent gains, leading the paper to conclude that current LLMs are "not yet" reverse engineers.

## Strengths
- **Broad evaluation matrix.** §4.1 + Tables 2–5 cover 4 architectures × 4 optimization levels × 9 models, sampling 40,000 unique functions; this is more comprehensive coverage than most prior single-architecture studies.
- **Decompiler-comparison + real-firmware test.** Table 6 evaluates all nine models on stripped real-world firmware decompiled with both Ghidra and IDA Pro, providing some external-validity grounding beyond synthetic benchmarks.
- **Concrete observation about fine-tuning cost.** Table 7 + Finding 7 document a ~4× spread in 200-step fine-tuning time across models and trace it to prompt-template structure — an actionable, evidence-backed finding for resource-constrained practitioners.
- **CodeWordNet ablation is well-supported.** Figure 1 explicitly shows the impact of strict string matching vs. semantic matching, allowing readers to separate metric effects from model effects on the name-recovery results.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The verified concerns below are serious but do not single-handedly invalidate the core measurement.

### Major

- **The abstract's headline number contradicts the main tables.** The abstract claims "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." In Table 2 (function name) R1's best fine-tuned cell is 0.05; in Table 3 (variable name) it is 0.17; in Table 4 (type) it is at most 0.01. The 0.37 figure appears nowhere in the main-paper tables. This is a direct internal inconsistency at the level of the paper's most-publicized claim, and it is the only "positive" result the abstract leans on.
- **Title and conclusion overclaim scope.** The title ("LLMs as Reverse Engineers? Not Yet on Types and Names") and conclusion ("current LLMs remain fundamentally limited") make categorical statements about LLMs, while §4.2 explicitly restricts the population to 12–16B open-source models ("any larger and charged models are excluded from our measurement"). The abstract is more honest ("widely used open-source mid-sized LLMs"), but the rest of the paper is not, and the scope mismatch is not minor: prior RE-LLM work (SYMGEN uses CodeLlama-34B; commercial models exist) sits outside the population this paper measures.
- **DeepSeek-R1 is evaluated with reasoning suppressed, then claims are drawn about R1.** §4.5 says: "we modified the prompt template to suppress reasoning, since DeepSeek-R1 lacks a built-in mechanism for disabling it." Finding 5 then uses R1's weak result to argue that "DeepSeek-R1 … consistently underperforms Qwen2.5 … training data quality significantly impacts performance." The conclusion does not follow from the experiment: R1's main differentiator from Qwen2.5 is precisely the reasoning trace, which was disabled. Whatever Finding 5 measures, it is not what its prose claims.
- **No comparison to prior specialized RE systems on the same data.** §2 cites RESYM, VARBERT, SYMLM, DIRTY/Chen et al., and Debin as the directly comparable specialized systems, but §4 runs none of them on this benchmark. The paper's stated goal in §2 is to ground "principled model selection in future reverse engineering research"; without an anchor against the systems those LLMs would replace, the absolute F1 numbers (≈0.03–0.33) are uninterpretable as evidence about LLM capability.
- **Single fixed off-the-shelf prompt across nine very different model families.** §3.2 explicitly says, "Rather than optimizing prompts, we adopt an established reverse engineering prompt" (from a Notion page tuned for ChatGPT). §4.5 then catalogs exactly the symptoms suboptimal prompting would produce (refusals, extraneous reasoning, format violations, missed names) and attributes them to model limitations. For a paper whose entire purpose is cross-model comparison, a per-model prompt-sensitivity check (or at minimum two prompts) is needed to separate "model can't do RE" from "this prompt doesn't fit this model."
- **Undocumented auxiliary-LLM extractor sits in front of every reported number.** §3.2 says all model outputs are passed through "another LLM" to extract a normalized answer; the model, prompt, and any extraction-accuracy validation are not reported. §4.5 acknowledges that models routinely "miss some" of the requested names, which the extractor then has to handle. Every reported F1 is therefore a property of (target model + extractor) pairs, and the paper does not show the extractor itself is reliable across talkative vs. terse vs. refusal-prone models.

### Minor
- **Findings 4–6 generalize beyond the data.** Finding 6 is supported only by CodeLlama 7B/13B/34B on one architecture; the 70B variant is excluded due to "GPU limitations" (§4.3). Three points within one family cannot support a general statement that "larger variants … do not necessarily yield better results." Same critique applies to the architecture-vs-data-quality framing in Finding 5, which is contaminated by the R1-reasoning issue above.
- **Strict exact-match for types is acknowledged but not relaxed.** §3.4 uses strict equality, and Finding 2 itself notes that "improving performance may require … more flexible evaluation metrics." The metric is consistent with the paper's intent, but Finding 2's claim that "fine-tuning has limited impact on type inference" is partly a property of the metric, not just the model — this should be stated more cautiously.
- **Fine-tuning protocol is thin.** Table 7 reports only "200 steps" per model and times; no learning rate, batch size, LoRA rank/α, or learning curve is given. The fine-tuning conclusions (Findings 1–3) would be more credible with a learning-curve check showing the models have actually converged at 200 steps.
- **Table 5 (assembly) is suspiciously uniform.** All nine models, both original and fine-tuned, score 0.00. The paper attributes this to task difficulty, but uniformity at machine-zero across nine very different models is worth a short diagnostic (e.g., whether the inputs exceeded context, or whether the extractor failed) rather than being stated as a flat finding.
- **GNU-software-only training corpus is a memorization risk.** §4.1 notes that the dataset is "mostly from GNU software." GNU is heavily represented in code-LLM pretraining; some discussion of whether observed F1 differences could reflect memorization (favoring some models over others) would strengthen the comparison.

### Trivial
- Table 5's caption says "Different Optimization Levels" but the single column reports a pooled O0–O3 score; either give per-O0…O3 columns or rephrase.
- Finding 7's "up to 4 times longer" doesn't quite match Table 7's 186,720 / 33,120 ≈ 5.6× spread; tighten the numerical claim.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A small extractor-validation study (e.g., 200 hand-labeled raw responses showing extraction precision/recall per target model, ideally with two different extractor LLMs) would close the largest evidential gap in §3.2.
- Per-model prompt tuning, or at least reporting how much numbers move under 2–3 prompt variants, would let readers separate model from prompt effects.
- Reporting R1 *with* reasoning enabled as a separate row (even at higher cost) would make Findings 4–5 honest about what is being measured.
- One frontier or specialized-RE anchor (RESYM, DIRTY, SYMLM, or one closed-source LLM ceiling) on the same test functions would make the absolute F1 numbers interpretable.

None beyond the paper's own contributions. ## Suggestions
- Rewrite the title and conclusion to scope the claim to "mid-sized open-source LLMs in the 12–16B range," and either add at least one frontier or specialized-RE baseline as a ceiling, or explicitly disclaim that the paper does not measure the LLM frontier.
- Resolve the 0.37 vs. 0.05/0.17 inconsistency between the abstract and Tables 2–4 — either correct the abstract or surface the table cell that produces 0.37.
- Report DeepSeek-R1 with reasoning enabled as a separate row from R1-with-reasoning-suppressed; do not draw R1-specific conclusions from the suppressed configuration.
- Document the auxiliary extractor (model, prompt) and report an extraction-validation table on a small hand-labeled sample, ideally with two different extractor LLMs to show stability.
- Run at least one specialized prior system (RESYM, DIRTY, or SYMLM) on the same test functions to anchor the absolute F1 numbers.
- Disclose LoRA hyperparameters and include a learning-curve check so the "fine-tuning is limited" findings rest on more than 200 steps with unknown configuration.
- Diagnose the all-zero assembly result in Table 5 (context-length truncation? extractor failure? task genuinely intractable at this scale?) rather than report it as a flat finding.

## Axis Evaluation
- **Originality.** The premise — that model choice in RE-LLM papers is unjustified and the field needs a comparable head-to-head — is legitimate but not novel; comprehensive cross-model RE benchmarks of this exact stripe have not been previously published, so the contribution is incremental-empirical.
- **Importance of research question.** Real and well-motivated; §2 articulates the gap clearly.
- **Whether claims are well supported.** This is the paper's central weakness. The categorical conclusion ("LLMs remain fundamentally limited") is not supported by the population studied; the abstract's positive headline (0.37 F1) is contradicted by the body; R1 conclusions rest on a configuration that disables R1's defining feature.
- **Soundness of experiments.** Mixed. The breadth (architectures, opt levels, decompilers) is genuine; the controls (single off-the-shelf prompt, undocumented extractor, undisclosed LoRA setup, missing specialized baselines) are not.
- **Clarity of writing.** Adequate; the experimental description is mostly readable but the §4 results sections are dense and the over-strong findings are not flagged with caveats inline.
- **Value to the research community.** The dataset, infrastructure, and breadth are reusable; the conclusions, as written, are not safe to cite. With rescoping and the methodological fixes above, this could become a useful resource.
