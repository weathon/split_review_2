## Summary

This paper introduces LayerDAG, an autoregressive diffusion model for DAG generation. The key insight is a *layerwise tokenization* that decomposes a DAG into a unique sequence of bipartite graphs (layers) via longest-path partitioning. A DAG is generated by autoregressively predicting each layer's size, then using a discrete diffusion model (D3PM) to jointly generate the layer's node attributes and edges conditioned on previously generated layers. The method is permutation invariant by construction. Experiments on a synthetic dataset (logical preference DAGs with hard constraints) and three real-world computing-system benchmarks (TPU Tile, HLS, NA-Edge, up to ~400 nodes) show that LayerDAG consistently outperforms GraphRNN, D-VAE, GraphPNAS, and its own non-autoregressive variant (OneShotDAG) on validity, distributional fidelity, and downstream surrogate model accuracy — including a challenging out-of-distribution label generalization setting where all baselines fail.

---

## Strengths

**1. Novel and well-motivated layerwise tokenization (Section 3.1).** The longest-path-based layer decomposition is deterministic, invertible, and unique, providing a principled factorization of a DAG into a sequence of bipartite graphs. This avoids the non-uniqueness of topological orderings that forces prior autoregressive DAG models (D-VAE, GraphRNN) to rely on expensive data augmentation.

**2. Permutation invariance by construction (Proposition 1, Section 3.3).** The paper formally argues (and the reasoning is sound) that LayerDAG's autoregressive conditional probabilities are permutation invariant due to its use of BiMPNN (permutation equivariant), sum pooling (permutation invariant), and a transformer without positional encodings. This is a genuine advantage over node-ordering-based autoregressive models.

**3. Clean ablation isolating the contribution of multi-step diffusion (Table 1).** The comparison between full LayerDAG (validity 0.56 on LP ρ=0) and LayerDAG (T=1) (validity 0.26) cleanly demonstrates that the iterative refinement of diffusion — not just the autoregressive layerwise decomposition — is essential for capturing hard logical dependencies. OneShotDAG (0.37) further shows that autoregressive factorization alone provides a smaller but non-trivial gain.

**4. State-of-the-art results on real-world computing benchmarks (Tables 2–3).** LayerDAG achieves the best Pearson correlation and MAE on all three conditional generation datasets (TPU Tile, HLS, NA-Edge) and is the **only model that achieves positive Pearson correlation** in the challenging extrapolation setting (5th quantile, Table 3), where all baselines produce near-zero or negative correlations. The consistency across three different computing platforms (TPU, FPGA, mobile CPU) is strong evidence of generality.

**5. Scalability demonstrated on DAGs with up to ~400 nodes (Table data statistics).** Prior DAG generative models (D-VAE, GraphPNAS) were evaluated on DAGs with ≤24 nodes. LayerDAG successfully generates DAGs at this scale with good validity and distributional fidelity, which is critical for the motivating application (system benchmarking).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

**1. Circularity concern in the surrogate-model evaluation for Table 2.** The main evaluation protocol (Section 5.2) trains a BiMPNN surrogate on synthetic DAGs and tests on real DAGs — but BiMPNN is also the encoder used inside LayerDAG (Section 3, Implementation). This raises a concern: the generative model could be producing DAGs whose representations are particularly easy for *its own encoder* to map to labels, potentially inflating surrogate performance relative to what an independent evaluation would show. The paper partially addresses this with the Kaggle-based surrogate in the label generalization experiment (Table 3), which confirms the pattern using a completely different model. However, the main conditional generation results (Table 2) rely exclusively on BiMPNN surrogates. Adding a surrogate from a different model family (e.g., GIN, GraphSAGE, or an MLP on hand-crafted features) to Table 2 would resolve this concern cleanly. As it stands, the evidence is strong (the Kaggle result corroborates the overall trend) but the concern is not fully ruled out.

**2. No discussion of limitations or failure modes.** The paper does not include a limitations section. Important questions are unaddressed: (a) How does generation time scale as DAGs grow (autoregressive over layers + diffusion per layer implies a superlinear factor)? (b) How does the method handle DAGs with very wide layers (many source nodes) or degenerate structures (e.g., disconnected components)? (c) Are there failure modes when the training and test DAGs have very different layer-depth distributions? These are not fatal omissions, but the paper's completeness and trustworthiness would benefit from a brief limitations paragraph.

### Minor

**1. Baselines are somewhat dated; no recent DAG diffusion baseline.** The non-ablation baselines (GraphRNN 2018, D-VAE 2019, GraphPNAS 2020) are well-established but older. The paper does not compare against a modern directed-graph diffusion model (e.g., DiGress adapted for directed adjacency matrices, or DGDK). While the OneShotDAG and T=1 ablations partially fill this gap, the paper does not include a controlled baseline where an autoregressive model (e.g., D-VAE) is given the same layerwise tokenization and BiMPNN encoder but without diffusion. Such an ablation would isolate whether the gains come from diffusion or from the layerwise decomposition.

**2. Statistical significance of validity gaps not assessed.** In Table 1 (LP ρ=0), LayerDAG achieves 0.56±0.02 validity versus OneShotDAG at 0.37±0.02 — a clear gap. But no formal significance test (t-test, bootstrap CI) is reported. While the standard deviations suggest the gaps are reliable, adding significance tests would strengthen the claims.

**3. Generation-time comparison incomplete (Figure 1).** The trade-off figure shows LayerDAG's quality vs. time budget for different denoising schedules, but mentions baseline runtimes only in text without plotting them on the same axes. Including baseline time-quality points on the same plot would give a complete efficiency picture.

**4. HLS MAE tied with GraphRNN.** In Table 2 (HLS), LayerDAG and GraphRNN have identical MAE (1.1) within error bars, though LayerDAG has higher Pearson (0.85 vs. 0.79). The paper does not discuss why the absolute error is comparable while rank correlation differs. A brief explanation would improve clarity.

### Trivial

- The observation that one-hot layer-index encoding hurts performance is noted (Section 3, Layerwise positional encoding) but not explained. A short speculation would help.
- The figure caption in Figure 1 does not specify the metric on the y-axis.
