## Summary

This paper tackles covert misinformation injection in LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS), a timely and important problem. It contributes (1) MISINFOTASK, a dataset of 108 tasks with misinformation arguments designed for MAS red-teaming, and (2) ARGUS, a training-free two-stage defense framework that adaptively localizes critical communication channels (via topological importance, semantic relevance, and message frequency) and then rectifies misinformation through goal-aware chain-of-thought reasoning. Experiments across four LLMs (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4o, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.0-flash), three attack types (Prompt Injection, RAG Poisoning, Tool Injection), and five MAS topologies show consistent MT reductions of ~28–36% and TSR improvements of ~7–16% over undefended baselines.

## Strengths

1. **Consistent empirical evidence across diverse settings.** Table 1 shows ARGUS outperforms Attack-only, Self-Check, and G-Safeguard on all four LLMs and all three attack types, with no negative results. The pattern is clear: ARGUS achieves the lowest MT and highest TSR in 10 out of 12 attack-specific conditions, and in the remaining 2 conditions it is comparable to the best baseline. This breadth strengthens the generality claim.

2. **Ablation studies validate component contributions.** Table 2 shows that removing dynamic localization, CoT revision, or multi-turn correction each degrades performance, confirming that all three components contribute. Table 3 further breaks down the adaptive scoring weights (topology α, frequency β, relevance γ) and shows that removing any single weight hurts — with γ (information relevance) being the most critical. This level of ablative validation is thorough and demonstrates the authors systematically investigated design choices.

3. **Robustness across MAS topologies.** Figure 6 tests five distinct topologies (Chain, Full, Self-Determined, Circle, Star). ARGUS consistently reduces MT across all topologies under all attack types, showing the defense is not tied to a specific graph structure.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Small synthetic dataset (108 tasks) with no external validation.** MISINFOTASK consists of 108 tasks, all LLM-generated from seed examples and manually filtered. This is a thin foundation for evaluating both the vulnerability of MAS and the effectiveness of a defense. The dataset is used for all experiments, creating a closed loop: the method is tested on data generated by the same kind of model it defends. There is no evaluation on existing MAS benchmarks, real-world interaction logs, or independently constructed datasets. Without such external validation, it is unclear whether ARGUS's performance reflects genuine robustness or structure-specific patterns in this particular dataset. The authors should provide cross-dataset generalization evidence or at minimum a human evaluation on a subset.

2. **No variance or significance reporting in the main results table.** The subscript values in Table 1 are deltas from the Attack-only row, not standard deviations or standard errors. The Attack-only rows themselves have no variance reported. Figure 2 states "three independent experimental trials" but Table 1 does not specify whether results are averaged over multiple seeds, nor does it report any dispersion measure. This makes it impossible to assess whether ARGUS's improvements are statistically significant or stable across runs. For a paper making quantitative claims about average improvements (28.17%, 10.33%), this is a significant evidential gap that should be closed (with standard deviations across multiple seeds, and ideally significance tests).

3. **LLM-based evaluation metric used without human validation or calibration.** The MT and TSR metrics rely on GPT-4o as an automated judge to score semantic consistency on a 0–10 scale. There is no human evaluation, no calibration against human judgments, no analysis of judge bias (e.g., does the judge systematically favor verbose outputs, penalize short corrections, or exhibit agreement rate with human annotators?). While LLM-as-judge is common practice, the paper's primary quantitative evidence depends on these scores, and the lack of validation significantly weakens confidence in the reported numbers. The authors should provide human agreement rates on a sample of scored outputs.

4. **Narrow baseline defense comparison.** The paper compares ARGUS against only two defenses: Self-Check (a generic reflection prompt) and G-Safeguard (GNN-based agent pruning). Consensus-based methods (e.g., multi-agent debate, Chern et al., 2024, cited in the paper's own introduction), adversarial training approaches, and other graph-based defenses are acknowledged in the related work but not compared. Self-Check is a minimal intervention not designed for misinformation; G-Safeguard's edge-pruning mechanism may be ill-suited for covert misinformation scenarios where no single agent appears malicious. A convincing demonstration would include at least one consensus-based defense adapted to this setting.

### Minor

1. **Numerical inconsistency between abstract and introduction.** The abstract reports an average MT reduction of "approximately 28.17%," while the introduction (Section 1) states "reducing misinformation toxicity by approximately 38.24% across various core LLMs." These are different numbers for the same claimed quantity. The abstract's 28.17% is consistent with Table 1 (average of 28.18%, 20.38%, 35.95% per attack type), so the 38.24% appears to be an error. This should be corrected.

2. **Adaptive localization relies on noisy goal inference with limited analysis.** Figure 4 shows goal inference accuracy ranging from 0.50 to 0.80, with values below 0.70 for RAG Poisoning and Tool Injection. The paper does not analyze how these inference failures affect localization decisions, nor does it compare against simpler alternatives (e.g., static-topology monitoring without adaptive re-localization, or random localization). Table 2's "w/o Dynamic Local." ablation removes localization entirely, which conflates the value of localization with the value of adaptivity. A comparison against "ARGUS with static localization" (fixed top-k edges) would more cleanly isolate adaptivity's contribution.

3. **Threat model is limited to single-agent compromise.** The paper assumes one compromised agent at the initial round. Coordinated multi-agent attacks or dynamic misinformation that evolves across rounds are not considered. While single-agent compromise is a natural starting point, the paper should explicitly discuss whether ARGUS would generalize to more complex threat scenarios.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface an interesting tension that the paper does not fully address: ARGUS's adaptive localization is motivated as a core contribution, yet the goal inference it depends on operates at 0.50–0.80 accuracy. The paper's own ablation shows that removing dynamic localization entirely degrades MT from 3.50 to 4.55 — a significant drop, confirming localization matters. However, the question of whether a *simpler* static-localization baseline (e.g., always monitor the topologically most central edges) would achieve comparable performance at lower complexity remains unanswered. The reviews collectively point to a pattern where the evaluation validates that the *system works* but does not always disentangle *why* each design choice matters relative to simpler alternatives. Beyond this, no genuinely novel insight emerges from the reviews beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions

1. Report standard deviations across multiple independent runs (at least 3 seeds) in Table 1, and conduct statistical significance tests (e.g., paired bootstrap) between ARGUS and each baseline.
2. Add a human evaluation on a 20–30 instance subset of MISINFOTASK to validate the GPT-4o judge's scores against human judgments, reporting agreement rates (e.g., Spearman correlation or Cohen's κ).
3. Add at least one consensus-based defense baseline (e.g., multi-agent debate where agents cross-check information consistency) adapted to the misinformation setting.
4. Add an ablation comparing ARGUS against a static-localization variant (top-k edges by topological importance only, fixed across rounds) to separate the value of adaptivity from the value of localization.
5. Resolve the 28.17% vs 38.24% numerical inconsistency in the introduction.
