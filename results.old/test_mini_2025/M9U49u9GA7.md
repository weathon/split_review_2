## Summary

This paper proposes SiDyP, a two-stage framework for denoising LLM-generated labels in text classification tasks. Stage I fine-tunes a BERT classifier on noisy labels, extracting training dynamics and candidate label sets via neighbor consistency. Stage II uses a conditional diffusion model (on a ±k logit label encoding, which the paper terms "simplex diffusion") with a dynamic candidate distillation mechanism that progressively refines uncertain labels based on diffusion model feedback. Experiments span 4 NLP tasks, 5 LLMs (including GPT-4o and Llama-3.1-405b), and synthetic/real-world noise. SiDyP consistently outperforms all baselines (NPC, DyGen, Co-teaching, JoCoR) across every setting.

## Strengths

1. **Comprehensive and systematic evaluation across diverse settings.** The paper evaluates SiDyP on 4 tasks (NumClaim, TREC, SemEval, 20News) using 5 different LLMs (Llama-3-70b, Llama-3.1-70b, Llama-3.1-405b, GPT-4o, Mixtral-8x22b) in both zero-shot and few-shot prompting, plus synthetic noise (symmetric, asymmetric, instance-dependent) and real-world noise from WRENCH. SiDyP outperforms all baselines in every single configuration (Tables 2–4). This breadth provides strong evidence of robustness.

2. **Consistent and sometimes substantial improvements over the strongest baseline (DyGen).** On SemEval (the hardest task, with ~50% label noise), SiDyP improves over DyGen by 3.40% (zero-shot) and 4.00% (few-shot) under Llama-3-70b, and by 3.13–7.33% across the 8 diversified-LLM settings in Table 3. On the diversified LLM experiments, the average gain over DyGen is 4.47%.

3. **Novel dynamic candidate distillation mechanism.** The approach of (a) retrieving multiple label candidates via KNN on embedding space and (b) progressively distilling these candidates based on the diffusion model's own predictions during training (Algorithm 2) is well-motivated and clearly described. The ablation in Table 5 confirms that the dynamic prior adds 1.53% over a fixed prior, and the authors further validate this in Appendix F by comparing the fraction of correct labels collected.

4. **Realistic evaluation protocol.** The paper uses noisy validation sets for model selection (Section 5.2), acknowledging that clean validation data is often unavailable in practice. This makes the reported results more credible than methods that assume access to clean validation data.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Misleading "simplex diffusion" terminology.** The paper repeatedly calls its diffusion model a "simplex diffusion model (Mahabadi et al., 2024)" and titles itself accordingly. However, the actual method (Section 4, Equations 2–4) is Gaussian diffusion applied to a ±k logit encoding of discrete labels. The forward process is a Gaussian-Markov chain (Eq. 2), the noise is Gaussian (Eq. 3), and the reverse process is Gaussian (Eq. 4). In Mahabadi et al. (2024), simplex diffusion operates on the probability simplex using Dirichlet noise — a fundamentally different noise process. The paper's method is better described as "Gaussian diffusion on logit-encoded labels." This is not merely a naming issue: it conflates the method with a technically distinct line of work, and it obscures what the ablation in Table 5 actually demonstrates. The comparison "DP+Gau-Diff" vs. "DP+Sim-Diff (SiDyP)" in Table 5, which shows an 8.58% gap, is presented as a comparison of diffusion model types, but it actually compares Gaussian diffusion on different label representations (one-hot vs. ±k logits). The paper must clarify what exactly each row represents and justify why the ±k encoding is essential.

2. **Ablation study is conducted on only one dataset and one LLM.** Table 5 validates the component contributions on SemEval using only Llama-3-70b. The dynamic prior (+1.53%) and the simplex vs. Gaussian diffusion gap (+8.58%) are claimed to be the key technical innovations, yet their effectiveness is not demonstrated on a second task (e.g., TREC or NumClaim) or a second LLM. Given the variance observed across tasks in the main results, the reader cannot assess whether these component contributions generalize.

3. **No statistical significance testing.** The paper reports means and standard deviations over 5 seeds but conducts no significance tests (t-tests, confidence intervals). Several comparisons against DyGen show small margins with overlapping variability — notably NumClaim few-shot (95.41±0.28 vs. 95.97±0.15, gain 0.56%), TREC few-shot (84.80±0.86 vs. 85.60±0.44, gain 0.80%), and 20News zero-shot (71.42±0.31 vs. 72.66±0.58, gain 1.24%). These gains may or may not be reliable; the paper's claim to "outperform all baselines in all experiments" would be strengthened by statistical verification.

### Minor

1. **Lack of hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.** The pipeline involves several hyperparameters (σ, λ, γ, K, α, β, k, T, noise schedule parameters). The paper states that "all experiments are conducted using the same PLC fine-tuned results, and share the same value of hyper-parameters" (Section 5.5), but does not report what those values are, how they were selected, or how sensitive performance is to them. Values may be in the stripped Appendix D, but they should at least be stated in the main text or a table.

2. **Limited detail on the diffusion model architecture.** The reverse process (Eq. 4) conditions on s^ŷ and x, but the paper does not specify the neural network architecture (is it an MLP? Does it combine BERT embeddings and diffusion timestep embeddings?). The note "More training details are revealed in Appendix D" suggests this is deferred, but the main text should provide a sketch of the architecture for reproducibility.

3. **The multi-model framework (Figure 1) vs. single-model experiments.** Figure 1 shows M models with co-regularization, but the experiments (Section 5.2) state that "we use only one coherent PLC results" for baselines and the implementation uses BERT as the PLC. It is unclear whether M > 1 is actually used in SiDyP itself. If the framework is designed for multiple models but evaluated with a single model, this should be explicitly stated and the mismatch discussed.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify the diffusion model.** Rename or carefully qualify what "simplex diffusion" means in this paper. State explicitly: "We map one-hot labels to ±k logits and apply standard Gaussian diffusion in this logit space. We term this simplex diffusion because the ±k representation lies on the corners of a (scaled) probability simplex." This is honest and avoids conflating with Mahabadi et al.'s Dirichlet-based approach. Also clarify what "Gau-Diff" in Table 5 means — if it applies Gaussian noise directly to one-hot vectors, explain how that differs from the proposed method.

2. **Extend the ablation to at least one more task.** Repeat the component ablation (Table 5) on TREC or NumClaim with Llama-3-70b. Without this, the claim that the dynamic prior and the label encoding are the key contributors is only validated on one data point.

3. **Report significance tests.** Add paired t-tests or report 95% confidence intervals for the SiDyP vs. DyGen comparison on each key setting, especially where gains are small (NumClaim few-shot, 20News zero-shot).

4. **Report hyperparameter values.** Provide the values of σ, λ, γ, K, α, β, and k either in the main text or in a table. If robustness to these values was tested, report that analysis.

The paper tackles a timely and important problem, has a sound pipeline, and delivers consistent empirical improvements. The main issues are presentation clarity (the "simplex diffusion" terminology) and the narrowness of the ablation, both of which are addressable. I recommend **rejection** in its current form due to the unclarified terminology overclaim and limited validation of the core technical claims, but I encourage the authors to revise and resubmit.
