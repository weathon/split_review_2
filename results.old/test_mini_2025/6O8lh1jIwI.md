## Summary

This paper introduces DAG-TFRC, a method for learning window graphs (DAGs with instantaneous and lagged dependencies) from time-series data under the novel assumption that the data are generated by a small number of root causes (sparse inputs to an SVAR). The method minimizes the L1 norm of estimated root causes plus regularization on graph weights and an acyclicity constraint. Experiments show scalability up to thousands of nodes and strong accuracy on synthetic data. Simulated and real S&P 500 data demonstrate the viability of the few-root-cause assumption in finance, including interpretable sector clustering and event alignment.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated assumption for time-series causal discovery.** The "few root causes" assumption extends SparseRC (static data) to time series and is intuitively grounded (stock market examples, gene data). This is a genuinely new direction that clearly differentiates the method from the standard zero-mean-noise assumption in VAR models.

2. **Impressive scalability with strong empirical results.** Figure 2 and Table 1 show DAG-TFRC achieving near-perfect SHD (0) for graphs up to 4000 nodes with very few samples (N=4 for 1000-node graphs), while baselines like VAR-LiNGAM time out at 1000–2000 nodes. The runtime advantage is substantial (e.g., ~1000× faster than VAR-LiNGAM at 1000 nodes). This combination of accuracy and scale is the paper's strongest empirical contribution.

3. **Interpretable real-world validation on S&P 500 data.** The estimated instantaneous graph (Fig. 3a) clusters stocks by economic sectors, and discovered root causes (Fig. 3b) align with specific known events (META dividend announcement, NVDA sales forecast). The negative control (dividends: only 35/3796 coincide with a root cause) supports the interpretability claim. This goes beyond standard synthetic-only evaluation.

4. **Theoretical grounding for the L0 problem.** Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 establish identifiability of the window graph and show the ground truth is the unique global minimizer of the L0 problem (5) under the few-root-cause assumption. While these apply to the idealized objective, they provide principled motivation for the approach.

5. **Honest discussion of limitations.** The paper explicitly acknowledges linearity, stationarity, undersampling, and the Dream3 failure, which builds trust in the authors' assessment of their method's scope.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

### Minor

1. **Theory–algorithm gap.** Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 establish identifiability and global optimality for the *L0* problem (5), but the algorithm solves the *L1*-relaxed problem (6). The paper does not analyze the conditions under which the L1 relaxation recovers the ground truth. The paper frames (6) as a relaxation of (5) — a standard and reasonable practice — and does not explicitly claim the theorems apply to the algorithm. However, the presentation would be strengthened by either (a) providing a recovery guarantee for (6) under stated assumptions, or (b) clearly stating that the theory motivates the objective and the algorithm is a heuristic that works well empirically. As written, readers could over-interpret the theory as covering the actual algorithm.

2. **Narrow synthetic evaluation regime.** The synthetic data uses p=5% root cause sparsity, matching the method's assumption. There is no systematic test of how performance degrades when the few-root-cause assumption is violated (e.g., p=20%, p=50%, non-sparse root causes, or non-linear generation). The Dream3 failure is mentioned in the limitations but relegated to the appendix. Adding a controlled experiment where sparsity is varied would honestly characterize the assumption's robustness and give practitioners a clear sense of when the method will work versus fail.

3. **Post-hoc threshold selection in real-data analysis.** The threshold of 0.07 on estimated root causes is chosen to yield ~1% nonzero entries, and then 99.6% alignment is reported. The paper does not show how alignment varies with the threshold, nor does it provide a principled criterion for choosing it (e.g., based on estimated noise level). A precision-recall curve over thresholds or a sensitivity analysis would strengthen this result.

4. **Quantitative evaluation of sector clustering is missing.** Figure 3a is visually assessed as clustering stocks by sector, but no quantitative metric (adjusted Rand index, modularity, or comparison to a random baseline) is reported. While the visual pattern is suggestive, a number would make the claim more rigorous.

5. **SparseRC performs slightly better on simulated financial data (Table 2).** The paper honestly reports this (SparseRC SHD 9.92 vs DAG-TFRC 11.36, overlapping confidence intervals). This is not a weakness of the paper per se, but the abstract's claim of "superior performance" should be qualified to note that on the simulated financial dataset, the few-root-cause methods perform comparably, with SparseRC slightly ahead on SHD at small scale.

2. In the S&P 500 analysis, report how alignment between root causes and price changes varies as a function of the threshold, so readers can assess robustness.
3. Either remove or clearly separate the theoretical claims (Theorems 3.1, 3.2) from the algorithm description, making explicit that the theorems apply to the L0 objective and the algorithm solves a relaxed L1 version.
