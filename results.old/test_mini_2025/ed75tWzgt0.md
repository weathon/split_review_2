## Summary

This paper proposes a theoretical two-player RLHF framework with active exploration, where a max-player aims to approximate a Nash equilibrium and a min-player plays a best-response role. The authors show that under certain structural assumptions (Assumption 4, low TGEC), the theoretical framework reduces to two practical closed-form loss functions: a standard DPO loss for the max-player and a DPO loss with an exploration bonus for the min-player, yielding the TANPO algorithm. They also propose SADPO, a single-agent approximation via rejection sampling. Theorem 1 provides a sublinear regret bound under general function approximation. Empirical results on AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench, and several academic benchmarks show TANPO and SADPO outperforming baselines (Online DPO, Hybrid GSHF, SELM) using Zephyr-7B-SFT as the base model.

## Strengths

1. **Theoretical regret analysis under general function approximation.** Theorem 1 gives a regret bound of \(O(\sqrt{T \cdot d_{\text{TGEC}}(1/\sqrt{T}) \log(|\mathcal{R}|/\delta)})\) for the theoretical two-player framework, establishing sublinear average regret as \(T \to \infty\). The TGEC framework extends the GEC from single-agent to two-agent zero-sum games, which is a non-trivial generalization.

2. **Clean reduction of a theoretically-motivated framework to simple, implementable losses.** The derivation from the bilevel optimization objectives in Section 3.2 to the practical losses in Equations (11) and (13) is algebraically elegant. The max-player collapses to standard DPO (Equation 11), and the min-player becomes DPO plus an \(\alpha \cdot \mathbb{E}[\log \mu(a|x)]\) exploration bonus (Equation 13). This makes the algorithm immediately usable by practitioners.

3. **Controlled comparison isolating the effect of diverse training data.** The max-player in TANPO uses the identical loss function as Online DPO — only the training data differs (generated by a two-agent process instead of a single agent). The fact that the max-player outperforms Online DPO (27.66% vs. 24.36% LC win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0) provides direct evidence that the data diversity mechanism is responsible for the improvement, not a different loss function. This is the paper's strongest empirical finding.

4. **Evidence of sustained improvement across two epochs.** Figure 4 tracks six iterations (two full epochs on the same data) and shows continued improvement, with the min-player's LC win rate rising from ~19.5 at Iter1 to ~30.5 at Iter6. This directly addresses the overfitting problem that plagues many online RLHF methods, and the effect is visually clear and consistent.

5. **SADPO as a practical single-agent variant.** Algorithm 2 provides a one-model approximation via rejection sampling (K=4), selecting the highest and lowest \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) responses. SADPO achieves the highest LC win rate (28.43%) among all methods in Table 1, suggesting the two-agent dynamics can be approximated without maintaining two separate models, which is practically valuable.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Implausible academic benchmark numbers require explanation.** Figure 2 reports approximately 75% on MMLU and 80% on GSM8k for TANPO (7B model trained on UltraFeedback), with Online DPO baselines at ~65% and ~70% respectively. These numbers substantially exceed known performance for 7B models of this family. For reference, Zephyr-7B-β (which uses the same base model and data) scores ~61% on MMLU under standard evaluation. The paper does not explain the evaluation protocol (e.g., few-shot vs. zero-shot, prompt format, exact metric) or provide the exact numeric table — it is relegated to the missing Appendix D (Table 3). Given that these values are read from a radar chart (~estimates), the gap between reported and expected numbers requires clarification. The exact numerical results and evaluation setup for these academic benchmarks must be provided before the empirical claims can be fully trusted.

2. **No statistical significance or variance information.** Every result — AlpacaEval 2.0 LC win rate, MT-Bench score, PairRM win rates, academic benchmarks — is reported as a single point estimate with no error bars, confidence intervals, or indication of multiple runs. The improvement of TANPO max-player over Online DPO (24.36 → 25.05 in LC win rate, a ~0.69% difference, inferred from comparing Table 1 and the text) falls within a range that could easily be explained by evaluation noise in AlpacaEval 2.0. Without variance estimates, the reader cannot assess whether the reported advantages are reliable. This is a notable omission even by the standards of this field (the INPO paper at ICLR 2025 Oral, avg 6.0, received the same criticism but was accepted nonetheless — however, that paper's claims were larger in magnitude and on a stronger base model).

3. **Theory-practice gap for TANPO is acknowledged but significant.** The paper states (Section 5, line 219): "the theoretical analysis in this section also applies to TANPO (Algorithm 1), provided the reward function class \(\mathcal{R}\) meets Assumption 4 outlined in Appendix C." The practical implementation uses neural network policies with no verification that Assumption 4 holds. The theoretical guarantees of sublinear regret therefore do not automatically transfer to the implemented algorithm. The paper should either provide evidence that the practical setting approximately satisfies these conditions, or explicitly discuss the gap. The phrase "provably efficient" in the title and abstract applied to the practical algorithms is somewhat misleading given this gap.

### Minor

1. **SADPO lacks formal theoretical support.** The paper presents SADPO as "supported by both theoretical analysis and empirical evidence" (Abstract), but no theorem, regret bound, or formal guarantee is provided for SADPO. The connection to the theoretical framework is intuitive (min-player-style objective in Equation 16) but informal. This is acceptable for a practical variant, but the claims of theoretical support for SADPO are overstated.

2. **Min-player derivation contains a vague step.** The transition from Equation (12) to (13) involves "omitting irrelevant terms" without specifying which terms are irrelevant and why. This makes the derivation less reproducible than it should be for a core algorithmic contribution.

3. **No hyperparameter sensitivity analysis.** The parameter \(\eta\) controls the exploration-exploitation tradeoff inherited from the theoretical framework, and \(K\) in SADPO controls the diversity of the rejection sampling. Neither is studied. Given that the theoretical analysis sets \(\eta\) as a function of \(T\) and \(d_{\text{TGEC}}\), the practical choice of \(\eta\) is opaque.

4. **The data diversity analysis (Figure 1) is thin.** Only one diversity metric is used (difference in length-normalized \(\log \pi_{\text{ref}}\) probability), on only 500 response pairs, with no variance. Additional metrics (embedding distance, n-gram overlap) and larger samples would strengthen this mechanistic claim.
