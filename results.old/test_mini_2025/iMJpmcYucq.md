## Summary

This paper proposes a control-variate-based variance-reduced estimator for the Bures–Wasserstein gradient used in Gaussian variational inference. The core idea is simple and well-motivated: since the variational Gaussian distribution should progressively resemble the target, the score of the variational Gaussian (Σ⁻¹(X−m)) serves as a natural control variate for the stochastic gradient. The authors provide theoretical conditions under which variance reduction occurs (Theorems 1–2), derive improved optimization bounds conditional on variance reduction (Theorems 3–4), and demonstrate order-of-magnitude accuracy gains over prior Bures–Wasserstein methods on synthetic problems. The method adds negligible computational overhead (O(d²) reuse of the Cholesky factor).

## Strengths

- **Order-of-magnitude accuracy improvement over prior BW methods.** Figure 3 shows SVRGVI achieves a final KL divergence of ≈10⁻² at d=200, while SGVI and BWGD remain at ≈10³ — a difference of roughly five orders of magnitude. This is a concrete, quantitative improvement over the best prior BW-VI algorithms (Diao et al., 2023; Lambert et al., 2022). Verified in Section 5 (lines 264–265): "for d = 200, the difference between SVRGVI and SGVI/BWGD is 5 orders of magnitude, 10⁻² versus 10³."

- **Provable variance reduction in a neighborhood of the optimum (Theorem 1) and for large-variance distributions (Theorem 2).** Theorem 1 proves that under a mild smoothness condition on ΔV, the proposed estimator has strictly smaller variance than the MC estimator for every μ inside a Wasserstein ball around the optimal distribution. Theorem 2 covers strong-log-concave targets. These are the first such guarantees for single-sample BW gradient estimation; SGVI offered no variance-reduction analysis. Verified in Section 4.

- **Minimal computational overhead.** Section 3 explicitly argues that the control variate reuses the Cholesky factor already computed for sampling, adding only O(d²) cost to an algorithm that already requires O(d³) for the covariance update. Verified in lines 148–149.

- **Empirical validation of the U-shaped variance curve.** Figure 2 (right) plots empirical variance as a function of c, showing the predicted parabolic shape with minimum near c≈1.25 and variance reduction for all c∈(0,2). This directly confirms Lemma 1's formula. Verified in lines 138–139 and Figure 2 caption.

- **Clear advantage over Euclidean VI in high dimensions.** Figure 3 shows that SVRGVI outperforms EVI (full-rank Gaussian VI with Adam) at d=50 and d=200, while SGVI and BWGD do not. This demonstrates the method can beat a well-tuned parameter-space baseline where previous BW methods fail. Verified in Figure 3 and lines 265–266.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Conditional convergence theory does not bridge to the implemented algorithm.** Theorems 3 and 4 assume variance reduction at every iteration (condition (7) with τₖ < 1). However, Theorem 1 only guarantees variance reduction in a neighborhood of the optimum, and Theorem 2 only for large-variance distributions under strong convexity. The paper does not establish that the iterates generated by Algorithm 1 actually stay within either region, nor does it provide conditions on initialization or step size that would ensure this. Remark 2 offers a way to guarantee variance reduction at each iteration for convex V, but the required upper bound on cₖ depends on the unknown quantity Tr(𝔼μₖ∇²V). Since the practical choice c=0.9 is fixed, the theoretical guarantees are conditional in a way that the experiments do not verify. The impressive experimental results demonstrate the method works, but the theory as presented does not fully explain why.

### Minor

- **All experiments use synthetic data.** Even the Bayesian logistic regression uses synthetic covariates (Xᵢ ~ 𝒩(0, I_d)) and synthetic responses. While synthetic targets are reasonable for controlled comparisons, the lack of any real-data benchmark (e.g., a UCI classification task with a Bayesian logistic regression or Gaussian process) makes it harder to assess whether the variance reduction degrades when the target posterior deviates from Gaussianity in realistic ways. The paper acknowledges this limitation (line 293: "our experiments focused on synthetic targets"), but including even one real-data experiment would substantially strengthen the practical impact.

- **The convergence bounds improve constants but do not change the overall rate, and they rely on restrictive step-size conditions.** The improvement in the exponential contraction rate for the strongly convex case (from αNη/2 to (3−τ_max,∞)αNη/4) can at most double the effective rate if τ_max,∞ ≈ 0, but τ_max,∞ is not controlled by the algorithm. The noise-term improvement depends on τ_max,E. The bounds are asymptotic in small η (≲), and the step-size condition for Theorem 4 (η ≤ α²/(48β³)) is very restrictive — the experiments use η=1, which likely does not satisfy this condition for typical targets. The theory therefore does not strongly support the observed dramatic improvements; the experiments do, but independently of the theory.

- **Missing definition of Σ̃ in the main text (Theorem 3).** The quantity C = 24β³λ_max(Σ̃) uses Σ̃ which is not defined in the visible main text (it is deferred to the appendix). This makes the bound hard to interpret without consulting the appendix.

### Trivial

- **Convergence curves lack any measure of variability.** Figure 3 and 4 show only bold average lines over 10 runs. Adding shaded regions, error bars, or a brief note on variance would help assess reliability.

- **The "≲" asymptotic notation is somewhat loose.** Non-asymptotic bounds with explicit constants would be more informative, though this is consistent with the conventions of the field (SGVI also uses ≲).
