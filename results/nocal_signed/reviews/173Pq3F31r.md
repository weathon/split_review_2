## Summary
This paper proposes a unified framework for jointly modeling Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. It introduces a Seq2Seq formulation of EPR that eliminates the need for note-level alignment, and a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The approach is evaluated on the ASAP dataset with objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation analysis.

## Strengths
- **Seq2Seq EPR without note-level alignment is a practical contribution.** The paper demonstrates competitive alignment rates (Table 3, Ours PSR: 92.27%) without the expensive note-level alignment required by prior EPR systems (Rhyu et al., 2022; Jeong et al., 2019; Tang et al., 2023), relaxing a real bottleneck in EPR data preparation.
- **The Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module is well-motivated.** Prior EPR systems require composer labels or manual expressive parameters, excluding non-expert users. Generating style embeddings from score content alone via a diffusion model addresses this usability gap, and the modular separation from the joint model is a sensible design choice.
- **The inverse-task framing (EPR ↔ APT) is genuinely insightful.** The analogy to ASR/TTS duality in speech is clearly articulated in Section 1 and Figure 1, and provides a coherent motivation for the joint modeling direction.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The central claim of mutual supervision from joint modeling is not cleanly supported.** The paper's headline contribution is a *unified* framework where the two tasks "leverage the duality between the two tasks for mutual supervision." However: (a) there is no APT-only ablation of the proposed architecture — the APT baselines in Table 1 are entirely different systems, so we cannot tell whether joint training helps or hurts APT; (b) the EPR-Only variant (Table 2) is trained only on the ASAP dataset (967 performances), while the full joint model also uses 75K unpaired MusicXML files and YouTube-transcribed performances, so the confound of data quantity prevents isolating any joint-training effect. The paper's own phrasing acknowledges this ("joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data," line 235–236) but still presents the comparison as validating the joint framework. This is the most significant missing control.
- **The disentanglement claim is stronger than the evidence warrants.** (a) Content embeddings encode substantial composer information (29.99% accuracy vs. ~4% random chance for 25 composers, Table 4). The paper acknowledges this but still summarizes it as "confirming successful disentanglement" (line 257). (b) The paper does not test whether style embeddings can be decoded to recover content-level attributes — a natural control for verifying information separation in the style channel. (c) What is implemented is architectural separation (global style vector + KL regularization) rather than proven factor disentanglement where each latent dimension independently controls one generative factor. The evidence is more consistent with *partial* separation than true disentanglement.

### Minor
- **The subjective evaluation has meaningful limitations.** Eleven participants provides low statistical power for between-system comparisons. The style transfer evaluation (Figure 5) uses only 3 samples, making those results anecdotal. No inter-rater reliability metric (e.g., Fleiss' κ) is reported, which limits confidence in the subjective ratings.
- **The PSR module is not compared against simpler alternatives.** The evaluation shows era-level clustering (Figure 4) and overall subjective ratings (Figure 2), but there is no comparison against simpler baselines such as the mean style vector, nearest-neighbor style retrieval, or random Gaussian style vectors. Without these, the value added by the diffusion-based generation is unclear.
- **The "unpaired data" framing could be clearer.** Unpaired data contributes only to intra-domain masked reconstruction (Eq. 4), not to cross-modal mapping. The cross-modal tasks (EPR, APT) are trained exclusively on the paired ASAP data. While this is a legitimate use of unpaired data, readers may infer stronger cross-modal supervision than what is actually implemented.
- **The model systematically under-generates expressive variation** (Table 2): σ²(D) = 0.58 vs. Human 1.72, σ²(V) = 151.03 vs. Human 241.04. The gap on velocity variance is particularly large, and while the subjective results support the quality of the outputs, the variance gap could be discussed more directly as a limitation.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- An APT-only ablation (same architecture, remove the EPR decoder and loss) trained on the full data would cleanly test whether joint training benefits APT.
- Re-training EPR-Only on the full data (paired + unpaired) would isolate the effect of joint training from the effect of additional data.
- For PSR, a subjective comparison with simpler baselines (mean style, random style) would help quantify the benefit of diffusion-based generation.
- The paper could more directly acknowledge that content embeddings encode composer information well above chance, and position the contribution as *partial* disentanglement/separation rather than perfect factor disentanglement.

## Suggestions

- Compare the PSR module against simpler style baselines (mean vector, retrieval) in a subjective test.
- Acknowledge more directly that content embeddings encode substantial non-content information and clarify the framing as partial separation rather than perfect disentanglement.
- Report inter-rater reliability for the subjective evaluation and increase the number of samples in the style transfer experiment.

## Score and Decision
The paper makes concrete contributions — Seq2Seq EPR without alignment and the well-motivated PSR module — and the inverse-task framing is genuinely interesting. However, the paper's central narrative (mutual supervision from joint modeling) is not convincingly supported by the experimental design due to a missing APT-only ablation and a confounded EPR-Only comparison. The disentanglement claim is also stronger than the evidence warrants. These are addressable issues, but in its current form the paper overstates what it demonstrates relative to its own experimental evidence.
