## Summary

This paper proposes the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework and the Class Representation Index (CRI) metric to evaluate whether concept annotations automatically generated by LLMs/VLMs are semantically sufficient for concept-based XAI. The framework compares a "fast mode" (direct visual classification) against a "slow mode" (text-only concept-grounded reasoning across five hierarchical refinement steps). Across six models and five datasets, the paper finds that slow mode consistently underperforms fast mode on fine-grained classification (CRI dropping ~25%) while achieving >90% on general-domain datasets. A key experiment (Table 4) demonstrates that multimodal fusion masks annotation insufficiency, directly challenging the "utility-as-proxy" assumption common in the concept bottleneck model literature.

## Strengths

- **Utility-as-proxy critique (Table 4) is the paper's strongest contribution.** Showing that fused mode (vision + concepts) achieves ~90% CRI while slow mode (concepts only) languishes at ~50% is a clean demonstration that high downstream task performance can coexist with poor concept annotations. This finding directly challenges a widely held assumption in the CBM literature and is well-executed.

- **The preliminary contradiction test (Table 1) is a well-designed methodological validation.** The paper validates its distractor-selection strategy before running main experiments, showing that random distractors yield only 14–20% contradiction rates while semantically-related distractors produce 34–45%. This is the right way to design a challenging evaluation and makes the main results more credible.

- **Broad and appropriate model coverage.** Testing six models across three families (GPT, Llama, Qwen) at two scales each, plus a side experiment with DeepSeek-R1, gives the results reasonable generality. The consistent pattern across models (slow mode underperforms fast mode on fine-grained data) is more convincing than a single-model result.

- **Fine-grained vs. general dataset comparison (Table 3) adds useful nuance.** The finding that LLMs *can* generate sufficient concepts for general domains (CIFAR-100, Caltech-101: CRI > 90%) but fail on fine-grained domains (CUB, Cars, Flowers: CRI ~50–60%) localizes the problem rather than over-generalizing it.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **CRI conflates concept quality with text-reasoning ability, creating an attribution problem the paper does not resolve.** The CRI measures whether a model, given its *own* textual concepts, can select the correct class from a 5-way forced choice. A low CRI could mean (a) concepts are semantically insufficient, (b) concepts are adequate but the model cannot reason from text to make fine-grained distinctions, or (c) both. The paper consistently attributes low CRI to (a) — "current annotation methods fail to provide sufficient semantic coverage" (abstract), "annotation insufficiency" (throughout) — but the experimental design cannot distinguish (a) from (b). The dramatic t=0 to t=1 drops (e.g., GPT-4o on CIFAR-100: 84.84% → 29.23%, a 55-point drop from one annotation step) are far more consistent with a modality-switch cost (vision→text) than with annotation quality degradation. Cross-model evaluation (concepts from model A evaluated by model B) would help disentangle these factors without requiring human supervision.

### Minor

- **The "Slow Mode Superiority" framing is strained.** The paper hypothesizes that slow (concept-based reasoning) mode should outperform fast (direct visual) mode, citing dual-process theory, and treats the empirical refutation as a surprising finding. But slow mode operates on compressed textual concept descriptions while fast mode uses full visual input — there is no principled reason to expect text-only reasoning to match vision-based classification. The finding that text-mediated reasoning underperforms direct visual classification on fine-grained data is empirically interesting on its own merits, but the framing inflates the novelty by setting up an unreasonably favorable expectation for the slow mode.

- **Equation (2) has a notational error.** The CRI formula writes $\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^t$, using $t$ (annotation step, max 5) as both the step parameter and the summation bound. The sum should run over test instances: $\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}$ where $l$ is the number of test cases. At $t=0$ the formula as written is undefined (division by zero). The empirical CRI values in the tables clearly use the correct formula, but someone implementing from the paper alone would get the wrong result.

- **Some specification gaps.** (a) "Three runs with different seeds" is mentioned for Figure 3 but what the seeds control is not stated (model temperature? distractor selection? data ordering?). (b) Table 4 — the paper's key utility-as-proxy result — reports no variance estimates.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Cross-model evaluation: use model A to generate concepts, model B to evaluate via CRI. This would help determine whether the bottleneck is concept quality or reasoning ability.
- Variance reporting for Table 4, and specification of what the seeds in the three-run experiments control.
- Qualitative examples of concepts at each refinement step would help readers understand the nature of the insufficiency.

## Suggestions

- Correct the notational error in Equation (2): replace $\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^t$ with $\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}$ where $l$ is the number of test cases.
- Acknowledge the confound between concept quality and text-reasoning ability explicitly in the limitations section. Consider cross-model evaluation to strengthen the central claim.
- Report variance for Table 4 and specify what the "seeds" govern in the three-run experiments.
- Tone down the "Slow Mode Superiority" framing — the finding that text-mediated concept reasoning underperforms direct visual classification on fine-grained data stands on its own empirical merit.

## Score and Decision

This paper addresses a genuine and underexplored problem — systematic validation of automated concept annotations in XAI — and contributes a reusable evaluation framework along with solid empirical findings. The utility-as-proxy critique (Table 4) is the standout contribution and should be of significant interest to the concept-based XAI community. The framework's design is methodologically sound, with careful distractor validation and broad model coverage. However, the paper has a structural attribution problem: CRI conflates concept quality with text-reasoning ability, and the paper consistently interprets low scores as evidence of "annotation insufficiency" without ruling out the alternative explanation that the model's text-grounded reasoning is the bottleneck. This does not invalidate the contribution, but it narrows the headline claim. The paper would benefit from either cross-model evaluation or revised claims that more precisely characterize what CRI measures. Given the strength of the core contribution and the addressability of the main weakness, this is a borderline accept.
