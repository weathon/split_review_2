## Summary

This paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments standard RL with a KL-divergence penalty between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), and claims a Bayesian adaptation scheme for the trade-off weight β that eliminates manual tuning. The KL-based shaping idea is clearly presented and demonstrated in a toy MDP, but the paper's central claimed novelty — Bayesian β adaptation — is never specified as a concrete mechanism, and the experimental evidence is critically incomplete.

## Strengths

- **Clear formulation of the core idea.** The KL(p||q) incentive shaping term (Section 3.2, Eq. 1–2) is presented cleanly and well-motivated. The relationship to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based RPE provides credible intellectual framing.

- **Toy experiment convincingly illustrates the intuition.** The 2-state MDP (Section 5.2, Table 1, Figure 1) shows clear convergence improvements for all β > 0 over standard Q-learning, with 7 seeds providing reasonable statistical support.

- **Clear distinction from KL-regularized RL.** Section 2.2 correctly distinguishes IncentRL's KL over *outcome prediction* from KL over *policy change* used in TRPO/PPO/SAC.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian adaptation mechanism — the paper's central claimed novelty — is never specified.** The abstract, introduction, and conclusion all state that the key contribution is treating β as a Bayesian random variable updated online. However, the paper contains: no prior distribution over β, no likelihood function (what data is β conditioned on?), no posterior update rule or inference method, no definition of what a "round" is (Figures 3, 4, Table 3), and no integration with the RL loop. The terms "prior," "likelihood," and any update equations are entirely absent from the paper (verified via grep). The central contribution cannot be understood, implemented, or evaluated. This is a structural failure for a method paper whose headline contribution is a Bayesian scheme.

### Major

- **Bayesian β results are disconnected from actual RL performance.** The paper's main claim rests on: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (line 228). Figures 3, 4, and Table 3 show only posterior concentration statistics — not learning curves, success rates, or any task-level comparison between the Bayesian β variant and fixed-β variants. The claim that Bayesian adaptation eliminates manual tuning is unsubstantiated by the evidence presented.

- **The prediction model p(o|s,a) is underspecified for all non-toy experiments.** For MountainCar, the paper simply states "p(o|s,a) is the predicted outcome distribution" (line 199) without specifying the outcome space, the model architecture, training procedure, or how the distribution is instantiated. For MiniGrid, even less detail is provided. The latent Gaussian discussion in Section 4.3 uses "can be" language and does not confirm actual implementation. The method cannot be reproduced.

- **The experimental evaluation is too narrow.** IncentRL is compared only against β=0 (standard RL). There are no comparisons to existing intrinsic motivation methods (curiosity-driven exploration — Pathak et al. 2017, Burda et al. 2018; ICM, RND) or to other adaptive β schemes (meta-gradient, annealing). This is a significant gap given that the paper is positioned within the intrinsic motivation literature (Section 2.1).

### Minor

- **The theoretical propositions provide limited insight.** Propositions 1 and 2 are basic continuity and extreme-value arguments that hold for any bounded reward perturbation and do not yield insight specific to the KL(p||q) formulation.

- **MountainCar results (Table 2) show high variance (std 535–909 across means 875–4136) and are based on only 3 seeds, with no learning curves.** The method helps only at β=0.1 (+18%) and substantially hurts at β≥0.3 (−53% to −75%), indicating sensitivity that undercuts the claimed robustness of the overall approach.

- **MiniGrid experiments test only two β values (0.0 and 0.01) with 3 seeds.** The fixed-β sweep is too sparse to determine whether β=0.01 is genuinely a well-chosen value or a guess.

- **The MiniGrid fixed sweep uses β=0.01, but the Bayesian β results (Figures 3, 4) show posterior concentration near β≈0.1 — an order of magnitude larger.** This discrepancy is not explained, nor is evidence provided that β≈0.1 would work on MiniGrid.

- Comparisons against meta-gradient or annealing-based β schedules would better support the Bayesian adaptation claims.
- A more comprehensive β sweep in MiniGrid with more seeds.
