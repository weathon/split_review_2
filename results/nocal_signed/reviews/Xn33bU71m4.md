## Summary

This paper presents a systematic evaluation of nine open-source LLMs (CodeLlama, Llama2, DeepSeek-V2, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen2.5, Qwen3, WizardCoder, Phi4, Gemma3) on two reverse engineering tasks: name recovery and type inference from stripped binaries. The authors build an automated pipeline that normalizes heterogeneous LLM outputs using an auxiliary LLM, and evaluate across 4 ISAs, 4 optimization levels, decompiled code, assembly code, and real-world firmware. The headline finding is that zero-shot LLMs perform poorly (F1 ~0.03), while fine-tuning yields modest but inconsistent improvements (variable name recovery up to 0.33 on some models, but function name and type inference mostly below 0.1).

## Strengths

- **Automated post-processing pipeline (§3.2).** Using an auxiliary LLM to normalize heterogeneous outputs into a consistent schema is a practical and reusable contribution. LLM-based evaluation pipelines typically struggle with format variation, and this design choice solves a real methodological problem for multi-model benchmarking.
- **Broad and honest coverage.** Nine models × 4 ISAs × 4 optimization levels, plus two input representations (decompiled and assembly) and real-world firmware with two decompilers — this is more comprehensive than any prior work. The paper reports mostly negative results without over-spinning them, which provides a realistic baseline for the field.
- **Negative results are useful.** Establishing that current LLMs (especially zero-shot) are not effective at RE tasks is a finding worth documenting, as it prevents wasted effort and clarifies where future work is needed.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Single-prompt confound undermines the zero-shot comparison (§3.2).** The paper uses one prompt template from a ChatGPT-era study for *all* models, without specifying whether base or instruction-tuned variants are used. Models that cannot parse the prompt format will score poorly regardless of their RE capability. The paper acknowledges that "no LLM consistently adhered to the required structure" (Finding 8) and uses an auxiliary LLM to normalize outputs, but this only handles format variation after the fact — it cannot recover information the model never generated because it didn't understand the instruction. This means the zero-shot results conflate instruction-following ability with RE capability, and specific cross-model comparisons (e.g., "Qwen3 at 0.06 outperforms CodeLlama at 0.04") may reflect prompt-compatibility differences rather than RE skill. The fine-tuning results are less affected by this confound (fine-tuned models learn the target format), but they represent a separate experimental condition.

- **Abstract claims an F1 of 0.37 for fine-tuned DeepSeek-R1 not supported in the main body.** The Introduction (line 19) states DeepSeek-R1 reaches an F1 of 0.37 after fine-tuning. The highest DeepSeek-R1 score in any main-body table is 0.17 (variable name recovery, O0, Table 3). Results for other architectures are relegated to the appendix (which is stripped by the parser), but a headline number in the abstract should be clearly traceable from the main paper's tables. As written, a reader cannot verify this claim.

### Minor

- **No quantitative comparison with prior specialized RE methods.** The paper cites SYMGEN, RESYM, VARBERT, Debin, DirI, and others (§2) but provides no baseline numbers from these approaches. A benchmarking paper cannot contextualize whether F1=0.03 is worse than, comparable to, or better than what the field already achieves without such comparisons. This limits the paper's contribution as a reference benchmark.

- **No variance or statistical significance reporting.** All F1 scores are single numbers. LLM generation is stochastic, and at the low values reported (0.00–0.06 for most settings), a difference of 0.02 could easily be noise. The reproducibility statement acknowledges randomness but does not quantify it.

- **Strict type matching systematically penalizes correct inferences (§3.4).** The paper uses exact-match equality for type inference, including user-defined and composite types. Decompilers assign ad-hoc synthesized names (e.g., `undefined4`, `_DWORD`) that differ from ground-truth C type names — this will systematically undercount correct inferences in ways that do not reflect model error. The paper acknowledges strictness as a design choice but does not discuss this caveat.

- **Dataset distribution is narrow (§4.1).** All functions come from GNU software compiled with GCC. Results may not generalize to embedded software, malware, or binaries from other toolchains. The firmware experiment partially addresses this but is only evaluated zero-shot.

- **Findings 4–5 about "training data quality" are speculative.** The paper attributes performance differences (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 underperforming Qwen2.5 despite identical architecture) to "training data quality" without controlling for training objective, tokenizer, hyperparameters, or other confounding factors. These are interesting observations but the causal attribution is unsupported.

- **Fine-tuning contamination risk.** Both training (160k functions) and test (40k functions) sets are drawn from the same pool of GNU projects. The paper deduplicates at the function level but does not report project-level overlap, leaving a potential contamination concern unaddressed.

### Trivial

- The paper does not specify for any model whether the base, instruct, or chat variant was loaded via Hugging Face Transformers, making exact reproduction dependent on guesswork.
- Firmware evaluation (Table 6) reports only zero-shot results; adding fine-tuned results would be informative since the main paper shows fine-tuning sometimes yields large gains.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Adding 2–3 prior-art RE method baselines would substantially strengthen the benchmarking contribution.
- Reporting variance (even mean±std over 3 seeds) for a representative subset of results.
- A brief discussion of why fine-tuning sometimes *reduces* performance (e.g., DeepSeek-V2 under O1), beyond noting the observation.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own empirical findings, the reviews surface two observations worth synthesizing. First, the single-prompt design choice means the paper's most specific claims (which model is best under zero-shot) are the least reliable, while its most general claim (LLMs are not good at RE without fine-tuning) is the most robust — supported by the confound-free fine-tuning results. Second, the absence of prior-art baselines leaves a precise unanswered question: "How much worse are LLMs than specialized RE models?" This is a more actionable gap than the paper's current framing of "LLMs are limited."

## Suggestions

1. **Control for the prompt confound** by either (a) using each model's native chat template/instruction format, or (b) running a controlled ablation on a subset of models to demonstrate that prompt format is not the primary cause of low zero-shot scores.
2. **Resolve the 0.37 discrepancy**: either remove it from the abstract if it is not a representative main-result number, or clearly qualify which task/architecture/optimization-level combination it corresponds to and ensure it appears in the main paper.
3. **Add at least 2–3 baselines** from prior specialized RE methods (e.g., SYMGEN, VARBERT) to contextualize the F1 scores.
4. **Report variance** (mean±std over multiple seeds) for at least a subset of configurations.
5. **Discuss the decompiler-synthesized type name caveat** for strict type matching.
6. **Specify model variants** (base vs. instruct) and Hugging Face model IDs in the paper.
