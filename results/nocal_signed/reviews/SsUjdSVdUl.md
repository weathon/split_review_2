## Summary

This paper proposes Critique-RL, a two-stage online RL approach for training critiquing language models without requiring a stronger supervisor. The key insight is that indirect reward signals (based on whether the actor's refined output improves) optimize helpfulness but leave discriminability — the critic's ability to judge whether a response is correct — poorly optimized. The paper first diagnoses this failure mode empirically, then proposes Stage I (direct discriminability optimization via correctness-matching reward) and Stage II (helpfulness optimization with regularization to maintain discriminability). Experiments on math reasoning tasks across two model scales (3B, 7B) show consistent improvements over SFT, STaR, Retroformer, and CTRL baselines.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem with clear empirical diagnosis.** The paper identifies a non-obvious failure mode: training critique models with indirect rewards (based on actor refinement) improves helpfulness but leaves discriminability poorly optimized, producing conservative or aggressive critics. This diagnosis is concretely demonstrated with training dynamics (Figure 3, Section 4.1), showing discriminability for correct vs. incorrect responses diverges under indirect rewards. This is the strongest part of the paper.

- **Clean, principled two-stage solution.** Stage I optimizes discriminability via a direct correctness-matching reward; Stage II optimizes helpfulness while regularizing toward the Stage I policy. The design follows directly from the diagnosis and is not over-engineered. The ablation study (Table 3) confirms each stage contributes and that removing discrimination regularization in Stage II hurts performance — providing strong within-framework evidence for the core claim.

- **Consistent empirical advantage across models and tasks.** Across two model scales (3B, 7B), three in-domain datasets, and two OOD datasets, Critique-RL outperforms all baselines on accuracy and discriminability. Gains on MATH and GSM8K are substantial (e.g., +4.54 on MATH for 7B over CTRL). The OOD generalization (Table 4) provides credible evidence that training does not overfit to dataset-specific patterns.

- **Avoids stronger supervision.** The SFT initialization data is generated by the same-scale model (Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) rather than a much larger model like GPT-4. Combined with the online RL approach, this shows effective critics can be bootstrapped without a stronger annotator — consistent with the claimed motivation.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Confounding of RL algorithm with reward design in main comparisons.** The paper compares Critique-RL (which uses RLOO; line 274) against Retroformer (PPO) and CTRL (GRPO; line 250). Since PPO, GRPO, and RLOO differ in variance properties, sample efficiency, and training stability, the headline comparisons in Table 1 conflate reward design with RL algorithm choice. It is impossible to attribute performance differences to the two-stage reward structure alone. The ablation studies within a fixed RLOO framework (Table 3: w/o Stage I, w/o Stage II, etc.) partially address this by showing the two-stage design matters when the RL algorithm is held constant, but the paper's claims of outperforming Retroformer/CTRL specifically are not cleanly supported. A controlled comparison implementing baseline reward functions within the same RLOO framework would resolve this.

### Minor

- **No variance or significance reporting.** Table 1 reports single-point estimates without error bars, standard deviations, or significance tests. Several comparisons are close enough that variance matters (e.g., AQuA 7B: Critique-RL 65.75 vs CTRL 64.96 — a 0.79 point gap; AQuA 3B: 56.69 vs 53.54 — a 3.15 point gap). RL training is inherently noisy, and without multiple seeds or confidence intervals, the reader cannot assess whether smaller-margin gains are meaningful. The paper should report results over at least 3 random seeds.

- **The judgment extraction function f(x,y,c) is underspecified.** The Stage I reward (Equation 7) requires extracting the critique model's binary correctness judgment from free-text output via f(x,y,c). Section 4.2 states models are "prompted to give correctness judgments for each step, and also provide a judgment for the final answer," and Figure 2 shows structured labels. However, the parsing procedure is not described at all: how are format deviations handled? Is the judgment taken from step-level or final-answer assessment? What is the parsing success rate? Since the Acc@Dis evaluation metric depends on the same extraction, the reward and evaluation share a dependency on this unspecified parsing step.

- **Missing reproducibility details.** (a) The SFT data filtering rate is not reported: line 148 states "We filter the critique data based on the correctness of refinement to ensure the quality" but does not state what fraction of generated critiques is retained. (b) The relationship between D_RL and D_SFT is not clarified — whether they are the same 6,000 examples or different datasets is unclear.

- **Hyperparameter β1 = 0.2 lacks justification.** Both r_refine and r_dis are binary (0/1), so β1 = 0.2 gives r_refine an effective weight of 1.0 versus 0.2 for r_dis, making refinement correctness dominate the Stage II objective. The paper does not discuss this imbalance or provide sensitivity analysis for β1.

- **AQuA results noted but not analyzed.** The paper observes that SFT and STaR baselines produce negative Δ on AQuA (e.g., SFT 7B: Δ = -3.94), while Critique-RL yields positive gains. Since AQuA is multiple-choice with plausible distractors — structurally different from free-form MATH/GSM8K — this deserves discussion about where the method may differ in applicability and why.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a clear, well-motivated contribution. The core finding — that indirect RL rewards alone fail to optimize critique discriminability, and that explicit two-stage optimization is effective — is empirically demonstrated and practically useful. The ablation studies provide within-framework evidence that the two-stage design drives the gains. The main weakness is the RL-algorithm confound in the headline comparisons, which is meaningful but does not undermine the core claim (supported by within-framework ablations). The missing variance reporting is a secondary concern. These issues are fixable and do not diminish the paper's substantive contribution.
