## Summary

This paper proposes a multi-level conditional diffusion model for protein generation that operates at three levels (amino acid, backbone, all-atom), along with a proposed evaluation metric called Protein-MMD. The high-level motivation — generating functional proteins while ensuring conditional consistency — is well-framed and addresses a recognized gap. However, the paper suffers from a fatal structural flaw and several major omissions that prevent it from meeting the standard of a top-tier venue.

## Strengths

- **Multi-level hierarchical generation design (Section 3.1, Table 4):** The paper proposes generating at three distinct levels of protein representation with lower levels conditioning higher ones. The ablation study (limited though it is) shows that removing the backbone level, the all-atom level, or both degrades conditional consistency metrics on the EC dataset, providing some empirical evidence that the multi-level design contributes.

- **Systematic study of maximum sequence length (Section 4.3, Figure 5):** The paper investigates a practical hyperparameter (maximum sequence length 128, 256, 512) that prior work typically fixes without justification, and demonstrates a positive correlation between sequence length and conditional consistency. This is a concrete finding of practical value for the field.

- **Honest analysis on the GO dataset with fine-grained IoU case study (Section 4.2, Table 3):** The paper reports that its model ranks second on the GO dataset (rather than only showing favorable results) and performs a deeper analysis using an ESM-MLP classifier. While the IoU_max argument has issues (discussed below), the willingness to report and analyze a weaker result with additional diagnostics is a positive.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

**1. Protein-MMD — a main claimed contribution — is never defined in the paper.**

Protein-MMD is listed as a core contribution (abstract: *"We also propose a Protein-MMD, a new reliable evaluation metric"*; contributions bullet: *"propose Protein-MMD, a novel metric to evaluate conditional consistency for protein generation tasks by leveraging language models"*; introduction: *"We prove that our Protein-MMD provides a more accurate measure that reflects the given condition"*). It is used as the primary conditional consistency metric across all experiments (Table 2, Section 4.2, Section 4.3, Section 4.4).

Yet the paper contains **no definition** of this metric — no mathematical formulation, no algorithm, no description of how language models are used, no specification of the feature space or kernel. Appendix A.1, titled "PROTEIN-MMD," contains only a garbled "theorem" and "proof" that do not define any metric:

> Theorem: ∃N∈ℕ⁺, ∀i>N, d(xⁱ, Cᵏ) < min_{j≠k} d(xⁱ, Cʲ) where Cʲ is any other class.

The quantities d, xⁱ, Cᵏ are undefined; notation is internally inconsistent (Cⁱ vs Cᵏ vs C̄ⁱ vs Cᵏ̃); and the "proof" is circular. This does not constitute a definition, a validation, or even a sketch of a metric. Since the metric is claimed as a contribution *and* serves as the basis for evaluating the model itself, the paper's central experimental claims are uninterpretable. This is not a fixable omission — a paper that claims a metric as a contribution must define it within the submission.

### Major

**2. The multi-level diffusion model is critically underspecified.**

Section 3.1 describes graph-based *representations* at three levels (amino acid, backbone, all-atom) but never specifies:

- What is being diffused at each level? Raw 3D coordinates? Latent vectors? Discrete tokens?
- What are the forward/reverse process equations for the multi-level setup? The standard DDPM loss (Eq. 4) is given in preliminaries but not extended to three levels or hierarchical conditioning.
- How does lower-level conditioning work — cross-attention, adaptive layer norm, concatenation, or some other mechanism?
- Is there a single diffusion model operating jointly across levels, or separate models per level with information passing between them?
- What is the training objective that accounts for all three levels and their interactions?

The paper states only that information is "processed through a conditional diffusion flow where the condition comes from a lower level" (line 91). This is not a specification; it is a caption. Without these details, the method is not reproducible and the claim of a "novel multi-level conditional diffusion model" is unsubstantiated.

**3. Protein-FID is used as an evaluation metric but never defined.**

Protein-FID appears in Table 2 and throughout the results (Section 4.2, Section 4.3) as a conditional consistency metric. The FID preliminary (Section 2.2) describes the standard FID computed on Inception-v3 features for images, but the paper never specifies what feature extractor, embedding space, or reference distribution Protein-FID uses. Is it FID on ESM embeddings? On some other latent space? What dataset statistics are used as the reference? None of this is stated. A reader cannot reproduce or interpret these numbers.

**4. Several baselines are not appropriate for the task and their adaptation is undescribed.**

The paper compares against ESM2, ProstT5, and ProteinMPNN as baselines for conditional *de novo* protein generation from functional annotations (EC numbers, GO terms). ESM2 and ProstT5 are protein language models trained for sequence modeling and representation learning, not conditional generation. ProteinMPNN is an inverse folding model (structure → sequence), not a *de novo* generator. The paper does not describe how these models were adapted to generate proteins conditioned on EC numbers or GO terms. Without this information, the comparison is uninterpretable — the baselines may be solving fundamentally different tasks or may have been adapted in ad-hoc ways that disadvantage them. ProteoGAN and LatentDiff are appropriate baselines, but the inclusion of three mismatched ones undermines the evaluation's credibility.

**5. The "Protein Variational Auto-encoder model" appears without prior introduction.**

Line 131 (experimental setup) states "To construct the Protein Variational Auto-encoder model ..." but no VAE was introduced in the Method section (Section 3). Its relationship to the diffusion model — is the VAE the encoder? Does the diffusion model operate in the VAE's latent space? — is never explained. This is a significant missing architectural detail.

### Minor

- **Limited ablation:** The ablation (Section 4.4) removes backbone/all-atom levels from the full model and observes a drop. It cannot remove the amino acid level "because the amino acid is required for evaluation." This means the study never tests whether the *multi-level* approach is superior to a single-level (amino-acid-only) diffusion model trained from scratch, which is the most directly relevant comparison. The observed drop from removing components within an already-trained multi-level architecture is unsurprising.

- **IoU_max argument is not statistically sound:** The case study (Section 4.2) argues that a higher max IoU outweighs a lower mean IoU. For any generative model that samples more diverse outputs, the maximum over samples will tend to be larger by chance. Without controlling for the number of samples or the diversity distribution, this comparison is inconclusive.

- **No variance or statistical significance reported:** Results in Table 2 are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals, error bars, or significance tests. For a single run per configuration, it is impossible to assess whether observed differences are meaningful.

- **SE(3)-invariance through canonicalization has known limitations:** The autoregressive canonicalization (Section 3.1, Equations 4-5) sequentially anchors residues to a fixed frame. This approach is known to accumulate errors for longer chains and does not provide the same theoretical guarantees as true SE(3)-equivariant architectures. The paper does not discuss these limitations or compare against equivariant alternatives.

## Suggestions

The paper needs substantial revision before it could be considered for a top venue.
