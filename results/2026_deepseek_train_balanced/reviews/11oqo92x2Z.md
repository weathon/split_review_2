## Summary

This paper introduces Solis-seg, a deep neural network for segmenting solar farms in Sentinel-2 satellite imagery, and uses it to benchmark both transfer learning strategies (training directly on segmentation vs. pre-training on classification then fine-tuning) and DARTS-based Neural Architecture Search (Auto-DeepLab) on this task. The paper reports 96.26% mIoU and finds that direct segmentation training outperforms transfer from classification (F1: 0.962 vs. 0.89), while NAS produces architectures that rarely beat simple baselines.

## Strengths

- **Honest empirical accounting of NAS limitations in a real-world domain.** The paper provides a clear-eyed assessment: out of 14 NAS trials, only one architecture outperformed any baseline (Section 5.2), a randomly generated ChatGPT architecture outperformed nearly all NAS-discovered architectures (Section 5.2), and the computational cost of NAS (search + ~59h training) vastly exceeds training an off-the-shelf model directly (46h) with negligible performance gain (Section 5.3, Table 3). This candid documentation of NAS's practical limitations is useful empirical guidance for practitioners.

- **Controlled comparison between direct segmentation training and classification-to-segmentation transfer learning.** The paper explicitly states that "Solis-seg and Solis-transfer differ solely in their training methodology" (Section 5.1), holding the architecture constant. This isolates the effect of the training strategy and provides a cleaner test of transfer learning's value than is common in the remote sensing literature. The finding (F1: 0.962 vs. 0.89) suggests task-specific training can substantially outperform the default transfer-learning pipeline, even if this result needs stronger replication.

- **Real-world deployment to discover previously undocumented solar farms.** The model was deployed on New York State satellite imagery, identifying ~583 potential solar farms, including installations not documented in OpenStreetMap (Section 4.3). The paper honestly reports generalization challenges (partial detections, potential false positives), providing a realistic assessment of deployment behavior.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The central claim about transfer learning rests on a single run of Solis-seg without variance or replication.** The paper's most provocative finding — that direct segmentation training substantially outperforms transfer learning from classification — is supported by exactly one run of Solis-seg (Section 5.1: "the single experiment conducted with Solis-seg"). No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or multiple-seed trials are reported. A single run could be an outlier due to random seed, data split variation, or hyperparameter configuration. Given the strength of the claim ("clearly highlighting the effectiveness of task-specific training"), this level of statistical evidence is insufficient. The comparison is also weakened by the fact that mIoU was not captured for Solis-transfer (Section 3.4.2), forcing reliance on F1, and the paper does not document whether hyperparameters were tuned equally for both models.

- **The dataset is inadequately documented, making results unverifiable and comparisons to prior work uninterpretable.** The paper trains on "over 200,000 Sentinel-2 level-2A images" described only as "approximately half positive examples" from "various geographical regions" (Section 3.2). No information is provided about: (a) which countries or regions are included, (b) how ground-truth labels were created and by whom, (c) the temporal range of the imagery, (d) the distribution of solar farm sizes or types, (e) the source and curation of negative examples, or (f) the construction of the train/test split beyond "80/20." Without this documentation, the reported 96.26% mIoU is an unanchored number. The paper's abstract claims to "outperform existing solutions" (e.g., Kruitwagen et al.'s 90% IoU), but Section 5.1 itself acknowledges "an apples-to-apples comparison isn't feasible." The abstract and conclusion's framing of a "record" performance is misleading.

- **Solis-seg's architecture is never explicitly stated.** Despite being the paper's central proposed model, the paper never names Solis-seg's architecture. The closest the reader gets is: (a) Solis-seg and Solis-transfer "differ solely in their training methodology" (Section 5.1), (b) Solis-transfer has "a ResNet component" (Section 5.1), and (c) "an off-the-shelf model like ResNet50-DeepLab performed best on our dataset" (Section 5.3). The reader can piece together that Solis-seg is likely a ResNet50-DeepLab, but the paper should state this explicitly. For a paper whose headline contribution is a model achieving state-of-the-art performance, omitting the architecture specification is a basic documentation failure.

### Minor

- **The New York deployment lacks ground-truth validation.** The paper reports that Solis-seg found ~583 potential solar farms, "several of which are not documented in OpenStreetMap" (Section 4.3). However, no precision or recall figures are computed for this deployment because no held-out ground truth was collected. Without human verification or comparison to an independent reference dataset, the deployment experiment is anecdotal — it demonstrates the model can produce predictions, but not that those predictions are accurate in an unseen geographic region.

- **The ChatGPT-generated architecture is an uncontrolled baseline.** The "randomly generated architecture produced by ChatGPT" (Section 5.2) is labeled as a lower-bound performance measure, but its generation process is neither random nor reproducible. Without documenting the exact prompt used, another researcher cannot replicate this baseline. The fact that it outperformed nearly all NAS-discovered architectures would be more informative if it were a genuinely random architecture sampled from the DARTS search space.

- **The "outperforming existing solutions" framing in the abstract overstates the evidence.** While Section 5.1 properly notes that cross-dataset comparison is not apples-to-apples, the abstract (line 4) and conclusion (line 240) use unqualified language ("outperforming existing solutions," "record mean IoU," "sets new performance benchmarks"). Since the dataset and evaluation setup differ from prior work (Kruitwagen et al., DeepSolar), these claims should be explicitly qualified as dataset-relative.

### Trivial

- None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Reporting results from multiple seeds (3–5 runs) for Solis-seg would substantially strengthen the transfer-learning experiment.
- Documenting the dataset composition (countries per region, labeling protocol, temporal distribution) would enable reproducibility and allow readers to interpret the results.
- Providing the exact prompt used for the ChatGPT baseline or replacing it with a proper random sample from the DARTS search space would improve reproducibility.
- A human-verified evaluation on a held-out geographic region (e.g., sampling and manually labeling a subset of the New York predictions) would strengthen the deployment experiment.

## Removed Points

These points were considered but removed from the main weaknesses; treat them with caution:

1. **"Incompatible metrics for transfer learning comparison"** (Harsh Critic): The critic claimed the comparison between Solis-seg and Solis-transfer used different metrics, making it invalid. However, the paper reports F1 for both models (Solis-seg F1=0.962, Solis-transfer F1=0.89; lines 122, 175). While mIoU was not captured for Solis-transfer, a common metric (F1) is available for comparison. The real issue is the n=1 design, which is addressed above.

2. **"NAS equivocation/framing contradictory"** (Harsh Critic): The critic argued the paper equivocates by simultaneously saying NAS fails and that NAS "has potential." However, the paper honestly reports both sides — 1/14 trials beat baselines (Section 5.2), while the top NAS model "does not lag significantly behind" Solis-seg (Section 5.3). This is a nuanced finding, not equivocation, and does not constitute a concrete flaw.

3. **"Background reads as textbook summary"** (Harsh Critic): This is a subjective formatting/presentation critique that does not identify a factual or methodological error. The background section provides necessary context.

4. **"No description of Solis-transfer's architecture"** (Harsh Critic): The paper states Solis-transfer has "a ResNet component" (line 171) and that Solis-seg and Solis-transfer share the same architecture (line 171). This is sufficient for the experimental design.

5. **"Claim about NAS being uncharted territory overstates gap"** (Harsh Critic): The paper applies this specifically to NAS for solar farm identification (line 16: "its [NAS's] application in the field of solar farm identification... remains uncharted territory"). Prior work by Kruitwagen et al. and others did not use NAS, so this claim is accurate within its intended scope.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface no genuinely novel synthesis beyond what the paper itself states: that DARTS-based NAS does not justify its cost for this specific segmentation task, and that direct segmentation training outperforms classification-to-segmentation transfer. Both findings are honestly reported but are specific empirical observations rather than generalizable insights.

## Suggestions

1. State Solis-seg's architecture explicitly (e.g., "Solis-seg uses a ResNet50-DeepLab architecture, identical to Solis-transfer, with training differing only in the loss function and the absence of a classification pre-training stage").
2. Run Solis-seg with at least 3 random seeds and report mean ± std for both mIoU and F1, so readers can assess whether the 0.962 F1 is typical or an outlier.
3. Add a table documenting the dataset: number of images per country/region, labeling protocol (expert annotators? automated? quality control steps?), temporal range, and class balance per split.
4. Qualify all comparisons to prior work as dataset-relative in the abstract and conclusion — e.g., "on our European dataset, Solis-seg achieves 96.26% mIoU," not "outperforming existing solutions" or "record."
5. Either document the exact prompt used for the ChatGPT baseline or replace it with a genuinely random architecture sampled from the DARTS search space.

## Score and Decision

This paper addresses a practically important problem and contains some honest, useful empirical findings — particularly the candid assessment of NAS's limited value and the controlled transfer learning comparison. However, the experimental rigor does not meet ICLR standards. The paper's central claim rests on a single training run without variance reporting, the dataset that supports all quantitative results is barely documented, the main model's architecture is not explicitly specified, and performance claims relative to prior work are misleadingly framed. The paper reads more as an applied report suitable for a remote sensing venue than as a methodological contribution to a top-tier machine learning conference.
