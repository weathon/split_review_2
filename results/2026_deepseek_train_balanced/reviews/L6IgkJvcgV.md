## Summary

This paper proposes OASIS, a four-component toolbox for measuring and understanding stereotypes in text-to-image (T2I) models. Its core conceptual contribution is distinguishing *stereotypes* (directional deviations from real-world attribute distributions) from *biases* (any statistical imbalance), formalizing this with a Stereotype Score and spectral variance metric (WALS), plus two diagnostic tools: StOP (which discovers internal stereotypical associations via prompt optimization) and SPI (which traces when stereotypical attributes emerge during diffusion). Experiments on SDv2, SDv3, and FLUX.1 across three nationalities show that newer T2I models still exhibit significant stereotypes, and that stereotypical attributes are encoded early in generation.

## Strengths

- **Sociologically-grounded distinction between bias and stereotype.** The paper correctly formalizes that prior work relying on statistical parity conflates the two: a model generating predominantly female doctors is labeled "stereotypical" by existing metrics, yet the actual stereotype is that doctors are male (lines 15–16, Section 1). The directional definition (exceeding the real-world distribution in the stereotypical direction) is a genuine conceptual advance over uniform-parity baselines.

- **Stereotype Score + WALS as complementary measures revealing a quality–diversity trade-off.** Figure 5 (lines 69–70) empirically demonstrates that SDv3 achieves lower stereotype scores on some attributes while simultaneously collapsing attribute diversity (low WALS). This joint analysis shows that single-metric evaluations are insufficient — a model can appear "less stereotyped" merely by reducing variety, a finding that directly supports the paper's argument for multi-metric auditing.

- **StOP discovers internal stereotypical associations (not just output patterns).** The prompt optimization (Table 3, lines 73–75) reveals that the model internally encodes stereotypical terms such as "Imam"/"Sheikh" for Iranian and "brero" for Mexican, and that these optimized prompts produce images visually matching the cluster averages. This provides causal evidence that stereotypical associations are embedded in the model's representations, not just observable in output frequencies.

- **SPI and predisposition analysis trace stereotype emergence.** SPI (Section 4.5, Fig. 6) shows that stereotypical attributes (beard, traditional cloths, sombrero) form within the first 3–20 diffusion steps, with negligible change afterward. Section 4.6 (Fig. 7) further shows that the model's velocity at t=0 predicts stereotypical features even when the final image lacks them — demonstrating that stereotypes are embedded in the default generative trajectory.

- **Intersectional stereotype amplification demonstrated.** Table 2 (lines 51–52) shows that adding a nationality to a profession (e.g., "Iranian doctor") worsens gender imbalance beyond the profession alone, even in models where the single-profession imbalance was already partially mitigated. This concretely shows the insufficiency of data balancing for intersectional cases.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The core claim about Internet footprint and stereotype severity is supported by only three data points.** Section 4.2 (lines 57–62, Fig. 3) compares stereotype scores against Internet user counts for Indian, Mexican, and Iranian nationalities — three points. The paper asserts that "stereotypes worsen for nationalities with lower Internet footprints" and that "the maximum and the average stereotype scores for a nationality decrease as the number of Internet users increases." No correlation coefficient, fitted trend, or statistical test is reported. With *n* = 3, this is a pattern, not evidence for a general claim about a global population of nationalities. This claim in the abstract and conclusion substantially overstates what the data support.

- **LLM-generated stereotype candidate set introduces a circularity risk that is not discussed.** The set of stereotypes to measure is generated by querying ChatGPT o1-preview/4o (Eq. 2, line 39). Since LLMs are trained on Internet text that contains the same stereotypical associations the T2I models learn, the measurement framework may be auditing against the LLM's stereotypical worldview rather than independently assessing the T2I model. The paper neither discusses this limitation nor provides controls (e.g., using multiple LLMs to measure agreement, involving human validators). The same LLM is used across all three nationalities, so any systematic LLM bias would propagate through the entire evaluation.

- **The quantitative evaluation is limited to three nationalities for the main results.** Stereotype-score comparisons (Tab. 1), the Internet-footprint analysis (Fig. 3), and the Stereotype Score + WALS comparison (Fig. 5) use only Iranian, Indian, and Mexican. For a paper whose abstract makes general claims about stereotypes in T2I models — including about nationalities with low Internet footprints — this sample of convenience (all from Asia/Americas, no European, African, or East Asian nationality in the main comparisons) substantially limits the generality of the quantitative conclusions. The StOP analysis adds "American" (Table 3) and the appendix may add others, but the paper's headline quantitative claims rest on three nationalities.

### Minor

- **CLIP-based attribute detection is used without validation for the specific cultural attributes studied.** The paper uses CLIP ViT-G-14 (line 43) to estimate whether generated images contain attributes such as "turban," "beard," "traditional cloths," and "mustache." While CLIP zero-shot classification is standard practice, its accuracy for these specific, culturally-contextualized attributes — especially subtle distinctions like types of religious headwear or variations of traditional clothing across nationalities — is not validated against human judgment or ground-truth labels. The quantitative stereotype scores could partially reflect CLIP's classification biases rather than the T2I model's output distribution.

- **No uncertainty quantification for any reported comparison.** The paper reports percentages (e.g., 84.7% mustache for FLUX.1 Mexican, 77.8% for SDv2) and draws comparative conclusions without confidence intervals, Bayesian credible intervals, or significance tests. While 2000 images per condition is large, standard errors would allow readers to assess whether observed differences (e.g., 34.1% vs. 77.8% mustache in SDv3 vs. SDv2 for Mexican) are reliable. The Internet-footprint analysis additionally lacks any measure of uncertainty around the claimed decreasing trend.
