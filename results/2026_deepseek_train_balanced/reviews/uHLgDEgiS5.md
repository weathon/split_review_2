## Summary
This paper introduces Data Value Embedding (DVEmb), a framework for approximating trajectory-specific leave-one-out (TSLOO) influence — i.e., the effect of removing a training point from a specific iteration during SGD, accounting for data ordering and optimization dynamics. The key idea is to compute per-data-point embeddings via a recursive backward algorithm under the GGN approximation that captures cumulative interactions across training, enabling influence estimation via dot-product with test-point gradients. The method shows efficiency gains over the LoGRA influence function baseline on Pythia-410M and reveals a three-phase temporal pattern of data influence during LLM pretraining.

## Strengths
1. **Recursive backward formulation (Theorem 2, Section 4.1):** The paper derives a recursive expression for DVEmb under the GGN approximation that transforms an intractable sequential product over Hessians into a practical backward algorithm. This is the core theoretical enabler for scalability and a genuine technical contribution.
2. **Quantitative efficiency advantage over the strongest influence-function baseline (Section 5.2, Table 1):** DVEmb reduces peak GPU memory from 63.6 GB (LoGRA) to 0.84 GB and achieves over 15× speedup on Pythia-410M, demonstrating that the method scales to foundation-model-scale architectures where prior TSLOO approximations were computationally prohibitive.
3. **Validation against ground-truth LOO on MNIST (Section 5.1, Figure 3):** The paper computes actual ground-truth LOO by retraining models with individual data points removed while holding batch order fixed, showing high Spearman correlation with DVEmb. The comparison between "single-epoch removal" and "all-epoch removal" cleanly illustrates that influence functions cannot distinguish these settings while DVEmb can.
4. **Empirical discovery of three influence regimes with actionable validation (Section 5.3, Figures 1a and 1b):** The identification of a High-impact Warmup phase, Low-impact Basin, and Gradual Ascent phase is a novel insight. The follow-up data selection experiment — showing that focusing selection on the early and late phases closely matches continuous selection while reducing cost by over 5× — validates the practical relevance.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Disconnect between the motivating regime (single-epoch foundation model training) and the fidelity evaluation (multi-epoch MNIST).** The paper opens by arguing that influence functions fail because LLMs train for a single epoch, making data ordering decisive (line 14: "LLMs often undergo just one training epoch"). However, the ground-truth fidelity validation uses a small MLP trained on MNIST for *three* epochs (line 158). Multi-epoch training is qualitatively different from the single-pass setting the method is designed for: each data point is seen three times rather than once, and ordering effects are diluted. The "single-epoch removal" setting (removing a point from one epoch while present in others) does not validate the method's ability to distinguish between identical data points inserted at *different positions in a single pass*. Without demonstrating ordering sensitivity explicitly, it remains unclear whether DVEmb captures temporal dependence or is simply a good general LOO approximator.

2. **The method is derived for SGD, but the large-scale experiments do not specify the optimizer used, and the SGD/Adam gap is unaddressed.** The DVEmb derivation (Equations 1-2) assumes standard SGD updates. The limitations section (line 212) acknowledges that the method "is specifically tailored for SGD" and "not directly extendable to other popular optimizers like Adam." However, the Pythia-410M and GPT-2 experiments never state what optimizer was used. If Adam was used (standard for these architectures), there is a methodological mismatch: the approximation was derived under SGD dynamics but applied to a training process governed by Adam's adaptive learning rates. If SGD was used, the experiments are less representative of the foundation-model setting that motivates the paper. The defense that using "SGD as a proxy for Adam... has proved to be effective in our experiment" is unsubstantiated — no experiment validating this proxy is presented.

3. **The "three regimes" finding is demonstrated on a single experimental configuration.** The core empirical insight — data influence decomposing into high-impact warmup, low-impact basin, and gradual ascent — is shown for exactly one setting: Pythia-410M on 1% of the Pile (Section 5.3). The paper draws general conclusions ("for pretraining, data selection is most critical during the very early and later stages of training") but provides no ablation over model size, architecture, dataset, learning rate schedule, or optimizer. It is unclear whether this pattern is a general property of neural network training or an artifact of this specific setup. The learning rate normalization applied before plotting is described only briefly; if the "gradual ascent" phase is partially driven by the learning rate schedule rather than intrinsic data influence, the interpretation changes substantially.

4. **No comparison to TracIn or other trajectory-aware baselines.** TracIn (Pruthi et al., 2020) accumulates gradient dot-products across checkpoints and is the most directly comparable trajectory-aware influence method. Its absence from the fidelity comparison (Section 5.1) is a notable omission. The paper compares only to standard influence functions (via LoGRA), which by design do not capture ordering — making the comparison favorable but incomplete.
