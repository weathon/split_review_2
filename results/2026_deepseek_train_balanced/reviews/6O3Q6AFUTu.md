## Summary

The paper proposes NoiseDiffusion, a method for interpolating natural images (not generated by diffusion models) using diffusion models. It identifies that spherical linear interpolation (slerp) fails on natural images because the encoded noise does not match the expected isotropic Gaussian distribution. The method corrects this by introducing subtle Gaussian noise, clipping extreme values (boundary control), and injecting original image content into the noisy latent space to compensate for information loss. The formulation subsumes both slerp and SDEdit-based interpolation as special cases.

## Strengths

1. **Theoretical diagnosis of why slerp fails for natural images**: The paper identifies a concrete cause — the encoded noise from natural images does not match the isotropic Gaussian distribution diffusion models are trained to denoise — and uses high-dimensional concentration-of-measure (Theorem 1) to explain why only noise matching the denoising threshold produces clean results (Section 4.2, Figure 16). This goes beyond prior work that simply applied slerp without diagnosing its failure on non-generated images.

2. **Unified parameterized framework that generalizes existing interpolation methods**: The NoiseDiffusion formulation (Eq. 5, line 175) is proven to reduce to spherical linear interpolation (Section 5.4.1) and SDEdit-based noise-introduction interpolation (Section 5.4.2) as special cases through specific parameter choices. This provides a concrete mathematical relationship between previously disconnected methods.

3. **Dual mechanism addressing the artifact-information trade-off**: The paper identifies that boundary control (clipping) and added Gaussian noise reduce artifacts but cause information loss, and addresses this by injecting raw image information into the noisy latent space via μ/ν parameters (Figures 20, 22). The qualitative ablative experiments (Figures 25, 29, 20) separately demonstrate the distinct role of each component.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **Complete absence of quantitative evaluation invalidates the paper's central claim.** The paper claims to achieve "the best interpolation results" (abstract) and "superior interpolation outcomes" (conclusion), yet the Experiments section (Section 5) contains **no numerical results of any kind** — no FID, LPIPS, SSIM, user study, or any other metric. The paper states it "conduct[s] evaluations on diffusion models trained on LSUN Cat-256 and LSUN Bedroom-256 images" (line 235) but reports zero numbers from these evaluations. Every comparison rests entirely on a handful of qualitative figures whose source images are unspecified ("images collected from the Internet," line 292), with no stated number of image pairs, no random seeds, and no diversity analysis. At a top venue like ICLR, a new-method paper making a comparative empirical claim ("best interpolation results") cannot have its primary conclusion rest solely on visual inspection of an uncontrolled, unreproducible set of qualitative examples. This is not a missing ablation or a need for a larger dataset — it is a structural evaluation gap that makes the central contribution unverifiable.

### Major

- **Unreproducible final evaluation undermines scientific validity.** Section 5.5 describes results on "images collected from the Internet" with no dataset specifications, no image pair count, no selection criteria, and no source attributions. A reader cannot reproduce these results, assess their generality, or determine whether the chosen examples are representative or cherry-picked. Combined with the missing quantitative evaluation, this makes the experimental component effectively unverifiable.

- **No principled procedure for selecting the five free parameters** (α, β, γ, μ, ν). The paper explores their effects one-at-a-time while holding others fixed (Sections 5.1–5.4), but these parameters interact in complex ways. For a practitioner trying to apply the method, it is unclear how to choose parameter values for a new pair of images, or whether tuning is per-dataset or per-image. The method as presented is manually tuned per example without guidance.

### Minor

- **The theoretical justification is heuristic rather than tight.** The reasoning from Theorem 1 (concentration of measure) and Theorem 2 (near-orthogonality) to the design choices is loose. The constraint √(α²+β²+γ²)=1 is derived under assumptions of equal vector norms and pairwise orthogonality that the paper acknowledges are only approximate (line 182), but it does not analyze how deviations from these assumptions affect performance. The boundary control (clipping) is justified via the 68-95-99.7 rule for one-dimensional Gaussians but applied elementwise to high-dimensional latent vectors, with no connection established between these two uses of Gaussian statistics. The theoretical apparatus provides useful intuition but does not constitute a rigorous derivation.

- **Key experimental details are not specified.** The paper does not state the specific noise level *t* used for encoding, the number of ODE integration steps, the solver type, or the actual numerical clipping threshold (the clip function is defined with a generic "Boundary" parameter without stating its value). These omissions compromise reproducibility.

- **The motivating diagnosis is asserted without direct measurement.** The paper claims that encoded latents from natural images contain "noise components at levels higher or lower than the denoising threshold" (Section 4.2), but never actually characterizes the distribution of the encoded latent variables to verify this. The experiment in Figure 16 uses added Gaussian noise, not actual encoded latents from natural images, making the conclusion plausible but untested.
