## Summary
This paper identifies **Context-Parametric Inversion (CPI)** — a phenomenon where, during instruction finetuning (IFT), a model's reliance on context over parametric knowledge (when the two conflict) initially increases but then decreases as training progresses, while standard benchmarks continue to improve. Through controlled experiments across three model families and three IFT datasets, the paper isolates the cause: datapoints where the context is redundant with the model's parametric knowledge (non-context-critical examples) drive the inversion. A simplified theoretical analysis in a one-layer transformer formalizes the two-phase gradient dynamics, and mitigation strategies (data filtering, QK-only finetuning, counterfactual augmentation) are explored with honest disclosure of their limitations.

## Strengths
- **Cross-model, cross-dataset empirical documentation of CPI with clean causal identification.** The paper demonstrates CPI across Llama, Pythia 6.9B, and Mistral 7B on TULU, Alpaca, and UltraChat (Section 3, Figure 2). The controlled ablation in Section 4 is the strongest part: the paper systematically rejects three alternative explanations (memorization of overlapping facts, lack of context-dependent examples, and even SQuAD training), then shows that filtering out the 25% of Alpaca with lowest target perplexity *without context* — i.e., removing non-context-critical points — eliminates the drop entirely (Figure 3b green curve, line 264). This is a cleaner causal identification than prior work that only documents knowledge conflicts at single checkpoints.
- **Formal theoretical analysis reproducing the two-phase dynamics.** Proposition 1 (line 340) proves that in a one-layer transformer with a carefully structured embedding space, gradient signals from context-critical (C) points dominate initially, driving attention toward the context, while non-context-critical (C+S) points dominate later, shifting attention back toward the subject. Theorem 1 (line 386) shows the relative preference for the context answer increases after the first gradient step and decreases after the second. This goes beyond empirical description by providing a formal mechanism for the non-monotonic behavior, even if the setup is highly simplified.
- **Carefully designed evaluation datasets addressing known benchmark flaws.** The CF Biographies dataset uses algorithmic entity substitution instead of error-prone deep-learning-based methods (line 107). CF World Facts explicitly ensures answers appear at varied positions (line 112). CF Quotes tests knowledge conflicts beyond question-answering in a general instruction-following format (lines 114-117). These design choices improve on prior benchmarks (NQ-Swap, LLM-generated contexts with answer-position bias).
- **Honest assessment of mitigation strategies with clear disclosure of limitations.** The paper reports where counterfactual augmentation helps (CF-Biography) and where it fails (CF-Quotes), explicitly noting that "benefits of counterfactual augmentation are task-specific and do not generalize" (line 438). Similarly, QK-only finetuning is reported as yielding suboptimal standard benchmark performance (line 445). This candor is a genuine scientific strength.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The framing overstates the practical severity of the phenomenon relative to the reported data.** The paper calls CPI a "broad failure" (line 57) and "failure mode" (line 7), but the reported numbers tell a more nuanced story. On Llama with TULU, context reliance goes from 30% → 60% → ~35% — still *above* the starting point (line 46). On CF World Facts, it improves from 40% to ~90% then declines (line 154) — the paper never states the endpoint. On CF Quotes, it goes from 40% to 70% then declines. Across all explicitly reported cases, the net effect of IFT on context reliance is neutral or slightly positive; the core finding is non-monotonicity, not net degradation. The paper's framing as a "failure" and "deficiency" of IFT is at odds with the evidence shown. The practical phenomenon is that context reliance peaks early and then drops — but this is more about *instability* or a *training-dynamics tradeoff* (you must choose between maximizing context reliance and maximizing benchmark performance) than about IFT being broadly broken. The paper would benefit from recalibrating its claims to match what the data actually show.

### Minor
- **The gap between the theoretical analysis and the empirical setting is large, and the theory does not generate novel testable predictions.** The theory (Section 5) studies a one-layer transformer with three-token inputs, frozen embedding structure, frozen head, and orthogonal invariant directions for subject/context embeddings. The paper acknowledges this gap (line 361: "we do not make any causal claims about the exact dynamics of attention maps in deep networks"). The core empirical finding — that non-context-critical data drives the drop — was already established before the theory was presented (Section 4). The theory-derived mitigations (QK-only finetuning, counterfactual augmentation) produce weak or task-specific results. The most effective mitigation (data filtering) was discovered empirically. This weakens the claim that the theory "explains" the phenomenon in a practically useful sense; it provides a post-hoc formalization consistent with observations but does not yield actionable predictions that were not already evident from the experiments.
- **The paper conflates "context reliance" with "counterfactual accuracy" without addressing an alternative interpretation.** The paper defines context reliance as accuracy on counterfactual QA datasets where the context contradicts parametric knowledge. But a model that *rejects* a counterfactual context (e.g., because it detects implausibility or registers the conflict) and falls back to parametric knowledge is not necessarily "failing to use context" — it may be making a sensible epistemic judgment. The paper acknowledges this briefly in the conclusion (line 452: "The optimal desired behavior... varies"), but the entire evaluation framework treats any decrease in counterfactual accuracy as a failure. This alternative interpretation is neither tested nor discussed in the evaluation design, which weakens the conclusiveness of the paper's central claims.
- **The simplest baseline mitigation — early stopping at the peak — is never discussed.** The paper notes that the peak occurs "far before 1 finetuning epoch" (line 158) and that standard benchmarks keep improving after the drop (line 44). If peak context reliance occurs within ~0.5 epochs and standard benchmarks are still improving at that point, early stopping is the most obvious and practical intervention. The paper studies three mitigation strategies (data filtering, QK-only finetuning, counterfactual augmentation) without testing or even mentioning this simple baseline. This is a gap: early stopping may recover most of the benefit while avoiding the inversion entirely, which would substantially change how the phenomenon is interpreted.
- **No sensitivity analysis for the perplexity-based filtering threshold.** The paper uses a fixed 25% threshold for filtering out non-context-critical data (line 263) without exploring how the result changes at, say, 10%, 50%, or other thresholds. The filtering is the paper's strongest causal evidence, and the robustness of this result to threshold choice is unexamined.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- The paper does not discuss early stopping as a potential simple mitigation (see Minor weakness above). Evaluating whether stopping at the peak provides most of the benefit would contextualize the more complex mitigations and help calibrate the practical significance of CPI.
- The filtering threshold sensitivity analysis would be a straightforward addition.
- Reporting exact numerical values (not just figures) for counterfactual accuracy at initialization, peak, and end-of-training across all model-dataset combinations in a table would improve precision.

## Suggestions
1. **Recalibrate the framing.** Acknowledge explicitly that the net effect of IFT on context reliance is neutral to slightly positive across the reported cases, and reframe the contribution around the *non-monotonic trajectory* and the *training-dynamics tradeoff* between context reliance and benchmark performance rather than around a "broad failure" of IFT.
2. **Test and report early stopping as a baseline mitigation.** If stopping at the peak recovers most of the benefit with simple early stopping, this substantially changes how practitioners should interpret CPI.
3. **Add a bridging experiment connecting theory to practice.** For example, manipulate the ratio of C to C+S data in a controlled setting and show that the peak position shifts as predicted, providing a non-trivial test of the theory.
4. **Address the alternative interpretation** that models may learn to detect counterfactual scenarios rather than simply "losing" context reliance. A simple experiment comparing confidence/calibration on counterfactual vs. non-conflicting contexts would help.
5. **Add sensitivity analysis** for the perplexity-based filtering threshold to establish robustness.

## Score and Decision
This paper identifies a genuinely interesting and non-obvious phenomenon and supports it with careful controlled experiments that convincingly isolate the cause. The theoretical analysis provides a formal framework for the observed dynamics, and the honest disclosure of mitigation limitations is scientifically commendable. However, the framing significantly overstates the practical severity of the phenomenon relative to the data shown, the theory is too detached to generate novel actionable predictions, and the failure to address the most obvious baseline mitigation (early stopping) and an alternative interpretation (conflict detection) weaken the overall contribution. The core empirical finding is solid and useful, but the paper would benefit from recalibration.
