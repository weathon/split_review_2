## Summary

This paper proposes Quasi-Likelihood Constraint Relaxation (QLCR-GP), a framework for enforcing constraints on GP posteriors by conditioning on a quasi-likelihood that encodes a relaxed version of the constraint. The method unifies existing virtual-point approaches (Riihimäki and Vehtari 2010; Hansen et al. 2024) under a single formalism and extends them to handle a broader range of constraints including differential operators, positivity, monotonicity, and fixed values. The paper demonstrates the method on a synthetic 2D divergence-free gradient problem and a real Thomson scattering diagnostic problem with joint positivity and monotonicity constraints.

## Strengths

1. **Clean unification of existing constraint paradigms.** Section 2.3 explicitly shows that Hansen et al. (2024) conservation constraints are recovered as a special case (Φ(u) = Gu − b = ε, line 123), and the hinge-loss formulation provides an alternative to Riihimäki and Vehtari's probit likelihood for monotonicity. The mathematical presentation is clear and the notation is consistent throughout.

2. **Demonstration of a differential operator constraint without bespoke kernels.** The synthetic experiment (Section 3.1) enforces ∇²f = 0 (divergence-free gradient) using a standard squared-exponential kernel — prior work (Fuselier 2006; Baldassarre et al. 2010; Wahlström 2015; Jidling et al. 2017) required specially constructed kernels for such constraints. The paper correctly notes this limitation of kernel-based approaches (line 163).

3. **Real-world application with combined heterogeneous constraints.** The Thomson scattering example (Section 3.2) jointly enforces positivity and monotonicity under a heteroskedastic noise model — a genuinely challenging setting with practical relevance to fusion plasma diagnostics. The constrained posteriors (Figure 3) visually produce more physically plausible profiles than unconstrained GPs.

4. **Honest self-assessment of limitations.** The paper acknowledges the pointwise-enforcement limitation and the curse of dimensionality (line 240), an important caveat that many constrained-GP papers downplay.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **No quantitative evaluation metrics are reported.** The paper contains zero numerical measures of performance — no RMSE, no log-likelihood, no predictive log-score, no constraint-violation metric, no coverage or sharpness statistic. The synthetic experiment (Section 3.1) claims "Clearly, the residual divergence of the gradient... is non-zero but it has the required distribution ε ∼ N(0, 1²)" (line 197) based solely on visual inspection of Figure 2. The Thomson scattering experiment claims "constrained GPs show improved performance" (line 233) without any quantitative evidence. For a method paper submitted to a top venue, this is a decisive evidential gap: a reader cannot determine how well the method works, under what conditions, or whether it outperforms simpler alternatives.

2. **No baseline comparisons.** The paper positions QLCR-GP as a "generalisation of virtual point methods" (abstract) building on Riihimäki and Vehtari (2010) and Hansen et al. (2024), yet it compares against neither. The only comparison is the unconstrained GP (the *absence* of the proposed method). The Thomson scattering setting (positivity + monotonicity) is exactly the setting Riihimäki and Vehtari (2010) was designed for; the divergence-free gradient experiment is exactly the setting where divergence-free kernels (Fuselier 2006; Jidling et al. 2017) are available. Without any baseline comparison, the paper cannot substantiate its claim that QLCR-GP offers advantages over existing approaches.

3. **Internal inconsistency regarding the closed-form solution.** The paper states (line 123, line 171) that when the constraint is linear and the residual is Gaussian, "the Φ-constrained posterior is also a Gaussian with an exact solution" (closed-form conditional Gaussian update, Eq. 3). The divergence-free gradient experiment uses ∇²f(x) = ε with ε ∼ N(0, 1²) (Eq. 14) — a linear operator (Laplacian) with a Gaussian residual, which should yield an exact Gaussian posterior under the paper's own claims. Yet line 175 states "For both experiments, the constraints were imposed using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)." This is either wasteful (the closed-form solution would be more efficient) or indicates that the actual implementation differs from the mathematical description. Either way, it undermines confidence in the experimental execution.

### Minor

1. **No guidance on key methodological choices.** The paper does not discuss how to choose the relaxation parameter ν², how to select virtual points {x̃ᵢ} or how many are needed, and what MCMC sampler was used (no chain count, warmup, or sample sizes reported). These are non-trivial implementation decisions that significantly affect results.

2. **Only 2 of the 5+ claimed constraint types are demonstrated.** The introduction (line 18) lists fixed values, boundedness, monotonicity, integral conservation laws, and incompressibility as within scope, but experiments only cover divergence-free gradient (a type of incompressibility) and monotonicity+positivity. Integral constraints and boundedness are not shown.

3. **No sensitivity analysis.** The paper reports no experiments varying ν², virtual point density, or virtual point placement. The method's robustness to these choices is therefore unknown.

4. **No computational cost characterization.** The paper says nothing about runtime, scaling with virtual point count, or MCMC sampling efficiency.

### Trivial

1. **Typo in constraint direction (line 240).** The discussion states the QLCR-GP is required to be "monotonically increasing," but the Thomson scattering physics (lines 216-219, Eq. 13) and the experiment (line 175) both require *monotonically decreasing* profiles (derivative ≤ 0).

## Nice-to-Haves

- A quantitative comparison against at least one existing method (Riihimäki and Vehtari 2010 for Thomson scattering; divergence-free kernels for the synthetic example) would substantially strengthen the paper.
- Using the closed-form solution for the divergence-free experiment and comparing its results to the MCMC version would validate the framework and provide a computational cost comparison.
- A sensitivity analysis for ν² would help practitioners understand how to set this parameter.
- A demonstration of a third constraint type (e.g., integral constraints or boundedness) would better support the "broad range" claim.

## Suggestions

1. **Add quantitative metrics.** For the synthetic experiment: RMSE against the true Branin-Hoo function, a histogram or QQ-plot of the Laplacian residual against N(0, 1²), and the actual variance of the residual. For the Thomson experiment: held-out predictive log-likelihood or a cross-validation scheme.
2. **Add at least one baseline comparison.** The most impactful comparison would be against Riihimäki and Vehtari (2010) for the Thomson setting, and against a divergence-free kernel GP for the synthetic setting.
3. **Resolve the MCMC inconsistency.** Either use the closed-form solution for the linear-constraint experiment and explain why MCMC was used otherwise, or clarify what aspect of the constraint implementation prevents using the closed form.
4. **Add a sensitivity analysis** for ν² and virtual point placement/density, at least for one experiment.
5. **Report basic MCMC details** (sampler, number of chains, warmup, post-warmup samples, convergence diagnostics) if MCMC is kept.

## Score and Decision

**MY FINAL SCORE: <score>3.5</score>**
**MY FINAL DECISION: <decision>Reject</decision>**

The paper presents a mathematically coherent framework for constrained GP regression and demonstrates it on two problems, but the evaluation is fundamentally insufficient for a top venue: zero quantitative metrics, zero baseline comparisons, and an unresolved internal inconsistency about when the closed-form solution applies versus when MCMC is needed. The core mathematical contribution may be sound, but the experimental evidence does not meet the bar required to assess it.
