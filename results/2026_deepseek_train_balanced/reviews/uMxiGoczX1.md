# Consolidated Review

## Summary

This paper proposes EOPS (Expert-Objective-Personal-Subjective), a data-generation pipeline for creating human preference data for creative writing RLHF. The pipeline decomposes creative writing annotation into expert-defined objective criteria (theme extraction, correctness scoring, language scoring with 7 sub-dimensions) and a final subjective ranking step by annotators. Experiments on Baichuan models show that PPO training with EOPS data improves over base models across short story generation, expand writing, and style transfer tasks. The paper also tests (and honestly reports) limited transferability of this data to Qwen2-72B.

---

## Strengths

- **Principled decomposition of creative writing annotation with concrete, reproducible standards**: The EOPS pipeline provides a structured annotation protocol that goes beyond the typical black-box preference labeling found in prior RLHF work. The paper specifies theme extraction rules (Sections 3.4.1), a 5-point decrement correctness scoring system with worked examples (Section 3.4.2), and a 7-dimension language scoring rubric (plot richness, characterization, expression, depth of theme, creative imagination, emotional resonance, logical rigor) (Section 3.4.3). This level of specificity is a genuine contribution — it makes the annotation process reproducible and open to scrutiny, unlike many RLHF datasets whose annotation procedures are vaguely described.

- **Transparent reporting of cross-model transfer limitations**: The paper explicitly tests whether preference data collected from Baichuan models transfers to Qwen2-72B and honestly documents that "RLHF training improves the ability of short story generation while hurting the other two tasks" (Section 4.3.3, line 199). Tables 4–5 show negative or mixed results. This candor about the limits of data transfer is a methodological strength and stands in contrast to work that only reports positive results.

- **Consistent improvements across three distinct creative writing tasks on Baichuan models**: The method is validated on short story generation, expand writing, and style transfer. Tables 4–5 show PPO-new outperforms BC-new on all three tasks in both usability and winning rate. The breadth of evaluation shows the pipeline is not narrowly tailored to a single genre.

- **Out-of-domain evaluation**: The paper evaluates on both in-domain prompts (26 generated by the paper's method) and out-of-domain prompts (19 human-written prompts differing from the generated data) (Section 4.1.2). Improvements hold on both sets, with OOD improvements sometimes larger.

---

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The core claim — that the EOPS annotation scheme produces better data — is untested because no comparison against alternative annotation methods is provided.** The experiments compare a PPO-trained model (trained on EOPS data) against the base model without any RLHF training. This comparison conflates two effects: the effect of any RLHF training and the specific effect of the EOPS annotation scheme. Since PPO training on *any* reasonable preference dataset is known to improve model performance over a base model, the reported improvements do not validate the EOPS pipeline. To support the claim that EOPS is beneficial, the paper would need at minimum a controlled comparison where the same pool of annotators rank the same responses under (a) the full EOPS protocol vs. (b) a baseline protocol (e.g., pure subjective ranking with no expert criteria, or AIF). Without this, the reader cannot tell whether EOPS helps, hurts, or is irrelevant relative to simpler alternatives. This is a structural experimental design gap.

- **The evaluation protocol is circular with respect to the training data.** Section 4.1.2 explicitly states: "our evaluation process is the same as the process we annotate our training data, except that prompts are changed to our evaluate sets." Both the usability metric (correctness ≥ 4 and language score ≥ 4) and the winning rate are produced by the same EOPS annotation process — same criteria, same scoring standards, same annotator protocol. This means the evaluation intrinsically favors the EOPS-trained model: the evaluation criteria are exactly those the training data was optimized to satisfy. If EOPS-based annotation overweights certain surface-level features (e.g., theme coverage) at the expense of harder-to-capture qualities, the evaluation would show improvement while actual writing quality might not have improved proportionally. No independent evaluation is performed — no third-party human judges working from different criteria, no blind evaluation using an established creative-writing rubric, no automatic metrics correlated with human judgment. The evaluation is a self-consistency check, not a validation.

- **No measurement of inter-annotator reliability, despite "noise reduction" being a central motivation.** The paper repeatedly frames its contribution as reducing annotation noise from subjective preferences (lines 12, 45, 212), yet provides zero quantitative evidence for this claim. With 5–7 annotators ranking creative writing responses (a highly subjective domain), basic metrics of inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Fleiss' kappa, Krippendorff's alpha) are a minimal sanity check. Without them, the reader has no basis to believe the annotation produces consistent rankings rather than noisy ones.

- **Unspecified "other" preference data confounds experimental results.** The experiments repeatedly mention mixing EOPS creative-writing data with "other types of preference data to ensure the general ability" (line 126) and "some general task data" (line 167). These other data sources, their quantities, and their annotation protocols are never described. This makes it impossible to disentangle the effect of EOPS creative-writing data from the effect of auxiliary data. If the auxiliary data is large enough, the reported improvements could be driven primarily by that data rather than by the EOPS pipeline.

### Minor

- **The choice of objective criteria is asserted without justification.** The paper decomposes annotation into theme extraction, correctness score, and language score (with 7 sub-dimensions), but provides no citation, theoretical grounding, or empirical validation for why *these* dimensions are the right ones. Why 7 sub-dimensions for language? Why these specific 7? Are they complete or redundant? The criteria are presented as self-evident, but creative writing quality is a contested construct with a long literature in narratology and literary studies. A brief justification or reference to established frameworks would significantly strengthen the paper.

- **Critical implementation details are missing or vague.** The reward model is described only as "one relatively small Baichuan model" (line 126) — no parameter count, architecture variant, or training configuration is given. This makes replication difficult and raises questions about whether the RM is sufficiently capable to provide useful reward signals for creative writing, a task that arguably requires nuanced judgment.

- **Paper organization issues.** Section 3.3 is titled "Response Generation" but primarily describes the EOPS method, not response generation. Section 3.4 appears twice as a header (lines 53 and 59) with nearly identical RLHF background text. There is no Section 3.2. These organizational problems make the paper harder to follow.

- **No statistical uncertainty measures.** Results are reported as point estimates without confidence intervals, significance tests, or measures of variance. Given the small evaluation sets (19–100 prompts, depending on the condition), this is a concern for the reliability of the claimed improvements.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation of EOPS components**: The method has several moving parts (expert theme extraction, correctness scoring, language scoring, subjective ranking, remarks). An ablation study identifying which components drive observed improvements would strengthen the paper and provide practical guidance.
- **Annotator qualifications**: The paper mentions "domain experts" and "5–7 annotators" but does not specify who these people are, their literary expertise, or what training they received. This matters for reproducibility and for assessing the generalizability of the protocol.
- **Cost/resource comparison**: The abstract claims "minimal human resources" but the pipeline requires domain experts, design researchers, and 5–7 annotators in iterative communication — potentially more labor-intensive than standard pairwise preference annotation. A quantitative cost comparison against alternative annotation methods would be informative.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a controlled comparison against an alternative annotation protocol.** The most impactful addition would be: the same pool of annotators ranks the same set of responses under (a) the full EOPS protocol and (b) a simple pairwise preference protocol (no expert criteria, just "which response do you prefer?"). Train separate RMs and PPO policies on each, and evaluate both on a held-out set judged by an independent panel using their own holistic criteria (not the EOPS criteria).

2. **Decouple the evaluation from the annotation protocol.** Use a held-out evaluation with independent judges who do not follow the EOPS protocol and who evaluate on holistic quality — not on the specific sub-dimensions the data was optimized to satisfy. This would show whether EOPS improves *actual* writing quality or only performance on its own internal metrics.

3. **Measure and report inter-annotator agreement.** If EOPS genuinely reduces annotation noise, this should be directly measurable as higher agreement among annotators compared to a baseline protocol. Report Fleiss' kappa or a similar metric for both the EOPS objective scores and the subjective rankings.

4. **Specify the composition of "other preference data."** Disclose the sources, quantities, and annotation protocols of any auxiliary data used during training to allow the reader to isolate the effect of the EOPS creative-writing data.

---

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a plausible intuition for structured annotation in creative writing and provides a reasonably detailed annotation protocol. However, the experimental design has two structural gaps: (1) the core claim about EOPS is tested only against base models without RLHF, not against alternative annotation methods, and (2) the evaluation uses the same criteria as the training data annotation, creating circularity. Additionally, the central claim of "noise reduction" is unmeasured. These weaknesses prevent the paper from supporting its own contribution claims. A substantially revised version with proper baselines, independent evaluation, and reliability metrics could be a useful contribution, but the current version does not meet the standard for a top venue.
