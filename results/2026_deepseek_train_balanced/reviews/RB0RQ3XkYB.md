## Summary

This paper proposes HLCA (Harmonized Learning with Concurrent Arbitration), a brain-inspired deep reinforcement learning approach for motion planning. The method combines a Concurrent Arbitration Module (CAM) for exploration-exploitation balancing and a Harmonized Self-Improving Learning (HSIL) mechanism for selecting among multiple planning strategies. The algorithm is motivated by the concurrent reasoning mechanism in the human prefrontal cortex. Experiments on eight environments (2-DoF to 14-DoF) compare against RRT*, BIT*, NEXT, GNN, and other baselines.

## Strengths

- **Systematic buffer capacity experiment validates a non-trivial neuroscience prediction.** The paper tests inference buffer sizes {2, 3, 4, 5} across five diverse environments (Section 5.3, Figure 5) and finds performance peaks at size 3–4 and degrades at 5. This corroborates the neuroscience claim about human working memory limits (~4 strategies) and is not a foregone conclusion — a simpler "larger is better" approach would not have predicted this result. This is the paper's most compelling empirical finding.

- **Evaluation across an unusually wide range of DoF (2–14) and diverse robot morphologies.** Experiments cover point robots, stick robots, snake robots, single-arm manipulators, and dual-arm systems in both 2D and 3D workspaces (Section 5.1, lines 150–166). This goes beyond what most motion planning papers demonstrate and provides evidence of scalability.

- **Ablation studies I and II provide clean causal evidence for the two modules.** Ablation I inserts CAM into NEXT without changing any other component (Figure 6). Ablation II isolates HSIL's effect on buffer collection using a fixed inference buffer (Figure 7). These are well-designed — each preserves the full baseline otherwise — and show that both modules independently improve performance.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Evaluation is almost entirely qualitative, with no numerical results reported in the text.** The paper states it assesses "four key metrics" (success rate, path cost, collision checks, total running time — Section 5.2, line 179), then: (a) reports *no* numerical values — every result is deferred to figures that cannot be read from the text, (b) makes the extraordinary claim of "a 100% success rate across all test environments" (line 181) with no per-environment breakdown, no confidence intervals, and no variance measure — in motion planning even state-of-the-art methods have nonzero failure rates on constrained problems, and (c) the "total running time" metric is **mentioned as one of four key metrics and then never reported or discussed anywhere in the paper**. A claim this strong and a metric this basic demand precise numerical reporting; their absence makes the experimental evidence impossible to assess.

- **Ablation Study III is mentioned but contains no results.** Section 5.4 (line 213) reads in its entirety: "Ablation study III: varying the maximum sampling number. We perform experiments and discuss the influence of the maximum sampling number in our methods." This is followed by nothing — no figure, no table, no analysis. The paper feels incomplete.

- **Several core implementation details are missing or broken, and key notation is undefined.** Specifically: (1) The mapping from {ω₁, ω₂, ω₃} to specific algorithms is never stated in the Method section — the paper only says they are "non-learning planners" (line 48); only in Ablation II (line 205) does it become clear that RRT* and BIT* are being used as these strategies. (2) The kernel function k(s', s) used in the variance estimator (Eq. 1, line 83) and average reward (line 92) is never specified — this is critical because the method's exploration behavior depends on it. (3) The symbol 𝒵 appears in Eq. 3 (line 114) but is never defined; the surrounding text uses 𝒯 inconsistently (line 119). (4) The "regularization factors" Z_t^λ and Z_t^μ (line 119) are never given an explicit form. (5) The ex-post reliability equation (line 117) has a broken parenthetical structure: `(f_{colli} + Σ c([s,s'])` is missing a closing parenthesis and the denominator is ambiguous.

### Minor

- **The comparison against baselines is asymmetric in HLCA's favor and not acknowledged.** The HSIL component (Section 4.3.2) uses paths generated by RRT*, BIT*, and other methods to train ω_u. During planning, the inference buffer explicitly contains these same methods as candidate strategies. Ablation II (line 205) confirms that 𝒯 = {RRT*, BIT*, NEXT}. Comparing an ensemble that internally uses these baselines against the baselines individually is fundamentally unequal — the ensemble has access to the outputs of all these methods plus a learned arbiter. This is not necessarily invalid (ensemble methods are legitimate), but the paper presents it as HLCA "outperforming" these methods without controlling for the ensemble advantage (e.g., oracle selection per problem, random selector, or uniform ensemble baseline).

- **The neuroscience framing is asserted rather than structurally realized in the algorithm.** Section 2 describes two concurrent inference tracks (medial: reliability inference → exploration trigger → confirmation; lateral: alternative-strategy evaluation → exploitation/rejection). However, the CAM (Section 4.2) implements a single observation function φ(s) with a threshold β for switching — one decision rule, not two tracks. The HSIL reliability inference (Section 4.3.1) follows a standard Bayesian/bandit updating scheme. The neuroscience provides rhetorical framing, but the paper does not demonstrate that the brain mechanism *constrained* or *generated* the algorithmic design in a substantive way that simpler design principles would not.

- **Several mathematical informalities weaken the method description.** In the ex-ante reliability equation (line 114), the paper states that F(ω|U_t, 𝒯) "does not require calculation and can be directly used as a constant" (line 121) because all strategies have equal likelihood — but if F is truly constant, it would be absorbed into the normalization constant Z_t^λ, making the explicit multiplicative factor misleading. The notation h_t is introduced as "all possible histories" but then equated to μ̂_t (line 48), creating an inconsistency with the hat notation used later. In Eq. 2 (line 89), `c` is used both as a constant multiplier in the UCB-style term and as the path-cost function c([s', s_g]) — the same symbol for two different quantities in the same equation.

### Trivial

- Line 48 contains a duplicated word ("are are").
- The paper states "3000 different problems in each environment, with 2000 for training and 1000 for testing" (line 172) but does not clarify whether the same 1000 problems are used as test problems across all methods or if problems are resampled.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Adding an oracle-selection baseline (best single strategy per problem) and a random-selection baseline would cleanly isolate the value of the arbitration mechanism from the value of simply having multiple strategies available.
- Specifying the kernel function and the neural network architecture would substantially improve reproducibility without requiring the supplementary materials.

## Removed Points

These points were flagged by reviewers but removed per filtering rules (see justification):

- **Missing hyperparameter values (ε, c, β, ρ, τ, m, α), optimizer, learning rate, batch size, training iterations, network architecture:** Removed per the hard rule against "nitpicks about reproducibility such as undisclosed hyperparameters." While these are important for exact reproduction, they are standard details that would be included in a code release (which the paper states is provided in supplementary material, line 16).
- **Baselines are "somewhat dated":** Removed — RRT*, informed-RRT*, BIT*, NEXT, GNN are standard, well-established baselines in the motion planning literature. The criticism that more recent methods (MPNet, ETOMP) are absent is scope creep; the paper includes the relevant contemporary learning-based methods.
- **Criticism that the neuroscience connection is "standard ensemble/bandit methodology dressed in neuroscience terminology":** Characterizing the entire contribution as merely "dressed up" is an overstatement that does not account for the CAM's specific design (kernel-smoothed bandit with threshold-based switching) or the buffer-capacity experiment that validates a neuroscience-specific prediction. This criticism is moved here because it conflates a reasonable concern (the mapping is loose) with a dismissive characterization that is not fully supported by the evidence on the page.
- **Strength Finder claim about "explicit structured mapping of PFC circuits to algorithms":** Removed — the strength is inflated. The paper traces neuroscience concepts to general algorithmic roles but does not establish a precise structural correspondence that constrains design choices. The mapping is high-level and asserted rather than demonstrated.
- **Strength Finder claim about "formalization of exploration-exploitation trade-off":** Removed — this is a generic methodological description, not a concrete strength specific to this paper.

## Suggestions

1. **Report all experimental results in numerical tables** (mean ± std or equivalent) in the main text, with per-environment breakdowns for success rate, path cost, collision checks, and total running time. The 100% success rate claim must be accompanied by per-environment success counts and, ideally, confidence intervals.
2. **Either complete Ablation Study III or remove it** — a section header with no content is a clear indicator of incompleteness.
3. **Explicitly define the mapping** from {ω₁, ω₂, ω₃} to specific algorithms in the Method section, not just in the ablation description. This is necessary for the reader to understand what strategies are being arbitrated.
4. **Fix the notation issues:** define 𝒵 or replace with 𝒯, close the parentheses in Eq. 4, resolve the h_t/μ̂_t inconsistency, and use distinct symbols for the constant `c` and the cost function `c(·)` in Eq. 2.
5. **Add control experiments** to separate the value of having multiple strategies from the value of the arbitration mechanism: compare against an oracle that picks the best single strategy per problem, a uniform random selector, and an ensemble that averages without arbitration.
6. **Acknowledge the comparison asymmetry** explicitly and discuss whether the improvement is driven by the arbitration or simply by having access to multiple planners.
