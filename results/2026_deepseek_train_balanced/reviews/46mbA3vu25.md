## Summary
This paper presents a controlled empirical comparison of GAN-based and diffusion-based image super-resolution (ISR) models using the same Efficient U-Net backbone (~600-700M parameters), same training dataset (17M proprietary image-text pairs), and same batch size. The key finding is that a properly-scaled GAN matches or exceeds a same-architecture diffusion model on clean SR(×4), with 91% human preference (SbS) over their diffusion model, ~13× faster inference, and dramatically faster convergence. Additional experiments show text conditioning with generic captions provides no significant benefit, and that Real-ESRGAN augmentations asymmetrically slow diffusion training but not GAN training.

## Strengths
1. **Architecture-matched comparison isolates the paradigm, not model capacity** — The paper uses the same Efficient U-Net backbone (~600-700M params) for both GAN and diffusion (Section "Methodology" → "Models"). Prior work typically compared large diffusion models against small GAN baselines like Real-ESRGAN (17M params, Table 6), so this directly addresses a major confound in the literature.

2. **Human evaluation (SbS) with statistical significance testing** — The paper uses human side-by-side comparisons with p-value thresholds (lines 198-199) rather than relying solely on full-reference metrics. The resulting metric disagreements (e.g., ResShift has highest PSNR/SSIM but loses to GAN 86.0% in SbS, Table 2) demonstrate why perceptual evaluation is critical.

3. **Systematic text-conditioning ablation with two encoders** — Two different text encoders (CLIP-like XL, 1.3B params; UMT5, 3B params) are tested, and neither produces a statistically significant improvement for either paradigm (Figure 3). This challenges the assumption that generic image captions from large-scale datasets benefit ISR.

4. **Augmentation study reveals asymmetric convergence dynamics** — Real-ESRGAN augmentations slow diffusion training dramatically (940k vs 620k iterations for convergence) while GAN convergence is unaffected (Figure 2, lines 304-306). This is a practically important finding about paradigm-specific training dynamics.

5. **Efficiency gap quantified** — GAN inference is ~13× faster than the paper's own diffusion model (0.24s vs 3.20s on A100, Table 6) and orders of magnitude faster than prior-art methods (SUPIR: 18.11s, DiffBIR: 24.69s), grounding the quality comparison in deployment costs.

6. **Perceptual loss ablation challenges a long-held assumption** — The finding that VGG-based perceptual loss provides negligible benefit (94.2% "equal", Table 7, lines 437-453) contradicts a belief dating to SRGAN (2017). This is a useful empirical contribution regardless of whether one accepts the result at face value.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **"Similar quality in SR in the Wild" claim is not supported by the evidence.** Contribution (1) states GANs "yield similar quality in SR in the Wild." On the degraded SR task (Table 5, line 393), the authors' GAN loses decisively to SUPIR (30.0% SbS win rate, statistically significant) and DiffBIR (30.6% win rate, significant). These are large diffusion-prior models, and human annotators significantly prefer them. The paper acknowledges this in prose ("We speculate that presence of rich semantic information is more beneficial") but the headline claim remains unqualified. "Similar quality" is accurate only if one cherry-picks full-reference metrics while ignoring that human evaluators prefer the diffusion-prior models. This claim should be explicitly qualified.

2. **"Matched computational budget" framing is imprecise.** The abstract states both approaches have "matched architecture, model and dataset sizes, and computational budget" (line 5). However, the GAN converged at ~40k training iterations while the diffusion model required 620k (clean SR) to 940k (with augmentations) at the same batch size of 512. The diffusion model saw roughly 15× more training samples. The early-stopping protocol (train until three consecutive SbS evaluations show no improvement) is reasonable, but calling this a "matched computational budget" is inaccurate. What the experiment actually shows is: *when each paradigm is trained to human-judged convergence with matched architecture, data, and batch size*, GAN reaches saturation faster with competitive or better quality. The paper should state this directly rather than implying equal total compute.

### Minor
1. **Proprietary training data limits reproducibility and generalizability.** The core experiment depends on 17M proprietary image-text pairs and a proprietary text encoder. While not fatal — industry papers can still contribute insights — the primary value is the controlled comparison, and it cannot be reproduced or independently verified. A small-scale experiment on a public dataset (e.g., COCO, Flickr) would substantially strengthen the paper. The limitations section does not address this.

2. **SbS annotation pipeline is under-described.** The paper relies on human evaluation as its strongest evidence (91% GAN win rate, Table 2), but provides no details on how many annotators were used, their agreement rates, or the annotation interface. A p-value threshold of 0.05 is mentioned (line 199), but without these details it is difficult to assess the reliability of the central evaluation.

3. **Small evaluation set (244 images) for human evaluation.** For a study whose main claims rest on human preference judgments, 244 images is modest. Reporting confidence intervals on win rates would provide a more complete picture than p-value thresholds alone.

4. **No quantitative check for mode collapse.** The paper states "we did not encounter any difficulties" with GAN optimization (line 194) but provides no quantitative evidence (e.g., diversity metrics, distribution of outputs). Since mode collapse is a well-known GAN concern, this claim is anecdotal without supporting measurements.

## Novel Insights
The most interesting tension revealed by the review — beyond what the paper explicitly highlights — concerns the degraded SR results. The authors' GAN beats the authors' own diffusion model (66% SbS, Table 5) but loses decisively to SUPIR and DiffBIR (~30% each). This suggests the relevant axis is not simply "GAN vs. diffusion paradigm" but also "trained from scratch for SR vs. leveraging large-scale generative pretraining." The paper's framing conflates these two dimensions; clarifying them would make the contribution sharper and more honest about where the GAN advantage holds and where it does not.

## Suggestions
1. Reword the abstract and contribution list: replace "matched computational budget" with "trained to convergence under matched architecture, dataset, and batch size." Qualify the "in the Wild" claim to acknowledge that on degraded SR, large diffusion-prior models (SUPIR, DiffBIR) are preferred by humans.
2. Add a small-scale public-dataset experiment (e.g., 100k images from COCO or Flickr) to demonstrate the core finding generalizes beyond the proprietary data.
3. Provide details on the SbS annotation pipeline (number of annotators, inter-annotator agreement, interface).
4. Report confidence intervals on the SbS win rates in addition to p-values.
5. Add a quantitative diversity metric to support the "no mode collapse" claim beyond anecdotal observation.
