## Summary

The paper proposes CGST, a mini-batch training algorithm for GNNs on large graphs, built around two modules: (1) a balanced chordal graph partition module that splits the graph into chordal subgraphs, and (2) a batch random aggregation module to incorporate cross-cluster connections. Experiments on three large-scale datasets report improvements over five baselines.

## Strengths

1. **Novel conceptual direction.** Using chordal graph properties as a guiding principle for subgraph partitioning in mini-batch GNN training is a genuinely new idea. Chordal graphs have well-known structural properties (clique minimal separators, perfect elimination orderings) that could theoretically preserve graph structure during partitioning — a motivation that goes beyond standard METIS-based clustering and is sensible on its face.

2. **Competitive empirical results as reported.** Table 1 shows CGST achieving the highest F1/Accuracy across all three datasets against ClusterGCN, GraphSAINT, GAS, SIGN, and SAGN, with reasonable memory and time costs. The ablation study (Table 2) confirms that both modules contribute to the reported gains.

3. **Hyperparameter robustness analysis.** Figure 3 demonstrates that the key hyperparameter α (max subgraph ratio) is not overly sensitive across two orders of magnitude on two datasets, which is useful for practitioners.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The chordal graph partitioning algorithm is never specified.** This is the paper's central claimed contribution (Section 4.2). The paper defines what a chordal graph is (Definition 1) and repeatedly states "We propose a graph partition method to generate several well-partitioned chordal subgraphs" — but **never describes how this is actually done**. Algorithm 2 treats the partition as a black box ("Partition graph into c clusters with Chordal Subgraph Partition Module"), with no pseudocode, no algorithmic description, and no reference to an adapted existing method. Most real-world graphs are *not* chordal; the paper does not explain how a general graph is transformed into chordal subgraphs. Is it a triangulation (adding fill-in edges)? A decomposition based on clique separators? A selection of induced subgraphs that happen to be chordal? The paper is silent. Without this information, the method is not reproducible, the core contribution cannot be evaluated, and the results in Tables 1–3 cannot be attributed to any specific technique. This is a decisive flaw for a methods paper at a top venue.

### Major

- **Dataset mismatch: MalNet is not a single graph for node classification.** The paper describes MalNet as containing "over 1.2 million graphs, averaging over 17k nodes and 39k edges per graph" (line 156) — a collection of individual function call graphs designed for *graph-level* classification (classifying each graph into malware families). The paper frames its contribution as scaling node classification on a *single large graph*, yet lists MalNet as one of three "large-scale graph datasets" for node classification without explaining how the dataset is converted into a single-graph node-classification problem. This undermines the reported results on this dataset and raises serious questions about the evaluation protocol.

### Minor

- **BRAM's distinction from ClusterGCN is unclear.** Algorithm 2's training procedure — randomly selecting clusters to form mini-batches — is functionally identical to ClusterGCN (Chiang et al., 2019). The paper claims BRAM "incorporates between-cluster links," but Step 4 shows neighbor expansion N(V_t), which appears to re-introduce the neighbor-explosion problem the paper claims to avoid. The paper never clearly explains what BRAM adds beyond ClusterGCN's standard training loop, nor how between-cluster edges are actually incorporated.

- **Dataset count inconsistency.** The abstract says "three large-scale graph datasets" (line 4), the contributions bullet says "three" then "four" (line 31), and the conclusion says "four" (line 295). The experiments section lists exactly three datasets. This inconsistency signals carelessness in manuscript preparation.

- **Underspecified ablation baselines.** "CGST w/o CGPM" is described only by what is removed, not by what partitioning method replaces it. If random partitioning is used, the comparison is weak; if METIS is used, this should be stated and would effectively compare CGST's chordal partitioner against ClusterGCN's METIS partitioner. Either way, the reader cannot tell what the variant actually does.

- **Key hyperparameters not discussed.** Only α (max subgraph ratio) is analyzed. The number of clusters c, the number of sampled clusters per batch n, and GNN depth are not reported or studied, making reproduction harder and leaving important design choices opaque.

### Trivial

- Several writing issues (e.g., "the the influence" on line 23, "some baseline methods has difficulty" on line 229) reduce readability.
- Algorithm 2 Step 4 has notation issues (mismatched brackets, garbled indexing).

## Nice-to-Haves

- Provide a complexity analysis of the partitioning step itself, especially relevant for the billion-scale Hyperlink Graph dataset.
- Compare CGST against a controlled variant that uses METIS partitioning with the same BRAM training protocol, to isolate the benefit of chordality from the training algorithm.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the partitioning algorithm.** This is non-negotiable. Provide pseudocode, a clear algorithmic description, or a reference to an existing chordal decomposition/triangulation algorithm adapted for this purpose. Without this, the paper does not contain its claimed contribution.

2. **Clarify the MalNet evaluation protocol.** Explain how a collection of 1.2 million individual graphs is used for node classification on a single large graph, or correct the task framing.

3. **Position CGST precisely relative to ClusterGCN.** State explicitly what differs in the training loop and why BRAM does not re-introduce neighbor explosion.

4. **Fix the dataset count inconsistency and writing issues.**
