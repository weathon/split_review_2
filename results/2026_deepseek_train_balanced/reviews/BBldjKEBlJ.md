## Summary

QuantFormer proposes a transformer-based method for forecasting neural activity from two-photon calcium imaging data. The core ideas are: (i) using latent-space vector quantization to convert the continuous regression problem of neural activity forecasting into a discrete classification task, (ii) neuron-specific learnable tokens to handle arbitrary-sized neuronal populations with a single model, and (iii) self-supervised pre-training via masked auto-encoding with quantization. The method is evaluated on a subset (11 mice) of the Allen Visual Coding dataset for both neuron activation classification and response forecasting tasks.

## Strengths

- **Quantization reframes forecasting as classification, with component-level isolation.** Rather than only comparing against off-the-shelf forecasting models, the ablation study (Table 3, described in lines 181–184) isolates the contribution of VQ pre-training by comparing it against a standard auto-encoder pre-training and from-scratch training on the same backbone. This is a cleaner test of the quantization hypothesis than typical "our method vs. competitors" comparisons.

- **Neuron-specific tokens enable a principled solution to variable-sized populations.** The model prepends learnable `[NEURON]` tokens to handle arbitrary neuron counts without per-neuron model copies or fixed-size multivariate inputs (Section 3.4, line 132). This design also unlocks a class-imbalance weighting scheme (Eq. 2, line 119) that down-weights inactive observations per-sample — a concrete advantage that multivariate bundling methods cannot apply.

- **Leave-one-category-out generalization evaluation.** The paper tests generalization by holding out entire stimulus categories or mice from pre-training (line 167), going beyond simple in-distribution splits. This provides evidence that the pre-trained representations carry transferable information across conditions.

- **Clean separation of pre-training and downstream task design.** The two-phase approach (unsupervised pre-training via masked auto-encoding, then fine-tuning for classification/forecasting with neuron and stimulus tokens) is well-motivated and clearly described.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Contribution #1 claims optogenetic intervention as an achieved outcome, but no such experiment is performed.** The first listed contribution is: "Forecasting neural responses for optogenetic manipulation: We propose a novel approach that leverages neural activity predictions to guide optogenetic interventions, a completely original concept in the literature" (line 40). The abstract and introduction also foreground real-time closed-loop optogenetics as a primary motivation (abstract, line 23). However, the paper contains no optogenetic experiment, no closed-loop setup, no latency or throughput measurement, and no demonstration that the forecasts are suitable for real-time intervention. What the paper actually demonstrates is retrospective forecasting on static recordings. This is a significant framing gap — the first contribution is effectively unsupported. The technical work (forecasting from calcium imaging) is still valid, but the paper's self-presentation is misleading about what has been achieved vs. what is aspirational.

### Minor

- **Gradient-based normalization for forecasting metrics is non-standard and insufficiently justified.** The paper states that forecasting metrics are "computed on a gradient-based normalization process that scales each signal by dividing it by its accumulated gradient" (line 175), with a broken "see Sect." reference and no further explanation. Standard practice in neural time series evaluation is per-neuron z-score or min-max normalization. The choice of gradient-based scaling could systematically favor certain methods depending on how it interacts with signal smoothness. No comparison against standard normalization is provided, and no principled justification is given for why this normalization is appropriate. This makes the reported forecasting improvements difficult to interpret.

- **The forecasting targets depend on the pre-trained encoder's own quantization, creating an unanalyzed circular dependency.** In the response forecasting task (Section 3.4.2, lines 142–146), the ground-truth codebook indices used as training targets are obtained by feeding the full signal (baseline + response) through the pre-trained encoder and reading out the quantization indices for the response portion. This means the targets are generated by the same encoder whose representations are being learned. If the pre-trained encoder's quantization of the response is poor (e.g., because it was trained primarily on masked signals), the targets are noisy. This circular dependency is not discussed as a limitation, nor is its impact analyzed (e.g., by comparing against targets from a separately trained quantizer or against continuous regression).

- **Ablation study is confined to a single stimulus type.** The ablation (line 181) is performed only on "drifting gratings" for simplicity. This limits the generality of the ablation's conclusions, since different stimulus types may interact differently with the method's components.

- **Key numerical results are conveyed only qualitatively in the body text, with tables externalized via \input commands.** The prose describes performance in relative terms ("slightly better performance," "excels in both tasks") without reporting actual numbers. While the compiled PDF likely contains the tables, the body text should include at least summary statistics (e.g., the main metric values) so the reader can assess the magnitude of claimed improvements without relying on external files.

- **Interpretability analysis is entirely qualitative.** The t-SNE visualizations of codebook structure and neuron embeddings (Section 4.5, Figures 5–6) are assessed by visual inspection with threshold-based coloring. Claims about what the embeddings encode (activation frequency, response statistics) would be strengthened by quantitative evaluation (e.g., training a linear probe to predict these properties from the embeddings).

### Trivial

- The reference to the normalization method ("see Sect." in line 175) is broken, with no section number provided.

## Nice-to-Haves

- The mask ratio of 0.2 (line 162) is not motivated. A justification or ablation over mask ratios would strengthen the paper.
- Including per-neuron forecasting baselines (e.g., per-neuron LSTM or linear model) would test whether the neuron tokens and cross-neuron sharing provide added value beyond independent forecasting.
- Standard deviation values are claimed to be reported (line 167) but do not appear in the extracted body text; ensuring they are clearly visible alongside mean values would help interpret the results.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The two synthesized reviews largely recapitulate what the paper claims, with the harsh critic providing a necessary corrective on framing and evaluation rigor.

## Suggestions

1. **Reframe the paper honestly.** Remove or substantially qualify the optogenetics claim from Contribution #1 and the abstract. If the method is designed for retrospective forecasting, present it as such. The optogenetics framing does not add value — it creates an expectation the paper cannot meet.
2. **Justify or replace the gradient-based normalization.** Either provide a principled rationale (with citation) for why dividing by accumulated gradient is appropriate, or report primary results under a standard normalization (z-score) and show the gradient-based version as supplementary.
3. **Discuss the circular dependency in forecasting targets.** The fact that targets come from the pre-trained encoder's own quantization is worth acknowledging as a limitation, and ideally analyzing through a control experiment (e.g., using a separately trained quantizer).
4. **Include summary statistics of main results in the body text.** Even with full tables in the PDF, reporting key numbers (e.g., the main metric for QuantFormer vs. best competitor) in prose helps the reader assess the magnitude of improvements.
