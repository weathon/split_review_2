## Summary

The paper proposes Neural Field Classifiers (NFC), which convert standard neural field regression models (NeRF, DVGO, D-NeRF, NeuS) into "classification" models by encoding continuous RGB targets as 8-bit binary vectors and optimizing a clipped BCE loss with an MSE auxiliary term. The method replaces the final output layer (3 logits → 24 logits) and replaces the MSE rendering loss with a BCE-based classification loss. Experiments across four backbones show consistent improvements in novel view synthesis, surface reconstruction, sparse-input rendering, and robustness to image corruption.

## Strengths

1. **Consistent empirical improvements across diverse backbones and tasks**: NFC outperforms the regression baseline for all four tested backbones (DVGO, NeRF, D-NeRF, NeuS) on static scenes, dynamic scenes, sparse inputs, and corrupted images. On the T&T dataset (Table 2), improvements are consistent across all three backbones and three metrics. On the NeuS surface reconstruction task (Table 6), large geometric improvements are observed across all 8 Replica scenes.

2. **Minimal computational overhead**: Ablation tables (Tables 4–5) report only +4–6% training time increase (9.33→9.93 min for DVGO; 2.84→2.98 hours for NeuS) with near-zero rendering overhead. This makes the method practically appealing and the cost-benefit tradeoff clearly favorable.

3. **Robustness experiments beyond standard benchmarks**: The paper evaluates sparse inputs (20%–80% training data, Table 3) and Gaussian-corrupted training images (Table 4), showing meaningful gains (e.g., 14.87→19.38 PSNR at 20% data). These settings are rarely tested in NeRF papers and demonstrate practical value for real-world deployment with imperfect data.

4. **Addresses a numerical stability issue specific to neural fields**: The paper identifies that volume rendering can produce predictions outside [0,1], causing BCE gradient explosion—a problem absent in standard image classification—and proposes a clipped BCE+MSE hybrid loss (Eq. 9) with robustness to a wide range of λ (Fig. 5).

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Insufficient documentation of the Replica experimental setup undermines the headline result**: The paper's most striking claim—a 99.7% PSNR gain from 17.08 to 34.11 for DVGO on Replica (Table 1)—rests on regression baselines ranging from 13.03 to 22.54 PSNR. The paper describes Replica as having "sparse training views" (line 146) but never specifies how many views were used, what the train/test split was, or how this compares to the standard Replica evaluation protocol. Since the T&T regression baselines with the same DVGO backbone achieve a reasonable 22.41 PSNR, the enormous gap (13–22 on Replica vs. 22.41 on T&T) demands a specific explanation tied to the actual experimental configuration. The vague attribution to "complex scenes" combined with "sparse training views" is not sufficient—the paper should state exactly how many training views were used and whether the DVGO hyperparameters were tuned for the Replica setting. Without transparency on this setup, the paper's most impressive quantitative result cannot be properly assessed.

2. **The enormous NeuS geometry improvements are unexplained**: Changing only the color rendering loss (MSE→BCE) in NeuS produces a 273% F-score improvement (14.87→55.56) and 74.6% Chamfer-ℓ₁ reduction (30.24→7.69) on Replica (Table 6). The geometric component of NeuS (SDF network, Eikonal loss) is untouched. The paper reports these striking numbers without any mechanistic hypothesis. A result this large from a purely color-loss change either requires an explanation or verification that the NeuS regression baseline was properly configured. As written, it strains credulity.

3. **The generalization claim is supported by thin evidence**: The paper states that "the main gain of NFC comes from generalization rather than optimization" (line 473), supported only by a single learning curve on one scene (Replica Scene 6, DVGO). A broad mechanistic claim about why the method works should not rest on a single run with one model-scene combination.

### Minor

4. **"NFC w/o Target Encoding" ablation condition is not defined**: Tables 4–5 compare NFR → "NFC w/o Target Encoding" → full NFC, but the paper never explains what the "w/o Target Encoding" variant actually is. Does it apply BCE directly to 3-channel output without binary encoding? Does it use binary encoding with a different loss? Does it decode to continuous and apply BCE? This ambiguity reduces the informativeness of an otherwise well-structured ablation.

5. **No per-scene breakdowns or variance reported for T&T**: Only mean metrics over four T&T scenes are reported (Table 2) without per-scene results or standard deviations. Given the large scene-to-scene variability observed on Replica (PSNR range 13.03–22.54), reporting only means on T&T makes it impossible to assess consistency.

6. **Motivation about weak supervision is disconnected from the method**: The introduction motivates the work by noting that NeRF has "very weak and insufficient" supervision (line 31). However, switching from MSE to BCE does not change the amount of supervision—the same per-pixel signals are used. This framing is misleading and should be revised.

7. **Disproportionate LPIPS vs. PSNR improvement on D-NeRF Hook**: On the Hook scene (Table 3), LPIPS drops from 0.118 to 0.039 (a 67% improvement) while PSNR only improves from 29.25 to 29.45 (0.7%). This warrants explanation: is the classification loss producing different noise characteristics that LPIPS prefers, or is there an artifact in the rendering?

### Trivial

8. The term "gradient vanishing" (line 93) is imprecise: the gradient of the min-clip is intentionally zeroed for predictions outside (0,1), not vanishing due to saturating nonlinearities.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Comparison with L1 or smooth L1 losses would help isolate whether the improvement is specific to BCE or general to moving away from L2.
- A gradient or loss landscape analysis would strengthen the mechanistic understanding of why BCE helps.
- Per-scene breakdowns on T&T.
