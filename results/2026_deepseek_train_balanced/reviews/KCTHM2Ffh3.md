## Summary

This paper proposes the **Runtime Learning Machine**, a three-component architecture (HP-Student = Phy-DRL agent, HA-Teacher = verified physics-based safety controller with a real-time patch, Coordinator = switching logic) for safe online reinforcement learning on physical systems. The key technical novelty is the **real-time patch**: when the learning agent's actions approach a safety boundary, the HA-Teacher dynamically recomputes a verified safety controller using real-time state data (vs. a static backup controller in prior neural Simplex / runtime assurance work), and also corrects the student's unsafe actions and rewards via Equation (6) to enable learning transfer. Experiments on a cart-pole system and a real A1 quadruped robot, plus additional benchmarks claimed in the appendix, are presented.

---

## Strengths

1. **Two-way interaction that enables safety transfer from teacher to student** (Section 4.3, Equation 6). Unlike prior neural Simplex / runtime assurance architectures where the high-assurance module (HAM) and high-performance module (HPM) are independent — the HAM cannot teach and the HPM cannot learn — this paper's HA-Teacher corrects *both* the unsafe action *and* the reward stored in the replay buffer. The ablation in Figures 4 and 14 convincingly demonstrates that HP-Student progresses from requiring HA-Teacher intervention (episode 5: system states confined to safety set) to autonomous safe control (episode 20: states within the tighter safety envelope, HA-Teacher rarely triggered). This is the paper's strongest empirical evidence.

2. **Formal safety guarantee with practically feasible computation time** (Theorem 6.4, Remark 6.5). The real-time patch is provably a subset of the safety set (Requirement 2), and the HA-Teacher's action satisfies operation constraints (Requirement 3). The LMI-based computation runs in 0.01–0.04 seconds, establishing that the formal backup controller is real-time feasible — a genuine advance over static, pre-computed HAM designs.

3. **Real-robot validation under a Sim2Real gap**. The A1 quadruped experiment deliberately mismatches the simulator velocity command (0.6 m/s) from the real command (0.35 m/s), creating a genuine Sim2Real gap. The paper includes real-robot trajectory data and video demonstrations, adding practical credibility beyond simulation-only evaluations.

4. **Clear identification of the safety-regulation dimension gap** (Section 4.1). The paper correctly identifies that DRL cannot embed high-dimensional safety regulations into a one-dimensional reward, and explains how the safety envelope concept bridges this gap — a design rationale that is then shared with HA-Teacher for the patch design.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **"Unknown unknowns" claim is not supported by the experiments.** The paper defines unknown unknowns as outcomes that "cannot be predicted in time and distributions" with "almost zero historical data" (Section 1.1). Yet every experimental disturbance is a pre-enumerated, known type with a known parametric form:
   - **Cart-pole**: "disturbances generated by a randomized Beta distribution" — this is a known distribution family, i.e., a *known unknown* with random parameters.
   - **A1 robot**: Beta disturbances, packet drop (DoS), kick, side push — all are known disturbance types that the designer explicitly anticipated and enumerated. The paper frames these as "unknown unknowns that have never occurred in HP-Student's historical training and learning" which collapses the distinction between "out-of-distribution relative to training data" and "genuinely unanticipated in type." This is a significant overclaim. Tolerating randomized parameters from known disturbance families is a weaker and less novel property than tolerating genuinely unanticipated events. Since "tolerating unknown unknowns" is listed as characteristic (ii) in the abstract, this mismatch between claim and evidence is the paper's most serious weakness. The contribution would be better described as robustness to runtime disturbances with randomized parameters, not unknown unknowns.

### Minor

2. **Comparison baselines are predominantly ablated versions of the proposed system.** The paper compares against CLF-DRL-Simplex, Phy-DRL-Simplex, pure Phy-DRL, and pure CLF-DRL — all obtained by disabling components of the proposed architecture. While the paper argues these correspond to existing methods (neural Simplex, runtime assurance, Lyapunov-based DRL), this is an argument by correspondence rather than independent implementation. The Sim2Real comparison is limited to one family of baselines (delay + domain randomization). For a paper that claims to outperform "state-of-the-art safe DRL, fault-tolerant DRL, and approaches for addressing Sim2Real gap" (abstract), independent implementations of at least a few representative methods from these categories would substantially strengthen the evaluation.

3. **Limited quantitative rigor.** The cart-pole reward trajectories in Figure 3(c) are reported over only five random seeds with no error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical testing. The A1 robot experiment does not specify the number of trials, and the claims about safety guarantee ("in any sampled learning episode") are based on qualitative trajectory plots and video demonstrations rather than quantified safety violation rates across multiple runs.

## Suggestions

1. **Rebrand the "unknown unknowns" claim.** Replace this terminology with a precise description of what is actually demonstrated: robustness to randomized disturbances from known families applied at runtime, which are novel to the agent's training history. If genuinely unanticipated disturbances are to be claimed, the experiments need to include events the designer did not pre-enumerate (e.g., an unmodeled physical coupling, sensor degradation, or plant parameter shift discovered at runtime).

2. **Add at least one independent baseline from the safe RL or fault-tolerant DRL literature.** Implementing a representative method (e.g., a Lyapunov-based approach, an MPC shielding baseline, or a safety-gym method) would substantiate the claim of outperforming state-of-the-art approaches without relying entirely on the ablation argument.

3. **Report quantitative safety metrics with variance.** For the cart-pole experiments, report the mean and standard deviation of reward, safety violation rate, and HA-Teacher activation ratio across seeds. For the A1 robot, report the number of episodes/trials and the rate at which safety was maintained.

## Score and Decision

The paper proposes a genuinely interesting architecture — the real-time patch mechanism that dynamically recomputes the safety controller at intervention time is a meaningful advance over static HAM designs, and the two-way learning interaction (Equation 6) addresses a real limitation of prior work. The controlled ablation demonstrating automatic hierarchy learning is convincing, and the real-robot experiments add practical credibility.

However, the central claim about tolerating "unknown unknowns" is not supported by the experiments, which test known, pre-enumerated disturbance types with randomized parameters. This is not a minor overstatement — it is a characteristic listed in the abstract and the main motivation for Challenge 1. Additionally, the evaluation relies almost entirely on ablated versions of the proposed system rather than independent baselines, and quantitative rigor is limited. As submitted, the paper overclaims relative to its evidence.
