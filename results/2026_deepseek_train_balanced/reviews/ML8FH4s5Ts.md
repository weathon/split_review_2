## Summary

X-NeMo proposes a zero-shot diffusion-based portrait animation pipeline where motion is encoded as a compact 1D latent vector (via a learned motion encoder) and injected into the diffusion backbone through cross-attention rather than spatially-aligned additive guidance (e.g., ControlNet). The authors argue that prior spatially-aligned 2D control induces identity leakage by encouraging the UNet to shortcut on layout structure, and that explicit motion descriptors (landmarks, blendshapes) are too coarse for fine-grained expressiveness. They support the core design with several well-motivated training strategies (dual GAN head supervision, color/spatial augmentations, reference feature masking) and validate each through ablations.

## Strengths

- **Structure-agnostic cross-attention motion control is a principled and well-validated core insight.** The paper correctly identifies that 2D spatially-aligned control (ControlNet/PoseGuider) lets the UNet shortcut on layout similarity rather than learning semantic correspondences, causing identity leakage in cross-identity reenactment. The proposed solution — a 1D latent descriptor injected via cross-attention — directly addresses this, and the ablation against a ControlNet variant ("w/o cross-attn" in Table 2 and Figure 3(a)) confirms that the design is necessary: the 2D variant measurably reduces ID-SIM. This is the paper's strongest contribution.

- **End-to-end learned motion without pre-trained detectors escapes the expressiveness ceiling of prior work.** Previous implicit-motion methods (X-Portrait, Yang et al.) learned motion from synthetic pairs generated by off-the-shelf animators, which themselves fail on complex expressions (tongue protrusion, cheek puffing). X-NeMo learns motion directly from raw video (including the expressive NerSemble dataset), avoiding this bottleneck. The qualitative results (Figure 5) show capture of exaggerated expressions that baselines miss.

- **The dual GAN decoder head is a practical solution to a genuine failure mode of diffusion loss for fine-grained motion.** The observation that diffusion loss (equal weight per pixel) biases the motion encoder toward low-frequency/coarse motion, and that a lightweight GAN decoder provides complementary image-level gradients, is well-motivated and empirically supported by the "w/o GAN head" ablation (Table 2, Figure 4(a)).

- **Ablation studies validate each design choice individually.** Table 2 shows quantitative degradation when removing each component (GAN head, joint training, RFM, augmentations, cross-attention control), making the contribution of each component clear.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Asymmetric baseline evaluation protocol undermines the cross-reenactment quantitative comparisons.** The paper finetunes AniPortrait, X-Portrait, and PD-FGC on the authors' training dataset, while evaluating FYE, Echomimic, and LivePortrait using released pretrained models without finetuning (lines 110–111). The justification ("given the unavailability of their training code") is practical but does not make the comparison fair — methods evaluated out-of-the-box on a test distribution they were not trained for will predictably underperform relative to their true capability. Since the paper's claim of "state-of-the-art" performance rests substantially on Table 1, this asymmetry is a significant concern. The paper should at minimum (a) transparently flag this asymmetry as a limitation of the comparison, (b) report results under a unified protocol (all finetuned or all using pretrained weights), or (c) show that the qualitative/quantitative advantage persists even in a controlled setting where all methods use only pretrained models.

### Minor

- **No uncertainty estimates on any quantitative metric.** Table 1 reports point estimates without error bars, standard deviations, or confidence intervals across the 400 test videos. Given the diversity of the test data (real portraits, AI-generated art, expressive DFEW clips, licensed videos), variance is expected. Without it, the reader cannot assess whether reported margins (e.g., in AED or ID-SIM) reflect robust improvements or are within evaluation noise. This is especially consequential given the asymmetric baseline protocol above.

- **The AED motion metric is partially misaligned with the paper's central claim.** The paper argues that explicit representations (including blendshapes) are too coarse for fine-grained expressiveness (line 14, line 122). Yet AED is defined as the L1 difference between MediaPipe blendshape coefficients of driving and generated frames. If the method genuinely captures motions finer than what blendshapes represent, AED will not reflect that advantage — both strong and weak models could converge on similar blendshape scores. The paper partially addresses this by also reporting EMO-SIM (EmoNet-based emotion similarity), which is the more relevant metric for the expressiveness claim. However, the narrative still treats AED as a primary motion accuracy metric throughout. The paper would be strengthened by either (a) showing that AED and EMO-SIM are well-correlated in this setting, or (b) providing a per-expression-category breakdown where blendshapes are known to fail (tongue protrusion, cheek puffing) and demonstrating that X-NeMo succeeds where baselines do not.

- **No temporal consistency metric.** The method uses temporal modules and prompt traveling for cross-frame smoothness, but no quantitative temporal consistency metric (e.g., warping error, temporal LPIPS, face consistency over time) is reported. For a video animation method producing sequences, this is a noticeable omission that the qualitative results alone cannot fully address.

- **Motion encoder architecture is underspecified for reproducibility.** The paper states that the encoder uses "convolution-based feature extraction and self-attention, followed by MLP layers" (line 55), but does not specify the number of layers, channel dimensions, the output dimension of f_mot, or the detailed architecture of the cross-attention motion-attention layers. Since the motion encoder is central to the method, these details matter for reproduction.

- **No breakdown of results by portrait type or expression difficulty.** The benchmark includes diverse portraits (real photos, AI-generated, artistic), but performance is aggregated. Gains may be concentrated in one category. Similarly, no expression-specific breakdown is provided to directly test the expressiveness claim.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Runtime, parameter count, and inference speed comparison against baselines would help readers assess practical trade-offs.
- A sensitivity analysis of the motion latent dimension (e.g., 32, 64, 128, 256) on ID-SIM and EMO-SIM would strengthen the information-bottleneck motivation.
- The 30% masking rate for RFM is given but not justified; a sensitivity study would be informative.

## Suggestions

1. **Equalize the evaluation protocol.** Option A: Finetune all baselines on the same training data. Option B: Evaluate all methods (including your own) using only released pretrained weights, and clearly separate "zero-shot" from "finetuned" comparisons. Option C: Do both and present them separately.

2. **Report standard deviations (or per-sample distributions) for all metrics in Table 1.** This is standard practice and would substantially strengthen the quantitative evidence.

3. **Add an expression-category breakdown.** Categorize test frames by expression type (e.g., neutral vs. extreme: tongue protrusion, cheek puffing, eye squinting) and report per-category EMO-SIM and qualitative comparisons. This directly validates the central expressiveness claim.

4. **Provide a temporal consistency metric** (e.g., warping error or face landmark temporal smoothness) in the main paper.

5. **Specify the motion encoder architecture in detail** (layer counts, channel dimensions, latent dimension f_mot) for reproducibility.
