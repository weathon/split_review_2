## Summary

This paper provides a theoretical analysis of sample replay in over-parameterized continual linear regression, proving that replay can *increase* forgetting rather than only mitigate it. Theorems 3.2 and 3.6 give, respectively, (1) a worst-case construction where replaying a single adversarially chosen sample transforms forgetting from O(1/T) to Θ(1), and (2) an average-case construction in ℝ³ where replaying a *randomly* chosen sample increases expected forgetting. The mechanism — error exchange via replay exploiting intra-task sample interference — is clearly explained. The paper also provides supporting experiments on linear models, MLPs trained on linear tasks, and MNIST.

## Strengths

- **Theorem 3.2 (Worst-case amplification — Section 3.1.1):** The paper proves concretely that replaying a single adversarially chosen sample can change forgetting from vanishing O(1/T) to catastrophic Θ(1) in over-parameterized linear regression. The construction is explicit (three samples x₁, x₂, x₃ with a specific geometric configuration), and the error-exchange mechanism is clearly articulated. This goes beyond prior work (Evron et al., 2022) which only characterized forgetting *without* replay.

- **Theorem 3.6 (Average-case increase — Section 3.2.2):** This is the more surprising result — it proves that even when the replay sample is chosen *randomly* from the task distribution, forgetting can increase *in expectation* for certain task geometries. This rules out the objection that only adversarially selected samples cause harm. The geometric intuition (Figure 1) connecting the angle between task null spaces to replay's effect is well explained and gives actionable insight.

- **Clear mechanism and well-structured theory:** The paper identifies intra-task sample interference as the driver (Section 3, lines 93-94): replay introduces interference among samples within a task that is absent without replay. The projection dynamics framework is clean, building transparently on Evron et al. (2022). Remark 3.8 (necessary condition: some forgetting on the replay sample must exist) is a useful caveat that is properly noted.

- **Empirical non-monotonicity finding (Figure 2):** The experiments demonstrate that forgetting can be non-monotonic with respect to the number of replay samples — initially increasing, then decreasing. This is a practically relevant observation with implications for memory-constrained continual learning, where a small buffer might be worse than no buffer.

- **Contribution to a gap in the literature:** Existing theoretical work on replay in continual learning primarily analyzed cycling over full task sets (Evron et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2022) or required O(d²) memory via null-space storage (Peng et al., 2023). The low-sample replay regime was underexplored theoretically. This paper fills that gap.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None.

### Minor

- **Abstract overstates the "model-independence" claim relative to the evidence.** The abstract states: "We provide empirical evidence that this is a property of the tasks rather than the model used to train on them, by showing a similar behavior for a neural net equipped with SGD." The supporting experiments (Section 4.1) train an MLP with one hidden layer and ReLU on tasks that are *themselves linear* — the data is generated by a linear model. Showing that a nonlinear optimizer reproduces the same behavior on a linear problem does demonstrate robustness across *parameterization* (linear model vs. MLP), but the phrasing "property of the tasks rather than the model" could be read as claiming the effect extends to genuinely nonlinear task structures, which is not shown. The body text (Section 4.1, line 188) is more careful ("this behavior is not restricted to training with linear models"), and the abstract should match that precision.

- **Non-monotonicity is only empirically demonstrated, not theoretically established.** The abstract notes "Surprisingly, we find that forgetting can be non-monotonic with respect to the number of replay samples." While the word "find" is standard for empirical observations and the phenomenon is clearly shown in Figure 2, the paper's theoretical results (Theorems 3.2, 3.6) only prove that replay *can increase* forgetting, not that it goes up and then down. A reader might reasonably expect a theoretical result for a claim highlighted in the abstract. The paper would benefit from noting more explicitly that non-monotonicity is an empirical observation from the constructions, not a proved theorem.

- **The MNIST experiments provide supporting illustration but limited independent evidence.** The Split MNIST result (Figure 5, sequence 0,6–1,7 with 2 replay samples) shows a statistically significant increase in forgetting, which supports the paper's existence claim. However, this is a single task sequence with a specific parameter choice. The Rotated MNIST experiments (Figure 3) predominantly show replay decreases forgetting or is neutral. The paper's claims about MNIST are appropriately modest ("there exists sequences where forgetting can increase with replay"), but the experiments would carry more weight with additional task sequences showing similar behavior or a more direct test of the geometric mechanism (e.g., measuring subspace angles in the feature space of the penultimate layer).

### Trivial

- The "limited theoretical treatment of their efficacy" claim (line 15, Introduction) undersells the work cited in the paper's own Related Work section (Doan et al., 2021; Goldfarb & Hand, 2023; Lin et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023). The paper is not the first theoretical treatment of continual learning methods; it is the first to prove replay can *harm* in this setting. The framing could be more precise.

- In the worst-case construction, the Θ(1) constant's dependence on construction parameters is not specified. Clarifying would help readers calibrate what "catastrophic" means in absolute terms.

- The connection between the linear theory and MNIST experiments is acknowledged as a hypothesis ("We hypothesize that the visual similarity...") but no direct verification of the subspace-angle mechanism is attempted.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A formal equation showing how replay modifies the null-space projection dynamics (i.e., how the replay-augmented null space projection differs from the original) would sharpen the theoretical exposition beyond the current intuitive description.
- An experiment testing a genuinely nonlinear task structure (not just a nonlinear model on linear tasks) would genuinely extend the model-independence claim.
- A discussion of what happens when the replay buffer is dynamically updated or the replay sample itself is forgotten would be a natural next step.

## Removed Points

- **Framing overshoot (from Harsh Critic, Point 1):** The critic claimed the paper's title/abstract imply broader generality than the theorems establish. However, the title says "can provably increase" (an existence claim) and the abstract says "We construct tasks where..." — both are precisely matched to what is proved. The concern about hypothetical reader misinterpretation does not reflect the paper's actual claims. Removed.

- **MNIST evidence thin (from Harsh Critic, Point 3):** The critic argued the MNIST evidence for increased forgetting is thin. However, the paper's claim about MNIST is explicitly an existence claim ("there exists sequences where forgetting can increase with replay"), which is supported by the statistically significant Split MNIST result. The critic's concern about "the broader lesson" is not a lesson the paper actually draws from MNIST. Removed.

- **Non-monotonic not established theoretically (from Harsh Critic, Point 4):** The critic objected that the non-monotonicity claim is not a theorem. The paper says "we find" (standard language for empirical observation) and demonstrates it in Figure 2. The paper does not claim a theorem for non-monotonicity. This was downgraded to Minor rather than removed, as it is a reasonable observation about the presentation.

- **Strength about problem importance (from Strength Finder):** Several claimed strengths were generic or sycophantic and have been removed. The kept strengths are those with concrete, paper-specific evidence.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The core novel insight — that replay can *increase* forgetting through an error-exchange mechanism driven by intra-task sample interference — is the paper's own contribution and is well articulated in the text.

## Suggestions

1. **Tighten the abstract's "model-independence" claim** to match the body's more precise wording ("this behavior is not restricted to training with linear models"). This would resolve the main framing gap without weakening the paper.
2. **Add a brief remark** in Section 3 clarifying that non-monotonicity is observed empirically from the construction, not proved theoretically.
3. **Clarify what the Θ(1) constant in Theorem 3.2 depends on** — is it the angle between x₁ and x₂? The norm of w*? This would help readers understand the magnitude of the effect.
