## Summary

The paper proposes ELM (Embedding Language Model), a framework that trains adapter layers to map domain embedding vectors (from MovieLens 25M movie and user embeddings) into an LLM's token embedding space, enabling an LLM to generate natural language descriptions and engage in dialogue about those embeddings. The method uses a two-stage training procedure and is evaluated on 24 movie-interpretation tasks and a user-profile task using semantic consistency (SC), behavioral consistency (BC), and human evaluation with 100 raters per task.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated problem formulation.** The idea of using adapter layers to let an LLM "converse" about arbitrary points in a domain embedding space — including hypothetical entities with no ground-truth description — is genuinely novel and addresses a real gap in embedding interpretability. The paper clearly articulates why this goes beyond what text-only LLMs or traditional interpretability methods (t-SNE, UMAP, CAVs) can do.

2. **Two-stage training procedure is thoughtfully designed.** The paper identifies a practical convergence problem (a randomly initialized adapter struggles to map into a pretrained LLM's well-formed token embedding space) and proposes a two-stage solution: freeze all parameters except the adapter in stage one, then fine-tune everything in stage two (lines 79–83). This is a principled architectural insight that directly supports the method's viability.

3. **Large-scale human evaluation with strong results.** Most single-movie tasks score above 0.80 (and many above 0.90) on all three human evaluation axes (consistency with plot, language comprehensiveness, task quality), with 100 raters per task (Table 1). This provides direct evidence that ELM's outputs align with human expectations for familiar entities, going beyond automated metrics.

4. **SC and BC metrics enable evaluation where no ground-truth text exists.** The semantic consistency metric (re-embedding generated text into the same semantic space and measuring cosine similarity) and behavioral consistency (using generated user profiles to predict actual ratings) provide evaluation mechanisms for hypothetical embedding points — a key requirement the paper identifies that text-only LLMs cannot satisfy.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **No comparison baselines for the core 24 tasks.** Table 1 reports ELM's own scores on all tasks but compares against no alternative method. The only comparison in the paper (Figure 2) is against text-only LLMs on interpolation tasks, which is a sanity check (text-only LLMs cannot receive embedding vectors) rather than a meaningful baseline. Without comparisons to alternatives such as nearest-neighbor retrieval from the training set (find the closest movie embedding and return its PaLM 2-L description), a simple MLP mapping embeddings to attributes followed by template-based generation, or even ablating the LLM backbone entirely, the reader cannot assess whether ELM's adapter-based architecture contributes beyond much simpler approaches. This is the most significant weakness in the paper.

2. **No ablation studies.** The paper's technical contribution involves several design choices: (a) a 2-layer MLP adapter, (b) two-stage training, (c) multitask training over 24 tasks, and (d) fine-tuning the LLM backbone (PaLM 2-XS). None of these are ablated. The reader cannot determine whether the adapter is necessary (vs. a linear projection), whether two-stage training outperforms single-stage end-to-end training, or whether fine-tuning the backbone matters versus keeping it frozen. For a methods paper at a top venue, this is a substantial gap.

3. **No variance or statistical significance reported for any result.** Table 1 and all figures report only point estimates. No standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests are provided anywhere. With 100 raters per task, rater-level variance must exist but is not reported. Without variance measures, it is impossible to assess whether differences between tasks (e.g., "similarities" scoring 0.37 vs. "summary" scoring 0.87 on human evaluation) or between conditions are meaningful.

### Minor

4. **Semantic Consistency metric has a circularity concern.** SC re-embeds ELM's output text using the *same* DLM that produced the ground-truth semantic embeddings (line 125: "In our experiments we use the same DLM to serve as P"). If ELM learns to produce text that the DLM maps back to similar embeddings — even text that is generic or only loosely faithful to the movie — it will still score well. The human evaluation partially mitigates this concern, but the paper does not discuss this limitation or test SC using a different embedding model.

5. **Training targets are entirely generated by PaLM 2-L, creating a confound.** All training outputs for the 24 movie tasks and the user profile task are generated by prompting PaLM 2-L with movie titles (lines 106–112). ELM is therefore trained to reproduce "what PaLM 2-L would say about this movie." Since PaLM 2-L has extensive parametric knowledge about popular movies, it is unclear how much of ELM's performance comes from genuinely interpreting the embedding versus recovering PaLM 2-L's existing knowledge through the embedding as a cue. The paper acknowledges ELM receives no title information, but the training signal is entirely mediated by a model that *does* know the movies by name. This confound is not discussed.

6. **CAV generalization claims lack attribute-specific validation.** The paper extrapolates movie and user embeddings along CAV directions and reports SC/BC scores (Figures 3–4), but never validates whether the generated text actually reflects the intended attribute shift (e.g., does moving a movie embedding in the "funnier" direction produce text describing a funnier movie?). The finding that BC *increases* with extrapolation magnitude for user profiles is attributed to profiles becoming "more extreme" and therefore more predictable — which, if true, suggests the CAV manipulation is amplifying CF model artifacts rather than producing meaningful attribute shifts. Without human evaluation of the attribute-specific quality of CAV-guided outputs, or comparison to standard CAV usage, this claim is unsupported.

### Trivial

7. **The "neutral review" row in Table 1 shows an unexplained pattern.** This task achieves human consistency = 1.0 and language comprehensiveness = 1.0, but task quality = 0.56. This large discrepancy (perfect consistency/comprehensiveness but low task quality) is never discussed in the paper. If genuine, it merits analysis; if an annotation artifact, it should be clarified.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Adding nearest-neighbor retrieval and linear probe baselines would substantially strengthen the evaluation without requiring new model training — the training data (PaLM 2-L outputs) and embeddings already exist.
- Validating SC by using a *different* embedding model for re-embedding than the one used to produce semantic embeddings would break the circularity and strengthen the metric's credibility.
- A simple comparison where the LLM receives top-k nearest neighbors' textual descriptions (instead of the embedding) would isolate whether the adapter-based approach adds value over providing comparable information to a text-only LLM.
- Reporting standard deviations or showing per-example distributions for at least the main results would greatly improve empirical rigor.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting tension that emerges across the reviewer inputs is about what ELM actually measures: the SC metric's circularity, the PaLM 2-L training target confound, and the lack of baselines all converge on the same underlying question — is ELM genuinely interpreting the *information content* of the embedding, or is it learning a projection into the space of what a text-only LLM already knows about popular movies, using the embedding as a noisy cue? The human evaluation partially answers this (strong scores on consistency with plot suggest the outputs are genuinely about the right movies), but the paper never cleanly separates these two explanations. A simple experiment — testing ELM on embeddings of obscure movies that PaLM 2-L has little parametric knowledge about, or on synthetic embeddings that don't correspond to any real movie — would directly address this. The fact that the paper already evaluates on interpolated/hypothetical embeddings puts it halfway there, but the evaluation still uses the same PaLM 2-L-derived SC metric, so the confound persists.

## Suggestions

1. **Add baseline comparisons to Table 1.** At minimum: (a) nearest-neighbor retrieval (find the training movie with the closest embedding and return its PaLM 2-L description), and (b) a linear probe that maps embeddings to a bag of attributes or keywords followed by template-based generation. If ELM substantially outperforms these, its contribution is demonstrated on its own terms.

2. **Add ablation studies** isolating at minimum: (a) linear adapter vs. 2-layer MLP, (b) single-stage vs. two-stage training, (c) frozen vs. fine-tuned LLM backbone.

3. **Report variance** for all main results — standard deviations across test examples for SC/BC, and across raters for human evaluation.

4. **Validate CAV attribute shifts** with human evaluation: do human raters agree that text generated from an embedding extrapolated in the "funnier" direction describes a funnier movie than the original?

5. **Add a limitations section** explicitly discussing the PaLM 2-L training target confound, the SC circularity, and the restriction to a single dataset/domain.
