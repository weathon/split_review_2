## Summary
The paper validates Goal-Oriented Environment Inference (GOEI), a Bayesian model-based RL algorithm introduced in Takahashi et al. (2024), on the two-player card game Hol's der Geier. The central empirical finding is that GOEI achieves near-Nash-equilibrium performance (median reward rate −0.010 against the NE opponent) while compressing 15,542 reachable observations into only 452 representative states. A mutual information analysis (Figure 3) examines which observable features are retained vs. discarded after compression.

---

## Strengths

- **Concrete and quantified state compression result (Table 1, Figure 2B):** GOEI reduces to 452 representative states from 15,542 reachable observations (2.9%) while sustaining near-NE performance. At rounds t=2 and t=3, the inferred state count falls *below* the number of states NE itself requires—a counter-intuitive and meaningful finding.

- **Mutual information analysis (Figure 3, Section 4.2):** The decomposition of retained vs. lost information per feature across rounds is non-trivial. The finding that SD becomes strategically important only at round t=4 while AH and OH are almost entirely discarded despite intuitive relevance is a specific, quantified interpretability result—the paper's most original analytical contribution.

- **Transparent evaluation methodology (Section 3.3):** The authors explicitly separate environment inference (offline training on Rand vs. NE data) from strategy evaluation, and clearly state this design choice. This is methodologically honest and avoids circular feedback between inference and strategy.

---

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The core algorithm is entirely from prior work, leaving thin novel contribution.** The GOEI algorithm—variational Bayesian inference with Dirichlet process clustering, state reduction objective (Eq. 4), ELBO optimization, and the full parameter update procedure—is developed in Takahashi et al. (2024). The present paper applies it to one new card game under idealized conditions. For a venue like ICLR, this level of novelty is insufficient: the paper is an empirical note extending a published algorithm to a single new domain rather than a self-contained scientific contribution.

- **The "online learning" motivation is unsubstantiated.** Section 1 explicitly positions GOEI as advantageous for "tasks that require online learning to adapt to opponents," distinguishing it from DNN agents that rely on "offline learning with vast amounts of data." Yet the evaluation (Section 3.3) trains GOEI on 300,000 games generated by a *fixed* pair (Rand vs. NE) and tests in a completely separate phase—a batch offline inference setup. Section 5 acknowledges this gap ("The effectiveness of the GOEI function in interactive learning should be further confirmed"), but this is not a peripheral limitation: it is the primary motivating advantage that goes entirely untested.

- **Weak baselines.** The only comparator is tabular Q-learning with four learning rates. For a two-player zero-sum game, CFR (Counterfactual Regret Minimization) is the standard baseline; it is neither compared against nor discussed. Without it, there is no basis for asserting that GOEI's near-NE performance represents a meaningful advance in game solving rather than a straightforward advantage over an approach (tabular Q-learning) that is structurally expected to fail on a 15,542-state space from limited data. No model-based RL baseline with any form of state abstraction is tested.

### Minor

- **Observation count inconsistency.** Section 2.1 states 28,477 total possible observations; Section 3.3 uses 15,542 (observations reachable under Rand vs. NE play). The abstract's "2.9% of all possible observations" headline is based on the restricted denominator, a fact that is explained in Section 3.3 but not flagged in the abstract or introduction. This is not a flaw but reduces the precision of the headline claim.

- **Hyperparameter selection on the reported set.** Best parameters (β=0.2, α=25 for GOEI; η=0.2 for Q-learning) are selected by grid search visible in Table 1, which also reports the results. No held-out validation set or cross-validation is described, creating potential overfitting of the hyperparameter choice to the reporting data.

### Trivial
None beyond parser formatting artifacts.

In context of the reward scale (Q-learning sits at −0.079, the scale is [−0.527, 0.000]), a gap of 0.010 is genuinely small. Not a substantive flaw. Removed.

- **Claim about NE calculations requiring 247/945/69 states being an unfair denominator**: The paper correctly uses NE's required states as a reference point; this is a natural benchmark, not a methodological error. Removed.

## Novel Insights
The mutual information decomposition in Figure 3 is the paper's most original contribution: it reveals that neither AH nor OH—intuitively the most strategically relevant features (knowing what cards remain in both hands)—are individually preserved in the core state representation. The required strategic information appears to reside in complex non-linear combinations across all features, not in any single interpretable dimension. This is a concrete, game-specific finding about the structure of minimal sufficient statistics in competitive sequential decision-making that is interesting beyond this particular domain.

---

## Suggestions
1. Add at least a simple interactive/self-play setting to directly test the online adaptation claim from Section 1.
2. Add a CFR baseline to situate GOEI within the two-player zero-sum game-solving literature.
3. Clarify in the abstract and introduction that the 2.9% figure uses observations reachable under the training distribution (not all 28,477 total game states).
4. Report hyperparameter selection on a separate validation partition, not the result-reporting table.
