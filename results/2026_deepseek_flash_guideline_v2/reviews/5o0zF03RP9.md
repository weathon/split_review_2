## Summary

The paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments standard RL rewards with a KL divergence between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), weighted by a parameter β. The central claimed novelty is a Bayesian scheme for adapting β online. Experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid compare against β=0 baseline.

## Strengths

1. **Clear fixed-β formulation (Section 3.2, Eq. 1)** — The KL-based incentive shaping term is clearly presented: r_total = r_ext - β·KL(p||q). The core idea of penalizing misalignment between predicted and preferred outcomes is intuitively sensible and well-motivated.

2. **Substantial quantitative improvements on MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 (Section 5.4, Figure 2)** — With β=0.01, IncentRL achieves 98% success rate vs 90.5% for the DQN baseline and reduces mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps, averaged over 3 seeds and 2 million timesteps. These are concrete improvements on a standard sparse-reward benchmark.

3. **Honest reporting of negative results for high β on MountainCar (Section 5.3, Table 2)** — The paper transparently reports that β=0.3 and β=1.0 degrade performance (−52.76% and −75.01% respectively), providing useful diagnostic information about sensitivity.

4. **Variance reduction in 2-state toy MDP (Section 5.2, Table 1)** — Over 7 seeds, IncentRL reduces standard deviation from 11.30 (β=0) to ~6.66 (β>0) while improving mean final reward by ~74%. This cleanly illustrates the stabilizing effect of the KL term in an idealized setting.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Bayesian β adaptation — claimed as the central novelty — is never specified (Abstract, Section 3, Section 5.4, Appendix A).** The abstract calls this "the central novelty," and it is listed first among contributions. Yet the paper never states what prior is placed on β, what likelihood function updates the posterior, how β is sampled from the posterior and used during training, or what constitutes a "round" in the adaptation process. Section 3 (Methods) only describes the fixed-β formulation (Eq. 1) and never introduces Bayesian machinery. Section 5.4 mentions "tracking the posterior evolution of β using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" without saying what that scheme is. Appendix A reports summary statistics (Table 3) but again provides no description of the mechanism. A method paper whose primary claimed contribution is a black box cannot be accepted on that basis. The reader cannot evaluate soundness, novelty, or correctness of the Bayesian adaptation.

2. **No comparison against any existing intrinsic motivation method (all experiments).** The Related Work (Section 2) discusses curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al. 2017, Burda et al. 2018), empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende 2015), VIME (Houthooft et al. 2016), KL-regularized RL (Schulman et al. 2015, 2017; Haarnoja et al. 2018), and active inference (Friston et al. 2017). Yet every experiment compares only against β=0 (standard RL without intrinsic motivation). The paper claims IncentRL "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning, a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods," but provides no evidence that it is competitive with those methods. An improvement over β=0 is insufficient to demonstrate the usefulness of a new intrinsic motivation method, because any reasonable intrinsic signal could be expected to aid exploration in sparse-reward settings. The relevant comparison is against existing approaches, and it is absent.

3. **Core mechanics underspecified for non-toy environments (Sections 5.3–5.4).** The method requires computing KL(p(o|s,a) || q(o|s)). For MountainCar (continuous state space, 3 discrete actions) and MiniGrid (complex visual grid world), the paper does not specify: (a) what the outcome variable o is (next state? latent representation? something else?), (b) how p(o|s,a) is represented and learned (learned forward dynamics model? what architecture? how trained?), or (c) how q(o|s) is concretely specified — in MountainCar, "assigning all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)" in a continuous state space requires defining a specific distributional form, but no definition is given. Section 4.3 suggests p and q "can be modeled as Gaussians" in latent spaces but does not confirm whether this was done. This level of underspecification prevents reproducibility assessment.

### Minor

4. **Internal inconsistency in reported β convergence values (Section 5.4, Figures 3–4, Table 3).** The text (line 230) and figure captions repeatedly claim the posterior mean concentrates "near the effective region (β ≈ 0.1)." However, Table 3 shows the posterior mean evolving from 0.106 (Round 1) → 0.045 (Round 2) → 0.019 (Round 3) → 0.017 (Round 4). The Figure 3 caption confirms it "decreases to about 0.02 by Round 4." The posterior concentrates at β≈0.017, not β≈0.1. The actual convergence (0.017) is consistent with the best fixed β for MiniGrid (β=0.01), which is a positive result — but the paper's repeated claim of β≈0.1 is a factual error in describing its own data. This undermines credibility in the reporting.

5. **Theoretical analysis is observationally trivial (Section 4).** Proposition 1 states that for sufficiently small β the optimal policy is preserved; Proposition 2 states that as β→∞ the KL penalty dominates. These are the two end-members of any bounded interpolation between two objectives and follow from continuity of the objective in β. They provide no insight specific to IncentRL. The toy example in Section 4.1 merely illustrates that an action leading to a preferred outcome has lower KL divergence — which is definitional, not analytical.

### Trivial

6. **Toy 2-state MDP experiment is a sanity check (Section 5.2).** p(o|s,a) is set to the true transition probabilities and q to the optimal state, giving the agent perfect knowledge of the forward model and the optimal preference. This does not test any of the challenges arising in realistic settings (learning p(o|s,a) from data, specifying q without knowing optimal behavior).

## Nice-to-Haves

- The connection to dopamine RPE and the Free Energy Principle (Sections 2.4, 3.3, 6.3) is asserted rather than argued. The paper does not derive IncentRL from these theories nor test predictions derived from them. Moving this to a discussion section rather than framing it as a contribution would better match what is delivered.
- With only 3 seeds in MountainCar and MiniGrid, confidence intervals or statistical significance tests would strengthen the quantitative results.
- Explicit comparison of Bayesian β adaptation against a simple annealing schedule or meta-gradient approach would directly support the claim of superiority over manual tuning.

## Suggestions

1. **Fully specify the Bayesian adaptation scheme** — State the prior on β, the likelihood model, the posterior update rule (variational, MCMC, or conjugate), how β is sampled and applied during training, and what a "round" means. Without this, the core contribution is unevaluable.

2. **Add comparisons against existing intrinsic motivation methods** — At minimum, compare against ICM (Pathak et al. 2017) or RND (Burda et al. 2018) on the same benchmarks using the same base RL algorithm. The paper's central claim (superiority over manual tuning in intrinsic motivation) requires this.

3. **Correct the β inconsistency** — The text states the posterior concentrates at β≈0.1 but the data show it converges to ≈0.017. If the MiniGrid optimal β is 0.01, the actual convergence is a positive result that should be reported accurately.

4. **Specify implementation details for all experiments** — For each environment, state what o is, how p(o|s,a) is modeled and learned (architecture, training objective), and what specific distribution q(o|s) takes.

5. **Consider reframing the paper's contributions** — If the Bayesian mechanism is the centerpiece, specify it completely. If it remains underspecified, the paper's contribution should be honestly scoped to the fixed-β KL shaping method, with Bayesian adaptation presented as ongoing work.
