## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework for jointly modeling expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) using disentangled representations of note-level score content and global performance style. The model uses a sequence-to-sequence formulation that avoids note-level alignment, and introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The paper is evaluated through objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation visualizations.

## Strengths

- **Joint training demonstrably improves EPR quality over EPR-only training.** Table 2 shows Ours-Target outperforms EPR-Only on KL divergence for durations (5.51 vs 6.43) and velocities (1.76 vs 2.05), and on MAE for durations (0.37 vs 0.42) and velocities (10.33 vs 10.65). This directly validates the benefit of the unified joint formulation (Section 3.2, Eq. 6).

- **Strong quantitative evidence of content-style disentanglement.** Table 4 reports that classifiers using style embeddings achieve 77.46% composer accuracy (52.45 F1) vs. 29.99% (3.03 F1) for content, and 42.07% performer accuracy (25.82 F1) vs. 9.94% (0.74 F1) for content. These large, systematic gaps across four metric dimensions provide robust evidence that the disentanglement objective (Section 3.3, Eq. 5) separates content from style as intended.

- **Alignment-free Seq2Seq EPR achieves competitive alignment rates.** In Table 3, Ours (PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among all methods, including alignment-based baselines DExter (91.27%) and VirtuosoNet (91.88%). This validates the paper's claim that note-level alignment is unnecessary for competitive performance.

- **PSR module generates era-appropriate style embeddings from score content alone.** Figure 4 shows that PSR-generated style embeddings form clusters by historical era (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern) that closely match the clustering structure of style embeddings extracted from real human performances — a direct validation that the diffusion module (Section 3.4) captures stylistically meaningful information from content alone.

- **Thorough evaluation across multiple standardized benchmarks with statistical testing.** The paper reports 12 APT sub-metrics (Table 1) and 7 EPR sub-metrics (Table 2), all with Wilcoxon signed-rank significance tests. The APT results are competitive, achieving best or second-best on 8 of 12 metrics.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Claims about EPR "competitive performance" are incompletely supported due to absent contemporary baselines.** The paper limits EPR comparison to VirtuosoNet (2019, RNN-based) and DExter (2024), but its own related work section (Section 2.1) cites several more recent transformer-based EPR methods — Rhyu et al. (2022), Borovik & Viro (2023), Tang et al. (2023), and Renault et al. (2023). These methods share the note-alignment requirement the paper aims to overcome, but are nevertheless the most relevant points of comparison for the EPR task. By not including any of them, the paper's "competitive performance" claim (stated abstract, line 31, conclusion) rests on an incomplete comparison set. The paper should either add these comparisons, or explicitly acknowledge their absence and qualify its claims accordingly.

### Minor

- **Selective interpretation of variance-based EPR metrics.** Table 2 adopts "values closer to the Human reference are better" for variance (σ²). Under this criterion, DExter's velocity variance (238.86) is notably closer to the human reference (241.04) than Ours-Target (151.03). The paper dismisses this (line 235) by asserting DExter's large variance "does not translate to better quality, as listening tests suggest it results from unstable dynamics." While the subjective results in Figure 2 partially support this position (DExter receives lower subjective ratings), the paper uses the variance metric when it favors the proposed method and explains it away when it does not, without a principled justification for why variance proximity is sometimes valid and sometimes not.

- **Underpowered subjective evaluation.** The listening test (Section 4.3, Figure 2) involves only 11 participants rating 5 pieces. No confidence intervals, effect sizes, or significance tests are reported for the subjective ratings. The paper does not state whether raters were blind to condition, whether the same 11 raters evaluated all pieces, or report any inter-rater reliability measure. While the pattern of results is consistent (Ours-Target rated highest across all dimensions), the small N and absence of statistical reporting limit the weight this evidence can carry — especially given that subjective results are used to support key claims (EPR quality, style transfer effectiveness, PSR evaluation).

- **Disentanglement evidence may partially reflect scale-based rather than purely semantic separation.** Table 4 shows content→composer accuracy at 29.99% (vs. ~4% chance for 25 composers), indicating content embeddings carry non-trivial composer-specific signal. The paper acknowledges this (line 257), attributing it to composer-typical patterns in note-level features. However, this means the claimed "content vs. style" separation could partly be a "note-level vs. global" separation. While the performer identification gap (42.07% vs. 9.94%) is more convincing evidence for genuine style-content separation, the paper would benefit from addressing this granularity concern more directly.

- **Unpaired YouTube performance dataset lacks transparency.** The paper (Section 4.1) describes sourcing YouTube piano cover videos and transcribing them using "a state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" selected via a pilot study, without specifying which model. Errors from this upstream model would propagate into style embeddings extracted from unpaired performances. This component is not reproducible without further disclosure.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **APT-only ablation**: The paper demonstrates joint training benefit on the EPR side (EPR-Only vs. Ours-Target in Table 2), but an APT-Only variant (trained without the EPR decoder/style encoder) would provide symmetric evidence that joint training also benefits the APT task.
- **Quantitative distributional metrics for PSR**: The PSR evaluation relies on visual inspection of 2D projections (Figure 4). Reporting Fréchet distance or Wasserstein distance between PSR-generated and ground-truth style embedding distributions would strengthen the quantitative claim.
- **Disclosure of the audio-to-MIDI transcription model** used for the unpaired YouTube dataset would improve reproducibility.

## Suggestions

1. **Add at least one contemporary transformer-based EPR baseline to Table 2** (e.g., Rhyu et al. 2022 or Tang et al. 2023), or explicitly discuss in the paper why these comparisons are infeasible and qualify the "competitive performance" claim accordingly.
2. **Report confidence intervals and significance tests** for the subjective listening results in Figure 2. Even bootstrapped 95% CIs would substantially strengthen the credibility of the subjective claims.
3. **Disclose the audio-to-MIDI transcription model** used for the unpaired YouTube dataset (model name, architecture, and pilot study accuracy).
4. **Provide a principled discussion** of when variance proximity to human data is a meaningful metric vs. when it is not, to address the selective interpretation concern.
5. **Add an APT-only ablation** to symmetrically demonstrate the mutual benefit of joint training.

## Score and Decision

Based on my assessment, this paper presents a genuinely novel and well-motivated approach to joint modeling of EPR and APT with disentangled representations. The core contributions — the unified joint framework, the alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation for EPR, and the PSR module — are clearly articulated and supported by substantial evidence. The APT results are competitive, the disentanglement evidence is strong, and the joint training benefit on EPR is demonstrated. The weaknesses are real but addressable and do not undermine the paper's core contributions.

The paper sits in the borderline-to-accept range. It is a solid, well-executed paper with novel ideas and thorough quantitative evaluation, held back mainly by gaps in baseline coverage for EPR and the small subjective evaluation.
