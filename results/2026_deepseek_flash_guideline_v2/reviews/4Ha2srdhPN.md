## Summary

GRAID proposes a framework for generating high-quality spatial reasoning VQA data using only 2D bounding boxes from object detectors, avoiding cascading errors from monocular 3D reconstruction and generative hallucinations that plague prior methods. It introduces SPARQ, a predicate-based sieve accelerating question generation by up to 1400×, and releases over 8.5M VQA pairs across three driving datasets (BDD100k, NuImages, Waymo). Human evaluation shows ~91% valid GRAID VQA pairs versus ~42% for SpatialVLM's OpenSpaces dataset, and fine-tuning four VLM backbones across five benchmarks shows consistent gains.

## Strengths

- **Controlled head-to-head human evaluation (Section 4):** 317 GRAID-BDD and 250 SpatialVLM OpenSpaces pairs evaluated under the same protocol. GRAID achieves 93.69% valid answers; SpatialVLM has 57.6% *incorrect* answers (42.4% correct). Difficulty ratings (mean 2.97/5, σ=1.15) confirm the dataset spans easy to hard questions rather than being uniformly trivial.
- **Well-designed generalization experiment (RQ2, Section 5):** Training on only 6 question types and evaluating on all 19+ held-out types on both GRAID-BDD and the completely unseen GRAID-NuImages provides strong evidence of concept learning rather than template memorization. Results show +47.5pp on BDD and +38.0pp on NuImages overall.
- **Measured SPARQ efficiency gains (Section 3.2):** Concrete wall-clock data: predicates average 5.17ms vs. 46.95ms for full realization (9× faster); LargestAppearance achieves 1407× speedup; 78.8% of predicate checks are sufficient conditions that determine the answer without full realization. These numbers substantiate the efficiency claim with logged data rather than asymptotic analysis.
- **Multi-backbone, multi-benchmark verification (RQ3, Section 5):** Four VLMs (Llama 3.2 11B, Gemma 3 4B, Qwen2.5 VL 3B, Qwen3 VL 8B) fine-tuned on both GRAID and SpatialVLM data, evaluated on BLINK, NaturalBench, A-OKVQA, RealWorldQA, and VSR. GRAID-tuned models consistently outperform SpatialVLM-tuned models. Notably, only 10 of 143 BLINK Spatial Relations questions contain "car," yet GRAID improves this subtask by +30.77% — concrete evidence against driving-domain overfitting.
- **Configurable margin_ratio for depth questions (Section 4):** Replaces SpatialVLM's wide [50%, 200%] acceptance threshold with a principled parameter that suppresses questions when estimated depth distances are too close to be reliable, directly addressing the ambiguity problem.
- **SPARQ as an extensible predicate library:** The paper defines 22 VQA templates with reusable predicates, making the framework easily extensible to new question types and new object detection backends — a practical engineering contribution beyond the specific datasets released.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Numerical inconsistency in abstract and introduction vs. Section 4 (lines 9, 51 vs. line 182):** The abstract states that SpatialVLM's data has a "57.6% human validation rate" and compares GRAID's "91.16% human-validated accuracy" to "57.6% on a dataset generated by recent work." However, Section 4 (line 182) clearly reports that 57.6% of SpatialVLM's answers were **incorrect** — a failure rate, not a validation rate. The introduction (line 51) also says "only 57.6% of questions are valid" when the body shows 57.6% refers to answer-incorrectness (a different metric; the question-invalid rate is 41.6%). The related works section (line 82) correctly says "57.6% of the answers... are wrong," creating an internal inconsistency. This is not a formatting artifact: the paper's central quantitative comparison in the abstract and introduction is inverted in sign. **Fixing this error makes GRAID look even better** (~91% valid vs. ~42% valid on the same metric), but in its current form the inconsistency undermines reader trust.

### Minor
- **Human evaluation gaps (Section 4):** 317 GRAID pairs and 250 SpatialVLM pairs are modest sample sizes for characterizing 8.5M+ datasets. No inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's κ) is reported. It is not stated whether the same four evaluators judged both datasets — a basic control for evaluator bias. The SpatialRGPT dataset could not be evaluated at all due to masked region queries, so the human comparison only covers one prior method.
- **No variance reporting for fine-tuning experiments (Section 5):** All RQ1–RQ3 accuracy numbers are single points despite stochastic elements (200 training steps on 10% random subsets without stratified sampling, LoRA fine-tuning). Without error bars or multiple seeds, it is hard to distinguish genuine improvement from noise, especially for question types where Before/After values appear identical (e.g., "Count less than threshold?" shows +4.0 pp both before and after SFT). This is common practice in the field but weakens the evidentiary strength.
- **RQ1 "cross-dataset generalization" framing overstates the evidence (Section 5):** RQ1 tests generalization from GRAID-BDD to GRAID-NuImages — both driving datasets with cars, pedestrians, traffic signs, etc. This demonstrates cross-dataset robustness *within the driving domain*, not cross-domain generalization. The paper's broader claim that GRAID is "domain-agnostic" is supported by RQ3's non-driving benchmarks (BLINK, A-OKVQA, etc.), but the RQ1 framing conflates two different levels of generalization.

### Trivial
- Figure 3 is rendered as garbled text describing bar chart values, hindering independent verification of specific question-type improvements.
- The GRAID human evaluation was conducted on a corrected version of the data (5 labeling errors fixed), while the OpenSpaces comparison used the original uncorrected version — a small asymmetry in the comparison.

## Suggestions
