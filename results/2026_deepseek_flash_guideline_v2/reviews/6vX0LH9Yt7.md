## Summary

This paper proposes a hybrid neural-MPM system for real-time interactive fluid simulation. It combines (i) a GNN-based neural simulator operating at downsampled spatiotemporal resolution with a fallback to classical MPM triggered by a cosine-similarity complexity metric, and (ii) a diffusion-based controller trained via reverse simulation to generate force fields from freehand sketches. The system is evaluated on seven 2D/3D fluid scenarios with up to 4k particles.

## Strengths

- **Reverse simulation strategy for automated training data generation (Section 3.2.2):** The paper derives a closed-form expression (Eq. 3) that reverses MPM forward trajectories to automatically produce force fields paired with sketch-like control signals. This provides a scalable, physically grounded way to generate training data without manual annotation or per-scenario optimization, which is a concrete and useful contribution.

- **Fallback safeguard with empirically characterized complexity metric (Section 3.1.2):** The cosine-similarity metric on per-particle accelerations correlates negatively with simulation error (Spearman −0.3902, Figure 5). A full threshold sweep (Table 1) with both RMSE_m and latency is presented, enabling principled selection of r_c=0.8. Figure 10 shows the hybrid solver consistently improves the error-latency Pareto frontier over both pure neural physics and pure MPM across all six 2D/3D scenarios.

- **Grid-level RMSE_m for cross-resolution evaluation (Section 3.1.1):** The paper identifies that particle-level RMSE breaks when spatial resolution changes (particle correspondence is lost), and adapts a grid-level normalized mass-distribution metric (RMSE_m) that enables fair comparison between predictions at different resolutions. This metric is used consistently across all evaluations, providing a principled basis for the claimed trade-offs.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Evaluation at trivial particle counts undermines the "acceleration" claim.** The experiments use at most 4k particles (Table 2). At this scale, MPM on GPU is already fast: Sand 3D runs in 1.02ms per step, well under the simulation time step dt=2.5ms (Section 4.2). The hybrid solver's reduction to 0.90ms (11.8%) at this scale brings little practical benefit. The Water-Sand 2D scenario where MPM takes 114ms is the one case where acceleration matters, but the hybrid solver at 80ms is still ~32× slower than real-time. The paper does not demonstrate that the approach scales to particle counts (tens of thousands to millions) where MPM is genuinely expensive, which is where a hybrid approach would be most needed. Without this, the practical significance of the contribution is not established.

- **Fluid control evaluation lacks human-in-the-loop validation despite claiming "interactive" control.** The paper claims to support "user-friendly freehand sketches" (Section 1) and "intuitive tools that let users shape visuals directly" (Section 3.2.1), but all "sketches" in the evaluation are algorithmically generated from the reverse simulation pipeline (arrows and ovals, Appendix C). No human-drawn sketches are tested and no user study is conducted. The quantitative evaluation (Table 3) measures only RMSE against synthetic ground truth — it does not assess whether the controlled fluid actually follows a user's intent or produces physically plausible behavior. This is a significant gap between the paper's framing ("interactive," "user-friendly") and the evidence provided.

- **No statistical significance reported for any comparison.** None of the tables (1, 2, 3) or figures include error bars, confidence intervals, or variance across random seeds or runs. The control improvements are small (e.g., Sand 3D: 0.0022 vs 0.0019 RMSE_m in Table 3; Water 2D: 0.0908 vs 0.0802), and without uncertainty estimates it is impossible to assess whether these differences are meaningful. This undermines the reliability of all quantitative claims.

### Minor

- **Only one neural-physics baseline in the main paper.** The method is compared against GNS (Sanchez-Gonzalez et al., 2020) alone. More recent neural-physics works (Neural SPH, MPMNet, subequivariant GNNs) are cited in Related Works but not compared against. Appendix E is promised for additional comparisons, but the main paper's evidence for a 2026 ICLR submission would be substantially stronger with at least one contemporary baseline in the main body.

- **Fluid control evaluation measures fitting accuracy against a synthetic process, not real control quality.** The ground truth for the control task is generated by the same reverse-simulation procedure (Section 3.2.2) that produces training data. The model is trained to match this synthetic process and then evaluated on its ability to match it on held-out data. While this is a standard supervised learning setup, it measures how well the model reproduces the reverse-simulation pipeline rather than whether the generated force fields actually enable intuitive, physically plausible fluid control from freehand sketches.

- **Generalization is tested only within the training distribution.** The held-out test set is drawn from "the same distribution of initial conditions used for training" (Section 4.1). For a method claimed to be useful for interactive applications where users can draw arbitrary sketches, generalization to out-of-distribution inputs (e.g., different obstacle layouts, different initial geometries, unfamiliar sketch styles) is essential but not evaluated.

- **Notation issue in loss definition (Section 2.2):** The RMSE_β loss normalizes by the predicted acceleration p̃_i ("where p̃_i is the predicted acceleration from s_θ"), which is an unusual choice — normalizing by the ground-truth value would be more standard. The paper does not discuss the implications. The notation is also inconsistent with the earlier use of p̃ to denote particle velocity (Section 2.1).

### Trivial

- **"MPN" typo in Section 3.1.2 (lines 127, 129, 131, 140, 142, 144):** The term "MPN" appears repeatedly where "MPM" is clearly intended. While this does not obscure the method (MPM is properly defined in Section 2.1 and used consistently elsewhere), it is an editorial inconsistency in a core method section that should be fixed.
- **LLM statement placement:** The "Use of Large Language Models" section appears in the main paper body rather than in a metadata field, which is an unusual formatting choice.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Test with larger particle counts (50k–200k) to demonstrate where the hybrid approach provides meaningful acceleration over MPM.
- Include a small user study (5–10 participants) testing whether real human-drawn sketches produce the intended fluid behavior.
- Report the computational overhead of computing the cosine-similarity trigger metric separately and confirm it is included in latency measurements.
- Analyze the fallback behavior: what fraction of steps trigger MPM fallback across different scenarios, and whether the system chatters near the threshold.
- Compare against a simpler rule-based fallback strategy (e.g., random switching, periodic MPM, fixed schedule) to justify the complexity-based trigger design.
- Add error bars or confidence intervals to all quantitative comparisons.
