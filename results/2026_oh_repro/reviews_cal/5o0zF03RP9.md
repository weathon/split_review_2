## Summary
The paper proposes **IncentRL**, an RL reward-shaping framework that augments extrinsic reward with a **preference-matching KL penalty** between a predicted outcome distribution \(p(o\mid s,a)\) and a preferred distribution \(q(o\mid s)\), and further **adapts the incentive weight \(\beta\)** online via a Bayesian update. Experiments are reported on **MiniGrid** and **MountainCar**, with figures/tables visualizing the evolution of the inferred \(\beta\).

## Strengths
- **Clear high-level formulation of the shaping objective.** The core shaped reward is explicitly defined as
  \[
  r^{\text{total}}(s,a)=r^{\text{ext}}(s,a)-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(p(o\mid s,a)\,\|\,q(o\mid s)),
  \]
  which makes the method’s intended mechanism concrete at the objective level (Sec. 3; see lines around “The core idea … augment the standard reward …” and Eq. for \(r^{\text{total}}\)).
- **Empirical results include diagnostics of the adaptive-\(\beta\) mechanism rather than only returns.** The introduction explicitly points to posterior concentration plots for \(\beta\) (“empirically validated (Fig. 3, 4) through posterior concentration near task-relevant values”), and the paper reports summary statistics for sampled \(\beta\) (Table 3), which is the right kind of evidence to include for a “Bayesian adaptation” claim.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The core KL shaping term is not specified at the level needed to judge generality vs. hand-crafted shaping.** The method’s success depends on what the “outcome” \(o\) is and how \(q(o\mid s)\) is produced, but the paper stays at a broad level: it defines \(p(o\mid s,a)\) as “what the agent expects to happen” and says it “may be obtained from a forward model or from environment dynamics,” while \(q(o\mid s)\) “can be hand-crafted in toy settings, learned from demonstrations, or generated using language models or human instructions” (Sec. 3; lines ~63–67). This leaves it unclear, for the *actual experiments on MiniGrid and MountainCar*, (i) what \(o\) concretely is, and (ii) whether \(q\) uses task-privileged structure (effectively becoming manual reward shaping). Because the paper’s abstract claims broad applicability and “removes the need for manual trade-off tuning … a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods,” this under-specification is materially decision-relevant.
- **The “Bayesian adaptation” story is not fully pinned down to a verifiable probabilistic model in the main text.** The paper repeatedly claims \(\beta\) is “treated as a Bayesian random variable, updated online” (Abstract; Intro bullets), but (in the extracted main text) it is hard to locate a complete statement of: prior, likelihood/observation model, and exact posterior update used in the experiments. Given the headline claim is *Bayesian* adaptation (not merely “adaptive schedule”), the paper needs to make the inference procedure fully explicit and auditable in the main paper (at least as an algorithm box + equations), otherwise it is difficult to assess whether the method is principled Bayesian inference or a heuristic controller framed in Bayesian terms.

### Minor
- **Scope/claim mismatch: experiments are limited relative to the strength of the abstract’s general claims.** The abstract concludes that the results “demonstrate that Bayesian adaptation … removes the need for manual trade-off tuning, a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods,” but the empirical section is limited to “MiniGrid and MountainCar” (Abstract) and the paper does not (in the main text) establish that hyperparameters governing the \(\beta\) update and the construction of \(q\) are held fixed across tasks. With only two comparatively small benchmarks, the paper should either narrow claims or add evidence that the same mechanism/parameters transfer.
- **Baseline story is thin for the specific claim about intrinsic motivation trade-offs.** The abstract states comparisons are against “standard RL and fixed-regularization baselines,” but the paper does not clearly establish comparisons to other intrinsic-motivation-style exploration signals. If the claim is specifically about overcoming intrinsic motivation trade-off tuning, the most informative comparisons would include at least one representative intrinsic reward method or a strong adaptive-weight heuristic baseline.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

## Novel Insights
The paper is effectively proposing a *family* of algorithms parameterized by the design choices for \(o\) and \(q(o\mid s)\): depending on these choices, IncentRL ranges from a broadly applicable intrinsic objective to straightforward task-engineered reward shaping. The current draft does not sufficiently “identify the algorithm” within this family for the presented experiments, which is why the same mechanism can read as either a principled framework or an under-specified recipe—clarifying \(o\)/\(q\) and the exact Bayesian inference model would largely resolve this ambiguity.

## Suggestions
- Specify, for **each environment**, what the outcome variable \(o\) is (e.g., next observation vs. a hand-defined goal indicator), how \(p(o\mid s,a)\) is parameterized/trained online, and exactly how \(q(o\mid s)\) is constructed (including whether it uses privileged task information).
- Provide an **algorithm box** that fully specifies the Bayesian model for \(\beta\): prior, likelihood/observation, posterior update (or approximation), and which signals are treated as “data.”
- Tighten claims or expand experiments: either (a) re-scope the abstract’s “core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods” claim to “in the tested domains,” or (b) add broader benchmarks and at least one intrinsic-motivation baseline to support that positioning.
