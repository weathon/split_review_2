## Summary

This paper systematically evaluates nine open-source mid-sized LLMs (7B–16B parameters) on two core binary reverse engineering tasks: name recovery (function and variable names) and type inference. Using 40,000 unique functions across four architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS) and four optimization levels (O0–O3), the study finds that current LLMs perform poorly — most F1 scores are below 0.1, with fine-tuning only modestly raising the best to 0.33 on variable name recovery at O0. The paper also evaluates on real-world firmware decompiled by Ghidra and IDA Pro, and examines factors such as model size, training data quality, and prompt template design.

## Strengths

- **Comprehensive evaluation scope.** The study covers 9 models, 4 architectures, and 4 optimization levels (40,000 unique functions), which is significantly broader than individual prior RE studies — substantially more extensive than typical benchmarks in this space.
- **Clear gap identification.** The paper correctly identifies that prior work (SYMGEN using CodeLlama-34B, ReSYM using StarCoder-3B) selects LLMs without principled rationale, and provides a systematic benchmarking effort to address this (§1, §2).
- **Real-world firmware with two decompilers.** Evaluation on real firmware using both Ghidra and IDA Pro (§4.3, Table 6) adds ecological validity and reveals that decompiler choice measurably affects LLM performance.
- **Appropriate semantic matching metric.** Using CodeWordNet for name recovery (§3.4) is methodologically sound — exact string matching would unfairly penalize synonymous predictions that any reasonable reverse engineer would accept.
- **Transparent presentation of negative results.** The paper honestly conveys that nearly all models score F1 < 0.1 on most tasks. This kind of negative finding is useful for the community and helps calibrate expectations.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Unsupported headline F1 claim.** The introduction (line 19) states that after fine-tuning "DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." This number appears nowhere in any results table, figure, or result description. The best DeepSeek-R1 score actually reported is 0.17 (Table 3, variable name recovery, O0, fine-tuned). Even the best cross-model score is 0.33 (Qwen2.5/WizardCoder/Phi4 at O0 on variable name recovery). The paper provides no cross-reference, averaging description, or appendix pointer for the 0.37 figure. This is the paper's most prominent quantitative claim — the one a reader will take away from the abstract and introduction — and it is unverifiable from the evidence presented. This is not a minor slip; it fundamentally undermines the paper's credibility: if the headline result cannot be trusted, why should the reader trust the rest?

- **Unvalidated auxiliary LLM evaluation pipeline.** Section 3.2 describes using an auxiliary LLM to post-process and normalize model outputs, justified because models produce heterogeneous response formats and "a single parsing algorithm cannot robustly handle the degree of variation." The paper provides **no validation whatsoever** of this pipeline: no accuracy assessment, no human evaluation sample, no error rate analysis, no comparison against manual parsing. Given that models are documented to produce extended chain-of-thought reasoning that gets truncated (Finding 8), generate generic refusals, and occasionally produce multiple candidate names (§4.5), the post-processing step is doing non-trivial work. If the auxiliary LLM introduces false positives or false negatives at even a modest rate (e.g., 10–15%), it could materially affect F1 scores that are already very close to zero. Without validation, confidence in all reported absolute numbers is significantly weakened.

- **No non-LLM baselines for context.** The paper evaluates 9 LLMs but does not compare against any existing specialized tool for name recovery or type inference (e.g., DIRE, Debin, SYMLM, or even a simple statistical baseline like frequency-based name prediction). When every LLM scores F1 < 0.1 on type inference, the reader cannot distinguish between "this task is inherently near-impossible" and "LLMs are specifically bad at this task." The paper's main negative conclusion — "LLMs are not yet ready" — lacks actionable grounding without a floor from prior work.

### Minor

- **Fine-tuning without convergence analysis.** The paper fine-tunes for exactly 200 LoRA steps across all models (Table 7) and bases its conclusions (Findings 1, 2) on this regimen. No learning curves, validation loss plots, or analysis of whether convergence was reached are provided. For several tasks (type inference, assembly-based recovery), fine-tuning produces zero or negative improvement; without evidence that training converged, it is unclear whether these results reflect genuine performance ceilings or simply undertraining. The paper's central claim about fine-tuning's impact is weakened by this gap.

- **Training-test data may share function-level similarities.** Both training and test splits are drawn from GNU software projects with an 80/20 random split (§4.3). While the paper states the sets are "independent," a random split from the same project repositories does not guarantee that near-duplicate functions or structurally similar functions from the same codebases do not appear in both splits, potentially inflating fine-tuning results.

- **Several "findings" report generic ML observations as RE-specific discoveries.** Findings 4–6 ("larger model size does not guarantee better performance," "training data quality matters," "larger variants of the same family do not necessarily yield better results") are well-established patterns across the LLM literature. While validating them in the RE domain has some value, the paper presents them as novel discoveries rather than domain-specific confirmations. This overclaims what are essentially expected results.

- **Auxiliary LLM identity and LoRA hyperparameters not disclosed.** The paper does not specify which LLM was used for post-processing response extraction (§3.2) — if it were one of the evaluated models, there is a circularity concern; if proprietary (e.g., GPT-4), the pipeline is irreproducible. Similarly, LoRA rank, alpha, and whether quantization was used (§3.3) are not reported, affecting reproducibility.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Prompt optimization.** The paper uses a single prompt (from prior GPT-3.5 RE studies) for all models without per-model tuning. Given Finding 7 shows prompt templates affect training time by 4×, they likely also affect inference quality. A prompt-ablation study would strengthen the conclusions.
- **Error categorization.** The paper reports aggregate F1 scores but provides no breakdown of what kinds of errors LLMs make (plausible-sounding wrong names vs. refusals vs. truncation vs. hallucinated types). Error taxonomies would be more actionable than aggregate scores.
- **Confidence intervals or variance estimates.** Single-run evaluations are reported without variance, making it hard to assess whether differences between models are significant.

## Suggestions

1. **Retract or substantiate the 0.37 claim.** Either remove it entirely or add an explicit cross-reference to the table/computation that supports it. If it is an average across architectures/optimization levels, state this clearly and show the relevant table.
2. **Validate the auxiliary LLM pipeline.** Manually annotate a sample of at least 200 model outputs, report agreement rates between the auxiliary LLM extraction and human extraction, and discuss how extraction errors affect the reported F1 scores.
3. **Add at least one non-LLM baseline.** Even a simple frequency-based predictor (predict the most common name/type in the training set) or a lightweight published method would dramatically improve the interpretability of the F1 scores.
4. **Provide convergence evidence for fine-tuning.** Include learning curves or validation loss plots for at least a representative subset of model-task combinations, and consider running more than 200 steps where fine-tuning showed zero or negative improvement.
5. **Disclose auxiliary LLM identity and LoRA hyperparameters** for reproducibility.
