## Summary

This paper introduces Insertion Language Models (ILMs), which generate sequences by inserting tokens one at a time at arbitrary positions, removing the need for mask tokens (as in MDMs) or fixed left-to-right ordering (as in ARMs). The authors propose a denoising training objective with a biased approximation to avoid high-variance Monte Carlo estimates, and parameterize the model with a standard transformer encoder. Experiments on planning tasks (star graph path generation, zebra puzzles) show ILMs dramatically outperform both ARMs and MDMs (99.1% vs 23% and 21% on Star_hard). On unconditional text generation, ILMs are competitive with ARMs on TinyStories but show a larger gap on LM1B.

## Strengths

1. **Strong evidence on planning tasks.** The star graph results (Table 1) cleanly demonstrate a qualitative capability gap: ILM achieves 99.1% exact-match accuracy on Star_hard where ARM (23%) and MDM (21%) both fail. The three difficulty levels with variable arm lengths are well-designed to isolate the failure modes of fixed-order and fixed-length generation. The zebra puzzle result (ILM 90.0% vs ARM 81.2%, MDM 82.6%) corroborates the story on a second, more complex constraint-satisfaction task.

2. **Conceptually clean core idea.** Removing mask tokens entirely and having the model jointly predict insertion positions and tokens is a principled way to address the two key MDM limitations (fixed-length constraint and simultaneous unmasking) identified in the paper. The critique of MDMs is well-motivated, and the ILM design directly addresses both limitations.

3. **Reasonable infilling results.** The infilling evaluation (Table 3) shows ILMs consistently outperform MDMs on both single-segment and multi-segment infilling across two datasets, demonstrating a genuine advantage in this setting.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Text generation claims are overstated relative to the evidence.** The abstract states ILMs "perform on par with ARMs" in unconditional text generation, but the evidence is mixed. On LM1B, the NLL gap is substantial (ILM 4.67 vs ARM 3.94, Table 2). On Stories, while NLL is close (2.14 vs 2.11), ILM's average generation length (119 tokens) is far below the data average (205 tokens) while ARM matches it (201). A model generating texts roughly half the expected length has a systematic length-control problem that the paper does not adequately address. The LLM Judge results (Figure 5) are presented as bar charts without numerical values or confidence intervals, making it hard to assess the magnitude of any ILM advantage.

2. **No error bars or confidence intervals on any result.** Table 1 (star graph and zebra), Table 2 (text generation), and Table 3 (infilling) all report single numbers with no variance estimates. Given the modest model sizes (42M–85M params) and training budgets, reporting means and standard deviations over multiple seeds is standard practice and would meaningfully strengthen the evaluation.

3. **NLL under Llama-3.2-3B as a metric may favor ARMs.** The evaluator LLM is itself an autoregressive model, so it may naturally assign better NLL to text generated by ARMs (which also use left-to-right generation) compared to text generated by insertion-based models. The paper does not discuss this potential confound.

### Minor

4. **The biased training objective is acknowledged but not analyzed.** Section 3 states the training objective is a "biased" approximation of the exact denoising objective, motivated by high variance in Monte Carlo estimates (Appendix D). However, the paper provides no analysis of the bias magnitude, no comparison between the approximate and exact objectives on a small-scale task, and no evidence that the approximation is actually necessary or benign. While the strong planning results suggest the approximation works in practice, understanding its cost would strengthen the paper.

5. **No ablation studies.** Several design choices are not experimentally isolated: (a) the stopping classifier vs. EOS token (the comparison to Insertion Transformer on star graphs touches on this but is not a controlled ablation); (b) the biased training objective vs. alternatives; (c) sampling strategies (top-k for position, nucleus for token); (d) the uniform noising schedule. While not every paper needs ablations, given the novelty of the training objective, some ablation evidence would increase confidence in the design.

6. **Insertion Transformer baseline is partially evaluated.** IT appears only on star graphs (where it performs poorly at 35.2/22.1/17.5%) but not on zebra puzzles or text tasks (marked "-" in Table 1). As the most direct predecessor, including IT on at least one additional task would help isolate which components of ILM drive the improvements.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Discuss the potential confound of using an autoregressive LLM (Llama 3.2) as evaluator for non-autoregressive generation methods.
- Add numerical values or annotations to the LLM Judge bar charts (Figure 5) to improve interpretability.
- A comparison with fill-in-the-middle (Bavarian et al., 2022) for single-segment infilling would be informative, though the paper's scope (standard ARM training, not specialized fine-tuning) provides a reasonable justification for its omission.
- Analyzing the bias-variance tradeoff of the training objective on a small synthetic task would address the most significant open question about the method.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:

1. **"Training-inference mismatch is structural/fatal"** — The reviewer claimed this is a fatal structural issue. However, the model is trained to predict a distribution over (position, token) given a partial sequence, and at inference it samples from this same distribution. The "bias" is that the target aggregates counts over all dropped tokens rather than marginalizing over insertion trajectories — this is an acknowledged approximation with a bias-variance tradeoff, not a fundamental mismatch between the training and inference tasks. The strong empirical results (especially on planning) support that the approximation works. Demoted from Fatal/Structural to Minor.

2. **"Missing fill-in-the-middle baseline for infilling"** — The paper's scope compares models trained with their standard objectives. ARMs trained with standard left-to-right loss cannot perform infilling. Fill-in-the-middle requires specialized training (different objective, different data formatting). The paper acknowledges this baseline exists but correctly notes its limitations for multi-segment infilling. The infilling comparison against MDMs is within the paper's stated scope.

3. **"The 'chef' example is an artificial toy"** — The example is illustrative and clearly communicates a real limitation of simultaneous unmasking. The paper acknowledges inference-time strategies exist in Related Work.

4. **"Figure 6 description is confusing"** — A minor presentation inconsistency (the figure mentions ARM in the alt text but the body text discusses only MDM and ILM) that does not affect the substance.

5. **"Training/inference compute not compared"** — A nice-to-have but not central to the paper's claims or necessary for the evaluation.

## Suggestions

1. Reframe the text generation claims to accurately reflect the evidence: ILMs are "competitive with ARMs on some text datasets (e.g., TinyStories) but show a gap on others (e.g., LM1B)" and acknowledge the length-control issue.
2. Add error bars / standard deviations over multiple seeds to the main results tables.
3. Include a small-scale analysis of the biased training objective (e.g., comparing it to a more expensive Monte Carlo estimate on a synthetic dataset) to characterize what the approximation costs.
4. Add numerical values to the LLM Judge bar charts (Figure 5) or present them as a table.
5. Discuss the potential confound of using an autoregressive evaluator LLM for non-autoregressive generation methods.
6. Include Insertion Transformer on at least one additional task (e.g., zebra puzzles) for a more complete comparison.
