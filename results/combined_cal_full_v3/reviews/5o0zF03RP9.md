## Summary

This paper proposes IncentRL, a reinforcement learning framework that augments task rewards with a KL divergence between the agent's predicted outcome distribution $p(o|s,a)$ and a preferred outcome distribution $q(o|s)$, weighted by a scalar $\beta$. The paper claims as its central novelty a Bayesian scheme for adapting $\beta$ online. Experiments are conducted on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey.

## Strengths

- **Clean conceptual framing (Section 3.2).** The idea of shaping rewards via KL(predicted outcomes || preferred outcomes) is well-motivated and directly interpretable. Unlike many intrinsic motivation methods that define ad hoc exploration bonuses, IncentRL's shaping term has a clear semantics: it penalizes actions whose predicted consequences deviate from what the agent prefers. The paper correctly identifies the connection to the Free Energy Principle while noting where it diverges.

- **Well-targeted motivation (Abstract, Section 1).** The problem the paper sets out to solve — manual tuning of the trade-off between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards — is a real limitation of existing intrinsic motivation methods, and the paper articulates this concisely.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation — the paper's central claimed novelty — is never specified.** The abstract calls it "the central novelty" (line 9) and the contributions list calls it "Bayesian adaptation (novelty)" (line 21). Yet the entire Methods section (Section 3) contains zero description of the prior, likelihood, posterior update rule, or how $\beta$ is sampled from the posterior for use by the agent. The algorithm description in Section 5.1 lists 5 steps — none involve any Bayesian inference. When Bayesian $\beta$ finally appears in Section 5.4, it is described only qualitatively via posterior concentration plots (Figures 3, 4, Table 3) that are never connected to task performance. The only task-performance claim is a single unsupported sentence: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of $\beta$ achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (line 228). A paper's central claimed contribution must be reproducible; this one is not defined. Without specification, a reader cannot reproduce, critique, build on, or assess the correctness of this mechanism.

- **No comparison to any existing intrinsic motivation method.** Despite positioning itself against curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., Burda et al.), empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende), and information gain (Houthooft et al.) in Section 2.1, the paper compares only against $\beta=0$ (standard RL with no intrinsic motivation) in every experiment. A method that claims to address "a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods" must demonstrate competitiveness against those methods. This is not a missing-related-work issue — it is a missing-baseline issue that prevents any assessment of whether the proposed approach advances beyond the state of the art it seeks to improve.

- **The empirical evidence is too weak to support the paper's claims, even setting aside the baselines problem.** (a) *MountainCar (Table 2):* Only $\beta=0.1$ improves performance (18%), while $\beta=0.3$ and $\beta=1.0$ severely degrade it (-52.76%, -75.01%). This extreme sensitivity to $\beta$ undermines the claim that Bayesian adaptation could reliably find a good value. With only 3 seeds and large standard deviations (e.g., $\beta=0.1$: $4135.67 \pm 535.41$), even the reported improvement may not be statistically significant. (b) *MiniGrid (Section 5.4):* Only two $\beta$ values are tested (0.0 and 0.01) — the latter was not even part of the sweep used in the other experiments — and the modest improvement (98% vs 90.5% success rate) is compared only against standard RL, not against any exploration method an agent would actually use in this environment. (c) *Bayesian $\beta$ (Figures 3, 4, Table 3):* The paper shows only posterior concentration statistics (mean/variance across "rounds"), never actual task performance for the Bayesian $\beta$ agent. Showing that $\beta$ concentrates near ~0.02–0.1 is not the same as showing that the Bayesian $\beta$ agent achieves competitive task performance.

- **The source of $p(o|s,a)$ (predicted outcome distribution) is critically underspecified for non-toy settings.** Section 3.1 says $p$ "may be obtained from a forward model or from environment dynamics" (line 63), but the paper never states which case applies in MountainCar or MiniGrid. If $p$ comes from known dynamics, the method only works in simulated environments where the transition model is available. If $p$ comes from a learned forward model, the paper omits architecture, training procedure, and analysis of how model prediction errors affect the shaping signal. This distinction is fundamental to the method's practical relevance and reproducibility.

### Minor

- **The theoretical analysis (Propositions 1 and 2, Section 4) is too shallow to be informative.** Proposition 1 ("for small $\beta$, optimal policy is preserved") is a continuity argument that holds for any bounded perturbation to the reward function. Proposition 2 ("for large $\beta$, external reward is ignored") follows directly from the definition. Neither proposition provides insight into learning dynamics, convergence rates, or the conditions under which the KL shaping term actually accelerates learning. The theory section occupies roughly a page and contributes nothing beyond what is obvious from the objective's formulation.

- **Seed counts are too low.** Only 3 seeds are used for MountainCar and MiniGrid despite high variance (e.g., MountainCar $\beta=0.0$: std 472 on mean 3502, $\beta=0.1$: std 535 on mean 4135). Standard RL practice uses 5–10 seeds for meaningful statistical conclusions.

### Trivial

- **Figure 2 plots success rate (0–100%) and episode length (up to ~600) on the same y-axis**, making the success rate curves difficult to read proportionally.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Evaluate the Bayesian $\beta$ agent on actual task metrics (reward or success rate) with confidence intervals, not just posterior statistics.

## Novel Insights

The reviews surface one genuinely novel observation beyond the paper's own contributions: the Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation — touted as the central novelty — is never algorithmically specified anywhere in the paper, meaning the paper's headline contribution is not reproducible. This gap is more fundamental than typical "missing details" criticisms: the entire probabilistic model (what is being inferred from what data, using what likelihood) is absent. Combined with the absence of comparisons to any existing intrinsic motivation method, the paper's actual contribution is limited to a well-motivated but unevaluated conceptual framing.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the Bayesian mechanism.** Provide a self-contained description of the prior, likelihood, and posterior update for $\beta$, ideally with an equation or algorithm box. Without this, the claimed contribution does not exist as a reproducible method.

2. **Evaluate Bayesian $\beta$ on task performance.** Run the Bayesian $\beta$ agent on actual task reward or success rate and compare against the best fixed-$\beta$ sweep values with appropriate statistical testing. Showing that $\beta$ concentrates around ~0.1 is not the same as showing that the agent achieves competitive performance.

3. **Add existing intrinsic motivation baselines.** Curiosity-driven exploration (ICM or RND) or count-based exploration should be compared against, since these are the methods the paper positions itself to improve upon.

4. **Disclose $p(o|s,a)$ details.** State whether the predicted outcome distribution uses the true transition model or a learned forward model for each experiment. If learned, provide architecture, training procedure, and error analysis.

5. **Increase seed counts** to at least 5–10 for meaningful statistical conclusions.
