## Summary

This position paper argues that over-aligning image generation models to a generalized aesthetic preference systematically overrides explicit user intent when non-mainstream or "anti-aesthetic" outputs are requested, constituting a form of developer-centered value imposition that suppresses aesthetic pluralism and user autonomy. The paper constructs a wide-spectrum aesthetics benchmark using COCO prompts modified with "bad" attributes from VisionReward, then tests multiple reward models and generation models to show that aesthetic alignment actively degrades instruction-following on anti-aesthetic prompts—with HPSv3 performing below random chance—and validates the bias by scoring canonical artworks and finding they receive strikingly low rewards.

## Strengths

- **Clear, discussable central position that can be stated in one sentence**: When users explicitly request non-mainstream or anti-aesthetic outputs, instruction fidelity should take precedence over generalized aesthetic preferences. This is precisely the kind of debatable, consequential claim a position paper should make.

- **Striking empirical quantification of alignment conflict**: Table 4 is the paper's strongest result—HPSv3 achieves 0.381 accuracy (below random) at selecting the image matching the anti-aesthetic prompt, while its unaligned base model BLIP-L achieves 0.965. This directly demonstrates that aesthetic alignment *actively corrupts* instruction-following capacity rather than merely failing to improve it, which is a concrete, non-obvious finding.

- **Effective real-art validation that grounds the abstract argument**: The Scream receiving an HPSv3 score of 5.23 versus typical AI images scoring 10–15, and the large-scale LAPIS validation where ~10K artworks score below early models like SD1.4, makes the normative argument viscerally concrete. Section 4.3 further argues that domain gap is not a benign excuse but a structural indicator of exclusionary value judgments—paralleling facial recognition failures.

- **Productive engagement with alternative positions**: Section 5 directly addresses the two strongest counterarguments (safety/user experience justifications, and the "technical flaws not artistic subversion" objection) with specific rebuttals, including the useful distinction between truly unsafe content and ideologically filtered content. This invites exactly the kind of productive disagreement a position paper should provoke.

- **Well-designed experimental structure for a position paper**: Including both aligned and base model variants across multiple families (Flux, SDXL, SD3.5M), plus prompt-following versus aesthetic alignment comparisons, allows the paper to isolate the effect of aesthetic alignment from prompt comprehension—directly refuting the alternative explanation that anti-aesthetic prompts are simply too complex.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The benchmark does not cleanly separate legitimate artistic non-conformism from requests for degraded/defective output, weakening the link between evidence and the strongest normative claims.** The paper defines "wide-spectrum aesthetics" (Section 2.1) as including "unrealism, surrealism, clashing colors, unconventional scale, or the depiction of negative emotions"—clearly artistic choices. But the prompts (Figure 2) include requests like "the image is extremely blurry and fragmented...objects appear broken and nonsensical, proportions warp chaotically, making it impossible to discern individual items" and "suffering from poor lighting with no shadows or light effects, creating a low-quality, noise-filled visual." These are requests for *technical degradation*, not artistic non-conformism. Since the paper's position is specifically about suppression of legitimate plural aesthetics, not reluctance to produce defective images, this conflation is a central evidential gap. A reward model that rejects a request for a "noise-filled, low-quality" image may be protecting technical quality rather than enforcing a single aesthetic ideology. The paper partially acknowledges this in Section 5 ("only 'clarity' could be argued as a technical flaw; all other dimensions...represent stylistic or artistic choices"), but this defense is insufficient because the empirical results (Tables 2, 4) mix both categories together, making it impossible to determine how much of the observed bias stems from each.

- **The strongest normative conclusions—"aesthetic authoritarianism," "instrument of cultural assimilation," "pre-emptive governance"—outpace the evidence provided.** The evidence compellingly shows that reward models penalize anti-aesthetic images aligned with explicit prompts, and that aligned generators sometimes override those prompts. But the leap from "current systems underserve some prompts" to "functioning as aesthetic authoritarianism" and "instrument of cultural assimilation" (Conclusion, Section 7) requires evidence about *developer intent, governance motivation, and deployment effects* that the paper does not provide. The paper cites Lazar (2025) on "pre-emptive governance" (Section 2.4), but the empirical sections only demonstrate alignment-induced performance gaps, not deliberate governance design. For a position paper, strong rhetorical claims are acceptable, but they must be logically connected to the evidence—here, the gap between what is shown and what is claimed is too wide for the broadest conclusions.

### Minor

- **The evaluator-validity argument for LLM-as-judge ground truth deserved more explicit defense in the main text.** The paper uses Qwen3-VL as ground truth (Section 3.3, showing Cohen's kappa 0.80 with humans) and validates with GPT-5-Chat. This is reasonable but the paper acknowledges that the LLM-as-judge is used as a classification ground truth for Table 4's metrics. The paper also uses a prompt-independent judge J(I,d) to verify whether generation models "correctly followed" anti-aesthetic prompts—a construct validity question for a claim about prompt-faithfulness. The 0.80 kappa provides partial reassurance, but the main text could more explicitly address whether these evaluators capture the intended construct (intended anti-aesthetic expression vs. failed image quality).

- **Counterargument about user-controllable defaults is under-engaged.** Section 5 rebuts safety and "technical flaw" objections effectively, but a strong alternative position is that defaults optimized for broad user satisfaction are reasonable *if accompanied by controllable opt-out mechanisms*. The paper endorses such controls in the conclusion but does not engage with the product-design tradeoff as an alternative position in Section 5. This is a missed opportunity for productive discussion.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A clean separation of the benchmark into subcategories (artistic non-conformism vs. technical degradation vs. emotional negativity) with separate analysis per category would significantly strengthen the link between evidence and position.
- Direct engagement with the "defaults + opt-out controls" counterposition in Section 5 would improve the paper's refutability.
- Tighter normative language in the conclusion—grounding "aesthetic authoritarianism" specifically in the documented alignment-performance tradeoff rather than extending it to cultural assimilation—would better match the evidence.

## Novel Insights

The most novel and potentially important insight in this paper is the asymmetry revealed in Table 4: aesthetic alignment can *invert* model performance on instruction-following. HPSv3 doesn't merely fail to improve on anti-aesthetic prompts relative to its base model (BLIP-L)—it scores *below random guessing* (0.381 accuracy), while BLIP-L achieves 0.965. This means alignment is not just a neutral overlay that fails for niche use cases; it actively corrupts a capability that exists in the unaligned base. This finding, if robust, has implications beyond aesthetics: it suggests that any form of preference alignment that conflates "good output" with "preferred output" may be systematically eroding the model's ability to faithfully execute instructions that conflict with the trained preference, even when the model could otherwise handle them.

## Suggestions

- Partition the benchmark prompts into clearly labeled subcategories (artistic non-conformism vs. technical quality reduction vs. emotional negativity) and report results separately for each. Even without changing the overall findings, this would allow readers to assess which aspect of "wide-spectrum aesthetics" drives the observed bias and significantly strengthen the link between evidence and position.

- Moderate the conclusion's strongest normative claims to match what the evidence supports. For example: "the documented alignment-performance tradeoff functions as a form of *de facto* aesthetic authority that narrows admissible expression" would be better supported than "functioning as aesthetic authoritarianism" and "instrument of cultural assimilation."
