## Summary

This paper introduces *random set stability* as a new stability notion for data-dependent random sets generated by stochastic optimization algorithms. It derives expected worst-case generalization bounds combining a Rademacher complexity term with a stability parameter that explicitly accounts for algorithmic randomness. The framework is applied to remove intractable mutual information terms from topological/fractal generalization bounds, yielding fully computable topological bounds. Empirical evaluations on Vision Transformers and GraphSage demonstrate that the estimated bounds track generalization gaps across hyperparameter settings.

## Strengths

- **Novel stability concept incorporating algorithmic randomness.** Assumption 3.1 and Definition 3.1 define random set stability using data-dependent selections to handle measurability, explicitly incorporating algorithmic randomness $U$—addressing a gap in prior hypothesis set stability (Foster et al., 2019) that did not account for $U$.
- **Clean interpolation between singleton and fixed-set bounds.** Lemma 3.4 introduces parameter $J$ that continuously interpolates: $J=1$ recovers classical algorithmic stability bounds (Corollary 3.5) and $J=n$ recovers fixed-hypothesis Rademacher complexity bounds (Corollary 3.6). This is an elegant structural contribution.
- **Theorem 4.4 removes IT terms from topological bounds.** The bounds in Theorem 4.4 use topological complexity measures (weighted lifetime sums $\mathbf{E}^\alpha$, positive magnitude) without the intractable mutual information terms that prior fractal bounds require, replacing them with the stability parameter $\beta_n$.
- **Practical validity established via Corollary 3.3.** The paper proves that projected SGD under Lipschitz/smoothness assumptions satisfies random set stability with a concrete parameter scaling as $\mathcal{O}(T^2/n)$, bridging abstract theory and practical training dynamics.
- **Acknowledges key limitations transparently.** The authors explicitly state expected-only bounds, the optimistic estimation of $\beta_n$, and the slower $n^{-1/3}$ convergence in Section 6.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The topological bounds (Theorem 4.4) are never directly evaluated empirically.** Section 5.1 evaluates a Massart's lemma proxy of Lemma 3.4, not the actual bound in Lemma 3.4 nor the topological bounds in Theorem 4.4. Specifically, the paper states: *"we use Massart's lemma to bound the right-hand side of Equation (8) by $2\sqrt{2 \log(T)/J} + 2J\beta_n$."* The actual Rademacher complexity of the random trajectory $\mathcal{W}_{S,U}$ is never computed. Theorem 4.4's topological bounds are not numerically compared against worst-case error anywhere—only qualitative correlations between $\mathbf{E}^1$ and generalization gap are shown in Figures 2 and 3. This means the paper's central contribution ("first fully computable topological bounds") is not directly validated numerically.

- **The stability parameter $\beta_n$ is systematically underestimated.** The paper acknowledges (Section 5, page 254): *"Note that this method necessarily leads to an optimistic estimation of the stability parameter $\beta_n$, as it would be intractable to evaluate the supremum over the entire data space $\mathcal{Z}$."* Since $\beta_n$ appears linearly in Lemma 3.4 and as $\beta_n^{1/3}$ in Theorem 4.4, the reported bounds are underestimates of the true upper bounds. Table 1's claim that bounds are "typically close to an order of magnitude" of the actual error is therefore optimistic about tightness.

### Minor

- **The $n^{-1/3}$ convergence rate of the topological bounds is substantially slower than the classical $n^{-1/2}$ rate.** Theorem 4.4 yields $O(\beta_n^{1/3})$, which becomes $O(n^{-1/3})$ under standard stability scaling. The paper acknowledges this trade-off but does not quantify its practical impact: how much of the bound's looseness (Table 1 shows 1–2 orders of magnitude) is attributable to this rate penalty. A comparison of the achievable bound tightness against IT-based approaches (which, while intractable, may yield tighter *structural* bounds) would contextualize the trade-off.

- **The local Lipschitz constant $L_{S,U}$ appears in Theorems 4.3 and 4.4 but is absent from all empirical results.** Assumption 4.1 requires this quantity, and it enters the bound multiplicatively (e.g., in $K_{n,\alpha} = 2(2L_{S,U}\sqrt{n}/B)^\alpha$), yet no numerical value or estimate is reported in Table 1 or any figure. Massart's lemma proxy entirely sidesteps this parameter.

- **Correlation analysis in Figures 2 and 3 overinterprets alignment with Theorem 4.4.** The paper claims the figures "strongly support Theorem 4.4" because the slope of $\mathbf{E}^1$ vs. generalization gap increases with $n$. However, Theorem 4.4 is an *upper bound*, not a proportional relationship. The positive correlation is qualitatively consistent but does not constitute evidence for the specific functional form or rate predictions of the theorem.

## Novel Insights

The paper's insight that the stability parameter $\beta_n$ plays a dual role—it both multiplies topological complexity in the bound *and* is empirically estimable—exposes a structural coupling between optimization stability and trajectory geometry that prior IT-based frameworks obscured. The observation (Figure 1, right panel; Section 5) that $\beta_n$ systematically decreases with sample size and correlates with generalization gap across diverse architectures (ViT, GraphSAGE) provides empirical evidence for a theoretical mechanism that has been assumed but rarely measured at scale.
