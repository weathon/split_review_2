## Summary

GRAID is a framework that generates large-scale spatial reasoning VQA datasets using only 2D bounding boxes from standard object detectors, deliberately avoiding single-view 3D reconstruction and caption-based generation. The key insight is that qualitative spatial relationships (left/right, closer/farther, counting, ranking) can be reliably determined from 2D geometric primitives alone, eliminating the cascading errors introduced by depth estimation, camera calibration, and plane estimation. The framework includes SPARQ, a predicate-based early-rejection mechanism for efficient generation, and is instantiated on BDD100k, NuImages, and Waymo to produce over 8.5M VQA pairs. Fine-tuning experiments show that models trained on GRAID data generalize across datasets, held-out question types, and external benchmarks.

## Strengths

1. **Principled methodological choice.** The insight that qualitative spatial relationships can be reliably determined from 2D bounding boxes alone, bypassing single-view 3D reconstruction, is sound and well-motivated. Section 3.1 clearly articulates why cascading errors in depth estimation, camera calibration, and plane estimation make metric-level spatial VQA brittle, and why 2D discriminative reasoning avoids those failure modes. This cleanly separates GRAID from SpatialVLM and SpatialRGPT on a fundamental design axis. *(favorability: 0.89–1.00)*

2. **Clean comparison table.** Table 1 provides a direct feature-by-feature comparison with prior frameworks (SpatialVLM, SpatialRGPT, SpaRE) using seven binary criteria — operates on images only, no architecture changes, avoids 3D reconstruction, avoids LLM-based QA generation, etc. This lets a reader immediately see the methodological landscape. *(favorability: 0.61–0.70)*

3. **SPARQ is a practical engineering contribution.** The predicate-based early-rejection mechanism (Section 3.2, Algorithm 1) is lightweight but consequential: the 1407× speedup on `LargestAppearance` and the 9× average savings are concrete numbers, and the algorithmic description is clear and reproducible. This makes large-scale generation tractable. *(favorability: 0.83–1.00)*

4. **Cross-dataset generalization experiment (RQ1) is well-designed.** Fine-tuning on 10% of GRAID-BDD and evaluating on GRAID-NuImages (entirely different cities, scenes, and objects) yields a +29.1% gain (line 198). This is the strongest evidence in the paper that the model learns transferable spatial concepts rather than dataset-specific surface patterns. *(favorability: 1.00)*

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Headline comparison (91.16% vs 57.6%) compares non-equivalent metrics, with inconsistent framing across the paper.** The 57.6% figure is specifically the rate of **incorrect answers** in OpenSpaces (line 182: 144/250 answers incorrect). The 91.16% figure combines question validity, answer correctness, and labeling errors into a single compound rate (28 unique issues out of 317 pairs). The paper presents these as directly comparable — the abstract calls both "human validation rate" — and the introduction (line 51) even mislabels the 57.6% as "questions are valid" when it actually measures answer incorrectness. While the paper provides enough internal breakdown to reconstruct a fair comparison (GRAID answer validity: 93.69%; OpenSpaces answer validity: 42.4%), the headline framing overstates the gap and is misleading as presented. *(favorability: 0.12)*

2. **Human evaluation lacks basic rigor for supporting the paper's strongest quality claims.** The evaluation of 317 GRAID pairs uses four human evaluators but provides: (a) no inter-rater agreement metric (not even percent agreement, let alone Cohen's or Fleiss' κ), (b) no description of how disagreements were resolved, and (c) ambiguous methodology — the seed-per-name sampling (line 184) suggests each evaluator judged a disjoint subset rather than all 317 pairs independently, meaning no overlap-based reliability assessment is possible. Given that the paper's central claim of producing datasets of "the highest accuracy" rests on this evaluation, the absence of standard reporting is a significant gap. *(favorability: 0.00–0.06)*

### Minor

3. **The 91.16% figure applies to only one of six dataset variants.** Only GRAID-BDD without depth was human-evaluated (line 184). The other five variants (BDD with depth, NuImages with/without depth, Waymo with/without depth) are not human-validated, yet the abstract and conclusion (lines 9, 60, 293) characterize the 91.16% figure as representative of all GRAID datasets. *(favorability: 0.25)*

4. **Comparison with SpatialVLM uses a community reimplementation.** RQ3 and the human evaluation use OpenSpaces, a dataset generated by a community implementation of SpatialVLM, rather than the official SpatialVLM dataset. The paper is mostly transparent about this (lines 182, 202), but the conclusion (line 281) refers to "the SpatialVLM dataset" when it means OpenSpaces. The fidelity of the reimplementation relative to the original is never assessed, so comparisons framed as being against SpatialVLM are actually against an uncontrolled proxy. *(favorability: 0.29)*

5. **Fine-tuning experiments lack convergence evidence.** RQ1 and RQ2 use only 200 training steps with no reported learning curves or validation curves. The paper acknowledges this completes "only a fraction of an epoch" (line 200) for RQ2. Without convergence analysis, it is difficult to distinguish between genuine spatial concept learning and partial adaptation to the template format. A control experiment fine-tuning on non-spatial template-based data of similar scale would help disentangle these effects. *(favorability: 0.00)*

6. **The 1407× speedup for LargestAppearance needs contextualization.** The predicate for this question checks whether there are ≥2 classes — a trivially fast operation (0.02ms average). The speedup is largely an artifact of the question-realization being unusually expensive rather than the predicate being methodologically sophisticated. The paper should clarify why this outlier differs so dramatically from the 9× average savings across templates. *(favorability: 0.36)*

### Trivial

7. **Imprecise reference to ImageNet.** Line 94 states that "modern object detection models have achieved sufficiently high accuracy on prior global challenges such as ImageNet." ImageNet is primarily an image classification benchmark; the relevant detection benchmarks are COCO, LVIS, or the ILSVRC detection challenge. *(favorability: 0.48)*

## Nice-to-Haves
- A controlled experiment where both GRAID and an existing method generate data from the **same images** and **same detector outputs** would eliminate confounds in the RQ3 comparison.
- A control experiment fine-tuning on a non-spatial template-based dataset of similar scale would help isolate whether gains are spatial-specific or from additional supervised training on synthetic data.
- Error analysis on what fine-tuned models still get wrong (beyond noting that *LessThanThresholdHowMany* regresses) would deepen the contribution.
- Statistical significance reporting for fine-tuning gains would help assess which improvements are reliable.
