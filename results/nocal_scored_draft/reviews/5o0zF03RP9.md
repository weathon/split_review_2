## Summary

This paper proposes **IncentRL**, a framework that augments standard RL rewards with a KL-divergence penalty between predicted outcome distributions $p(o|s,a)$ and preferred outcome distributions $q(o|s)$, weighted by a scalar $\beta$. The paper claims as its central novelty a Bayesian scheme that adapts $\beta$ online, removing the need for manual tuning. Empirical results on MountainCar and MiniGrid are presented for fixed-$\beta$ variants, along with Bayesian $\beta$ posterior distribution plots.

## Strengths

- **The KL-based shaping formulation ($r_{\text{total}} = r_{\text{ext}} - \beta \cdot \text{KL}(p\|q)$) is clearly presented** and provides a clean way to combine external rewards with internal preference signals. The formulation is simple enough to be implemented on top of any RL algorithm.

- **The MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 results with fixed $\beta=0.01$ show a genuine improvement over standard RL** (98% vs 90.5% success rate, 29 vs 75 episode length), suggesting the shaped reward can help in sparse-reward settings. This is the most substantive empirical result in the paper.

- **The paper correctly identifies a practical limitation of intrinsic motivation methods** — the need to manually tune the trade-off weight between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards — and proposes adaptive $\beta$ as a sensible direction to address it.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No comparison to any existing intrinsic motivation method.** The paper cites curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al. 2017, Burda et al. 2018), VIME (Houthooft et al. 2016), and empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende 2015) as relevant prior work in Section 2, yet the experiments compare IncentRL *only* against $\beta=0$ (standard RL). Without any comparison to ICM, RND, VIME, or count-based exploration, the paper cannot substantiate its claim to improve over existing intrinsic motivation approaches. The contribution of IncentRL relative to the extensive literature on intrinsic motivation is entirely unexamined empirically.

- **The central empirical claim about Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation is unverifiable from the reported results.** Line 228 states "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of $\beta$ achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" — the actual RL performance (success rate, reward, episode length) for the Bayesian adaptive $\beta$ is never reported. Only $\beta$ distribution plots (Figures 3, 4) and summary statistics (Table 3) are provided. Showing that a Bayesian procedure can produce $\beta$ values near a fixed choice does not demonstrate that it "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning" — what matters is whether the *RL policy* trained with adaptive $\beta$ achieves competitive reward/success. That evidence is absent from the paper.

- **The empirical evaluation is too narrow to support the paper's claims.** Only two environments (MountainCar-v0, MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8) are tested, with 3 seeds each, compared only against $\beta=0$ baselines. The MountainCar results (Table 2) show that $\beta=0.3$ and $\beta=1.0$ *degrade* performance relative to baseline (52.76% and 75.01% worse), meaning the method is harmful for most hyperparameter choices — this undermines the "no manual tuning" claim since choosing a bad $\beta$ actively hurts. The MiniGrid experiment tests only one non-zero $\beta$ value ($\beta=0.01$), providing no sense of the method's sensitivity.

### Minor

- **Internal inconsistency in the Bayesian $\beta$ results.** The paper text (line 239, Figure 4 caption) repeatedly states the posterior concentrates near "$\beta \approx 0.1$" and that distributions are "centered around 0.1." However, Table 3 shows the posterior mean converging to 0.017 in Round 4 — an order of magnitude smaller, and close to the best fixed $\beta=0.01$ from MiniGrid. The "target effective $\beta = 0.1$" reference line drawn in Figures 3 and 4 does not match where the posterior actually goes (≈0.02). This is a factual inconsistency in the paper's presentation.

- **The theoretical analysis (Propositions 1 and 2) is too weak to constitute a meaningful contribution.** Proposition 1 states that sufficiently small $\beta$ preserves the optimal policy — this is a basic continuity argument that holds for any bounded reward perturbation in a finite MDP with a positive reward gap. Proposition 2 states that as $\beta \to \infty$ the policy minimizes KL — this is definitional. Neither provides insight into the method's behavior at practically relevant $\beta$ values nor connects to the Bayesian adaptation mechanism.

- **Key implementation details for the deep RL experiments are unspecified.** The paper does not specify how $p(o|s,a)$ is parameterized or learned in MountainCar and MiniGrid (lines 63–64 only say "may be obtained from a forward model or from environment dynamics"). For MiniGrid, $q(o|s)$ is not described at all. These details are necessary to understand what the method actually does in practice and to assess whether the KL penalty provides a meaningful signal.
