## Summary

This paper introduces ConCuR, a curated dataset of CUDA kernels with chain-of-thought reasoning traces, and KernelCoder, a 32B model fine-tuned on it via LoRA. The core insight is an observational finding: for the same kernel-writing task, shorter reasoning traces correlate with correct kernels while reasoning length has negligible correlation with speedup. On KernelBench Levels 1 and 2, KernelCoder (58/59 pass@1 Exec) outperforms much larger models including DeepSeek-R1-0528 (52/55) and Kevin-32B (50/46), and the ablation shows the specific curation criteria matter significantly.

## Strengths

- **Clear, well-documented observational finding (Section 3.4, Figures 2-3):** The paper documents a non-obvious empirical phenomenon — shorter reasoning traces are strongly associated with correct kernel generation (accuracy dropping from ~0.65 for 0–256 token traces to ~0.04 for >19,000 tokens), while reasoning length has essentially zero correlation with speedup (r = −0.047). The within-task analysis partially controls for task difficulty, making this finding stronger than a simple aggregate correlation.

- **Strong empirical results on KernelBench Levels 1 and 2 (Tables 1-2):** KernelCoder (32B, LoRA SFT) achieves pass@1 Exec of 58/59 on Levels 1 and 2, beating DeepSeek-R1-0528 (685B, 52/55), Kevin-32B (50/46), and all other baselines. At pass@10 it reaches 91/95, competitive with DeepSeek-R1-0528's 90/97 despite being ~20× smaller.

- **Ablation study convincingly demonstrates curation matters (Table 4):** Replacing the ConCuR curation with random selection, max-length-first, min-length-first, or speedup-only selection drops pass@1 Exec from 58/59 to 34–42. This shows the specific combination of criteria is important, not just the amount of data.

- **Genuinely low training cost:** 4,892 samples and 64 A100-hours for a 32B model makes the approach practically reproducible. The three-way base model ablation (Table 5) shows the dataset benefits multiple architectures.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Causal claim in title and framing is not supported by the evidence:** The title states "Conciseness Makes State-of-the-Art Kernel Generation" (causal) and the abstract says concise reasoning traces "result in robust generation." However, the evidence only demonstrates a correlation between short reasoning and correct kernels. An equally consistent explanation is that task difficulty (or model uncertainty) is the confound — easy tasks require less reasoning and produce correct kernels, while hard tasks produce long meandering reasoning AND incorrect kernels. The ablation study does not isolate conciseness from reasoning quality/content. This does not invalidate the empirical results (the pipeline works, and the within-task analysis in Figure 3 partially mitigates the concern), but the causal framing should be softened throughout.

- **Efficiency comparison omits the dominant data-generation cost:** Section 4.3 and Table 3 compare KernelCoder's "64 A100 GPU hours" against Kevin's ">600 H200 GPU hours" and AutoTriton's costs. However, the 64 hours covers only LoRA training. The 90,810 samples generated by Kevin-32B (5 batches × 18,162 tasks) require substantial inference compute that is not accounted for. Meanwhile, competitors' reported costs include both training and their data generation/exploration. This asymmetry makes the efficiency comparison misleading. A fair comparison would include (or at minimum disclose) the data generation cost.

### Minor

- **No discussion of potential overlap between training and evaluation data:** Training uses tasks from KernelBook, evaluation uses KernelBench. While these are different collections, the paper does not discuss whether tasks could overlap between them. Since Kevin-32B (the data generator) was itself trained on kernel generation tasks including KernelBench problems (as noted in Table 3's footnote), the possibility of benchmark leakage should be addressed.

- **Difficulty division metric inherits generator-specific biases:** Section 6's ARL-based difficulty metric is computed from Kevin-32B, which is also the data generator. While partial validation using DeepSeek-R1-0528 is provided (showing the same decreasing trend), the thresholds (ARL < 4000, 4000–8500, >8500) and classification are conditioned on Kevin's behavioral patterns. The metric's reliability across different generator models is not systematically studied.

- **Missing evaluation details and statistical uncertainty:** The paper does not report the temperature, top-p, or other decoding parameters used for generation, nor does it provide confidence intervals or variance estimates. Given the modest number of tasks per KernelBench level (~100–200), a few percentage points of difference could fall within statistical noise.

- **Ablation study does not isolate the three-part curation composition:** The ablation compares ConCuR against datasets built with different selection criteria (random, max-length, min-length, speedup-first), but does not ablate the three components of ConCuR itself (part a: shortest+fastest, part b: speedup>5, part c: single-operator balance). It is unclear whether the gain comes from the specific composition or simply from having more diverse data.

## Suggestions

- Soften the causal framing throughout. Replace "conciseness makes" / "result in" with language about correlation and curation effectiveness (e.g., "concise reasoning traces are a reliable signal for selecting high-quality training data").
- Provide a complete cost accounting that includes the inference cost of generating 90,810 samples from Kevin-32B, even if approximate ("estimated X A100-hours for inference").
- Add a discussion of potential overlap between KernelBook training tasks and KernelBench evaluation tasks, and any decontamination steps taken.
- Report decoding parameters (temperature, top-p, etc.) and add confidence intervals or bootstrap estimates for the main results.
- Include an ablation that varies the three components of ConCuR (parts a, b, c) individually to clarify what drives the gain.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a genuine contribution: a well-motivated observational finding, a carefully curated dataset, and a model with strong empirical results supported by convincing ablations. The two major issues — overstated causal claims and an incomplete efficiency comparison — are both fixable through rewording and additional disclosure rather than new experiments. Neither invalidates the core empirical contribution. I recommend acceptance conditional on addressing these issues.
