## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework for joint expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT), framed as inverse tasks with disentangled note-level score content and global performance style representations. It also introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, and formulates EPR as a sequence-to-sequence task without note-level alignment. The evaluation spans objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation analysis across multiple datasets.

## Strengths

- **Joint modeling of EPR and APT as inverse tasks is a genuinely novel framing.** While prior work treats these separately, the paper correctly identifies their dual nature and draws a productive analogy to ASR/TTS joint modeling. The architecture (shared content space, separate style space) is a reasonable instantiation of this insight.

- **Seq2Seq formulation of EPR without note-level alignment addresses a real bottleneck.** Current EPR methods require fine-grained note alignment preprocessing. Eliminating this requirement is practically meaningful for scaling and handling ornamented passages. Table 3 shows alignment rates competitive with note-aligned baselines despite this relaxation.

- **The PSR module concept is well-motivated.** Automatically generating style embeddings from score content — rather than requiring composer labels or manual expressive parameter tuning — is a genuine usability improvement. The diffusion-based approach is a natural choice for this conditional generation problem.

- **Evaluation breadth is above average.** The paper provides: objective EPR metrics (Table 2), alignment rates (Table 3), APT metrics on MUSTER and ScoreSimilarity (Table 1), subjective listening tests (Figure 2), performer/composer classification on latent representations (Table 4), style transfer subjective evaluation (Figure 5), and latent space visualizations (Figures 3, 4).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No explicit content alignment loss between z_x and z_y, weakening the disentanglement claim.** The paper states that score-content features z_x and z_y "are encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption) and that "Both domains share a latent content space Z_c" (Section 3.2). However, examining the loss functions in Sections 3.2–3.3, there is no explicit objective that aligns z_x and z_y — no contrastive loss, cosine similarity, cycle-consistency term, or shared weight constraint. The only forces acting on them are domain-specific autoencoding and cross-domain translation losses (L_EPR, L_APT, L_rec). These do not by themselves guarantee that z_x and z_y encode the same "content" in the same latent space. The downstream classification probes (Table 4) test whether content and style are *different*, not whether z_x and z_y encode the *same* content. This undercuts a central framing claim of shared content representation.

- **EPR objective metrics have an inherent validity limitation that is not adequately addressed.** EPR is a one-to-many mapping (acknowledged in Section 3.2), yet the main quantitative EPR evaluation (Table 2) computes MAE, KL divergence, and variance against a single human reference performance. These metrics measure imitation fidelity to that specific performance, not rendering quality as music. The subjective evaluation partially addresses this, but uses only 11 participants and 5 pieces, with no inter-rater reliability reported. The finding that Ours(Target) outperforms the human reference on "Overall" for some composers (Figure 2) is striking but receives no discussion — the paper does not mention whether participants were blinded to which samples were human vs. generated (only that order was randomized).

### Minor

- **No ablation isolating the contribution of unpaired data.** The EPR-Only variant (Table 2) differs from the full model in two confounded ways: (a) it removes the APT/joint modeling components, and (b) it is trained "solely on the ASAP dataset" (no unpaired data). The paper claims "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance" (Section 5.1), but the relative contribution of unpaired data vs. joint architecture cannot be separated.

- **PSR evaluation has a circular dependency.** The PSR module is trained on style embeddings extracted from the frozen joint model's style encoder, and its outputs are evaluated against those same style encodings. The similarity between PSR-generated and "real" styles in Figure 4 may partly reflect that both pass through the same encoding. A stronger evaluation would compare PSR-driven renderings to renderings driven by human-performer style vectors from real recordings.

- **Statistical significance testing in Table 1 is selective.** The dagger symbols indicate significance "with respect to the end-to-end baseline" (Beyer & Dai 2024) only. Significance is not tested against other baselines (Liu et al., 2022; Shibata et al., 2021; MuseScore; Finale), several of which outperform the proposed method on certain metrics.

- **Subjective studies are small-scale and lack certain methodological detail.** The main listening test has 11 participants and 5 pieces; the style transfer test uses only 3 samples. No inter-rater reliability metric is reported. The finding that Ours(Target) matches or exceeds the human reference warrants discussion of whether participants could distinguish human from generated performances.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Add an explicit content alignment loss (e.g., cosine similarity or contrastive objective between z_x and z_y for paired data) to architecturally substantiate the shared-content-space claim.
- Run an ablation keeping the full joint architecture but removing unpaired data, to isolate whether improvements come from joint modeling or data scale.
- For the PSR evaluation, compare PSR-driven renderings against renderings using a human-performer's style vector from a real recording of a different piece, with subjective listening.
- Report inter-rater reliability and clarify whether participants were aware which samples were human vs. generated.

## Score and Decision

This paper makes a genuinely novel contribution — joint modeling of EPR and APT as inverse tasks with disentangled representations is a worthwhile direction, and the individual components (Seq2Seq EPR without alignment, PSR module) are well-motivated and competently executed. The evaluation breadth is commendable. However, two significant weaknesses hold it back: (1) the claimed shared content space lacks an explicit alignment mechanism, relying only on implicit task-based pressure; and (2) the EPR evaluation metrics measure imitation fidelity for a one-to-many problem, with the subjective component too limited to fully compensate. These weaken but do not invalidate the contributions. The paper is a solid borderline accept with clear revision paths.
