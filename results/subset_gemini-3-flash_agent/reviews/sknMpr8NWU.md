## Summary
The paper introduces Multi-Agent Evolve (MAE), a framework for LLM self-improvement using a multi-agent reinforcement learning approach. It instantiates three roles—Proposer, Solver, and Judge—from a single backbone model to create a self-rewarding loop that does not require ground-truth labels or verifiable environments (like compilers). The Proposer generates questions, the Solver answers them, and the Judge provides the reward signal based on reasoning quality and correctness, with the Proposer also receiving an adversarial difficulty reward when the Solver fails.

## Strengths
- **Self-Improvement without Verifiable Rewards:** The paper successfully demonstrates that LLMs can self-improve in general domains (e.g., general knowledge, reasoning) where external verification (code execution or math ground truth) is unavailable. The "MAE (zero)" setting shows a 3.18% average improvement starting from only 16 self-generated questions.
- **Novel Multi-Agent Synergy:** The study demonstrates that the co-evolution of Proposer, Solver, and Judge is more effective than standard self-play. Ablation studies in Section 5.3.1 show that disabling training for any one of the three roles leads to a performance drop (up to 2.63%), justifying the multi-agent design.
- **Automated Self-Curriculum:** The adversarial reward mechanism ($R_{difficulty} = 1 - \bar{R}_s$) effectively drives the Proposer to generate increasingly difficult questions. Figure 3 shows difficulty scores rising from ~0.25 to ~0.85, correlating with improvements in overall accuracy.
- **Strong Empirical Results on Small Models:** Using Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, the framework achieves significant gains on competitive benchmarks (e.g., MATH 60.4 -> 68.2, SQuAD 78.2 -> 92.2), suggesting high efficiency in extracting performance from smaller models.

## Weaknesses

### Major
- **Evaluation Bias from LLM-as-a-Judge:** The primary evaluation for most benchmarks (except coding) relies on a "strong LLM" to judge correctness against ground truth (Section 5). Because the model is trained via an internal LLM-Judge to prioritize certain reasoning styles or formats, there is a risk that the reported gains reflect alignment with the evaluator's specific preferences rather than objective reasoning improvements. The paper lacks a calibration or "Judge Accuracy" analysis comparing the internal Judge's scores against ground-truth correctness on a held-out set to ensure the signal remains reliable.
- **Weak SFT Baseline Comparison:** The SFT baseline is trained on a very small seed of ~1K questions (Section 5). However, MAE (no reference) generates and trains on thousands of additional synthetic questions over 250 steps (Batch Size 128, totaling ~32k episodes). It is unclear if the improvement stems from the RL framework or simply from the significantly higher volume of synthetic training data. A fairer baseline would be SFT on a similarly sized synthetic dataset generated by a stronger model (e.g., Qwen2.5-72B).
- **Risk of Reward Hacking in 3B Models:** Relying on a 3B model (Qwen2.5-3B) to act as its own "Judge" for complex reasoning (e.g., GPQA) is conceptually risky. Without external grounding (verifiable rewards), a small model might reward itself for "plausible-sounding" but incorrect reasoning, creating a feedback loop of hallucinations. While quality filtering helps, the paper does not rigorously verify if the internal Judge's precision stays high as the Solver's answers become more sophisticated.

### Minor
- **Counter-intuitive Results on Reference Questions:** The "MAE (no reference)" setting, which generates questions from scratch, outperforms the "MAE (with reference)" setting. While the authors suggest this acts as a self-curriculum, it is surprising that completely ungrounded generation leads to better results than starting from high-quality human-curated topics. This warrants a more detailed diversity or content analysis of the generated questions to ensure the model isn't narrowing its focus.
- **Comparison to Self-Rewarding LLMs:** The paper would benefit from a more direct comparison or discussion regarding how this triad-agent approach specifically improves upon existing self-rewarding literature (e.g., Yuan et al., 2024), particularly concerning whether the triad structure helps mitigate the "reward model drift" typically seen in single-agent self-rewarding loops.

### Trivial
- **Clarity in Table 1/2:** Some nomenclature (e.g., "MMU" for "MMLU", "Winground" for "Winogrande") is inconsistent but easily understood.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Judge-Ground Truth Correlation:** A plot showing how the internal Judge's score correlates with real ground-truth correctness throughout the training steps on a validation slice.

## Novel Insights
The most significant insight is the implementation of an adversarial "difficulty reward" for the Proposer within a self-rewarding framework. By incentivizing the Proposer to find the Solver's failure points while constrained by a Judge's quality filter, the system creates a stable, domain-agnostic self-curriculum. This moves beyond simple self-play (which usually requires a verifiable environment like a game engine) to open-ended natural language reasoning, showing that even small 3B models can manage this co-evolution without collapsing into reward-hacking immediately.

## Suggestions
- Perform a manual audit of 100-200 generated question-answer-judge triplets to report "Judge Precision/Recall" against ground truth to validate the reward signal.
- Train an SFT baseline on a larger synthetic dataset (matching the token volume of MAE's training) to isolate the benefit of the RL loop vs. simple data volume increase.
