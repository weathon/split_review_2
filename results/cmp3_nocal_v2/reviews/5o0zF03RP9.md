## Summary

IncentRL proposes a reinforcement learning framework that augments the standard reward with a KL-divergence term between a predicted outcome distribution \(p(o|s,a)\) and a preferred outcome distribution \(q(o|s)\), weighted by a scalar \(\beta\). The paper's headline claim is that \(\beta\) is treated as a Bayesian random variable and updated online, eliminating manual trade-off tuning between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards. Empirical results are reported on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar-v0, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8.

## Strengths

- **Cognitively motivated framing that is internally coherent.** The connection to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error is clearly articulated (Section 3.3, lines 79–81), and the conceptual distinction from active inference (fixed preferences vs. belief revision) is well drawn. This provides a principled rationale for the KL-based shaping term.

- **The core idea — adaptive \(\beta\) via Bayesian inference — addresses a real limitation.** The claim that manually tuning the trade-off between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards is a bottleneck for existing intrinsic motivation methods is correct. If fully specified and validated, a Bayesian scheme for adapting \(\beta\) would be a genuine contribution.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

1. **The central claimed novelty — Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) — is never specified as a method.**  
   The abstract (line 9) and contribution list (lines 21–25) both present Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) as the paper's primary contribution. Yet **Section 3 (Methods, lines 55–81) contains zero description of any Bayesian inference procedure.** There is no prior over \(\beta\), no likelihood function, no description of what data conditions the posterior, no inference algorithm (MCMC, variational inference, conjugate update, or other), and no explanation of how the posterior or its samples are deployed in the RL loop. The only mentions of "Bayesian" or "posterior" occur in Section 5.4 (lines 230–247) and Appendix A, where posterior statistics are presented *without any explanation of how they were computed*.  

   A reader cannot reproduce, evaluate, or even understand the claimed central contribution. This is not a missing implementation detail — it is the paper's headline novelty, and its specification is entirely absent from the paper's technical content. This flaw is verifiable directly from the visible portion of the paper: the Methods section contains no Bayesian inference content, and no forward reference to an appendix describing it is provided.

### Major

2. **The predicted outcome distribution \(p(o|s,a)\) is underspecified for all non-trivial experiments.**  
   The KL shaping term \(-\beta\cdot\text{KL}(p(o|s,a)\|q(o|s))\) requires a concrete \(p(o|s,a)\). For the toy 2-state MDP (Section 5.2), this is given explicitly as the transition probabilities — possible only because the environment dynamics are known and the state space is trivial. For **MountainCar** (Section 5.3), the paper states only that "\(p(o|s,a)\) is the predicted outcome distribution" (line 199) and that \(q(o|s)\) assigns all probability to the goal region \(x \ge 0.5\). For **MiniGrid** (Section 5.4), no specification of \(p(o|s,a)\) is given at all. The paper does not describe how outcomes \(o\) are represented (next state? latent embedding?), how \(p(o|s,a)\) is parameterized or learned (neural network architecture, training loss), or how the KL divergence is computed against a continuous or high-dimensional \(q\). Section 4.3 mentions Gaussian variational encoders and diffusion priors as possibilities, but these are not instantiated for any experiment. This makes the method non-reproducible even at the conceptual level for the actual environments evaluated.

3. **The experimental evaluation is far too weak to support the paper's claims.**  
   - **No comparison to any existing intrinsic motivation method.** The paper positions itself as addressing "a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods" (line 9) and cites curiosity (Pathak et al., Burda et al.), VIME (Houthooft et al.), and empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende) in Section 2, but never compares against any of them empirically. The baselines are only \(\beta=0\) (standard RL without the KL term).  
   - **Very few seeds.** MountainCar and MiniGrid results are averaged over only 3 seeds. With MountainCar standard deviations of 472–909 (Table 2), the reported 18% improvement for \(\beta=0.1\) may not be statistically significant.  
   - **The Bayesian \(\beta\) agent's actual performance is not shown.** The paper states that "in additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (line 228), but the learning curves or final performance of the Bayesian agent are never presented. Figures 3–4 show only posterior statistics (mean, variance across rounds), not task performance. The central claim — that Bayesian adaptation eliminates manual tuning — is therefore supported only by hearsay.  
   - **The fixed-\(\beta\) results themselves undermine the "no manual tuning" claim.** In MountainCar (Table 2), \(\beta=0.1\) improves 18% but \(\beta=0.3\) degrades performance by 53% and \(\beta=1.0\) by 75%. This demonstrates that \(\beta\) is highly sensitive and *must* be carefully tuned when used as a fixed parameter — which is precisely the limitation the paper claims to solve, but the Bayesian solution's performance is not directly evaluated against the swept results.

### Minor

4. **The theoretical results (Propositions 1 and 2) are basic sanity checks, not substantive contributions.**  
   Proposition 1 (small \(\beta\) preserves the optimal policy) follows from continuity of \(J_\beta(\pi)\) in \(\beta\) and the positive reward gap in finite MDPs. Proposition 2 (as \(\beta\to\infty\) external reward is ignored) is definitional. These are not incorrect, but they do not analyze convergence rates, regret, sample complexity, or the dynamics of the Bayesian adaptation — which is the actual claimed novelty. The paper would not be weakened by their removal.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A direct comparison against curiosity-based exploration methods (ICM, RND) on the same benchmarks would substantially strengthen the empirical claims, given the paper's stated motivation.
- Reporting learning curves for the Bayesian \(\beta\) agent alongside the best fixed-\(\beta\) agent on MountainCar and MiniGrid would make the central claim verifiable rather than asserted.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface the fundamental gap between the claimed contribution (Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation) and what the paper actually specifies and validates, but this is an assessment of the submission's completeness, not a novel observation about the subject matter.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the Bayesian inference scheme in full.** Provide the prior over \(\beta\), the likelihood function, what data conditions the posterior (e.g., recent episode returns, success rates), the inference algorithm (closed-form if conjugate, or a specific MCMC/VI procedure), and how the posterior is used to set \(\beta\) in the RL loop (posterior mean? sample? full distribution?). This must appear in the Methods section, not only in results.
2. **Specify \(p(o|s,a)\) concretely for each experiment.** For MountainCar and MiniGrid, describe what constitutes an "outcome" \(o\), how \(p(o|s,a)\) is parameterized and learned, the architecture and training loss, and how the KL divergence is computed tractably.
3. **Show the Bayesian agent's actual performance curves** on MountainCar and MiniGrid, directly compared to the best fixed-\(\beta\) sweeps, with confidence intervals over at least 10 seeds.
4. **Add comparisons to at least one existing intrinsic motivation method** (e.g., ICM or RND) to ground the claim that the approach addresses a limitation of those methods.
