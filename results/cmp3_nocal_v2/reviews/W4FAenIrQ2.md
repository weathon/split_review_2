## Summary

This paper presents RedSage, an open-source 8B cybersecurity LLM built through a comprehensive pipeline: large-scale continual pretraining on 11.7B tokens of cybersecurity-filtered text (CyberFineWeb) plus 0.85B tokens of curated high-quality seed data (RedSage-Seed), agentic augmentation of seed documents into 266K multi-turn SFT conversations, DPO alignment with general preference data, and a new 30K-item benchmark (RedSage-Bench) covering knowledge, skills, and tool proficiency. RedSage variants show consistent improvements over 8B baselines on external cybersecurity benchmarks (CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SECURE, SecBench, SecEval) and on general LLM leaderboard tasks after post-training, while the full pipeline (data, code, models) is committed for open release — exceeding prior efforts in completeness and transparency.

## Strengths

1. **Completeness of the open pipeline.** The paper tackles the full stack — large-scale web filtering for CPT, curated high-quality seed data, LLM-based agentic augmentation for SFT, CPT, SFT, DPO, and a new benchmark — and commits to releasing models, data, and code. Table 2 shows this is notably more complete than prior work (PRIMUS uses only 835 SFT samples, Foundation-Sec-8B's dataset remains closed per the paper's own text, DeepHat has no CPT, and none use agentic augmentation). The openness is a genuine contribution to reproducibility.

2. **Benchmark fills a real gap.** Table 1 documents that existing cybersecurity benchmarks (SecEval, CyberMetric, SECURE, CTI-Bench, etc.) cover either knowledge or skills, but none cover knowledge, skills, *and* tool proficiency with quality scoring. RedSage-Bench's explicit coverage of tool-related competence (CLI commands, Kali Linux) targets an underevaluated dimension.

3. **Consistent empirical results across multiple independent evaluation axes.** RedSage variants outperform 8B-class baselines not only on their own benchmark but also on *external* cybersecurity benchmarks (CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SECURE, SecBench, SecEval) *and* general LLM leaderboard tasks. The improvement is not an artifact of benchmark design; it generalizes. The ablations (CFW-only, Seed-only, Base, Ins, DPO) are well-structured and reveal complementary strengths of the two pretraining corpora.

4. **Data decontamination is explicitly addressed.** Section 3.3 documents a semantic-similarity-based deduplication step between the benchmark and training data (removing 2.96% of benchmark-sized data), which is more care than many contemporaneous papers take.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **No measure of variance or statistical significance is reported anywhere.** Every result in Tables 4, 5, and 6 is a point estimate. Several of the improvements over Qwen3-8B-Base are small — e.g., +0.81 macro accuracy (RedSage-8B-Base vs Qwen3-8B-Base on RedSage-Bench MCQ, Table 4), +0.97 (RedSage-8B-Seed), +1.85 mean on external benchmarks (RedSage-8B-CFW vs Qwen3-8B-Base, Table 5). Without confidence intervals, standard deviations across runs, or significance tests, it is impossible to know whether these narrow-margin gains are meaningful or within noise. This is an evidential weakness that undercuts the precision of the claimed improvements, especially for the small-margin claims. (Note: many LLM evaluation papers do not report variance, but when margins are as slim as sub-1-point, the absence of any variability estimate becomes consequential.)

2. **The claim about "avoiding post-tuning degradation" is stated without supporting evidence.** The conclusion (Section 6, line 377) says RedSage "avoid[s] the post-tuning degradation observed in prior models." No analysis anywhere in the paper demonstrates post-tuning degradation in prior models as a general phenomenon, nor does it show that RedSage specifically avoids it. Table 4 shows that Qwen3-8B (81.85) scores lower than Qwen3-8B-Base (84.24) on RedSage-Bench while RedSage-8B-Ins (85.73) scores higher than RedSage-8B-Base (85.05), but this could simply reflect that RedSage's SFT used cybersecurity data while Qwen3's instruct tuning did not — not evidence of a general phenomenon. This claim should be removed or explicitly supported with a controlled analysis.

3. **RedSage-Bench is partially circular as a primary evaluation instrument.** The benchmark MCQs and open-ended Q&A are generated by an LLM teacher from RedSage-Seed (Section 3.3), and RedSage-Seed is itself used in CPT and is the source material for the agentic augmentation that produces the SFT data. Thus a model that more thoroughly memorizes the seed corpus will score higher on RedSage-Bench, but this does not cleanly measure cybersecurity competence independent of training data proximity. **This issue is substantially mitigated** by the strong results on external benchmarks (CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SECURE, etc.) where RedSage also outperforms baselines. But the paper's framing foregrounds the new benchmark as a core contribution, and several of the strongest precision claims rely on it. The paper should acknowledge this circularity more explicitly and weight the external benchmarks more heavily in its headline claims.

### Minor

1. **No quality analysis of the agentic augmentation outputs.** The pipeline (Planner Agent → Augmenter Agent → multi-turn conversations) is described in detail, but there is no quantitative analysis of output quality: no failure rate, no hallucination frequency, no pass rate for the "filtering outputs for format validity, consistency, and topical relevance" step, no human evaluation of factual accuracy or diversity of the generated conversations. Given that the teacher models (70B-class) are themselves capable of hallucinating cybersecurity content, this omission weakens the claim that the pipeline produces high-quality SFT data.

2. **Abstract-to-conclusion number inconsistency.** The abstract states "up to +5.59 points on cybersecurity benchmarks and +5.05 points on Open LLM Leaderboard tasks." The conclusion states "up to +5.9 points on cybersecurity tasks and +5.0 on general LLM benchmarks." Both the cybersecurity number (5.59 vs 5.9) and the general number (5.05 vs 5.0) differ with no explanation. (The +5.9 appears to be computed differently or from a different comparison; the discrepancy should be resolved.)

3. **Human quality control for the benchmark lacks specificity.** Section 3.3 states "random audits confirmed that items passing the final stages met both Stage 1 and Stage 2 requirements" but does not report what fraction of items was audited, how many auditors participated, or what the inter-rater agreement was. For a claimed benchmark contribution, these are standard reporting elements.

4. **Qwen3-8B's HellaSwag score (56.70) is unusually low and unexplained.** The base model scores 79.62 on HellaSwag, representing a ~23-point drop after instruction tuning (Table 6). This is remarkable and could indicate a prompt-format or chat-template mismatch with Qwen3-8B. Since HellaSwag contributes to the mean used in comparisons, this single potentially anomalous number could inflate RedSage's apparent advantage. The paper should at minimum remark on this.

### Trivial

1. **The discussion of limitations (Section 5) is only three sentences.** Given the scale and complexity of the pipeline (LLM-generated content, potential hallucination propagation, benchmark contamination risk, limitations of LLM-as-judge), a more thorough discussion would strengthen the paper.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Add variance or confidence intervals** for the key comparison tables, especially where margins are small (sub-2-point). Bootstrapped confidence intervals or results across multiple random seeds for the SFT stage would substantially strengthen evidential value.
- **Analyze the agentic augmentation quality** — a human evaluation of factual accuracy, relevance, and diversity on a sample of 200-300 generated conversations, with inter-rater agreement, would support the claim that the pipeline produces high-quality SFT data.
- **Add an ablation that separates domain-data effects from data-quantity effects.** The paper claims domain-specific training improves general reasoning. But RedSage-Ins/DPO were trained on more total SFT data than baselines. An ablation training Qwen3-8B on a matched quantity of general SFT data (without cybersecurity content) would isolate whether general benchmark gains come from domain knowledge transfer or simply from more SFT data.
- **Validate the ModernBERT cybersecurity classifier** with precision, recall, and F1 on a held-out test set. (This may be in the stripped appendix; if so, disregard.)
