## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT), two traditionally separate inverse tasks in music information retrieval. The key ideas are: (1) disentangling score content and performance style into separate latent spaces, (2) formulating EPR as a Seq2Seq task without requiring note-level alignment, and (3) a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The evaluation spans APT benchmarks (MUSTER, ScoreSimilarity), EPR objective and subjective listening tests, representation disentanglement analysis, style transfer, and PSR visualization.

## Strengths

1. **Joint EPR–APT formulation is genuinely novel and well-motivated.** The paper correctly identifies that EPR and APT are inverse tasks and proposes a unified framework with disentangled content and style representations (Section 1, Figure 1). Existing work has treated these tasks independently, and the paper presents a concrete architectural path for unifying them — this is a substantive contribution.

2. **Seq2Seq EPR without note-level alignment is a practical contribution.** The model achieves a 92.27% alignment rate (best among all compared systems, Table 3) while eliminating the preprocessing bottleneck of note-level alignment that limits existing methods on expressive techniques like trills. This has real deployment relevance.

3. **The PSR module is a creative and well-motivated idea.** Generating style embeddings directly from score content using a diffusion model (Section 3.4), mimicking how pianists infer appropriate expression from a score, is an interesting approach to making style-aware rendering accessible without requiring performer labels or manual parameter tuning.

4. **Evaluation is broad in scope.** The paper runs APT benchmarks (MUSTER, ScoreSimilarity), EPR objective metrics, subjective listening tests, performer/composer identification (Table 4), style transfer tests, and PSR visualization (Figure 4). This breadth provides multiple angles for assessing the claims.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The ablation for joint modeling is confounded by data quantity.** The EPR-Only variant (Section 5.1) is trained solely on the ASAP dataset (967 pieces), while the full joint model is trained on ASAP plus 75,913 unpaired scores and an unlabeled YouTube performance dataset. When Ours(Target) outperforms EPR-Only, the paper attributes this to "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data" — but these factors are never disentangled. The improvement could be entirely driven by the vastly larger training set, with joint modeling contributing nothing. Since the claimed benefit of joint modeling is a central thesis of the paper, this confound significantly weakens the evidence. An EPR-Only variant trained on the same total data (ASAP + unpaired data) is needed to isolate the effect.

2. **Missing comparisons against recent EPR baselines.** The paper cites several transformer-based EPR systems in Related Work — Rhyu et al. (2022), Borovik & Viro (2023), Tang et al. (2023) — but the experimental comparison (Tables 2, 3) includes only VirtuosoNet (2019) and DExter (2024). The claim of "competitive performance" (abstract, conclusion) is not fully supported without comparisons against these more recent methods. While some may require note-aligned data, this does not excuse their absence from comparison when the paper is making competitiveness claims.

### Minor

3. **Style transfer evaluation is thin with an unexplained anomalous result.** The style transfer listening test (Figure 5) uses only 3 samples. More importantly, for Sample 2, the Target condition (explicit style transfer to a reference) receives a lower style similarity rating (~2.4) than the Mean interpolation (~3.0). The paper states "the Target condition achieves the highest style similarity ratings in Samples 1 and 3" (which is accurate for those two samples), but never discusses why Sample 2 contradicts the expected pattern or what this reveals about limitations of the style transfer. The paper should either explain this result or acknowledge it as a limitation.

4. **Subjective listening test has 11 participants with no inter-rater reliability reported.** The evaluation in Section 4.3 uses 11 musically trained participants rating on a 5-point Likert scale. No inter-rater agreement metric (e.g., Fleiss' kappa or ICC) is reported, and the nature of the error bars in Figure 2 (standard deviation, standard error, or confidence intervals) is not stated. This makes it difficult to assess whether the visually modest differences between conditions are reliable.

5. **PSR module evaluation relies primarily on qualitative visual inspection.** The main evidence for the PSR module (Section 5.3) is Figure 4, showing 2D projections of real vs. PSR-generated style embeddings. The claim that they "closely mirror" each other is supported only by visual comparison — no quantitative metric (e.g., cosine similarity between ground-truth and PSR-generated style vectors on held-out pairs, or Earth Mover's Distance between latent distributions) is provided. Given that the PSR is listed as one of three core contributions, stronger quantitative evidence is expected.

### Trivial
- The paper introduces a distinction between *style* and *genre* (Section 1) but never formalizes or evaluates genre in the experiments. The genre framing is used to motivate the PSR module but is dropped operationally — the paper would be cleaner without it or should evaluate it.
- The YouTube unpaired performance transcription model is mentioned but its accuracy and potential biases are not characterized, which matters since errors propagate into training data.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Adding statistical significance for the style transfer listening test comparisons.
- A brief limitations paragraph discussing when the PSR module produces mismatched styles or how the model handles technically complex passages.
- Reporting the YouTube transcription model's accuracy on the pilot study used to select it.

## Novel Insights

The harsh review surfaces one genuinely non-obvious observation: the paper's central claim about joint modeling being beneficial cannot be separated from the effect of having ~78x more training data because the ablation comparison is confounded. This is not a standard nitpick — it targets the evidential foundation of the paper's headline contribution. The reviewer also correctly notes that the paper's own hedging language ("joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data") implicitly acknowledges the confound without resolving it. Beyond this, no novel insight emerges beyond what the paper itself articulates.

## Suggestions

1. **De-confound the joint modeling ablation.** Train an EPR-Only variant on the same data mix (ASAP + unpaired scores + YouTube performances) to isolate whether the joint framework itself provides a benefit. Alternatively, train the joint model on only the paired ASAP data. Either experiment would directly support or qualify the central claim.

2. **Add at least one recent transformer-based EPR baseline** (e.g., Rhyu et al., 2022 or Tang et al., 2023) to the comparison tables, even if it requires note-aligned preprocessing. This would substantiate the "competitive performance" claim.

3. **Strengthen the PSR evaluation with a quantitative metric**, such as cosine similarity between PSR-generated \( \hat{z}_s \) and ground-truth \( z_s \) on held-out pairs, or a distribution distance (e.g., FSD) between real and generated style distributions.

4. **Increase the style transfer sample count** and report inter-rater reliability for all subjective tests. Discuss the anomalous Sample 2 result.
