## Summary

This position paper argues that LLMs' over-cautious health responses constitute a safety harm—not merely a helpfulness deficiency—especially for users with OCD and anxiety disorders. It introduces the concept of "over-alignment" (analogous to overfitting) and presents OCD-Eval, a 225-query evaluation across 20+ models showing 20–70% over-cautious rates on low-risk OCD-themed queries, with emergency recognition preserved. The paper proposes that over-cautiousness stems from value pluralism failures and corporate liability pressure, and that this harms vulnerable users through reinforced anxiety, reassurance-seeking, and cascading physical effects.

## Strengths

- **Clear, discussable position that challenges a prevailing assumption**: The paper's central claim—that more caution is not always safer—directly challenges the dominant safety-alignment paradigm. The "over-alignment" framing gives the NeurIPS community a concise, technically grounded term to engage with and potentially refute, going beyond the existing over-refusal literature (which frames the drawback as mere helpfulness loss) (Section 1, lines 10–22; Section 3).

- **Empirical grounding beyond philosophical assertion**: OCD-Eval demonstrates a nontrivial pattern: strong emergency recognition (mostly >90%) coexists with substantial over-escalation on low-risk OCD-themed queries, and specific response patterns like "reassurance undermining" are documented across models. This gives the position concrete teeth rather than relying solely on argumentation (Section 4, Table 1, Figure 2).

- **Productive distinction between general medical appropriateness and OCD/anxiety-sensitive communication**: The paper identifies a real gap in HealthBench-style evaluation—general-user queries with neutral tone versus emotionally-loaded, recurring reassurance-seeking patterns—and shows why this distinction matters for vulnerable populations (Section 2.3, Figure 1b).

- **Explicit counterargument engagement**: Section 6 considers four alternative positions with substantive rebuttals. The response to "vulnerable users shouldn't use LLMs" is particularly effective, citing that OCD diagnosis takes 5–15 years, only 17.5% with lifetime diagnosis used services in a 12-month period, and fewer than 4% of online health seekers know reassurance-seeking is counterproductive (lines 349–374).

- **Specific harm pathway identification**: Rather than vaguely claiming harm, the paper identifies concrete mechanisms—reassurance undermining, symptom checking, unnecessary escalation—and documents their frequency across models (Table 1), which invites focused debate about which patterns are most problematic.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper has a clear position, coherent argumentation, and invites productive disagreement.

### Major

- **Overclaiming relative to evidence on the core causal claim**: The title states LLM over-cautiousness "is hurting vulnerable users," and Section 3 claims over-cautious responses "intensify users' anxiety" and produce physical harms including infection, dietary harm, and suicide risk. The empirical evidence (OCD-Eval) demonstrates over-escalation patterns in model outputs. It does not demonstrate that these patterns actually harm users—the causal chain from "model says low risk instead of negligible" to "user worsens" is argued from cyberchondria literature rather than shown for LLM interactions. For a position paper, arguing this from evidence and reasoning is acceptable, but the title's definitive "is hurting" overclaims what is demonstrated. The position would be more defensible stated as "risks hurting" or "can itself be a safety harm." (Abstract, Section 3 lines 153–166, Section 4)

- **Underengaged with the reassurance-seeking paradox**: For OCD/anxiety users, reassurance-seeking is the core maladaptive behavior that therapy targets for reduction. The paper advocates for "calibrated" responses that provide reassurance, but providing any form of reassurance—even medically accurate reassurance—can function as reinforcement of compulsive checking. The paper acknowledges this (lines 305–314) but does not resolve the tension: if reassurance-seeking is pathological, then LLMs providing reassurance may reinforce the very pattern the paper identifies as harmful, regardless of the response's factual accuracy. The counterargument section addresses whether users should use LLMs at all (they will, so we must design for them) but not whether optimized reassurance actually helps or harms this specific population. This is a genuine conceptual gap in the argument (Section 3, Section 6).

### Minor

- **Ethical arguments are rhetorically broad but loosely connected to empirical findings**: The Kantian and utilitarian analysis (lines 176–186) is understandable framing, but reads more as rhetorical reinforcement than argument sharpened by the benchmark results. A more grounded ethical analysis would reason from the specific tradeoff structure revealed by the data—e.g., what is the expected cost of a false reassurance vs. false alarm for this specific population? (Section 3)

- **Dataset representativeness limitation acknowledged but not fully addressed**: The 225 queries were sourced from two authors with OCD experience, which is reasonable for a seed set but narrows claims about "vulnerable users broadly." The paper does not obscure this but could be more explicit about how generalizable the specific OCD-anxiety patterns are to the broader population seeking health information online (Section 4, lines 240–241).

- **Threshold justification for OCR > 0.16**: The argument that the 0.16 fraction of cases where second-round labels exceeded first-round labels serves as an "upper bound on the probability that random disagreement affects over-cautious metrics" (lines 269–273) is not a rigorous statistical bound—it's a point estimate from a small relabel sample. This is a minor methodological concern since the overall OCR rates are well above this threshold for most models (lines 295–319).

### Trivial
None that merit listing.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Clinical psychologist or psychiatrist involvement in evaluation design: GPs provide risk labels, but if the paper's central claim is about OCD/anxiety-appropriate communication, then input from mental health professionals on what constitutes "therapeutic" vs. "reinforcing" response patterns would strengthen both the position and future versions of the benchmark.

- Separation of three currently conflated notions—objective risk estimation, escalation/recommendation level, and linguistic framing/tone—into distinct analytical categories. The paper touches on all three (e.g., risk labels, medical visit suggestions, reassurance undermining) but does not systematically distinguish them, which would sharpen the argument about which form of over-cautiousness is most harmful.

## Removed Points

These points were flagged but are removed from the main review, with justification:

- **"No direct evidence that over-cautiousness is alignment-induced (vs. sensible caution under uncertainty)"**: As a position paper, the authors are arguing that this is an alignment artifact. The paper provides both the theoretical argument (value pluralism, liability pressure) and empirical evidence that models with strong medical knowledge still over-escalate (lines 327–338). Whether this is "proved" is not the standard for a position paper—the argument is coherent and evidence-supported.

- **"GP risk labels insufficient for defining clinically appropriate OCD responses"**: This is true but the paper explicitly acknowledges and justifies this choice (lines 211–214). The paper argues for GP risk labels as an objective baseline while acknowledging that appropriate communication for OCD users requires further work. This is a reasonable scoping decision, not a flaw.

- **"Demand for user studies or clinical validation"**: This would strengthen the paper, but position papers argue positions from reasoning and available evidence. Demanding novel user studies is asking for a different paper.

- **"The response to 'better to be careful' is moralized, not analytical"**: The paper's response to this counterargument (lines 343–358) includes both moral argument and empirical data (30–50% of health searchers have health anxiety). While the moral dimension could be better balanced, it is not purely moralized.

- **"Physical harms chain (infection, diet, suicide) is speculative"**: The paper explicitly states "LLMs likely will not directly suggest these behaviors; however, the reinforced anxiety might lead users to them as a form of secondary harm" (lines 160–161). This is a reasonable inferential claim in a position paper, clearly flagged as secondary rather than direct.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most novel contribution is identifying "reassurance undermining" as a distinct response pattern—where models acknowledge low risk but then hedge with potential dangers—because this pattern is specifically problematic for OCD/anxiety users who fixate on the hedging rather than the reassurance. This is a genuinely new analytical category that is not captured in existing over-refusal or medical QA evaluations.

## Suggestions

- Reframe the title and strongest claims from "is hurting vulnerable users" to "risks hurting vulnerable users" or "can itself be a safety harm." This preserves the provocative position while making the evidence match the claims.

- Explicitly address the reassurance paradox: acknowledge that providing LLM reassurance to OCD users may itself reinforce maladaptive patterns, and articulate how the paper's proposed "calibrated" responses differ from mere reassurance—one possible resolution is that the distinction is between acknowledging low risk (informative) vs. providing emotional soothing (reinforcing).
