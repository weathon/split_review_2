## Summary

This paper reformulates lead-lag detection in financial markets as a temporal link prediction problem on dynamic graphs, where assets are nodes and directed edges capture predictive influence across time. It introduces a custom dataset of 37 stocks/commodities with 5 years of daily data, adapts 7 deep learning models (LSTM, JODIE, DySAT, TGAT, TGN, APAN, GraphMixer, GraphMixer-TNF) to this task, and evaluates them on two scenarios (positive+negative vs. only positive lead-lag relationships).

## Strengths

- **Novel formulation.** Reformulating lead-lag detection as a temporal link prediction task on dynamic graphs (Section 3.1) is a genuinely new perspective that the prior literature has not explored. This is the paper's most creative contribution. [impact=+9.99]

- **Statistical testing rigor.** The use of Friedman + Conover post-hoc tests (Section 4.3, Figure 2) rather than just reporting point estimates adds methodological rigor that many papers in this space omit. [impact=+9.84]

- **Thorough model adaptation.** Adapting JODIE, DySAT, TGAT, TGN, APAN, and GraphMixer within a unified TGL framework (Section 3.4) is a nontrivial engineering effort. The specific adaptations — converting JODIE from bipartite to homogeneous graphs, reinterpreting DySAT's snapshots as windowed lead-lag observations — are technically detailed. [impact=+3.79]

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Central claim not supported by experimental design.** The paper claims that TGNNs "effectively model complex lead-lag relationships" (Abstract, Conclusions). However, the ground-truth labels are generated by a simple threshold rule (Equation 1: if asset j's return on day t−1 exceeds 5% and asset i's return on day t also exceeds 5% in the same direction, a directed edge exists). The models are evaluated on how well they predict these rule-based labels, not on whether they discover patterns the rule misses. The near-perfect recall scores (GM: R@10 = 0.99 in Table 1, 0.996 in Table 2) are consistent with the model learning this simple rule rather than discovering genuinely complex lead-lag structure. Including the threshold rule itself as a baseline would clarify whether TGNNs add value beyond the labeling heuristic. The paper's framing in the Conclusions also refers to detecting "lead-lag effects," but the labels capture only single threshold-exceedance coincidences, not systematically recurring effects — the claims outpace what was actually measured. [impact=-10.00]

### Minor

- **GM-TNF underperformance is underexplored.** GM-TNF, intended as an enhancement of GM, performs uniformly worse across all metrics (Tables 1 and 2). The paper's explanation — "the additional temporal node features did not contribute meaningful extra information" — is plausible but untested. The asymmetry where GM improves with all features (Table 3: 0.78→0.79) while GM-TNF does not warrants deeper investigation rather than a post-hoc rationalization. [impact=-3.72]

- **The LSTM baseline comparison is insufficient.** The LSTM baseline is structurally blind by design. Its near-random performance confirms it cannot use graph structure, but this does not compare the TGNNs against the natural baseline — the threshold rule itself (Equation 1). Demonstrating that TGNNs outperform the simple rule that generated the labels would provide meaningful validation. [impact=-0.33]

- **Model selection policy conflates two scenarios.** Models are validated on the positive-and-negative scenario and applied "as-is" to the positive-only scenario (Section 4.2). Since hyperparameters were selected for the broader case, the rankings for the restricted scenario may shift with task-specific tuning. [impact=-0.00]
