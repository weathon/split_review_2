## Summary

This paper introduces GMD-25, a benchmark for evaluating compositional generalization in machine learning force fields (MLFFs). The benchmark comprises four tasks—length extrapolation, functional group composition, functional group duplication, and functional group combination—each designed to test whether models can recombine known functional groups and chain lengths in novel ways. The paper evaluates five neural network architectures (SchNet, PAINN, DimeNet++, GemNet, EquiFormerV2) and finds that (1) all models degrade substantially on out-of-distribution molecules, with errors often 1–2 orders of magnitude above in-distribution errors, and (2) ID and OOD performance are decoupled—models that perform best on ID data do not necessarily generalize best.

## Strengths

- **The benchmark fills a genuine gap.** Current MLFF evaluation (MD17, MD22, WS22, Transition1x) tests either equilibrium dynamics on the same molecules or broad coverage without controlled splits. GMD-25 is the first benchmark to systematically construct train/test splits specifically for testing compositional generalization (Section 1, Section 2.3). This is a well-motivated and underexplored question.

- **The four task designs are principled and diagnostic.** Length extrapolation, functional group composition, duplication, and combination each isolate a distinct aspect of compositional generalization. The inclusion of base and augmented variants (Tasks 1 and 2) allows the benchmark to distinguish whether additional compositional examples help, providing more information than a single pass/fail test (Section 3.1).

- **The finding that ID and OOD performance are decoupled is genuinely informative.** The paper shows that models with strong in-distribution accuracy (e.g., EquiFormerV2 on forces) can fail dramatically on OOD energy, while models with weaker ID performance (e.g., SchNet, DimeNet++ on energy) sometimes generalize more stably (Section 4.3, Figure 2). This is the kind of insight that can drive architectural innovation and is not available from existing benchmarks.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **No statistical uncertainty reported for any result.** The paper evaluates five neural network models across four tasks but reports only point estimates of MAE from what appears to be a single run per model. Neural network training is stochastic (random weight initialization, minibatch order, potential GPU nondeterminism). Confirmed by grep: there are zero mentions of seeds, standard deviations, error bars, confidence intervals, or related terms anywhere in the paper. Without multiple seeds, the reader cannot tell whether observed performance differences between models are significant or within training noise, whether the OOD generalization gap is robust across runs, or whether the ranking of models on ID vs. OOD would replicate. For a benchmark paper whose findings are intended to guide future architecture design, this is a meaningful weakness. At minimum, 3–5 runs per model-task combination should be reported with mean and standard deviation.

- **The physical realism of the training trajectories is unvalidated.** The paper uses FlashMD with a 16 femtosecond timestep (Section 3.2, line 92), whereas standard MD for organic molecules uses 0.5–1 fs. The paper cites FlashMD as a "long-stride" method (Bigi et al., 2025) but offers no validation that the resulting geometries are physically realistic. While forces are recalculated with GFN2-xTB on each snapshot, the concern is whether the *geometries* themselves correspond to physically realizable configurations or are artifacts of an unstable trajectory. If the geometries are unrealistic, the benchmark may test generalization to pathological geometries rather than to new molecules. The paper should at minimum report energy conservation in the FlashMD trajectories or compare to a standard small-timestep reference trajectory.

### Minor

- **Figure captions include model names not described in the methods section.** Figure 2 lists "PBE0" (a DFT functional) as a compared method, and Figure 3 lists "m4s" (unidentified). Section 4.1 (Models) describes only five models: SchNet, PAINN, DimeNet++, GemNet, and EquiFormerV2. These names may be parser artifacts from PDF extraction, but if genuine, they represent a significant omission from the evaluation setup that must be clarified.

- **The paper calls its trajectories "ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) trajectories"** (lines 26, 56) and frames the benchmark as testing whether models learn "physical principles" (abstract, line 13). However, the trajectories are generated by FlashMD (a fast approximate method, not ab initio MD), and the energy/force labels come from GFN2-xTB, a semi-empirical tight-binding method which the paper itself describes as "semi-empirical" (line 56). While the paper does disclose GFN2-xTB's nature, the AIMD label is factually misleading, and the "physical principles" framing overstates what GFN2-xTB-level reference calculations can validate. The benchmark measures generalization to the GFN2-xTB approximation, not to true quantum mechanics. The authors should either reframe these claims or add a small-scale validation correlating GFN2-xTB with DFT for these molecules.

- **The "functional group composition" framing in Task 2 is chemically imprecise.** The paper says a carboxylic acid's functional group "can be seen as a composition of the functional group of the former two" (alcohol and aldehyde, line 72). Conceptually this is a useful pedagogical analogy, but the electronic structure of a carboxylic acid involves resonance between the two oxygen atoms that has no counterpart in isolated alcohol + aldehyde. The task remains a meaningful OOD challenge, but the compositional framing should be described more carefully.

- **Limited diagnostic analysis of *why* models fail.** The paper documents that models fail on OOD tasks but offers no per-atom error decomposition (e.g., errors near the functional group vs. on the alkyl chain) or analysis of which structural features correlate with failure. For a diagnostic benchmark, this kind of analysis would provide much more actionable signal for model developers.

### Trivial

- The claim that EquiFormerV2 "failed completely" on energy MAE (line 166) is stated without quantifying what "fail" means in practical terms. A concrete numerical threshold or comparison alongside the qualitative language would be more precise.

- The description of the augmented Task 1 variant (lines 64–66) is dense and would benefit from a table or clearer exposition. The reader has to work hard to understand the train/test split logic.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A simple baseline (e.g., k-nearest-neighbor predictor in descriptor space, or linear regression on histogram-of-bond-lengths features) would help separate "generalizing the physics" from "memorizing the training set" and show that the OOD tasks are learnable.
- Including foundation models (e.g., MACE-MP-0, SevenNet) as additional baselines, even if only as a reference point with the caveat about memorization, would help practitioners understand how current practical tools fare on these tasks.
- A validation of GFN2-xTB reference quality for the specific molecule types in the benchmark (correlation plots against DFT) would strengthen the claim that the benchmark tests physically meaningful properties.

## Novel Insights

The key insight from the reviews that goes beyond the paper's own contributions is the asymmetry between the paper's methodological framing ("AIMD", "physical principles") and the actual reference quality (GFN2-xTB semi-empirical). This gap is common in MLFF benchmark work but is worth highlighting: the field needs to calibrate what it claims about "physical understanding" when labels come from approximate reference methods. A deeper observation is that the FlashMD 16 fs timestep concern points to a broader methodological risk in the benchmark's trajectory generation pipeline that the community should be aware of when adopting this benchmark.

## Suggestions

1. Report all results with at least 3–5 random seeds, with mean and standard deviation, to establish the robustness of all comparative claims.
2. Validate the FlashMD trajectory quality—show energy conservation, RMSD vs. standard MD (1 fs), or at minimum acknowledge the limitation explicitly and discuss its potential impact.
3. Reframe the reference to GFN2-xTB more honestly: drop the "AIMD" label for the trajectories and calibrate "physical principles" claims, or add a small-scale validation correlating GFN2-xTB with DFT for the relevant molecules.
4. Clarify what PBE0 and m4s refer to in Figures 2 and 3 (or confirm they are parser artifacts).
5. Add per-atom error analysis (e.g., errors near functional groups vs. alkyl chains) to make the benchmark more diagnostically useful.
6. Add a simple baseline (e.g., k-NN or linear regression) to establish whether the OOD tasks are learnable at all.
