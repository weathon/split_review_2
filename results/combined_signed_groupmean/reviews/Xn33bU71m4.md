## Summary

This paper conducts an empirical benchmark evaluating nine open-source LLMs on two reverse engineering tasks (name recovery and type inference) across multiple architectures, optimization levels, input formats, and decompilers. The work identifies a real gap — systematic LLM benchmarking is lacking in the binary reverse engineering community — and carries out a commendably broad experimental campaign. However, the paper has three high-severity issues that collectively undermine its conclusions: (1) the central F1=0.37 claim in the introduction is not supported by any main-table result, (2) no non-LLM baselines are compared, making the core finding ("LLMs are not yet ready") uncalibrated, and (3) the fine-tuning setup is critically underspecified. Despite strong experimental scope, the paper is not yet in a publishable state.

## Strengths

- **Broad experimental scope (+6.97):** Evaluates 9 models across 4 architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS), 4 optimization levels (O0–O3), 2 decompilers (Ghidra, IDA Pro), and both decompiled and assembly input formats. This is a significant experimental investment that, taken at face value, would be useful to the community.

- **Real-world firmware evaluation (+8.35):** Table 6 tests actual stripped firmware binaries with two different decompilers, demonstrating that the poor synthetic-benchmark performance transfers to realistic settings. This is a meaningful contribution that strengthens the paper's practical relevance.

- **CodeWordNet ablation (+3.28):** Figure 1 shows that the choice of evaluation metric (with vs. without CodeWordNet) substantially affects measured performance. This is a useful methodological insight for the field.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No comparison against non-LLM baselines (impact -10.00).** The paper concludes "LLMs are not yet ready for reverse engineering tasks" but evaluates only LLMs against other LLMs. Prior specialized approaches cited in the paper's own related work — SYMGEN (Jiang et al.), RESYM (Xie et al.), VARBERT (Pal et al., 2024), Debin (He et al., 2018) — are not evaluated under the same conditions. Without this anchor, the reader cannot tell whether the reported F1 scores (most below 0.1) are competitive with the state of the art or far below it. The paper's framing as a guide to model selection is hollow if the only actionable finding is "none of the models work." This is the single most important evidential gap in the paper.

- **Fine-tuning setup is underspecified (impact -9.94).** The paper reports only "200 steps" of LoRA fine-tuning (Section 4.3) on 160,000 training functions. Missing details include: LoRA rank, alpha, target modules, learning rate, batch size, optimizer, sequence length, prompting strategy during fine-tuning, and whether 200 steps was sufficient for convergence. Without these details, the fine-tuning results cannot be reproduced or meaningfully interpreted. At 200 steps, each training sample may be seen fewer than once depending on batch size — this raises the question of whether "fine-tuning" actually occurred in a meaningful sense.

- **Unsupported headline result in the introduction (impact -9.93).** Line 19 states: "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." However, no main-table result for DeepSeek-R1 exceeds 0.17 (Table 3, variable name recovery, O0 fine-tuned). The paper notes that results for other architectures are in the appendix (line 217), which cannot be checked here, but the introduction presents 0.37 as a general headline finding without referencing which table or condition supports it. This inconsistency between the paper's advertised best result and its visible evidence undermines reader trust in the reported numbers.

### Minor

- **No variance or confidence intervals.** The paper acknowledges randomness (line 428: "The number will be similar, but not identical, due to randomness") yet reports all results as single-point estimates. Differences as small as 0.01–0.02 (common in Tables 2–4) are discussed as meaningful trends, but without variance measures, these could be noise. (Impact: -0.47)

- **Auxiliary LLM for output normalization is a confound (impact -0.04).** Section 3.2 uses an auxiliary LLM to normalize heterogeneous model outputs but never identifies which LLM is used, what prompt is employed, or how accuracy is validated across models. If the auxiliary LLM systematically favors or disfavors certain models' output styles, the measured performance differences could be pipeline artifacts.

### Trivial

- **"Eight" vs "nine" models inconsistency (impact -4.53).** Section 4.2 (line 143) states "We support eight mainstream code LLMs" but Table 1 lists nine models. The abstract and conclusion correctly say "nine." This is a minor but unnecessary error.

- **DeepSeek-R1 naming imprecision (impact -0.01).** The text refers to "DeepSeek-R1" throughout, while Table 1 lists "Deepseek-R1-Qwen." The description in line 360 ("a fine-tuned variant of Qwen2.5") is technically correct for the distilled Qwen variant evaluated, but the shorthand name and the full identifier are inconsistent, creating confusion about which model was actually tested.
