Now let me write the complete review document.## Summary

This paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments standard RL rewards with a KL-divergence penalty between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), weighted by an incentive parameter β. The paper claims Bayesian online adaptation of β as its central novelty, along with theoretical analysis and empirical validation on three environments. The fixed-β KL shaping idea is cleanly motivated. However, the paper's headline contribution—the Bayesian β adaptation scheme—is completely unspecified, and the evidence that is presented for it contains internal contradictions and does not show RL performance.

## Strengths

- **Conceptually clean framing.** The KL-divergence formulation in Equation (1) connecting predicted and preferred outcome distributions is intuitive, naturally ties to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine RPE literature, and provides a clear cognitive grounding.

- **Well-motivated problem.** The paper correctly identifies the need to avoid manual tuning of the trade-off between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards—a genuine limitation of current intrinsic motivation methods.

- **Clear presentation and organization.** The paper is well-structured and the fixed-β version of the method is described accessibly, making the omissions all the more apparent.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian adaptation of β — the paper's headline contribution — is completely unspecified.** The abstract, introduction, and conclusion all identify Bayesian β adaptation as the central novelty ("The central novelty is treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online," "We introduce a Bayesian scheme for adapting the incentive weight β"). Yet the paper contains **zero specification** of: the prior distribution on β, the likelihood function (what data conditions the posterior?), the inference procedure (exact? variational? MCMC?), the update schedule (per-step, per-episode, per-round?), or how posterior samples translate to the β used during RL training. Section 5.4 says "we tracked the posterior evolution of β using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" without ever explaining what the scheme is. A reader cannot understand, implement, or evaluate the central claimed contribution. This is not a missing detail — the core method of the paper is absent.

### Major

- **Internal contradiction in the Bayesian β evidence.** The paper claims the posterior "quickly concentrates near the effective region (β ≈ 0.1)" (Section 5.4, Figure 3 caption), and Figures 3-4 mark a dashed red line at "target effective β = 0.1". However, Table 3 (Appendix A) shows the posterior mean dropping from 0.106 (Round 1) → 0.045 (Round 2) → 0.019 (Round 3) → **0.017** (Round 4). The posterior does not concentrate at β≈0.1; it converges to approximately one-sixth of that value. Moreover, the "target β=0.1" comes from MountainCar (where β=0.1 was the best fixed value), yet these Bayesian results are presented in the MiniGrid section (where the best fixed β was **0.01**). The actual convergence (~0.017) is close to MiniGrid's optimum, but the paper repeatedly asserts convergence at 0.1. This inconsistency undermines the central quantitative evidence.

- **No RL performance results for the Bayesian β agent.** The paper's main empirical claim for its headline contribution is stated as "additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (Section 5.4). Figures 3-4 and Table 3 only report posterior β statistics (mean, std, min, max of sampled β values). **The actual RL return, success rate, or episode length of the Bayesian-β agent is never compared against fixed-β baselines.** Showing that the posterior concentrates is not evidence of good RL performance.

- **No comparison against existing intrinsic motivation methods.** The Related Work (Section 2.1) cites curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., Burda et al.), empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende), information gain (Houthooft et al.), and potential-based reward shaping (Ng et al.) — yet **none** of these are compared against. The only baseline is β=0 (standard RL). The paper claims IncentRL "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning, a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods," but never compares against those methods to establish relative performance. There is also no comparison against meta-gradient or annealing baselines that could serve as alternatives for the adaptive β claim.

- **The predictive model p(o|s,a) implementation is unspecified in the deep RL experiments.** For MountainCar and MiniGrid, the paper never states how p(o|s,a) is obtained. Is it a learned neural network? What architecture? What loss function? Is it updated jointly with the policy or separately? Section 4.3 discusses possibilities (variational encoders, diffusion priors) but does not specify what was actually used. The entire shaping term depends on p(o|s,a), and without this information the deep RL experiments are not reproducible.

### Minor

- **The theoretical analysis (Section 4) is overclaimed.** Proposition 1 ("for small β, the optimal policy is preserved") is a direct consequence of continuity of the objective in β and would hold for any bounded reward perturbation — it does not leverage the specific structure of the KL shaping. Proposition 2 ("as β→∞, the KL term dominates") is definitional. Neither result addresses actual learning dynamics (convergence rate, sample complexity, whether the Bayesian β update converges). The paper describes this as "theoretical analysis" and "convergence behavior" but provides no substantive analysis of the learning algorithm.

- **Limited empirical evaluation for a claimed general-purpose framework.** Three environments (2-state toy, MountainCar, MiniGrid) with only 3 seeds for the deep RL experiments. Only 2 β values tested on MiniGrid (0.0 and 0.01). Standard RL benchmarks (Atari, MuJoCo, DM Control) are absent. The narrow evaluation base makes it difficult to assess the claimed generality or robustness.
