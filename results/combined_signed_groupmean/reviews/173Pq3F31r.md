## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. The key ideas are: (1) a joint Seq2Seq formulation that shares a content space between the two inverse tasks while maintaining a separate style space, trained on both paired ASAP data and large-scale unpaired data via masked reconstruction; (2) a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automated rendering. The paper reports competitive APT results on ASAP, convincing disentanglement evidence (Table 4, Figure 3), and subjective listening tests showing strong EPR output quality.

## Strengths

1. **Novel and well-motivated problem framing (impact +7.35).** The idea of jointly modeling the inverse tasks of EPR and APT through disentangled content/style representations is the paper's clearest contribution. The duality argument (Section 1, Figure 1 top-left) is articulated concisely, and the analogy to ASR/TTS joint modeling provides legitimate structural justification.

2. **Clean architectural design for the core idea (impact +7.83).** The formulation in Section 3.2 — EPR as one-to-many, APT as many-to-one, with shared content space Z_c and separate style space Z_s — is simple and conceptually elegant. Equations (1)–(6) tie the four tasks together straightforwardly with a well-integrated objective.

3. **The PSR module is a genuinely useful extension (impact +9.71).** The diffusion-based style generation from score content alone (Section 3.4) tackles a practical limitation of prior EPR systems requiring manual style specification or composer labels, and it is cleanly separated from the joint model.

4. **Disentanglement analysis is convincing (impact +9.77).** Table 4 provides strong evidence: Style→Comp accuracy of 77.46% vs. Cont→Comp accuracy of 29.99%, and Figure 3 shows clear clustering by composer and performer. The Style→Perf accuracy of 42.07% (F1=25.82) across 49 performers is modest in absolute terms but the gap with Cont→Perf (9.94%) convincingly demonstrates that style and content encode distinct information.

5. **Seq2Seq formulation of EPR without note-level alignment is a practical contribution (impact +6.17).** The paper demonstrates competitive EPR performance without requiring fine-grained note-level alignment (Section 3.5), addressing a significant practical bottleneck in prior work.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Joint training benefit not isolated from additional data (impact -9.92).** The paper's central claim that joint modeling improves EPR performance rests on comparing Ours(Target) against an EPR-Only variant that is "trained solely on the ASAP dataset" (line 209), while the full joint model also receives 75,913 unpaired scores and unlabeled YouTube piano performances. The paper acknowledges this (line 235: "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data"), but this framing makes it impossible to determine whether the improvement comes from cross-task sharing or simply from having more data. A proper ablation would train EPR-Only on the same total data using the same masked reconstruction losses so that only the presence of cross-task sharing differs. Additionally, EPR-Only is absent from Table 3 (alignment/insertion/miss rates), making even the partial comparison incomplete.

2. **EPR objective metrics conflict with one-to-many framing (impact -6.38).** The paper describes EPR as a "one-to-many mapping" (Section 3.2) — a given score can be rendered in multiple valid expressive ways. Yet the primary objective evaluation in Table 2 evaluates methods using MAE and KL divergence against a single human reference performance. This metric penalizes any valid alternative interpretation that differs from the reference. The variance metrics (σ²) in Table 2 partially address this concern by measuring distributional spread rather than reference fidelity, but the paper does not acknowledge the tension between its one-to-many framing and its distance-to-single-reference metrics. Without evidence that lower MAE/KL against one reference corresponds to perceptually better quality (rather than closer mimicry of that specific performance), these numbers are difficult to interpret as quality measures.

### Minor

3. **DExter variance dismissal lacks direct evidence (impact -0.30).** The paper claims DExter's higher variance (closer to human values for velocity variance) "results from unstable dynamics rather than meaningful expressiveness" (lines 235–236). The subjective listening test (Figure 2) does show DExter rated lower, which partially supports the broader claim, but the specific characterization of "unstable dynamics" is an attribution not directly evidenced by any analysis of DExter's outputs. The claim should either be supported or reframed more cautiously.

4. **PSR module evaluation lacks quantitative rigor (impact -0.02).** The PSR module — a stated contribution — is evaluated primarily through a qualitative visualization (Figure 4) where generated styles "closely mirror" real ones. No quantitative distributional metrics (e.g., Wasserstein distance, MMD) are reported to substantiate this claim. While subjective results (Figure 2) show Ours(PSR) outperforming baselines, this does not directly validate the distributional fidelity of PSR-generated styles against the real style distribution.

5. **Unpaired performance dataset reproducibility (impact -0.05).** The audio-to-MIDI transcription model used to create the unpaired YouTube performance dataset is not named (footnote 2 is empty), and video IDs are not provided. While this level of detail is not uncommon at submission time, the opacity around the transcription model is a concern for reproducibility.

6. **Subjective evaluation is modest in scale (impact -0.06).** The main listening test uses 11 participants and 5 pieces; the style transfer evaluation (Figure 5) does not report the number of listeners. Error bars are shown but no significance testing or inter-rater reliability statistics are reported. The results are directionally consistent across conditions, which mitigates this concern somewhat, but the sample sizes are at the low end for generalizable conclusions.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Train EPR-Only on the same total data (ASAP + unpaired) with masked reconstruction losses to cleanly isolate joint training effects.
- Adopt distributional evaluation metrics for EPR (e.g., Wasserstein distance between generated and human distributions) or explicitly reframe MAE/KL as faithfulness measures.
- Add quantitative distributional comparison for PSR (MMD or Wasserstein distance between real and PSR-generated style distributions).
- Disclose the audio-to-MIDI transcription model identity.
- Report inter-rater reliability (Fleiss' kappa or ICC) for subjective evaluations.
