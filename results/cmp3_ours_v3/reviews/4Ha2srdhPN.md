## Summary

GRAID proposes a framework for generating large-scale qualitative spatial VQA data using only 2D bounding boxes, bypassing 3D reconstruction. It generates 8.5M+ VQA pairs across three driving datasets (BDD100k, NuImages, Waymo) with 22 templates spanning spatial relations, counting, ranking, and size comparisons. Fine-tuning on GRAID data improves VLM spatial reasoning on held-out question types and cross-dataset settings, and the SPARQ predicate system achieves up to 1400× speedup.

## Strengths

1. **Clean, pragmatic method design (Section 3).** The core insight — determining qualitative spatial relationships from 2D bounding boxes alone, avoiding single-view 3D reconstruction errors — is well-motivated and directly addresses a known failure mode of prior work (SpatialVLM's [50%,200%] tolerance window for metric answers is a clear symptom of the problem GRAID avoids).

2. **Cross-dataset generalization evidence (RQ1, Section 5).** Training on GRAID-BDD and evaluating on GRAID-NuImages (different cities, scenes, objects, visual contexts) yields +29.1% accuracy improvement (38% → 67.1%). This is the most informative experiment — it cleanly shows that the model acquires transferable spatial concepts rather than memorizing dataset-specific patterns.

3. **Compositional primitive learning result (RQ2, Section 5).** Training on only 6 question types improves performance on over 10 held-out types across GRAID-BDD and GRAID-NuImages, with overall gains of +47.5 pp and +38.0 pp respectively. The finding that basic spatial concepts (left/right, counting, centeredness) compose into more complex reasoning supports the paper's thesis about spatial reasoning primitives.

4. **SPARQ speedup (Section 3.2).** The 1400× speedup on the heaviest templates through predicate pre-filtering (5.17ms predicate vs 46.95ms realization in RightOf) is a practical engineering contribution that makes the 8.5M-scale generation feasible.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The headline human evaluation comparison (91.16% vs 57.6%) conflates question type with framework quality.** GRAID generates **qualitative binary yes/no questions** (e.g., "Is there at least one traffic sign to the left of any truck?") while the SpatialVLM comparison generates **metric/quantitative questions** (e.g., "How far is X from Y?"). Binary questions are inherently easier to validate than metric ones requiring exact distances. The paper partially acknowledges this (Section 4, line 157: "rather than asking how far an object is in terms of metric distance, it's easier to answer which object is closer") but continues to present the raw 91.16% vs 57.6% comparison without this caveat in the abstract, introduction, and conclusion. A controlled comparison on the same question type is needed to attribute the gap to framework quality rather than question difficulty. *(Verified: abstract line 9, introduction line 53, Section 4 line 157, conclusion line 294)*

2. **Only one baseline comparison (OpenSpaces), and it is a community reimplementation, not the official dataset.** RQ3 compares against a single baseline generated by the community implementation of SpatialVLM, not the official 2B-pair dataset from the SpatialVLM authors. No comparison against SpaRE (listed in Table 1 but absent from experiments), no comparison against training on raw bounding boxes without templated questions, and no comparison against a matched-size random subset. A single non-official baseline cannot distinguish whether improvements come from GRAID's specific design or simply from the presence of any spatial training data. *(Verified: Section 5 RQ3, line 202)*

3. **The 32.5% improvement on A-OKVQA is extraordinary and unexplained.** A-OKVQA tests *general visual commonsense reasoning* (object attributes, scene understanding, human-object interactions, etc.). A 32.5% gain from fine-tuning exclusively on spatial reasoning data from driving scenes receives only a single sentence (lines 271-272) with no mechanism analysis, no breakdown by question category, no discussion of base model characteristics, and no ablation study. This either requires extensive analysis or should be heavily caveated. *(Verified: Section 5, lines 269-274)*

### Minor

1. **Several question types show no improvement or regression in RQ2.** In Figure 3, "Count less than threshold?" stays at 4%, "Rank top-k largest" stays at 14%, "Location by quadrants" stays at 10% after fine-tuning. The paper briefly attributes this to overfitting (line 200) but provides no analysis of why *only* these specific types degrade or whether this pattern is robust across random seeds. *(Verified: Figure 3 data in lines 226-240)*

2. **No statistical significance or variance reporting.** RQ1 reports point estimates (80.7%, 67.1%) without confidence intervals or multiple seeds. Given the small training budgets (200 steps), these numbers could vary substantially. *(Verified: Section 5 RQ1, line 198)*

3. **No analysis of what 2D-only spatial reasoning cannot capture.** The paper claims domain-agnosticity but does not discuss spatial concepts that 2D bounding boxes inherently cannot represent (occlusion reasoning, behind/in-front-of, relative 3D distance). Acknowledging these limitations would strengthen credibility. *(Verified: Section 3.1)*

4. **LoRA rank choices differ between experiments without explanation.** RQ1 uses rank 16, RQ2 uses rank 32, making it unclear whether different results across experiments are attributable to training configuration differences. *(Verified: RQ1 line 198, RQ2 line 200)*
