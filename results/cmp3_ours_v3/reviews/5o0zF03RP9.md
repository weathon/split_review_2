## Summary

This paper introduces IncentRL, which augments RL rewards with a KL divergence between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), weighted by β. The paper frames its central novelty as treating β as a Bayesian random variable that adapts online to eliminate manual tuning. Experiments are conducted on a 2-state toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8.

## Strengths

- **Conceptual framing of KL(p∥q) as an intrinsic reward.** The idea of penalizing misalignment between predicted and preferred outcomes occupies a distinct position from both KL-regularized RL (which constrains policy change) and active inference (which adapts preferences). This is intellectually appealing and could be actionable if the implementation were fully specified.

- **Toy experiment cleanly illustrates the mechanism.** The 2-state MDP (Section 5.2, Table 1, Figure 1) demonstrates the regime where the KL shaping term provides a dense exploration signal before sparse external rewards are received. Convergence curves are clear and variance reduction is tangible (16.57±11.30 → ~28.86±6.66 across 7 seeds).

- **Proposition 1 provides a basic sanity check.** Establishing that the optimal policy of the original MDP is preserved for sufficiently small β (Section 4.1) is not deep but is a reasonable verification that the shaping does not trivially break the original objective.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The paper is a complete submission with a coherent (if incomplete) narrative.

### Major

1. **The Bayesian β adaptation mechanism — the paper's central claimed novelty — is never specified.** The abstract (line 9) states "The central novelty is treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online to balance exploration and exploitation without manual tuning." The contribution list (line 21) states "Bayesian adaptation (novelty)." Yet no algorithmic specification is provided: no prior distribution on β, no likelihood function, no observation model, no update rule (conjugate, MCMC, variational, or heuristic). The algorithm outline in Section 5.1 (lines 149–155) lists five steps, none of which involve Bayesian inference. The heading "Bayesian β adaptation" (line 230) only accompanies descriptive accounts of posterior concentration. A reader cannot reproduce, evaluate, or build on the paper's core claimed contribution because the mechanism is not disclosed.

2. **The Bayesian β adaptation is not empirically validated on any non-trivial environment.** On MountainCar (Section 5.3), the Bayesian variant is not mentioned at all — only fixed-β sweeps are reported. On MiniGrid (Section 5.4), the paper states (line 228): "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value." This is an unsupported assertion, not evidence. The only shown Bayesian results (Figures 3–4, Table 3) are from a setting where the target β value (≈0.1) is unclear — the figure captions mention β≈0.1 (aligned with MountainCar's best fixed β) but the subsection sits within the MiniGrid section where β=0.01 was used. Moreover, the toy environment (Table 1) shows that any β>0 achieves nearly identical performance (28.00–28.86), so posterior concentration there demonstrates nothing about whether the mechanism selects a useful β on tasks where it matters. The paper's headline claim — that Bayesian adaptation "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning" — is therefore unsupported by the experiments as presented.

3. **The predicted outcome distribution p(o|s,a) is unspecified for all non-toy experiments.** The method's signal depends entirely on computing KL(p(o|s,a)∥q(o|s)), yet for MountainCar and MiniGrid the paper never states: what model produces p(o|s,a) (a learned neural-network forward model? environment dynamics?)? What architecture and loss function are used? What is the outcome space o? For MountainCar, q(o|s) is described as "assigning all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)" (line 199) — a condition on a continuous state, not a well-defined distribution. Without specifying p(o|s,a), the method is underspecified and the experiments are unreproducible.

4. **No comparisons to existing exploration or intrinsic motivation methods.** The paper benchmarks only against its own β=0.0 baseline (standard RL without the KL shaping term). The Related Work (Section 2) discusses curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al. 2017, Burda et al. 2018), empowerment (Mohamed & Rezende 2015), and information gain (Houthooft et al. 2016) as closely related approaches, but none are compared against. The MiniGrid results (β=0.01: 98% success vs. β=0.0: 90.5%) show that *some* intrinsic reward helps compared to none — which is already well-established — but do not establish that IncentRL's specific KL(p∥q) formulation is competitive with or preferable to existing intrinsic reward methods.

### Minor

5. **Low statistical power.** MountainCar and MiniGrid experiments use 3 seeds each. The β=0.3 MountainCar result has mean 1654.67 with std 908.94 (coefficient of variation 55%), making it essentially noise. No statistical testing (e.g., bootstrapped confidence intervals) is reported. Combined with the absence of comparisons to existing methods, the empirical evidence is weaker than the ICLR standard for a method contribution.

6. **Neural architecture and hyperparameters not reported.** The paper states "the same neural architecture and training hyperparameters across conditions" (line 224) but never reports what they are — number of layers, hidden units, learning rate, optimizer, exploration schedule, target network update frequency, etc. This impairs reproducibility.
