## Summary

PolicyFlow proposes an on-policy RL algorithm that integrates continuous normalizing flow (CNF) policies with PPO-style clipped objectives. The key technical innovation is an approximation of the importance ratio that uses velocity field variations along a linear interpolation path (Eqs. 9–10), avoiding costly ODE backpropagation during training while only requiring ODE simulation during sampling. The paper also proposes a Brownian-inspired entropy regularizer to encourage exploration and mitigate mode collapse. Experiments on MuJoCo Playground (against FPO, DPPO, PPO), IsaacLab (against PPO), and a MultiGoal environment provide partial support for the method.

---

## Strengths

1. **Clever and well-motivated importance ratio approximation.** The core idea — replacing the integrated ODE displacement with an expectation over velocity field variations along an interpolation path (Eqs. 9–10) — correctly identifies the key bottleneck in applying CNF policies to on-policy RL and sidesteps it in an elegant and practical way. This is the paper's strongest contribution and is genuinely novel.

2. **Good breadth of ablation studies.** Sections 5.3–5.5 provide sensitivity analyses for clipping range, network initialization, time-sampling strategy, and interpolation path choice. These are informative and demonstrate that the method is not brittle to design choices. The interpolation path comparison (Table 3) goes beyond what is standard.

3. **Transparent computational cost reporting.** Table 2 provides per-iteration timing on an RTX 5090 across 8 IsaacLab environments, showing PolicyFlow is at most ~2× slower than PPO. Such reporting is uncommon and adds credibility.

4. **MultiGoal qualitative results are visually compelling.** Figure 2 shows a clear progression from PPO → DPPO/FPO → PolicyFlow with the Brownian regularizer, with increasingly multimodal coverage. This provides the most striking evidence that the CNF policy combined with entropy regularization yields genuinely different behavior from Gaussian policies.

---

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The Brownian regularizer's theoretical grounding is overclaimed.** The paper calls the regularizer "principled" (lines 24, 50, 226) and derives it using the score-velocity relation for rectified flows (Eq. 14 from Liu et al., 2025). However, Eq. 14 describes the *true* score of the probability path generated by a velocity field that is, by construction, the rectified flow velocity field. In PolicyFlow, the velocity field is an arbitrary neural network trained by RL gradients — there is no guarantee the induced distribution's score satisfies Eq. 14. The paper acknowledges this in a Remark (line 228: "the velocity field in our policy is not obtained via flow matching gradients, and thus does not strictly correspond to the rectified flow dynamics"), but the surrounding text continues to present the regularizer as principled. The regularizer may still work as a useful heuristic (it encourages the velocity field to stay close to a reference in a weighted norm), but the paper's framing inconsistently oscillates between claiming theoretical grounding and disclaiming it. This tension should be resolved by honestly framing the regularizer as a heuristic.

2. **IsaacLab benchmarks lack flow-based baselines, but the abstract claims superiority over them across all environments.** The abstract states PolicyFlow "achieves competitive or superior performance compared to... flow-based baselines including FPO and DPPO" across all experiments. On IsaacLab, however, FPO and DPPO are absent. The paper explains (lines 286–287) that the JAX implementations could not be directly compared to the PyTorch implementation, but this still means the headline claim is unsupported by a significant fraction of the evaluation. On IsaacLab (Table 1), PolicyFlow matches or slightly exceeds PPO on 4 of 8 tasks with statistical significance on only 2 (Navigation, G1; p < 0.05), while PPO is numerically ahead on 4 tasks and statistically significantly ahead on H1 (p = 0.0069). Without flow-based baselines here, the IsaacLab results only show that a CNF policy roughly matches a Gaussian one — they do not show improvement over flow-based alternatives.

### Minor

3. **MultiGoal comparison confounds architecture choice with presence of entropy regularization.** FPO and DPPO are compared without *any* entropy regularization, while PolicyFlow uses the Brownian regularizer. The paper attributes their mode collapse to the "lack of an effective entropy regularization mechanism" (line 250), which means the comparison conflates two interventions: the CNF architecture and the specific regularizer. Figure 2(d,e) shows that even simpler forms of regularization (uniform noise injection, Gaussian entropy) substantially improve PolicyFlow's coverage over FPO/DPPO, suggesting the main driver may simply be having *some* entropy regularization rather than the specific Brownian formulation. Adding Gaussian entropy regularization to FPO/DPPO would isolate the benefit of the specific regularizer.

4. **No statistical significance tests on MuJoCo Playground results.** Table 1 provides p-values for IsaacLab, but the MuJoCo Playground results (Figure 3) are shown only as learning curves without formal hypothesis tests or final-score tables. Given that many advantages claimed are qualitative ("converges faster," "outperforming DPPO"), formal tests would strengthen the evidence.

5. **The importance ratio uses a conditional (single-$\mathbf{z}$) approximation without discussing added variance.** The derivation from Eq. 8 to Eq. 10 uses the conditional likelihood ratio $\pi(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{s})/\bar{\pi}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{z},\mathbf{s})$ rather than the full marginal ratio $\pi(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})/\bar{\pi}(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{s})$, implicitly using a single-sample Monte Carlo approximation. The $\mathbf{z}$ marginalization means the ratio depends on which latent was sampled, introducing additional variance that is not discussed.

6. **No ablation of the Brownian regularizer on MuJoCo Playground tasks.** The regularizer is ablated on MultiGoal but not on the main benchmark tasks. It is unclear how much of the MuJoCo Playground improvement comes from the CNF architecture itself vs. the regularizer.

7. **Single-environment ablation studies.** Sections 5.4 (initialization, time sampling) are conducted on a single environment each (ANYmal-D, Navigation). The limited generality should be noted.

### Trivial

8. **Notation inconsistency in Eq. 11** (line 126): Uses $\hat{\varphi}_1$ where earlier text uses $\bar{\varphi}_1$, and uses $\hat{\sigma}^2$ in the denominator where Eq. 10 uses $\sigma^2$ throughout.

9. **Algorithm 1 asymmetry between numerator and denominator variances** (line 181): The numerator uses $\sigma^2$ (current) while the denominator uses $\hat{\sigma}^2$ (reference). This asymmetry is not discussed.

10. **Table 3 results on ANYmal-D show essentially identical performance across interpolation paths** (all within one standard error), weakening the claim that path choice matters.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Re-center the contribution.** The importance ratio approximation is the paper's most novel and well-supported idea. Framing the Brownian regularizer explicitly as a heuristic complement (consistent with the Remark) rather than as a co-equal "principled" contribution would resolve the current tension in the paper's narrative.
- **Add FPO/DPPO baselines to IsaacLab.** Even a rough comparison using the JAX implementations on corresponding environments (with appropriate framework caveats) would substantially strengthen the evidence. Alternatively, providing a PyTorch re-implementation of FPO's objective would enable fair comparison.
- **Disentangle the MultiGoal comparison.** Adding Gaussian entropy regularization to FPO and DPPO would isolate whether the benefit comes from having any entropy regularization versus the specific Brownian formulation.
- **Discuss the variance of the importance ratio estimator.** Since the ratio uses a single $\mathbf{z}$ sample and a single $t$ sample, its variance could be high and should be analyzed.
- **Characterize the interaction between $\sigma$ optimization and the importance ratio approximation.** The ratio depends on $\sigma$ in both numerator and denominator (Eq. 13), so updating $\sigma$ changes the ratio in ways unrelated to the policy mean.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's most useful observation is that the paper's theoretical framing of the Brownian regularizer is in tension with its own Remark — the score-velocity relation (Eq. 14) holds for rectified flows by construction but not for an RL-trained velocity field, so the claimed connection to entropy growth via the heat equation is severed. This is a structural issue that the paper acknowledges but does not resolve. Additionally, the critic correctly identifies that the MultiGoal comparison conflates architecture and regularizer, meaning the headline qualitative result may be driven more by the presence of *any* entropy regularization than by the specific Brownian formulation. Beyond these two points, the insights in the review largely recapitulate observations the paper itself makes (computational efficiency, the Remark's caveat, the limitation of single-environment ablations).

---

## Suggestions

1. Resolve the tension between the "principled" framing and the Remark by explicitly re-framing the Brownian regularizer as a heuristic with a clear physics-inspired motivation but no exact theoretical guarantees.
2. Add at least a subset of FPO/DPPO comparisons on IsaacLab — even on 2–3 environments — to support the claim that PolicyFlow improves over flow-based baselines across all benchmarks.
3. Add statistical significance tests (e.g., p-values or effect sizes) to the MuJoCo Playground results.
4. Add an ablation of the Brownian regularizer on at least one MuJoCo Playground task to disentangle architecture effects from regularization effects.
5. Clean up the notational inconsistencies in Eqs. 10–11 and Algorithm 1 ($\bar{\varphi}_1$ vs. $\hat{\varphi}_1$, $\sigma^2$ vs. $\hat{\sigma}^2$).
