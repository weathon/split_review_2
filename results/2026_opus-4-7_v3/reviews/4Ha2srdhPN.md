## Summary
GRAID is a framework for generating spatial reasoning VQA data from images using only 2D bounding box outputs from object detectors, avoiding error-prone single-view 3D reconstruction. It includes SPARQ, a predicate-based early-rejection scheme for efficient question generation (up to 1400× speedups). Applied to BDD100k, NuImages, and Waymo, it produces 8.5M+ VQA pairs. Human evaluation shows 91.16% validity on 317 pairs. Fine-tuning experiments across four VLMs demonstrate that spatial primitives transfer to held-out question types and external benchmarks.

## Strengths

- **Sound core insight with effective motivation.** The observation that qualitative spatial relationships can be reliably determined from 2D bounding box geometry alone avoids cascading errors from 3D reconstruction. Figure 1 concretely illustrates the problem: SpatialVLM claims a clock tower and bus are "61.0 centimeters apart," which is obviously wrong. The contrast with GRAID's clean, verifiable output is compelling. (Section 1, Figure 1)

- **Compositional transfer finding (RQ2, Figure 3).** Training on only 6 question types (LeftOf, RightOf, HowMany, AreMore, LargestAppearance, IsObjectCentered) and observing improvements across 19 question types on an entirely unseen dataset (GRAID-NuImages, +38.0pp overall) is the paper's most distinctive experimental finding. It provides genuine evidence that basic spatial primitives compose into broader spatial reasoning ability rather than template memorization. (Section 5, Figure 3)

- **Breadth of downstream evaluation (RQ3).** Testing across four VLMs (Llama 3.2 11B, Gemma 3 4B, Qwen2.5 VL 3B, Qwen3 VL 8B) on five external benchmarks (BLINK, NaturalBench, A-OKVQA, RealWorldQA, VSR) provides substantial evidence of practical utility. The +15.94% BLINK overall improvement and +32.5% A-OKVQA improvement with Llama are notable. (Section 5, lines 269–284)

- **SPARQ is a practical engineering contribution.** The predicate-based early rejection scheme is well-explained (Algorithm 1), with concrete timing analysis: 5.17ms predicate vs. 46.95ms realization, and up to 1407× speedup on LargestAppearance templates. The predicate success → realization success rate of 78.8% demonstrates the approach's effectiveness. (Section 3.2)

- **Framework extensibility and scale.** Supporting three detector backends (Detectron2, MMDetection, Ultralytics) with a standard interface, plus the ability to bring labeled data or custom detectors, makes the framework practical for adoption. The 8.5M+ pairs across three datasets demonstrate this scalability. (Section 3.1, Table 2)

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None

### Major

- **Central comparison against community reimplementation, not the original SpatialVLM system.** The headline comparison—91.16% vs. 57.6% validity—is against "OpenSpaces," a community implementation of SpatialVLM, not the authors' original pipeline. Table 1 confirms SpatialVLM's authors did not release code ("Open-source implementation by authors: ✗"). A community reimplementation may introduce its own bugs or use inferior depth models. The paper then draws a causal claim that this data quality gap explains SpaceLLaVA's poor benchmark performance (Section 4, line 182: "Our results suggest that the poor quality of the data generated by the community implementation of SpatialVLM... is a primary contributor to this performance gap"), which is not fully warranted when comparing against a potentially unfaithful reimplementation. This weakens the paper's strongest quality claim, though it does not invalidate the framework's independent contributions.

- **No statistical reporting across experiments.** All results in RQ1–RQ3 appear to be single runs with no error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests. LoRA fine-tuning on subsets (e.g., 200 steps on 10% of data in RQ1) can be sensitive to random seeds, making variance reporting especially important. The human evaluation (317 pairs, 4 evaluators for GRAID; 250 pairs, unspecified evaluators for OpenSpaces) reports no inter-annotator agreement (e.g., Cohen's kappa). The reported precision of "91.16%" overstates the reliability given the sample size—the 95% confidence interval around this estimate would span approximately ±3 percentage points.

### Minor

- **Scope framing slightly overreaches relative to contribution.** The introduction cites medical imaging, robotics, and autonomous vehicles, positioning GRAID as improving VLMs' "spatial reasoning" broadly. However, the 22 templates (Figure 2, Appendix A.1) are restricted to relationships derivable from 2D bounding box geometry: left/right, above/below, counting, size comparison by pixel area, and localization. The paper does include an italicized footnote acknowledging this (line 53: "the 22 templates we implement are merely to demonstrate GRAID's effectiveness as a framework"), but the abstract does not reflect this qualification. A more precise framing as "2D spatial relationship reasoning from bounding boxes" would better match the actual scope.

- **IoU=0 filtering constraint unanalyzed.** Algorithm 1 (line 127) requires IoU(b₁, b₂) = 0 for spatial relation questions. In cluttered scenes—common in driving—many legitimate spatial relationships between partially overlapping objects would be filtered out. The paper does not quantify how much data this discards or whether it introduces bias toward spatially sparse scenes, which could affect the distribution of spatial configurations the model learns.

- **Regression on counting questions unexplained.** In RQ2, regressions on LessThanThresholdHowMany and MoreThanThresholdHowMany are attributed to "overfitting" (line 200), but with only 200 training steps and "a fraction of an epoch," overfitting seems unlikely. An alternative explanation—that the answer distribution of training questions biases the model's counting priors—is more plausible but not investigated. The paper does not report answer distributions for any question type.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Deeper analysis of RQ2 compositional transfer.** Currently RQ2 is a single bar chart. Characterizing *which* held-out question types benefit most and *why*—specifically, whether shared geometric primitives (e.g., x-coordinate comparison shared between LeftOf and RightOf) predict transfer—would elevate this from an interesting observation to an actionable insight about spatial reasoning curricula.

- **Multi-seed experiments.** Running 3–5 seeds for RQ3 and reporting means ± standard deviations would address the variance concern, particularly given the light LoRA fine-tuning regime.

- **Answer distribution analysis.** Reporting the balance of yes/no answers, counting answer distributions, etc. would help rule out answer-prior confounds in the SFT improvements.

- **Quantify IoU=0 filtering impact.** Report what fraction of potential object pairs are discarded by the non-overlap constraint.

## Novel Insights

The RQ2 finding—that training on six basic spatial primitives (left/right positional comparison, counting, size ranking, centering) transfers to 19 held-out question types including entirely new cognitive categories (Size & Aspect) and a completely unseen dataset (NuImages)—is a genuinely novel empirical observation about spatial reasoning compositionality in VLMs. This aligns with but extends prior work by Tang et al. (2025b) on simulated-to-real spatial reasoning transfer, and suggests that spatial reasoning may be more compositional than previously assumed, with a small basis set of geometric primitives sufficient to bootstrap broader spatial understanding.

## Suggestions

1. **Fair baseline comparison.** Apply the published SpatialVLM pipeline (depth estimation → point cloud → metric measurement) to the same BDD/NuImages images using best-available depth models, then compare data quality head-to-head. This isolates the methodological difference from implementation quality.
2. **Report inter-annotator agreement** for the human evaluation (Cohen's kappa or Fleiss' kappa across the 4 evaluators).
3. **Tighten abstract framing** to match the actual scope: "2D spatial relationship reasoning from bounding boxes" rather than broad "spatial reasoning."
4. **Analyze RQ2 transfer patterns** to identify which geometric primitives predict cross-type transfer, converting the observation into an actionable curriculum design principle.
5. **Report answer distributions** across question types to rule out answer-prior confounds.
