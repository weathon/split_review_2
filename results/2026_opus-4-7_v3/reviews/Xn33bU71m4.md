## Summary
This paper benchmarks nine mid-sized open-source LLMs (12B–16B) on two reverse engineering tasks — name recovery and type inference — from stripped binaries. It evaluates models under zero-shot and LoRA fine-tuned conditions across four ISAs, four optimization levels, and two decompilers. The main finding is negative: all models achieve near-zero F1 scores, with fine-tuning providing modest gains for name recovery but minimal (or harmful) impact on type inference.

## Strengths
- **Genuine gap and breadth of conditions**: The motivation is real — prior RE papers (SYMGEN selecting CodeLlama-34B, RESYM selecting StarCoder-3B) choose LLMs without justification (Section 2). The evaluation matrix is substantial: 9 models × 4 ISAs × 4 optimization levels × 2 decompilers × zero-shot/fine-tuned, covering 40,000 functions.
- **CodeWordNet ablation (Figure 1)**: The comparison of name recovery with and without CodeWordNet demonstrates that semantic similarity metrics matter for fair evaluation, showing models often generate semantically similar but not string-identical names. This is a useful methodological observation for the subfield.
- **Real-world firmware evaluation (Table 6)**: Testing on stripped real-world firmware with both Ghidra and IDA Pro goes beyond synthetic benchmarks and adds practical validity. The observation that IDA Pro outputs generally yield slightly better LLM performance is a concrete and useful finding.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None

### Major
1. **Internal numerical inconsistency between Figure 1 and Table 2** — Figure 1 reports function name recovery F1 with CodeWordNet on x86-64 as ~0.15 for CodeLlama at O0 and ~0.25 for Qwen3 at O0 (approximate values from the bar charts at lines 331–348). Table 2 reports the same condition (CodeLlama original, x86-64, O0) as 0.04, and Qwen3 as 0.05. Both are described as function name recovery with CodeWordNet on x86-64 architecture (Section 4.3: "restricted to the x86-64 architecture"; Table 2 header: "x86-64"). The 3–5× discrepancy is never acknowledged or explained, and undermines confidence in the reported numbers. For a benchmarking paper whose contribution depends entirely on the trustworthiness of reported metrics, this is a serious problem.

2. **Unvalidated auxiliary LLM in the evaluation pipeline** — Section 3.2 states: "we employ an auxiliary LLM to post process outputs... producing a normalized result in a consistent schema." The paper does not identify which auxiliary LLM is used, does not validate its accuracy, and does not report its error rate. If this component systematically misparses one model's output format more than another's, it biases all comparisons. A benchmarking paper whose contribution is fair comparison must validate every component of its comparison pipeline.

3. **Single GPT-3.5 prompt conflates prompt suitability with model capability** — Section 3.2 explicitly states the prompt is "an established reverse engineering prompt" from a GPT-3.5 prompt engineering guide. Different models have different instruction-following conventions, system prompt expectations, and chat templates. The paper itself demonstrates this problem: Section 4.5 notes DeepSeek-R1 "frequently generated a chain of thought reasoning... so long that the model failed to return the final output" and produced refusals. These are prompt-model mismatch issues, not model capability issues. The paper is therefore measuring "how well do these LLMs respond to one GPT-3.5 prompt" rather than "how good are these LLMs at reverse engineering" — a substantially weaker claim than the paper makes.

4. **Fine-tuning systematically degrades type inference without investigation** — Table 4 shows that after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-V2, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen2.5, Qwen3, WizardCoder, Phi4, and Gemma3 all drop to 0.00 F1 on most optimization levels. When fine-tuning consistently destroys performance across 7 of 9 models, the most parsimonious explanation is a problem with the training procedure (e.g., catastrophic forgetting, inappropriate data format), not a meaningful finding about the task. The paper presents Finding 2 ("Fine-tuning has limited impact on type inference") without investigating why.

### Minor
1. **Near-zero results limit discriminative power** — Most cells in Tables 2–5 range from 0.00 to 0.06. The paper claims "Qwen3 achieves the best function name recovery with an F1 score of 0.06" (Section 4.4, line 263), but the difference from most other models (0.03–0.05) is 0.01–0.03. Without any statistical analysis — confidence intervals, multiple runs, or significance tests — and given the acknowledged randomness (Section 5: "The number will be similar, but not identical, due to randomness of LLMs"), these differences are likely within noise. This limits the benchmark's ability to guide model selection, which is its stated purpose.

2. **Introduction claims an F1 of 0.37 for DeepSeek-R1 not supported in main-body tables** — The Introduction (line 19) states: "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." The highest fine-tuned F1 for DeepSeek-R1 in any main-body table is 0.17 (variable name recovery at O0, Table 3). While this number may appear in appendix results for other architectures, the discrepancy between the headline claim and visible results should have been reconciled in the main text.

3. **Findings are generic and well-established** — Findings 1 ("Fine-tuning generally improves... but gains are inconsistent"), 4 ("Larger model size does not guarantee better performance"), 5 ("Training data quality significantly impacts performance"), and 6 ("Larger variants... do not necessarily yield better results") are well-known observations in the LLM literature. They do not require this specific experimental setup to establish and contribute limited new insight. A benchmarking paper earns its contribution through the depth and novelty of its insights, not the volume of experiments.

4. **No comparison with specialized RE tools** — The paper does not compare against DIRTY, VARBERT, SYMLM, or RESYM, which report substantially higher F1 on the same tasks. Contextualizing general-purpose LLM performance against these would significantly increase the paper's informativeness. While the paper scopes itself to LLM-vs-LLM comparison, even a reference comparison would strengthen the motivation.

## Novel Insights
The CodeWordNet ablation provides a methodological contribution to the reverse engineering evaluation subfield: it demonstrates concretely that strict string matching severely underestimates LLM name-recovery performance, since models often produce semantically similar but lexically different identifiers. The observation that fine-tuning consistently destroys type inference (Table 4), if confirmed as a genuine phenomenon rather than a training bug, would be a noteworthy negative result about LoRA fine-tuning under strict-match evaluation regimes.

## Suggestions
1. Resolve the numerical discrepancy between Figure 1 and Table 2 — either explain what accounts for the 3–5× difference or correct the error.
2. Validate the auxiliary LLM post-processing component: identify the model used, measure its parsing accuracy on held-out examples, and show it does not systematically bias model comparisons.
3. Investigate why fine-tuning degrades type inference across 7 of 9 models — is it catastrophic forgetting, training data format mismatch, or something else? This could become a genuinely useful finding.
4. Use each model's recommended prompt template, or at minimum run an ablation demonstrating prompt sensitivity per model.
5. Add statistical significance testing (multiple runs with different seeds) to support fine-grained model comparisons at the 0.01–0.02 F1 level.
6. Include at least a reference comparison with one specialized RE tool (e.g., SYMLM) to contextualize LLM performance.
