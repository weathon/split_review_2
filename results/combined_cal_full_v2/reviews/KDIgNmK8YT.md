Score: 5.0, Decision: Reject## Summary

WorldAlignment introduces a benchmark for evaluating LLM alignment with human preferences across three domains: instruction following, mathematical reasoning, and code generation. It extends AlpacaEval 2.0's length-controlled win-rate methodology with a domain-aware logistic regression and evaluates 7 state-of-the-art models across these domains. The benchmark provides useful empirical findings about domain-specific model performance and the interaction between alignment methods (DPO, SimPO) and model architectures.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated gap.** Existing human-preference benchmarks (AlpacaEval 2.0, MT-Bench, WildBench) focus predominantly on general instruction-following and do not systematically cover domain-specific expertise in math and code. This motivation is clearly articulated and concrete (Section 1, lines 134–148).

- **Sound length-control methodology.** The paper extends AlpacaEval 2.0's length-controlled win-rate framework with a domain-aware logistic regression. The dual-metric approach (WR vs. LC) reveals that length bias is a real confound in multi-domain evaluation (e.g., O3-Mini's high WR but much lower LC across domains, Table 1). The use of two different judges (GPT-4o and GPT-4.1-Mini) provides some consistency check.

- **Informative empirical findings.** The evaluation reveals non-obvious patterns: (a) SimPO vs. DPO effectiveness varies by architecture (SimPO works better on Gemma, worse on Llama for math/code, Figure 5); (b) most models including recent open-source ones underperform GPT-4.1 on math and code alignment (Table 1); (c) the gap between WR and LC varies substantially across domains. These results are genuinely useful for practitioners.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **No human validation of the core claim.** The paper is framed as a "human preference benchmark" (title, abstract, line 162: "approximates human preference") but provides **no correlation analysis** between WorldAlignment scores and actual human judgments. AlpacaEval 2.0 — the paper's primary comparison point — is validated through a Spearman correlation of 0.98 with Chatbot Arena. Without equivalent evidence, it is unclear whether WorldAlignment measures human-aligned preferences or simply agreement with GPT-4o's own stylistic preferences on GPT-4o-generated data. This gap between the paper's framing and the evidence presented is the most significant weakness.

- **GPT-4o-centric pipeline creates circularity concerns.** Three components involve GPT-4o: the dataset is generated by GPT-4o (line 178), the baseline responses are from GPT-4o (Section 4.1), and the primary judge and quality/difficulty/feasibility assessor are all GPT-4o (Sections 3.2.2, 4.1). Models that share stylistic features with GPT-4o may be systematically favored regardless of human preference. The secondary judge (GPT-4.1-Mini) is from the same model family, so shared evaluation heuristics are likely.

- **Small sample sizes in domain-specific analysis undermine conclusions.** Table 2 reports results on five domains with samples as small as N=27 (engineering). With N=27, the standard error for a 50% win rate is ~9.6 percentage points, making model-to-model comparisons unreliable. The paper draws concrete conclusions about domain-specific model strengths from these noisy estimates (Section 4.4, lines 344–350).

### Minor

- **Ceiling effect in quality scores raises questions about preference signal.** Figure 3c shows quality scores with μ=9.95/10, concentrated almost entirely between 9 and 10. The paper itself notes this (line 208). If both responses in preference pairs are rated near-perfect by GPT-4o, the preference discriminations may reflect small stylistic differences rather than meaningful alignment quality differences.

- **Ambiguous notation in Equation 2.** The term `d((ψ_m − ψ_b)γ)` uses domain `d` in an unclear way — it is not specified whether `d` is a scalar, indicator, or learned vector, nor how domain modulates the prompt-difficulty contribution. The equation cannot be unambiguously implemented from the description alone (line 220).

- **No inter-evaluator agreement analysis.** The paper uses two judges (GPT-4o and GPT-4.1-Mini) but never reports their agreement rate (Spearman correlation, Cohen's κ, or similar). The two judges give notably different absolute scores (e.g., GPT-4.1-Mini consistently rates models higher on code, Table 1), and the paper notes this in passing without quantifying it.

### Trivial

- The conclusion (lines 352–354) does not discuss the absence of human validation or other limitations, which is a notable omission for a benchmark paper.

- The claim of being "the first comprehensive, multi-aspect evaluation benchmark" (line 142) could be more carefully qualified — the novelty is specifically in preference data for specialized domains, not in evaluating math/code capability per se, for which established capability benchmarks already exist.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A human correlation study (even on a subset of a few hundred samples) would substantially strengthen the paper's central claim and is the single most impactful improvement possible.
- Publishing the dataset and evaluation code would increase the benchmark's utility to the community.
- A weaker baseline (e.g., a smaller open-source model) instead of GPT-4o could give better resolution for distinguishing among lower-performing models.
- Adding a general discussion of limitations (the GPT-4o-centric design, lack of human validation) in the conclusion would improve the paper's scholarly credibility.

If the correlation turns out lower, that is itself an important finding worth reporting.
