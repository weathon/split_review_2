## Summary

This paper introduces the Open Proof Corpus (OPC), the first large-scale, open-sourced, human-validated dataset of LLM-generated mathematical proofs at the Olympiad level. The OPC comprises 5,062 human-evaluated proofs across 1,010 problems from prestigious competitions (IMO, USAMO, Putnam, etc.), generated by six state-of-the-art LLMs and graded by 13 expert judges. Using the OPC, the paper addresses three questions: (1) the gap between informal and formal proof generation, (2) the relationship between final-answer accuracy and proof correctness, and (3) best-of-n selection for proof quality. A fine-tuned 8B model (OPC-R1-8B) achieves 88.1% proof-judging accuracy, matching Gemini-2.5-Pro.

## Strengths

- **The OPC fills a genuine, well-motivated gap.** Prior resources were tiny (Petrov et al.: 6 problems), used outdated models, lacked open-sourcing, or had few correct proofs. The OPC — ~5,000 human-evaluated proofs across ~1,000 Olympiad-level problems from multiple SOTA LLMs — is meaningfully larger and more diverse than any existing resource. (§1, §4)
- **Rigorous data-collection methodology.** The use of 13 highly qualified judges (former IMO participants), a structured pilot phase with ~35% double-grading, ongoing calibration of instructions, ~10% overall double-grading with 90.4% agreement, and a coordinator to resolve discrepancies demonstrates a serious approach to annotation quality. The estimated individual judge error rate of 5% is credibly low for this task. (§3.3, §4)
- **Demonstrated utility via fine-tuning.** Training R1-QWEN3-8B on the OPC with GRPO achieves 88.1% judgment accuracy, matching GEMINI-2.5-PRO and approaching GPT-5's 90.8%, providing concrete evidence that the dataset is useful for downstream training, not just static analysis. (§5.2, Table 2)
- **Honest limitations and contamination analysis.** The paper explicitly acknowledges distributional shift for OPC-R1-8B (§5.2), potential contamination with a clever ground-truth solution experiment (§5.6, Table 4), a bug in the Rank (Swiss) implementation (footnote 1), and the exclusion of GPT-5 and GROK-4 as proof generators (§6). This transparency is above the standard for this kind of work.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The core contribution — the OPC dataset — is solid and not undermined by any of the following issues.

### Major

1. **The MathArena proof-correctness numbers in Fig. 5 are conditional on correct final answers but presented as unconditional "proof correctness."** The paper states that for the MathArena subset it "only retained solutions with a correct final answer" (§3.1) and "first collect instances where models generate correct final answers, and then manually evaluate the validity of the accompanying proofs" (§5.4). This means the reported numbers (e.g., 77.6% for Gemini-Pro) are P(correct proof | correct final answer), not P(correct proof). The figure labels them as "Pcorrect proof" without this qualification. The unconditional rates would be substantially lower — for o3: 87.6% × 59.5% = 52.1% rather than the reported 59.5%. The paper's conclusion that final-answer accuracy is a poor proxy for proof correctness is still valid, but the magnitude of the gap is significantly understated. The paper is transparent about the methodology in the text, so this is a presentation/evaluation framing issue, but it directly affects how a central result is interpreted.

### Minor

2. **The informal-vs-formal comparison headline ("informal solves 4x more problems") compares fundamentally different verification standards** — human judgment (which can tolerate minor omissions) against automated formal verification (all-or-nothing). While the paper is transparent about the setup and this comparison has inherent value as a head-to-head of two paradigms, the headline framing does not adequately communicate these differences. The paper also appends the final answer to the problem statement for informal models to "mirror the setup for formal models" (§3.1), which may create an informational advantage for problems where the final answer is a derived result rather than the statement itself. The claim is directionally correct, but the evidence does not cleanly isolate a pure capability gap.

3. **Adaptive problem selection shaped the dataset's difficulty distribution.** The paper states: "more problems from international competitions were added when initial results indicated that models were performing very well (§3.1)." This means aggregate accuracy numbers like "43% correct proofs" (§1) reflect a curated distribution actively tuned to maintain ~50% difficulty, not absolute model performance on these competitions generally.

4. **Three of the most active judges had prior experience evaluating LLM-generated proofs** (§3.1), but the paper does not examine whether this experience correlates with stricter or more lenient grading compared to judges without such experience. This is a subtle but unexamined confound in the annotation process.

5. **The best-of-n analysis shows that all automated selection methods underperform simple random sampling with human evaluation** (Pass@n reaches ~54% at n=8 vs. Rank (Swiss) at ~47%), yet the paper focuses on improvement over pass@1 rather than discussing this gap to the human-guided baseline, which is arguably the more striking finding about current limitations of automated selection.

6. **The contamination experiment for judging (§5.6, Table 4)** tests whether providing the ground-truth solution during *judging* affects accuracy. However, this tests a different contamination mechanism than the possibility that models may have memorized problem solutions during training and applied them during *reasoning/generation*. The paper correctly scopes the experiment to judging capabilities, but the caveat is larger than implied.

7. **The human baseline (90.4%) is measured on all double-graded proofs rather than on the exact test set.** The paper argues this is acceptable because test samples are uniformly drawn from the OPC (§5.2), but this is a heuristic argument rather than a rigorous guarantee that the double-graded and single-graded subsets have identical difficulty distributions.

### Trivial
None.
