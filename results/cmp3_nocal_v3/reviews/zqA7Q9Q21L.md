## Summary
This paper presents R2PS, an approach to real-time pursuit strategies under partial observability and varying graph structures. It extends a dynamic programming (DP) algorithm for Markov pursuit-evasion games to handle asynchronous evader moves with a clean theoretical proof (Theorem 2, Corollary 1), proposes a belief preservation mechanism (Equations 4–7) to maintain a distribution over possible evader positions under partial observability, and embeds this into the EPG cross-graph reinforcement learning framework to train a GNN-based pursuer policy that can generalize zero-shot to unseen graphs with O(n²m) inference time.

## Strengths

1. **Theoretical extension of DP to asynchronous moves (Section 3.1, Theorem 2, Corollary 1).** The paper proves that the distance table D generated by Algorithm 1 yields strictly optimal strategies when the evader moves asynchronously (after seeing the pursuers' action). This is a non-trivial result — the DP algorithm was designed for synchronous moves, and showing it handles asynchronous moves without recomputation is a genuine theoretical contribution.

2. **Belief preservation mechanism (Section 3.2, Equations 4–7).** Maintaining both a crisp set Pos and a belief distribution over it with Õ(|V|) update cost is a clean, practical approach to partial observability that avoids the exponential explosion of full POSG formulations. Table 1 shows Dp_belief consistently beats Dp_Pos (e.g., 0.78 vs 0.59 on Grid Map), demonstrating the value of belief averaging over the minimax set-based approach.

3. **Complexity analysis and real-time viability (Section 4.2).** The O(n²m) inference bound for the RL policy versus Õ(n^{m+1}) for DP recomputation is compelling, backed by concrete numbers (2+ minutes DP on CPU vs. <0.01 seconds GPU for the RL policy on n=1000, m=2).

4. **Comprehensive evaluation across diverse graphs.** The 10 test graphs (Grid Map, Scotland-Yard, Downtown, and 7 real-world landmarks) provide meaningful variety in topology, diameter, and degree. Multiple opponent types (Stay, DP_sync, DP_async, BR_async), scalability tests on larger graphs (Table 3), and belief ablation (Table 4) make the evaluation thorough.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **"Worst-case robust" is overclaimed for the partially observable setting.** The formal worst-case guarantee (Theorem 2, Corollary 1) applies to the perfect-information setting. The belief-extended policies (5) and (6) do not inherit this guarantee — Lemma 2 only shows reduction to perfect information when Pos is a singleton (i.e., effectively no partial observability). The paper itself acknowledges that "D(·) becomes an optimistic estimator under partial observability" (line 234), which is the opposite of a worst-case bound. The empirical results against best-responding adversaries show success rates as low as 0.10–0.20 on several graphs (Table 2: Hollywood Walk of Fame 0.10, Sagrada Familia 0.20 against BR_async). Despite this, the phrase "worst-case robust" appears in the title, abstract (line 9), introduction (line 25), contribution list (line 30), and conclusion (lines 311, 313), implying a formal guarantee that the partially observable system does not satisfy. **Impact:** A significant framing issue. The core contributions (theoretical DP extension, belief mechanism, cross-graph RL) remain solid, but the central claimed contribution is overstated relative to what is proved versus what is only empirically observed.

2. **The PSRO baseline comparison lacks specification of the policy architecture.** The paper compares against PSRO (line 240) but does not state what policy representation PSRO uses (e.g., GNN, MLP, or tabular). Since the proposed method explicitly uses a GNN (line 199), and a GNN's graph-awareness is a significant advantage for graph-structured states, this missing detail makes it difficult to attribute the improvement to the paper's specific contributions (belief preservation + DP-guided training) versus the choice of architecture. **Impact:** Weaker than it could be — the "consistently outperforms" claim would be substantially strengthened by specifying or controlling for PSRO's architecture.

### Minor

3. **Belief update uses uniform evader policy by default, with the gap noted but not discussed upfront.** Equation (7) requires the evader's policy ν; since this is unknown, it defaults to uniform over neighbors (line 157). Table 4 shows that using the actual evader policy ("Known Opponent") substantially improves success rates on most graphs (e.g., Scotland-Yard: 0.99 vs 0.73; Big Ben: 0.82 vs 0.65). The paper is transparent about this in Section 5.3, but the mechanism is presented in Section 3.2 without noting this limiting assumption. Moving a brief caveat from Section 5.3 to Section 3.2 would better manage reader expectations.

4. **No confidence intervals or variance reported for success rates.** Results are averaged over 500 tests (Table 1) but no standard deviations or confidence intervals are provided. Given that success rates vary substantially across graphs (e.g., 0.20 to 1.00 against DP_async in Table 2), reporting variance would help assess the reliability of the comparisons.

### Trivial

5. **Inference time comparison mixes CPU and GPU without explicit note.** The paper reports DP taking >2 minutes on an Intel Core i9-13900HX CPU (line 201) and contrasts with RL taking <0.01 seconds under "GPU accelerations" (line 201, clarified on line 288 as RTX 2080 Ti). While the RL policy also achieves <1 second on CPU (line 201), the headline comparison uses different hardware, and noting this asymmetry explicitly at the point of comparison would improve precision.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A controlled comparison where PSRO uses the same GNN architecture (trained on test graphs) would cleanly isolate the value of DP-guided training and cross-graph generalization from architectural advantages.

## Novel Insights

The most insightful observation from this review process is the structural gap between the paper's clean theoretical guarantee for asynchronous-move DP under perfect information and the heuristic nature of its partial-observability extension. The paper's actual contribution — a practical system combining theory (async DP optimality), a lightweight belief tracker, and cross-graph RL — is stronger than a strict reading of its "worst-case robust" framing suggests. The tension is that the framing inflates what is otherwise a solid empirical paper with a genuine theoretical component; decoupling these claims would make both contributions clearer.

## Suggestions
