## Summary

This paper introduces VisualPRM400K, the first large-scale multimodal process supervision dataset (~400K samples, ~2M steps), along with VisualPRM (an 8B multimodal Process Reward Model trained on that data) and VisualProcessBench (a human-annotated benchmark of 2,866 samples with 26,950 step-level labels for evaluating step-wise correctness in multimodal reasoning). The core contribution is demonstrating that PRM-based Best-of-N test-time scaling consistently improves multimodal reasoning across three model families (MiniCPM-V, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5) and four scales, with overall gains of 3.7–8.9 points on a suite of seven multimodal reasoning benchmarks.

## Strengths

1. **First large-scale multimodal process supervision dataset.** Prior process supervision datasets (PRM800K, MathShepherd, OmegaPRM) are text-only. Extending this paradigm to the multimodal setting at this scale (~400K samples) is a genuine forward step that fills a recognized gap. The dataset will be released, enabling further research.

2. **Consistent and substantial reasoning improvements across diverse models.** Table 2 provides strong evidence: improvements hold across 3 distinct model families (MiniCPM-V, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5) at 4 different scales, across nearly every individual benchmark. The overall gains (+8.0, +3.7, +8.4, +8.9, +6.3, +5.9) are substantively meaningful, not narrow or brittle.

3. **Well-designed ablation study (Section 4.3, Figure 4).** The paper directly addresses the key question: does the PRM add value beyond cheaper alternatives at the same compute budget? The comparison of PRM vs. ORM vs. Self-Consistency at matching N values shows PRM consistently wins, and the gap widens with N (reaching 3.1–4.3 points at N=128). The analysis of value-based vs. advantage-based PRMs, different aggregation methods, and early stopping is thorough and diagnostically informative.

4. **Carefully constructed human-annotated benchmark.** VisualProcessBench's 2,866 samples with 26,950 step-level annotations from 5 data sources and 5 solution-model families, with a 10% per-split author review protocol and "identify all errors" (not just first-error) framing, represents a meaningful resource investment. The macro F1 metric is appropriate for the imbalanced step distribution.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **No calibration analysis for the Monte Carlo labels (N=16, mc_i > 0 threshold).** The training labels are generated by sampling 16 continuations per step and labeling a step correct if at least 1 of 16 random completions yields the correct answer (mc_i > 0). With N=16, a step with a true success probability of ~0.10 has a ~19% chance of being labeled correct, and the paper provides no analysis of this noise — no histograms of mc_i values, no comparison of automatic labels against human judgments on a held-out subset, no analysis of false positive/negative rates. The paper mentions trying a higher threshold (deferred to Section B in the appendix) and finds it hurts performance, which is reported but not deeply analyzed. This is a methodological gap: the PRM may be learning to imitate the sampling model's error patterns rather than genuine step correctness, and the current evidence does not rule out this possibility.

2. **No decontamination analysis between training data and evaluation benchmarks.** VisualPRM400K sources questions from MMRP/MMLR v1.1; the evaluation benchmarks (Table 2) include MMMU, MathVision, MathVerse, DynaMath, and WeMath — some of which are also sources for VisualProcessBench. The paper does not report whether any training samples share questions or images with evaluation samples, nor how results would change if overlapping samples were excluded. This is a structural concern because contamination could inflate reported improvements.

### Minor

1. **No variance or statistical significance reporting.** None of the tables report confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. For BoN results where sampled completions introduce randomness, small per-benchmark gains (e.g., InternVL2.5-78B on MMMU: +0.7; MiniCPM-V2.6 on MathVision: +1.3) are difficult to assess without error bars. While single-run evaluation is common in this field, the lack of any variance estimate weakens confidence in the fine-grained per-benchmark claims.

2. **Monte Carlo rollout model and sampling parameters not specified.** The data construction pipeline (Eq. 1, line 136) uses an unspecified model M for completions and does not report temperature or sampling parameters. The paper states solutions are generated using "InternVL2.5 series models" but does not specify which variant is used for the MC rollouts or the decoding hyperparameters. This hinders reproducibility.

3. **Step merging procedure under-specified.** Line 142 states that if a solution exceeds 12 steps, steps are "evenly merge[d]" — but it does not specify how (concatenation? averaging of mc values?). The resulting merged "steps" may contain multiple reasoning actions, making the per-step correctness label ambiguous.

4. **Inter-annotator agreement not reported for VisualProcessBench.** The annotation pipeline (13 annotators, 39 person-days, 10% author review) is well-described, but no inter-annotator agreement metric (e.g., Cohen's κ or Fleiss' κ) is provided. This is a standard quality signal for human-annotated benchmarks and should be included.

5. **Ambiguous description of evaluation metric aggregation.** Section 3.3 describes the metric as macro F1 (averaging F1 for correct and incorrect steps), but Table 3's caption states "the overall score is the micro average of the score from different data sources." The relationship between macro F1 (across step classes) and micro averaging (across data sources) is not clearly distinguished.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Validate a subset of automatically generated VisualPRM400K labels against human judgments (even 200–300 steps) to provide an empirical estimate of label noise.
- Report variance estimates (bootstrap or multiple seeds) for the BoN results, particularly for per-benchmark scores with small deltas.
- Add decontamination analysis by checking question/image overlap between VisualPRM400K, VisualProcessBench, and the Table 2 evaluation benchmarks.

## Removed Points

Points that were flagged to be removed; treat with caution:

- **"Three types of MLLMs" framing is inflated** — The critic claimed "two of the three (InternVL2.5-8B and Qwen2.5-VL-7B / InternVL2.5-78B) are from the same InternVL2.5 family." This is factually incorrect: Qwen2.5-VL is from Alibaba's Qwen team, not InternVL2.5. The three families (MiniCPM-V, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5) are genuinely distinct.
- **Pass@1 vs. BoN conflates two effects** — The paper transparently reports BoN results (which inherently combine sampling and selection) and provides the proper same-N decomposition in Figure 4. The headline comparison is standard practice and not misleading.
- **"Chart in Figure 1 uses a garbled table"** — This is a PDF parsing artifact, not an author issue.
- **mc_i > 0 threshold finding deferred to appendix** — The main text (line 154) already states the finding ("such a threshold negatively impacts the PRM performance"); the appendix provides supporting details, which is standard.
- **Self-Consistency baseline strength** — This was not a weakness raised as such but rather a generic sweep. Removed as it does not constitute a concrete criticism.

None beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions

- Add a dedicated section or paragraph calibrating the automatic MC labels: report the distribution of mc_i values, the effect of the mc_i > 0 threshold vs. alternatives, and ideally a small human-evaluation subset to estimate label noise. This would directly address the most significant concern about training data quality.
- Perform and report decontamination: check n-gram or exact-match overlap between VisualPRM400K training samples and all evaluation benchmarks (Table 2 and VisualProcessBench), and report results with and without any overlapping samples excluded.
- Specify the exact model variant and decoding parameters (temperature, top-p, etc.) used for the Monte Carlo rollouts in the data pipeline.
