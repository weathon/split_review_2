## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. The framework is trained with only sequence-aligned data (no note-level alignment) on both paired and unpaired data. A separate diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module generates style embeddings directly from score content to enable automated, style-appropriate rendering. The core idea of joint modeling of these inverse tasks via disentangled representations is novel and well-motivated.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel and well-motivated formulation.** The insight that EPR and APT are inverse processes that can benefit from joint modeling with disentangled content/style representations (Figure 1, Section 1) is, to this reviewer's knowledge, not proposed in prior work. The analogy to ASR/TTS joint modeling (line 17–18) is effective and correctly scoped.

- **Convincing disentanglement evidence.** The performer/composer identification experiment (Table 4, Figure 3) provides strong evidence that the style representation $z_s$ and content representation $z_c$ capture distinct information. The gap between Style→Comp (77.46% accuracy) and Cont→Comp (29.99%), and between Style→Perf (42.07%) and Cont→Perf (9.94%), is large and clean. This is the paper's most solid empirical result.

- **PSR module is a genuinely new idea.** The diffusion-based module that generates style embeddings from score content alone (Section 3.4), removing the need for manual style specification or composer/performer labels during inference, is original and practically motivated.

- **Competitive APT results.** On the ASAP benchmark (Table 1), the joint model achieves the best overall MUSTER E_avg (12.48) and strong ScoreSimilarity results, outperforming prior specialized APT systems on several sub-metrics despite using a shared architecture.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **"Ours (Target)" is an oracle condition that receives inappropriate emphasis.** The Ours (Target) condition extracts the style embedding $z_s = f_{s,\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{y})$ from the *target human performance itself* — privileged information unavailable at inference time. The paper acknowledges this implicitly by also reporting Ours (PSR), but the discussion in Section 5.1 repeatedly highlights Ours (Target)'s superiority without adequate caveats: "our models achieve lower KL and MAE scores than most baselines (especially Ours-Target)" (line 235) and "Subjective results in Figure 2 show that Ours (Target) achieves the highest ratings across all attributes and styles" (line 237). The fair comparison for EPR is Ours (PSR) vs. DExter and VirtuosoNet. In that comparison, the picture is mixed — Ours (PSR) wins on alignment rate (92.27%, Table 3) and KL(V) vs. VirtuosoNet, but DExter beats Ours (PSR) on KL(D) (1.48 vs. 6.19), KL(V) (2.32 vs. 2.67), and variance matching. The rhetoric overstates the evidence for the realistic condition.

2. **Confounded ablation between Ours (Target) and EPR-Only.** The paper attributes Ours (Target)'s improvement over EPR-Only to "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data" (line 235). But EPR-Only is trained solely on the ASAP dataset (967 performances), while the full model trains on ASAP plus 75,913 unpaired scores *and* an undocumented number of YouTube-derived performances. This introduces three simultaneous confounds: (a) joint APT+EPR training vs. EPR-only, (b) additional unpaired score data, and (c) additional unpaired performance data. The reader cannot tell which factor (or combination) drives the improvement. An ablation that trains the full architecture on only ASAP paired data would isolate the effect of joint modeling.

### Minor

3. **Thin EPR baseline set.** The comparison includes only two baselines: VirtuosoNet (Jeong et al., 2019, now 7 years old) and DExter (Zhang et al., 2024). Several more recent transformer-based EPR systems (Rhyu et al., 2022; Borovik & Viro, 2023; Tang et al., 2023) are cited as related work but not compared against. While these methods require note-level alignment (a difference the paper acknowledges), omitting them leaves the reader unable to assess how the proposed alignment-free approach fares relative to the full state of the art. At minimum, a discussion quantifying the expected cost of removing alignment would help.

4. **Small-scale subjective evaluation with limited statistical reporting.** The listening test uses 11 participants and 5 pieces. No confidence intervals, inter-rater reliability (e.g., Fleiss' κ), or effect sizes are reported for Figure 2. With this sample size, the apparent superiority of Ours (Target) over baselines may not be statistically robust. The style transfer listening test (Figure 5) uses only 3 samples — too few to draw general conclusions.

5. **Missing documentation of the unpaired performance dataset.** The paper states that it "source[s] piano cover videos from YouTube and transcrib[es] the audio into performance MIDI using a state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" (Section 4.1) but provides no count of collected performances, no selection criteria, and no details about the transcription model's architecture or accuracy. Since this dataset is used for training, the omission is a reproducibility concern.

6. **PSR evaluation relies on qualitative visual inspection.** The claim that "PSR-generated styles closely mirror those extracted from real performances" (Section 5.3, line 287) is supported only by visual comparison of 2D t-SNE-like projections (Figure 4). Two-dimensional projections can be sensitive to hyperparameters and can make different distributions appear similar. A quantitative measure (e.g., Fréchet distance or MMD between real and generated style distributions) would substantially strengthen this section.

7. **Variance claim is partially contradicted by the data.** The paper states that "our models exhibit duration and velocity variances that more closely match those of human performances compared with other baselines" (line 235). For velocity variance σ²(V) in Table 2, DExter (238.86) is substantially closer to Human (241.04) than Ours (Target) (151.03) or Ours (PSR) (161.51). The paper offers a post-hoc justification (DExter's variance "results from unstable dynamics rather than meaningful expressiveness"), but the initial claim is misleading as written.

8. **No explicit content alignment loss.** The paper states that content features $z_x$ and $z_y$ "are encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption, line 71), but the total loss (Equation 6) contains no term that directly aligns them — no cycle-consistency loss, contrastive loss, or distribution alignment. Alignment is only indirect through task sharing (EPR decodes from $z_x$, APT decodes from $z_y$). The degree to which the two content encoders actually converge to a shared space is not analyzed, which weakens the theoretical grounding of the joint formulation.

### Trivial

- The data representation asymmetry ($k_x=8$ attributes for scores, $k_y=4$ for performances, line 73) is unexplained in the main text and relegated to Appendix A.2, making the main paper slightly harder to follow.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation that trains the full architecture on only ASAP paired data (without any unpaired data) to isolate the effect of joint APT-EPR training from the benefit of data scale.
- A quantitative metric for PSR quality, such as Fréchet distance or MMD between real style embeddings (from ATEPP performances) and PSR-generated embeddings (from corresponding scores).
- Comparison against at least one recent alignment-based EPR system (e.g., Tang et al., 2023) to provide context on the performance cost of removing note-level alignment.
- Probing analysis of the style embedding to show what specific expressive dimensions (tempo, dynamics, articulation) individual latent dimensions control — this would substantiate the "interpretability" claim made in Section 1.

## Removed Points

The following points from the source reviews were removed after verification:

1. **Criticism about the unpaired loss style encoder seeing unmasked input.** The reviewer claimed the unpaired reconstruction loss (Eq. 4) "does not truly force the model to learn style from incomplete information." This misunderstands the design: the style encoder is *intended* to extract global style from the full unmasked performance; the masking is applied to the content encoder. The design is consistent with the disentanglement goal.
2. **Criticism about distinguishing what is inherited from Beyer & Dai (2024).** The paper explicitly acknowledges adopting their Seq2Seq framework for APT (Section 2.2: "Building on this insight, we adopt a similar Seq2Seq framework") and the contribution is the *joint* modeling, not the APT architecture per se. No additional clarification is needed.
3. **Criticism about statistical significance markers only appearing on Ours's row.** Table 1's caption states significance is computed "with respect to the end-to-end baseline" — standard practice. Marking inter-baseline significance is unnecessary.
4. **Criticism about the Score baseline being treated unfairly.** The paper explicitly states (line 209–210) that Score is "shaded in gray… to indicate that it is not an EPR model and serves only as a comparison anchor."

---

## Novel Insights

The key insight that emerges across the reviews is that the paper genuinely delivers on its two most novel claims — the unified joint framework and the PSR module — but the EPR evaluation narrative is structured in a way that makes the contribution appear stronger than the evidence supports. The disentanglement results (Table 4, Figure 3) are robust and independently convincing, while the EPR results depend heavily on whether one treats Ours (Target) or Ours (PSR) as the primary condition. A clearer separation of the oracle upper bound from the practical method would align the paper's presentation with its actual evidence.

---

## Suggestions

1. Restructure the EPR evaluation: make Ours (PSR) the primary comparison against baselines, and clearly label Ours (Target) as an upper bound / oracle analysis. Revise the abstract, results discussion, and conclusion accordingly.
2. Add an ablation that trains the full joint architecture on only the paired ASAP data, so the effect of joint training can be isolated from the effect of data scale.
3. Document the unpaired performance dataset (count, transcription model, estimated accuracy).
4. Add a quantitative PSR evaluation metric (Fréchet distance or MMD between real and generated style distributions).
5. Add confidence intervals or at minimum report effect sizes for the subjective listening test.
