## Summary

This paper proposes the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework for evaluating whether automated concept annotations generated by LLMs/VLMs are "sufficient" — i.e., whether the generated concepts alone enable accurate class inference. The framework incrementally collects concepts over five stages (fast visual mode at t=0, then slow text-based mode at t=1…5) and measures the Class Representation Index (CRI), the accuracy of predicting the class from the accumulated concepts against semantically similar distractors. Experiments across 6 models and 5 datasets find that slow-mode CRI drops 25%+ below fast-mode on fine-grained datasets, and that high fused-mode accuracy (~90%) coexists with low slow-mode accuracy (~50%), challenging the common "utility-as-proxy" assumption.

## Strengths

- **The utility-as-proxy critique (Table 4) is a valuable empirical contribution.** The finding that fused (image + text) mode achieves ≈90% CRI while slow (text-only) mode scores ≈50% under identical conditions cleanly demonstrates that high downstream accuracy does not guarantee sufficient concept annotations. This single result directly challenges a widely used evaluation practice in the concept-based XAI literature and justifies the paper's existence.

- **Broad and systematic model coverage.** Evaluating 6 models across 3 families (GPT, Llama, Qwen) at multiple scales, across both visual-grounded and post-hoc annotation scenarios, gives the results reasonable generality.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **CRI conflates annotation quality with the model's text-based reasoning ability, undermining the central claim.** The evaluation procedure is: (a) the model generates concepts, then (b) the *same* model attempts to predict the class using only those concepts. A low CRI is interpreted as "annotations are insufficient" — but the model could fail at step (b) because it is bad at reasoning from its own textual outputs, even if the concepts are indeed sufficiently discriminative. These are two different failure modes, and the metric cannot distinguish them. The paper's framing throughout — "annotation sufficiency," "semantic coverage," "adequately capture class semantics" — attributes failure to the *annotations themselves*, when the effect could equally be driven by the model's inability to perform text-based inference from its own outputs. The paper acknowledges this indirectly (e.g., "it remains challenging for them to conceptualize this knowledge in the slow mode") but does not treat it as a confound. This matters because it directly affects what claim the evidence supports: the results show that models struggle to classify from their own generated text, not necessarily that the generated text lacks discriminative content. A decoupled evaluation (e.g., generating concepts with one model and evaluating classification with a different model, or a human-in-the-loop evaluation) would be needed to isolate the annotation-quality component.

### Minor

- **CRI formula (Eq. 2) has a typo that makes it incorrect as written.** The equation reads $\frac{1}{t} \sum_{i=1}^t \mathbb{1}[y_i^t = y_i]$, summing from $i=1$ to $t$ and dividing by $t$. The test set $\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}$ has $l$ instances (not $t$). At $t=5$, this formula would average over only 5 cases, which is clearly not what the implementation does (reported CRI values like 93.75% are computed over hundreds of samples). The text correctly describes CRI as "the proportion of correctly predicted labels" over the full test set, so the equation simply has a typo ($t$ should be $l$ or $N$). While not fatal, a metric-defining equation in the paper's central contribution must be correct.

- **Eq. 1 (concept chain gathering) is notationally unclear at $t=1$.** The union $\bigcup_{j=1}^{t-1} \mathcal{F}(c_i^j, X_i; \Theta)$ for $t=1$ ranges over $j=1,\dots,0$, i.e., the empty set, leaving $c_i^1$ undefined. The intended incremental process can be clarified.

- **The fast-vs-slow comparison is confounded by modality, weakening the "Slow Mode Superiority" hypothesis.** Fast mode (t=0) uses rich visual input; slow mode (t>0) uses only text concepts. The paper expects slow mode to outperform fast mode based on dual-process theory from human cognition, but the analogy is stretched: in humans, both fast and slow processes operate on the same perceptual input, whereas here the comparison is cross-modal (pixels vs. text). The observed 25% gap could partly reflect an expected modality gap rather than annotation insufficiency per se. The paper acknowledges this to some degree but could more explicitly temper the "Slow Mode Superiority" framing.

- **The dramatic dip at t=1 in Table 3 (common datasets) is not discussed.** On CIFAR-100 and Caltech-101, CRI drops from ~85-91% at t=0 (fast mode) to ~29-33% at t=1 before recovering to ~94-95% at t=5. This non-monotonic behavior is an interesting phenomenon that the paper does not address.

- **The Semantic Similarity Dictionary for distractor selection is built using ResNet-18 predictions (line 197), which introduces a particular model's confusion patterns into the evaluation design.** Different backbone models would yield different distractor sets, potentially affecting CRI values. The paper does not discuss this as a limitation.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Human baseline or oracle upper bound.** Without knowing what CRI human-written concepts would achieve, the absolute CRI values (50-70% in slow mode) are hard to interpret as "insufficient" — they may be reasonable for text-based concept inference.
- **Decoupled generator–evaluator experiment.** Using one model to generate concepts and a different model (or a held-out set of human-authored concepts) to evaluate them would disentangle annotation quality from reasoning ability.
- **Discussion of the non-monotonic CRI trajectory** (the sharp t=1 drop in Table 3) could yield insight into how concept refinement interacts with classification difficulty.
- **Confidence intervals or statistical comparisons** between conditions would strengthen the quantitative claims, though the reported standard deviations (stated as negligible) partially address this.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's most useful insight — beyond what the paper itself provides — is the identification of the confound between annotation quality and text-reasoning ability in the CRI. The paper discusses the model's difficulty in "conceptualizing" its knowledge but does not frame this as a confound that undermines the attribution of low CRI to annotation insufficiency specifically. The critic also usefully flags the unexamined t=1 dip in Table 3 and the cross-modal asymmetry in the fast-vs-slow comparison, both of which the paper currently glosses over. ## Suggestions
