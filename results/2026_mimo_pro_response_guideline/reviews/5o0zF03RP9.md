## Summary
IncentRL proposes augmenting RL objectives with a KL-divergence penalty between predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) and preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), controlled by a weight β. The paper's headline contribution is a Bayesian adaptation scheme for β that eliminates manual tuning, but the paper never specifies the adaptation mechanism (prior, likelihood, posterior update rule, or what "rounds" mean). The KL-based incentive shaping framework itself is clearly presented and demonstrates some empirical benefit in sparse-reward toy and navigation tasks.

## Strengths
- **Clean KL-based incentive shaping framework**: The core formulation (r_shaped = r_ext − β·KL(p||q)) is clearly presented in Section 3.2 (Equation at line 69), with a concrete algorithm in Section 5.1 (lines 149–155) and useful special cases. The conceptual distinction from TRPO/PPO — regularizing outcome distributions rather than policy distributions — articulated in Section 2.2 (line 43) is a genuine structural insight.
- **Honest characterization of β sensitivity**: Table 1 (lines 178–183) shows all β>0 yield ~70% improvement on the toy MDP, while Table 2 (lines 211–216) reveals that β≥0.3 degrades MountainCar performance by 53–75%. This transparently demonstrates both the benefit and the risk of the approach, motivating the need for adaptation.
- **Meaningful MiniGrid results**: Figure 2 (lines 218–222) shows IncentRL (β=0.01) achieves ~98% success rate with ~29-step episodes vs. baseline at 90.5%/75 steps (line 226). The improvement in both success rate and efficiency is substantial.
- **Honest reporting of limitations**: Section 6.2 (lines 265–271) explicitly catalogs three failure modes (preference misalignment, KL dominance, latent mismatch), and MountainCar negative results for high β are presented without spin.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Bayesian adaptation mechanism is never specified** — The paper identifies "treating the incentive weight β as a Bayesian random variable, updated online" as "the central novelty" (line 9, line 21). Yet there is no prior distribution over β, no likelihood function, no posterior update rule, and no definition of what "rounds" mean in the training context. The algorithm in Section 5.1 (lines 149–155) has five steps — none involve updating β. Figures 3–4 and Table 3 show posterior concentration across "rounds," but the reader cannot reproduce, verify, or build on this claimed contribution. The paper cannot claim Bayesian adaptation as its primary contribution and then fail to specify how it works.

- **No comparison to any existing intrinsic motivation or reward shaping method** — Every experiment compares only against vanilla RL (β=0) and the same method with different fixed β values. There is no comparison to curiosity-driven exploration (ICM, RND), empowerment, information gain methods (VIME), potential-based reward shaping, or meta-gradient methods that adapt auxiliary reward weights. Without such comparisons, it is impossible to determine whether the observed gains stem from the specific KL-divergence mechanism or simply from having any form of dense auxiliary reward signal.

- **Bayesian adaptation not demonstrated with task performance** — The paper's thesis is that Bayesian adaptation "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning" (line 9). However: the toy MDP (Section 5.2) and MountainCar (Section 5.3) use only fixed β; in MiniGrid (Section 5.4), Bayesian adaptation is mentioned only in passing: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (line 228). The actual learning curves under Bayesian β are never shown. Figures 3–4 show β distributions, not task performance. For a paper whose headline contribution is eliminating manual β tuning, this is a critical evidential gap.

- **Outcome distribution instantiation is under-specified in deep RL experiments** — In MountainCar, q(o|s) "assigns all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)" and p(o|s,a) is "the predicted outcome distribution" (line 199), but how either is computed over the continuous state space is never specified. Is p(o|s,a) the known dynamics? A learned model? Is KL computed analytically or via sampling? If q(o|s) is simply a point mass on the goal, the KL term is effectively a distance-to-goal metric — a hand-crafted reward shaping signal dressed in probabilistic language. The paper's claim to generality requires showing it works with non-trivially defined preferences.

### Minor
- **Claimed "ablation studies" are absent** — Line 139 states "Our evaluation spans from controlled toy domains to navigation and ablation studies," but no ablation studies are presented anywhere in the paper.
- **MountainCar shows high β sensitivity without Bayesian adaptation demo** — Table 2 shows β=0.3 and β=1.0 dramatically hurt performance (−52.76% and −75.01%), which is precisely the scenario where Bayesian adaptation should help most, yet it is not demonstrated here. This makes the sensitivity finding a missed opportunity rather than supporting evidence.
- **Text vs. figure inconsistency for MiniGrid baseline** — The text states "90.5% for β=0.0" (line 226), while the figure description indicates β=0.0 reaches "approximately 75" (line 218). This discrepancy should be resolved.

### Trivial
- Propositions 1 and 2 are trivial boundary cases (small β preserves optimal policy by continuity; large β makes KL dominant). The paper acknowledges Proposition 1 "follows from basic continuity" (lines 105–106). They are correct but offer limited analytical depth.
