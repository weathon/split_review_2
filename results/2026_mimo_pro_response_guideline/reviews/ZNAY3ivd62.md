## Summary

GUI-Spotlight equips a 7B MLLM with three visual tools (extract, find_color, crop) for iterative GUI visual grounding, trained via a three-stage pipeline: SFT warm-up on 2,561 trajectories generated by Qwen2.5-VL-72B, followed by two stages of modified GSPO reinforcement learning with an auxiliary cross-entropy loss to prevent training collapse. The paper reports 52.8% on ScreenSpot-Pro with 18.5K curated training samples, and provides systematic ablations of RL algorithms, reward design, and inference strategies including documented negative results.

## Strengths

- **Auxiliary cross-entropy loss prevents RL collapse in multi-turn tool use**: Figure 3 (right panel) clearly shows vanilla GRPO and GSPO oscillate around 300 steps with degrading accuracy, while the tool-filtered CE variant (method ⑦) maintains monotonically improving reward to 0.9. The mechanism is explained at lines 118–122: without the auxiliary term, exploration generates non-parseable tool formats causing sparse/volatile rewards and parameter drift. This is a specific, empirically validated technical contribution that extends beyond GUI grounding to any RL-trained agent producing structured tool calls.

- **Transparent documentation of negative results**: Section 4.1 and Figure 3 (left panel) systematically benchmark seven RL variants under identical conditions (same SFT checkpoint, 400 RL steps). Two variants—top-p% most-uncertain prompts (36.7%) and continuous reference policy updates (37.8%)—are shown to degrade accuracy and explicitly discarded. This level of transparent reporting of failed alternatives is uncommon and provides actionable guidance for practitioners.

- **Trained tool-use policy substantially outperforms untrained iterative baselines**: Section 5.4 and Figure 5 demonstrate that naive multi-turn conversational inference achieves only 7.6%, repeated single-turn crop-and-click reaches 47.6%, while trained GUI-Spotlight attains 52.8%. The 7.6% result is striking—it shows the base model has essentially zero native think-with-image capability, and the 5.2-point gap over the single-turn heuristic demonstrates the RL-trained tool coordination policy yields genuine improvement.

- **Improvement over own base model across all benchmarks**: GUI-Spotlight improves over UI-TARS-1.5-7B on ScreenSpot-Pro (38.7→52.8, +14.1), UI-Vision (18.1→23.4, +5.3), and OSWorld-G (61.9→62.7, +0.8), covering professional software, desktop applications, and OS-level tasks.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None

### Major

- **Comparison framing is misleading — iterative vs. single-step models**: The headline claim in the abstract and Section 5.1 compares GUI-Spotlight (multiple forward passes with tool invocations) against single-pass models (V2P-7B, GTA-1-7B). Table 3 foregrounds "18.5K samples → 52.8% vs. 9.6M samples → 50.6%" without acknowledging the compute asymmetry. The fairer comparison is in Section 5.4: a simple untrained iterative baseline achieves 47.6%, narrowing the real contribution to 5.2 percentage points. This fairer comparison should be presented alongside the main results, not in a separate section.

- **Selective emphasis across benchmarks; results are mixed**: GUI-Spotlight leads all 7B models only on ScreenSpot-Pro. On OSWorld-G (Table 5), GTA1-7B achieves 67.7% while GUI-Spotlight achieves 62.7%—a 5-point deficit from a same-size model, and only +0.8 over its own base, with degradation on Element Recognition (64.5→60.6, −3.9) and Layout Understanding (65.2→63.2, −2.0). On UI-Vision (Table 4), UI-Venus-Ground-7B (26.5%) outperforms GUI-Spotlight (23.4%). The conclusion's claim of "remaining competitive with substantially larger models" is not supported by the full results.

- **No inference cost analysis**: GUI-Spotlight performs multiple forward passes per prediction (Algorithm 1 loops to T_max), but the paper does not report T_max, typical number of tool-call rounds, wall-clock latency, or total FLOPs. For a method targeting practical GUI agents where click latency affects user experience, this omission makes the accuracy-vs-compute tradeoff impossible to assess.

### Minor

- **"18.5K samples" framing obscures reliance on a 72B teacher**: Stage 1 SFT trajectories are generated by Qwen2.5-VL-72B (line 106), and the data cleaning pipeline uses Qwen2.5-VL-72B as a judge (lines 94–100). While the raw sample count is 18.5K, effective supervision comes from a 72B model. The paper acknowledges this usage but does not flag it as a confound when comparing to methods using millions of samples without a large teacher.

- **SFT accuracy drop insufficiently analyzed**: Figure 2 shows Stage 0 (39.3%) → Stage 1 (17.8%), a 21.5-point drop after SFT. The paper says the model "learns to invoke multiple tools but remains under-aligned" (line 136) but provides no diagnostic analysis—how many tool calls are made, whether they are correct, whether the model issues malformed calls. Understanding this drop would strengthen the narrative about why RL is necessary.

- **Table 3 contains an unlabeled duplicate**: "Qwen2.5-VL-72B-Instruct" appears twice in the 72B section with vastly different scores (1.0% at line 259 and 53.3% at line 262). If the second entry represents a UI-fine-tuned variant, it should be labeled distinctly.

## Novel Insights
The paper's most novel insight is that multi-turn tool-use training for GUI grounding collapses without an auxiliary cross-entropy loss on format-valid, result-correct samples (lines 118–122). Without this regularization, exploration generates non-parseable tool formats leading to sparse rewards, high-variance gradients, and parameter drift. This finding extends beyond GUI grounding to any RL-trained agent producing structured tool calls, and the paper documents it clearly with controlled experiments (Figure 3). The comprehensive negative results in Section 4.1 (e.g., uncertainty-based prompt selection and continuous reference policy updates both degrade performance) are also valuable community contributions.

3. Add a paragraph honestly discussing why the method excels on ScreenSpot-Pro but not consistently on OSWorld-G or UI-Vision (e.g., does iterative zoom help more on high-resolution professional software?).
