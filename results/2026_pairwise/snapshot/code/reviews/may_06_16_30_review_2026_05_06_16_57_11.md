## Summary

This position paper argues that LLMs' over-cautious responses to health-related queries constitute a genuine safety harm—especially for vulnerable populations like individuals with OCD and health anxiety—rather than merely a helpfulness shortcoming. It supports this position with both normative argumentation (over-cautiousness redirects reassurance-seeking toward costlier compulsions, and "over-alignment" maximizes safety at the expense of other values) and empirical evidence from OCD-Eval, a 225-query dataset evaluated across 22 LLMs, showing an average Over-Cautious Rate of 43.9% on low-risk scenarios while maintaining high emergency recognition.

## Strengths

- **Novel reframing of over-cautiousness as a safety harm rather than a helpfulness deficit**: The paper introduces the concept of "over-alignment" and argues that over-cautious responses don't merely fail to be useful—they actively cause harm through reinforcing compulsive behaviors, driving unnecessary medical visits, and increasing infection risk from over-cleaning (lines 160–172). This transitions the problem from a UX concern to a legitimate safety argument.

- **Novel argument that over-cautious responses redirect rather than eliminate reassurance-seeking**: This is the paper's most original contribution (lines 396–406). The paper argues that when a user has already queried an LLM, the reassurance-seeking episode has already occurred; an over-cautious response simply redirects it toward costlier compulsive actions like ER visits, rather than containing it. This reframes the trade-off in a way prior over-refusal work does not.

- **Strong empirical contribution**: OCD-Eval with 225 queries across 22 models provides systematic, measurable evidence of the over-cautiousness problem. The demonstration that some models (e.g., claude-sonnet-4.6, Gemma 4 Flash) achieve both low OCR (~15–20%) and high emergency recognition (~98–99%) directly undermines the natural objection that reducing over-caution necessarily sacrifices safety (Figure 2).

- **Effective counterargument engagement**: The response to "OCD patients shouldn't use LLMs" (lines 377–414) is particularly well-argued, cleanly separating whether users *should* seek reassurance from what response causes least harm *given that they do*. The response to the "minimizes harm at scale" counterargument is also substantive (lines 337–376).

- **Negative result on OCD-context disclosure (Section 4.4)**: The finding that explicitly providing OCD context to models degrades emergency recognition for some models (e.g., Gemma 3 27B drops to 68%) while inconsistently reducing over-cautiousness is a practically important finding that directly constrains the solution space and strengthens the argument for systemic recalibration.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The central prescription—"recalibrate models"—is underspecified for general-purpose LLMs that cannot identify vulnerable users**: The paper argues that models should be recalibrated (line 12, line 308) and explicitly shows that relying on users to disclose their OCD status is unreliable (Section 4.4) and that most OCD users are undiagnosed (lines 306–307). But this raises the core design question: when a general-purpose LLM receives a health query from an unknown user, should it default to calibrated or cautious responses? The paper does not provide a clear answer to this. Section 5's proposals (industrial standards, user education) are complementary but do not resolve this tension. This matters because the position's persuasiveness depends on the feasibility of its proposed remedy, not just the existence of its identified problem.

- **The "not in tension" claim overstates what cross-model comparison shows**: The paper argues that reducing over-cautiousness and maintaining emergency recognition are "not in tension" (lines 354–355). Cross-model comparison shows that some models achieve both—establishing that the trade-off is not *inevitable*—but does not establish that reducing over-cautiousness *within* a given model through alignment changes would preserve emergency recognition. The within-model-family trend data (Gemma 3→4, Gemini 2.0→3, Claude 3.7→4.6) provides suggestive evidence but comes with the caveat that "this pattern is not consistently maintained over time" (line 360). The claim should be tempered: reducing over-caution is *achievable* (as demonstrated by existing models), but whether it is achievable in a given model without sacrificing emergency recognition remains to be established. This matters because the policy recommendation depends on the claim that both goals are simultaneously achievable.

### Minor

- **The "over-alignment" analogy to overfitting is loosely drawn**: Overfitting is about a model failing to generalize due to over-optimization on training data; over-alignment as described here is about the alignment *target itself* being miscalibrated (maximizing safety rather than balancing values). These are fundamentally different—overfitting is a problem of means, over-alignment is a problem of ends. The analogy could confuse rather than clarify for readers encountering the concept for the first time (lines 33–35). A brief acknowledgment of the disanalogy would strengthen the framing.

- **The causal chain from over-cautious response to physical harm is asserted rather than evidenced**: The Position section (lines 160–172) describes physical harm pathways (excessive cleaning → infections, avoidance of clinics, unnecessary tests), but all are labeled as secondary harms where the LLM "will not directly suggest these behaviors" but "reinforced anxiety might lead users to them." This is plausible but rests on clinical literature about OCD-related behaviors rather than on evidence about LLM-driven exacerbation specifically. As a position paper, this is acceptable reasoning, but the paper would benefit from more clearly distinguishing the well-established clinical pathways (OCD → compulsive behaviors → physical harm) from the assumed pathway (LLM over-cautiousness → exacerbated OCD → physical harm).

- **The ground-truth risk annotations from GPs capture objective risk but not communicative appropriateness for OCD patients**: The paper explicitly acknowledges this (lines 272–275) and uses additional metrics (anxiety index, tag analysis) for the communicative dimension. However, the headline metric (OCR 43.9%) measures only objective risk overstatement, while the central argument is about communicative harm. This is not fatal—the paper has supporting evidence from tags and anxiety scores—but the mismatch means the 43.9% figure does not directly measure the paper's claimed harm.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Within-model ablation experiments**: Fine-tuning or prompting experiments that reduce OCR within a single model and measure effects on emergency recognition would significantly strengthen the "no trade-off" claim, moving it from plausible to demonstrated. This would be ideal but is not strictly required for a position paper.

- **Deeper engagement with the ERP clinical literature**: The paper references cyberchondria research but could engage more specifically with Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP) literature, which has nuanced findings about how different forms of reassurance affect compulsion reinforcement. This would sharpen the argument about what "well-calibrated" reassurance looks like.

- **Clearer specification of what "recalibrated" behavior looks like for general users**: Even a brief discussion of whether models should default to calibrated risk-level communication for all users, or adopt user-context-adaptive approaches, would substantially strengthen the practical contribution.

- **More engagement with regulatory and liability considerations**: The paper briefly mentions legal pressures (lines 146–150) and uses the wet-floor sign analogy to dismiss them, but the economic asymmetry between one catastrophic under-cautious case and millions of over-cautious cases is a powerful counter-countervailing force that deserves explicit engagement.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treated with caution:

- **Demand for novel experiments, baselines, or ablations**: Position papers argue for a viewpoint; they do not need novel experimental validation. The empirical evidence in OCD-Eval is illustrative support, not the main contribution. Requesting within-model ablations is a nice-to-have, not a requirement.

- **"Overclaiming" or "too provocative" criticism**: Position papers are expected to make strong claims. The paper's assertion that over-cautiousness is a safety harm and that "always err on the safe side" is itself harmful is exactly the kind of debatable claim that drives productive discussion at NeurIPS.

- **Reproducibility concerns about OCD-Eval**: The dataset and methodology are described sufficiently for a position paper. The inter-annotator agreement metrics (AC2 of 0.93) are provided.

- **Criticisms that the paper should be more of a standard research paper**: e.g., demanding control groups, causal intervention experiments, or formal proofs. This is a position paper with illustrative empirical evidence.

- **The claim that the paper's hybrid structure (position + empirical evaluation) muddies which claims rest on normative argumentation versus measurement**: The paper is clear that Section 3 is the position, Section 4 provides evidence, and Sections 6 is counterarguments. The hybrid structure is a strength, not a weakness.

- **Criticisms about single-GP annotation**: The paper addresses this with a validation subset (lines 214–226), showing high inter-annotator agreement. This is appropriate for a position paper's illustrative evidence.

## Novel Insights

The most novel insight is the paper's reframing of over-cautious health responses as *not* avoiding the reassurance loop but redirecting it toward costlier compulsions. This is a genuinely new argument in the over-refusal literature, which has primarily focused on helpfulness deficits rather than safety harms. Additionally, the empirical finding that providing OCD context to models degrades rather than improves performance (Section 4.4) is a striking result that paradoxically strengthens the position's call for systemic recalibration rather than user-side solutions.

## Suggestions

- **Add a brief paragraph explicitly addressing the general-user design question**: Acknowledge the tension between recalibrating for vulnerable users versus serving all users, and either argue that calibrated responses serve both populations well (low risk is low risk for everyone) or explain how calibration could be context-adaptive. This would close the paper's most significant gap.

- **Temper the "not in tension" claim**: Replace language like "not in tension" (line 355) with something like "not inherently in tension" or "not necessarily zero-sum," and explicitly note that while existing models demonstrate the achievability of both goals, within-model alignment changes have not yet been validated to preserve this.

- **Distinguish the OCR metric's scope from the communicative harm claim**: Add a sentence or two noting that OCR captures objective risk overstatement while the anxiety index and tag analysis capture the communicative dimension, and that the full harm picture requires both.
