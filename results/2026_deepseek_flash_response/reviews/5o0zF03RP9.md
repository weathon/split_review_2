## Summary

IncentRL proposes augmenting standard RL rewards with a KL divergence between predicted outcome distributions \(p(o|s,a)\) and preferred outcome distributions \(q(o|s)\), weighted by an incentive parameter \(\beta\). The paper claims Bayesian online adaptation of \(\beta\) as its central novelty, alongside theoretical analysis and experiments on a toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid. The KL shaping formulation is clean and the MiniGrid results are positive, but the paper's headline contribution (Bayesian adaptation) is never described, the evidence for it is internally contradictory, and the empirical evaluation lacks any comparison against existing intrinsic motivation methods.

## Strengths

1. **Proposition 1's optimality guarantee (Section 4.1):** The paper formally shows that for sufficiently small \(\beta\), the optimal policy under standard RL remains optimal under IncentRL. This is a non-trivial guarantee that many intrinsic motivation methods cannot offer — it tells practitioners that a small \(\beta\) cannot distort the optimal solution, only accelerate learning.

2. **MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 results (Section 5.4, Figure 2):** IncentRL with \(\beta=0.01\) achieves a 98% final success rate vs. 90.5% for the baseline, and reduces mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps on a standard sparse-reward benchmark. This is a clear improvement on a challenging domain, and the magnitude of the efficiency gain (2.6× shorter episodes) goes beyond what typical regularization schemes deliver.

3. **Toy 2-state MDP controlled demonstration (Section 5.2, Table 1):** The paper systematically sweeps \(\beta \in \{0.0, 0.1, 0.3, 1.0\}\) and shows that all positive \(\beta\) values yield 69–74% improvement over \(\beta=0\), with variance dropping from \(\sigma=11.30\) to \(\sigma\approx6.0\)–\(6.7\). This cleanly isolates the effect of the KL shaping term.

4. **Clear conceptual distinction from active inference/FEP (Section 3.3):** The paper explicitly contrasts its design with the Free Energy Principle: IncentRL assumes fixed or slowly evolving preferences and shapes *behavior* to close the prediction-preference gap, rather than adapting preferences to reduce surprise. This is a principled design choice.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The Bayesian adaptation scheme — advertised as the paper's central novelty — is never described (lines 21, 228–247).** The abstract calls it "the central novelty," the contribution list places it first, and the paper title references "Bayesian Adaptation." Yet the paper contains zero specification of: what prior is placed on \(\beta\); what likelihood function relates \(\beta\) to observed data; how the posterior is computed (conjugate update? MCMC? variational inference?); what "data" the posterior conditions on; or how the posterior is used during rollouts. The Methods section (Section 3) describes only the KL shaping term. The experimental section says "we tracked the posterior evolution of \(\beta\) using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" without saying what that scheme is (line 230). A paper whose self-declared primary contribution is a Bayesian method cannot omit the description of that method. This flaw alone makes the headline contribution unreviewable and unreproducible.

2. **The evidence for Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation is internally contradictory (lines 239, 245; Table 3; Figures 3–4).** Table 3 shows the posterior mean converging from 0.106 (Round 1) to **0.017** (Round 4). Yet the paper repeatedly claims (line 239, Figure 3 caption, Figure 4 caption) that the posterior concentrates "near the effective region (\(\beta\approx 0.1\))" and draws a dashed red line at \(\beta=0.1\) labeled "target effective \(\beta\)." This is not what the data show: the posterior converges to \(\sim0.02\), not 0.1. The actual best fixed \(\beta\) used in the MiniGrid experiment was 0.01 (line 224), which is consistent with 0.017 but not 0.1. The paper's own figures and tables undermine its central empirical claim.

3. **The predicted outcome distribution \(p(o|s,a)\) is concretely defined only for the toy MDP (lines 63, 166, 199).** For the toy 2-state MDP, \(p(o|s,a)\) is clearly the environment's transition probabilities. But for MountainCar and MiniGrid, the paper does not specify what the outcome space \(\mathcal{O}\) is, whether \(p(o|s,a)\) is a learned forward model or directly taken from dynamics, or how the KL divergence is computed in practice. Since the KL shaping term is the method's core mechanism, this gap affects reproducibility.

4. **No comparison against existing intrinsic motivation methods (Sections 5.2–5.4).** Every experiment compares IncentRL only against \(\beta=0\) (standard RL). The paper claims to address "a core limitation of intrinsic motivation methods" (Abstract, line 9) but provides no comparison against curiosity-driven exploration (ICM, RND), count-based exploration, or other KL-regularized methods (SAC). Without such baselines, it is unclear whether the observed gains come from the specific IncentRL formulation or simply from adding any dense reward signal. For a methods paper at a top venue, this is a significant omission.

### Minor

5. **"In additional runs (not shown)" for the Bayesian adaptation performance comparison (line 228).** The paper states that the Bayesian adaptation achieved performance comparable to the best fixed \(\beta\) — the central performance claim for the headline contribution — but these results are not shown. Figures 3–4 and Table 3 show posterior statistics, but the actual task performance (success rate, episode length) under the adaptive scheme is withheld.

6. **Only 3 random seeds for MountainCar and MiniGrid (lines 201, 224).** This is on the low side given the high variance observed (e.g., MountainCar \(\beta=0.3\) has std=908.94, nearly 55% of the mean of 1654.67).

7. **"Rounds" in the Bayesian adaptation are undefined (Figures 3–4, Table 3).** The paper does not specify how many timesteps constitute a round, how \(\beta\) is sampled within a round, or how the transition between rounds works.

### Trivial

8. **Proposition 2 (Section 4.1):** The statement that as \(\beta\to\infty\) the policy minimizes only the KL term is a straightforward consequence of the objective's definition, not a meaningful theoretical contribution.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Compare against a simple meta-gradient or annealing scheme for tuning \(\beta\) to show the Bayesian approach adds value beyond basic adaptation.
- Test \(\beta=0.1\) on MiniGrid (the value labeled as "target effective") to resolve the contradiction in the posterior analysis.
- Clarify what \(o\) is in MountainCar and MiniGrid and how \(p(o|s,a)\) is obtained.

## Suggestions

1. **Describe the Bayesian mechanism in full.** Specify the prior, likelihood function, posterior update procedure, and how the posterior is used to select \(\beta\) during rollouts. Without this, the paper's headline contribution cannot be evaluated.
2. **Reconcile the contradictory evidence.** If the posterior converges to \(\sim0.02\), state this clearly and either remove the "target effective \(\beta=0.1\)" line or explain what it represents. The data support convergence near 0.02 (consistent with MiniGrid's best fixed \(\beta=0.01\)), and the paper should reflect this accurately.
3. **Add at least one strong intrinsic motivation baseline** (e.g., ICM or RND) to the empirical comparison to demonstrate that the method is competitive with existing approaches, not just better than doing nothing.
4. **Specify \(p(o|s,a)\) implementation details** for all environments: what is the outcome space, how is \(p\) obtained, and how is the KL divergence computed?
5. **Show the Bayesian adaptation performance curves** alongside fixed-\(\beta\) curves, rather than referencing them as "not shown."
