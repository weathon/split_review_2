## Summary

This position paper argues that universal aesthetic alignment in image generation models constitutes "aesthetic authoritarianism"—it overrides explicit user intent, suppresses artistic pluralism, and reflects developer-centered value imposition. The paper supports this claim through systematic experiments showing that (1) aesthetic-aligned generation models resist producing anti-aesthetic outputs even when explicitly instructed, (2) reward models penalize images faithful to anti-aesthetic prompts, and (3) unaligned base models (BLIP-L, CLIP-L) dramatically outperform their aligned descendants at identifying which image best matches a wide-spectrum aesthetic prompt (BLIP-L: 0.965 accuracy vs. HPSv3: 0.381). A philosophical framework builds on "pre-emptive governance" and "resistibility" to argue this is not merely a technical failing but a democratic deficit.

## Strengths

- **Compelling core empirical finding (Table 4)**: The result that unaligned base models dramatically outperform purpose-built aesthetic reward models at identifying images matching anti-aesthetic prompts (BLIP-L: 0.965 vs HPSv3: 0.381, below random) cleanly isolates the problem to aesthetic alignment itself rather than prompt comprehension capability. This is a powerful and counterintuitive result that directly supports the paper's central position.

- **Well-designed within-family comparisons (Table 1)**: By comparing aligned and base models within the same architecture family (e.g., DanceFlux vs. Flux Dev, Playground vs. SDXL), the paper controls for base model capability and demonstrates that the degradation in wide-spectrum instruction following is attributable to aesthetic alignment specifically, with most comparisons showing statistical significance at p < 10⁻⁵.

- **Philosophical framework with genuine depth (Section 2.4)**: The five interrelated concerns—developer vs. user preference, inherited bias, individual vs. collective preference, sanitized reality, and toxic positivity—go beyond "alignment is biased" to identify specific mechanisms of governance. The "pre-emptive governance" framing (citing Lazar, 2025) and the concept of "resistibility" as a democratic safeguard provide a coherent theoretical foundation that elevates the paper above purely empirical critique.

- **Clear and arguable position**: The paper takes an unambiguous stance—"strict alignment to [generalized aesthetic] preference is problematic"—that is specific enough to invite productive disagreement. The position is stated within three sentences in the introduction and consistently maintained throughout.

- **Constructive conclusion with four concrete proposals**: The recommendation for (a) diverse style recognition in reward systems, (b) user-controllable alignment strength, (c) diverse annotator pools, and (d) transparency about alignment criteria gives the position actionable direction rather than remaining purely diagnostic.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The paper's own data partially undermines its claimed scope, and the engagement with this tension is insufficient.** The position claims that universal aesthetic alignment *itself* is problematic, but Nano Banana and Flux Krea demonstrate that strong prompt-following and aesthetic quality can coexist (Table 2; Section 5 last paragraph). This suggests the problem may be one of *implementation* (how alignment is done) rather than *principle* (whether alignment per se is authoritarian), which narrows the normative force of the argument. The paper briefly acknowledges this ("enabling wide-spectrum aesthetic outputs does not inherently degrade the quality of conventionally 'good' images") but doesn't reconcile it with the broader claim that universal aesthetic alignment constitutes authoritarian governance. If some alignment approaches preserve pluralism, the position should specify which forms of alignment are authoritarian rather than indicting the entire category—which matters because the paper's normative conclusions are framed at the category level.

- **The experimental prompt design conflates deliberate aesthetic deviation with technical quality degradation.** The "wide-spectrum aesthetics" prompts are constructed from VisionReward's "bad" rating descriptions (Section 3.1), producing prompts like "extremely blurry and fragmented," "no prominent main object," and "heavy object deformation." While the paper argues that only "clarity" could be seen as a technical flaw, several dimensions (noise, distortion, detail loss) blur the line between aesthetic choice and technical failure. A reward model penalizing "randomly composited, making objects unrecognizable and chaotic" may be functioning as a quality filter rather than imposing "aesthetic authoritarianism." The normative force of the argument—that pluralism is being suppressed—depends on this being about *aesthetic* preference rather than *quality assessment*. Testing established artistic styles (Expressionism, Dadaism, Fauvism) alongside "bad quality" prompts would have cleanly isolated this concern, and their absence weakens the bridge from data to position.

### Minor

- **Section 5 (Alternative Positions) is too brief for a position paper.** Two counterarguments are addressed in a few paragraphs each. The safety counterargument is dismissed largely by assertion ("Political critique, depictions of decay, horror... are not inherently unsafe; they are historically central to art") rather than by engaging with the practical difficulty of drawing boundaries between artistic expression and genuinely harmful content at scale. A more substantive treatment would strengthen the paper's defensibility.

- **Section 6 (Mitigation) is very thin.** For a paper calling for fundamental reconsideration of aesthetic alignment, the single paragraph referencing appendices and one LoRA figure provides minimal alternative vision. While the conclusion's four proposals are constructive, they appear without development.

- **The domain mismatch in the real artwork evaluation (Section 4.3) limits its evidentiary value.** Reward models are trained on AI-generated images and photographs, so evaluating them on fine art involves a domain shift. The paper provides three justifications for this, which are reasonable, but presenting The Scream's low HPSv3 score (5.23) as a prominent opening anecdote (Figure 1) implies it is a key finding rather than supportive illustration, inflating its evidentiary role beyond what the methodology warrants.

### Trivial
None.

## Novel Insights

The Table 4 finding—that aesthetic alignment *impairs* rather than merely *fails to help with* anti-aesthetic recognition—is genuinely novel and important. The paper reveals that aligned reward models perform *worse than random* (HPSv3: 0.381 accuracy), meaning alignment actively corrupts the model's ability to recognize prompt-matching anti-aesthetic images. This is a stronger result than merely showing alignment doesn't help; it shows alignment is actively counterproductive for this task category, which significantly sharpens the normative argument.

## Suggestions

- Reframe the core position as targeting *unconditional* or *single-objective* aesthetic alignment rather than "universal aesthetic alignment" per se, since the Nano Banana and Flux Krea results show that some forms of alignment can preserve pluralism. This would preserve the normative argument while accommodating the paper's own evidence.

- In the experimental design, test prompts requesting established artistic styles (e.g., "in the style of German Expressionism" or "Dadaist collage") to provide a clean dissociation between aesthetic pluralism and technical quality. Even a small supplementary experiment would substantially strengthen the bridge from evidence to position.

- Expand Section 5 to seriously engage with the "safety gradient" counterargument—the practical difficulty of defining boundaries between anti-aesthetic art and genuinely harmful content at scale—and discuss how user-controllable mechanisms (already proposed in the conclusion) could operationalize this distinction.
