## Summary

This paper systematically evaluates nine open-source mid-sized LLMs (12B–16B parameters) on two reverse engineering tasks—name recovery and type inference from stripped binaries—across multiple architectures, optimization levels, and decompilers. The central finding is that all models perform very poorly without fine-tuning (F1 ≈ 0.03), and even with LoRA fine-tuning, performance remains limited, with the best results around F1 ≈ 0.33 for variable name recovery and below 0.1 for type inference.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated research question.** The paper clearly identifies a genuine gap: prior reverse engineering works (SYMGEN, ReSYM) select LLMs without justification, and no systematic comparison exists across models for both name recovery and type inference. This is a real and practical concern for the community.
- **Broad experimental scope.** The evaluation covers 9 models, 4 architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM32, MIPS32), 4 optimization levels, 2 decompilers (Ghidra, IDA Pro), assembly code, and real-world firmware—yielding 40,000 test samples and 160,000 fine-tuning samples. This breadth is commendable.
- **Useful finding on CodeWordNet.** The ablation showing that CodeWordNet is essential for fair name recovery evaluation (Figure 1) is a concrete and actionable contribution for future benchmarking in this space.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Shallow analysis of negative results.** The paper's main conclusion is that LLMs perform poorly, but provides limited insight into *why*. There is no systematic failure mode analysis, no investigation of what types of functions/variables are harder, and no exploration of whether the prompt design, task formulation, or evaluation methodology might be the bottleneck rather than the models themselves. The "findings" (1–8) are largely descriptive observations rather than analytically grounded insights.

- **No comparison with existing baselines.** The paper does not compare LLM performance against any prior reverse engineering methods (SYMGEN, ReSYM, VARBERT, SymLM, Debin, etc.) or traditional ML approaches. Without this context, it is impossible to judge whether the low F1 scores reflect a fundamental LLM limitation or simply a suboptimal experimental setup. This is a critical omission for a benchmarking paper.

- **Unvalidated auxiliary LLM for post-processing.** The use of a second LLM to normalize outputs across models introduces an uncontrolled source of error and potential bias. The paper does not validate this pipeline (e.g., by checking agreement with human judgment or a deterministic parser on a subset), nor does it report which auxiliary model was used. If the auxiliary LLM systematically misparses certain model outputs, the reported F1 scores could be misleading.

- **Minimal prompt engineering.** The paper uses a single fixed prompt template without any optimization, acknowledging this explicitly. Given that prompt engineering is well-known to dramatically affect LLM performance (especially for instruction-following), and that the models show wildly different formatting behaviors, the lack of even basic prompt adaptation weakens the claim that the results reflect true model capability rather than prompt-model mismatch.

- **Inconsistent and unexplained fine-tuning results.** Fine-tuning sometimes degrades performance (e.g., DeepSeek-V2 under O1 variable name recovery drops from 0.10 to 0.05; several models show 0.00 after fine-tuning on type inference). The paper does not investigate these anomalies—whether they stem from overfitting, data distribution issues, or LoRA configuration problems. This undermines confidence in the fine-tuning evaluation.

### Minor

- **Narrow model size comparison.** Finding 4 ("larger model size does not guarantee better performance") is based solely on CodeLlama 7B/13B/34B from the same family. This is too narrow a comparison to support such a general claim, especially since the 70B variant was excluded.

- **Exclusion of larger/proprietary models without adequate justification.** The paper excludes GPT-4 and other large models because they are "not free" and "not adequate for comparison." However, including even a few results from GPT-4 or Claude would have provided a valuable upper-bound reference point and strengthened the benchmarking contribution.

- **Real-world firmware results are underexplored.** Table 6 shows near-zero performance on real firmware, and the only observation is that IDA Pro outputs yield slightly better results than Ghidra. There is no analysis of what makes real-world firmware harder (e.g., stripped library functions, obfuscation, unfamiliar code patterns).

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison with at least one traditional ML baseline (e.g., a graph neural network or transformer trained specifically for binary analysis) to contextualize the LLM results.
- Error analysis breaking down performance by function complexity, name length, or code pattern type.
- Validation of the auxiliary LLM post-processing pipeline against human annotations.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most novel contribution is the empirical demonstration that CodeWordNet-based semantic matching is essential for fair evaluation of name recovery—without it, models that generate semantically correct but lexically different names are unfairly penalized. The observation that DeepSeek-R1 (a reasoning-augmented model) underperforms its base model Qwen2.5 on these tasks, despite identical architecture, is also noteworthy and suggests that reasoning-oriented training may not transfer well to binary analysis. However, neither of these insights is deeply investigated.

## Suggestions

- Add a comparison with at least 2–3 existing reverse engineering baselines (e.g., ReSYM, SymLM) on the same dataset to contextualize the LLM results.
- Validate the auxiliary LLM post-processing pipeline by comparing its outputs against a deterministic parser or human annotations on a sample of responses.
- Investigate the failure cases more deeply: categorize errors by type (formatting failures, semantic errors, truncation, refusals) and report their relative frequency.
- Experiment with at least basic prompt engineering variations to rule out prompt-model mismatch as a confound.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses a legitimate gap and has reasonable experimental breadth, but the analysis is too shallow for a top venue. The absence of baseline comparisons, the unvalidated post-processing pipeline, and the lack of insight into *why* models fail (beyond "they just do") significantly limit the paper's contribution. The findings are mostly descriptive rather than explanatory, and the practical guidance for future work is limited to general suggestions like "better training data" and "better fine-tuning strategies."
