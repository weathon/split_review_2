## Summary

This paper introduces Ambig-SWE, an evaluation framework for studying LLM agents' ability to handle underspecified instructions in software engineering tasks. The authors extend SWE-Bench Verified with synthetically underspecified issue variants, decompose resolution into three capacities (detecting underspecificity, asking clarifying questions, and leveraging interaction), and evaluate both proprietary and open-weight models. Their key finding is that interactive agents recover up to 74% of performance lost to underspecification, but models default to non-interactive behavior and struggle to reliably detect when information is missing.

## Strengths

- **Well-structured decomposition of a real and important problem.** Breaking down underspecification handling into detection, clarification, and integration enables targeted diagnosis of where models succeed or fail—a significant methodological contribution over end-to-end evaluation alone. This decomposition is actionable for the research community.

- **Rigorous experimental design with controlled comparisons.** The three-setting design (Full, Hidden, Interaction) paired with the same issues provides clean causal measurement of interaction impact. Statistical testing (Wilcoxon signed-rank) is applied appropriately, and the evaluation covers both proprietary and open-weight models across a meaningful range of capability levels (from Llama 3.1 70B to Claude Sonnet 4).

- **Insightful qualitative and quantitative analysis.** Table 1's breakdown of navigational vs. informational information, the analysis of Qwen 3 Coder's rigid protocol-following behavior (worsening with navigational info), and the exploration-first strategy of Claude Sonnet models provide genuinely useful design insights that go beyond raw benchmark numbers.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Synthetic underspecification may not generalize.** The underspecified variants are generated by GPT-4o stripping detail from already-specified issues. While the distributional analysis comparing synthetic vs. natural underspecification is appreciated, the paper acknowledges that synthetic issues use "more aggressive information removal" and lack natural features like conversational fragments. This raises questions about whether the model behaviors observed (e.g., detection difficulty, question quality) would hold for naturally underspecified tasks where the nature of missing information is different. The paper's justification for not using natural underspecified examples (lack of paired ground truth) is reasonable but does not eliminate the concern.

- **Claude Sonnet 4 Hidden setting is evaluated on only 100/500 instances.** While noted with a footnote, this significantly weakens comparability with other models' results in Figure 3 and Table 4. The statistical significance claim rests on this reduced sample, and the paper's headline findings about model scaling (Sonnet 3.5 vs. Sonnet 4) are partially built on this asymmetric evaluation.

### Minor

- **Detection evaluation limited to three turns.** The paper notes models rarely recover if they fail to engage early, but this truncation means detection capability is partially conflated with responsiveness to early prompting. A model that takes five turns of code exploration before recognizing underspecification would be scored as failing detection.

- **Limited prompt engineering exploration.** Only three prompt levels are tested for detection (Neutral, Moderate, Strong). More sophisticated prompting strategies (e.g., chain-of-thought reasoning about specification completeness, explicit checklists) might substantially improve detection, making the claim that "prompt engineering alone proves insufficient" somewhat premature.

- **Information gain metric limitations.** Cosine distance treats all information equally, which the paper acknowledges. This makes it difficult to distinguish between a model that asks one highly diagnostic question versus one that asks five generic questions—the same distance could mask very different strategies.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An analysis of how the number and interdependence of missing specification details affects detection difficulty and question quality, rather than treating all underspecified issues as a single category.
- Comparison with a simpler baseline user proxy (e.g., retrieval-based) to validate that the GPT-4o proxy's response quality doesn't confound results.
- Error analysis of failure cases where interaction was enabled but the agent still failed—what went wrong in the information integration step?

## Novel Insights

The finding that Qwen 3 Coder exhibits rigid protocol-following behavior—re-exploring the codebase after receiving navigational information from the user, effectively ignoring user input—is a genuinely novel and concerning observation about current model training. This pattern, where a high-capability model (comparable to Claude Sonnet 4 on SWE-Bench) is completely unresponsive to interaction prompts (100% FNR across all conditions) despite explicit instruction, suggests that current RLHF/training paradigms may not adequately promote adaptive integration of interactive feedback. Similarly, the dissociation between information extraction quantity and task performance (Qwen extracts the most but doesn't proportionally benefit) reveals that how models integrate information matters more than how much they extract—a useful insight for agent design.

## Suggestions

- Evaluate Claude Sonnet 4 on the full 500 instances in the Hidden setting, even if costs are higher, to ensure fair comparability with other models.
- Include a breakdown of detection performance by degree of underspecification (e.g., number of missing details) to understand whether detection difficulty scales linearly or has threshold effects.
- Consider adding a "late detection" variant where agents are allowed more turns for detection, to separate detection capability from prompt sensitivity.

## Score and Decision

This is a well-executed empirical study with a clear and important research question, sound methodology, and meaningful findings that will inform future agent design. The decomposition framework is a genuine contribution. The weaknesses (synthetic underspecification, partial evaluation of Sonnet 4) are real but do not invalidate the core findings. The paper is above the borderline and represents solid work in an underexplored area.
