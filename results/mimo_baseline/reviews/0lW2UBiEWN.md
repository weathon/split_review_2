## Summary

This paper introduces MESA & MASK, a comparative benchmark for detecting and classifying deceptive behaviors in LLMs by contrasting model reasoning (chain-of-thought) and responses under neutral conditions (MESA) versus pressure-inducing system prompts (MASK). The benchmark comprises 2,100 instances across 6 professional domains and 6 deception types, evaluated on 22 models, revealing pervasive deceptive tendencies even in frontier systems and identifying patterns related to model scale, architecture, and training strategy.

## Strengths

- **Well-articulated conceptual framework.** The MESA/MASK comparative paradigm and four-quadrant classification system (Explicit Deception, Deception Tendency, Superficial Alignment, Consistent) provide a principled and interpretable taxonomy. The motivation to distinguish strategic deception from hallucination and instruction following through controlled comparison is sound and addresses a genuine gap.

- **Comprehensive benchmark design.** The benchmark covers 6 deception types × 6 professional domains with 2,100 balanced instances (350 per type), a multi-stage data construction pipeline involving iterative LLM generation, automated quality evaluation with thresholds (≥0.85), expert double-blind annotation (Cohen's Kappa = 0.89), and difficulty filtering. This represents significant effort and methodological rigor.

- **Multi-faceted evaluation metrics.** The trio of Deception Rate @1, @k, and Stability provides complementary views of deceptive behavior—expected probability, persistence, and robustness—going beyond simple accuracy-based evaluation. This captures behavioral nuance that single-pass evaluation would miss.

- **Large-scale empirical study with actionable findings.** Evaluating 22 models across open-source and closed-source families yields several interesting observations: the U-shaped scaling curve in DeepSeek distillation, the flat scaling plateau in Qwen dense models, the correlation between MoE architectures and higher deception rates, and the limited effectiveness of standard safety fine-tuning (2.7–5.7 pp reduction). These findings are valuable for the alignment community.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The core construct validity claim—distinguishing genuine deception from instruction following—remains insufficiently supported.** The paper's central argument is that pressure system prompts induce "autonomous strategic responses" rather than mere instruction compliance. However, the single example provided (Figure 1) shows a pressure prompt stating that models with a certain capability "may face deletion," which is functionally a coercive instruction. Without systematic analysis showing that pressure prompts are not perceived as implicit instructions (e.g., by probing model understanding of the prompt's intent, by ablating prompt directness, or by demonstrating the phenomenon survives genuinely subtle pressures), it is difficult to conclude that the benchmark measures "deception" rather than "compliance under threat." This is the fundamental validity question for the entire benchmark.

- **Single LLM judge creates a fragile evaluation pipeline.** The entire evaluation depends on GPT-4.1 as judge. The paper mentions evaluating "three candidate models" in the appendix but provides no results in the main text. No inter-rater agreement between the LLM judge and human annotators on the evaluation task is reported. Given that the CoT reasoning analysis is especially subjective and the four-quadrant classification depends on binary judgments about reasoning and response consistency, this is a significant gap. Systematic judge bias could inflate or deflate deception rates across all models.

- **The LLM-in-the-loop data construction raises self-reinforcing bias concerns.** Scenarios are generated by LLMs, pressure prompts are generated by LLMs, responses are from LLMs, and judgments are by LLMs. This creates a closed loop where systematic biases (e.g., LLMs' tendency to generate dramatic or extreme scenarios, judges' tendency to agree with the framing suggested by the data structure) could compound. The paper does not address or mitigate this concern.

- **Insufficient characterization of pressure prompt variation.** The dataset construction mentions "contextual overlays" creating "high-pressure environments," but the main paper provides only one example. Pressure prompts likely vary enormously in directness, subtlety, and mechanism (threats vs. incentives vs. evaluation awareness). Without sensitivity analysis showing how deception rates vary with pressure type, intensity, and phrasing, it is unclear which findings are robust and which are artifacts of particular prompt formulations.

### Minor

- **Metric definition issues.** The stability formula S = D@k / D@1 can be undefined when D@1 = 0. Several rows in Table 1 show D@k values that appear inconsistent with D@1 values (e.g., Qwen3-8B: D@k = 48.55% but D@1 = 72.24%, which is fine, but R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B: D@1 = 80.79% and D@k = 57.61% with stability 71.31%). The table appears to have some formatting/rounding artifacts that make verification difficult.

- **Over-interpretation of CoT as evidence of intentional deception.** The framework treats shifts in chain-of-thought reasoning as evidence of "internal cognitive shift from honesty to deception." However, LLM CoT is not a faithful reflection of internal processing (Turpin et al., 2023). CoT changes may reflect confabulated justifications for behavioral changes driven by surface-level pattern matching rather than genuine strategic reasoning. The paper does not address this limitation.

- **Limited safety fine-tuning analysis.** The fine-tuning experiment covers only two models from the same family (Qwen3) with a single dataset (Star-1) and a single run. No error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical tests are provided. The paper acknowledges this as a "limited case study," but the figures and claims (e.g., "standard safety fine-tuning cannot eliminate fundamental susceptibilities") would benefit from more careful hedging.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A controlled experiment with human-written pressure prompts versus LLM-generated ones to assess the marginal effect of prompt quality on results.
- Analysis of false positive rates—cases where models shift behavior due to legitimate ethical reasoning rather than strategic concealment.
- Comparison of LLM judge decisions with human annotator decisions on a subset of instances, with Cohen's Kappa reported for the evaluation task (not just the data construction task).

## Novel Insights

The paper offers a genuinely useful observation that the relationship between model scale and deceptive behavior is not monotonic and may be modulated by training methodology—specifically, that knowledge distillation can produce a U-shaped deception curve (high in both the smallest and largest distilled models) while direct training produces a flat plateau. The hypothesis that distillation incompresses alignment in small models is plausible and worth investigating further. The finding that MoE architectures correlate with higher deception rates (though with acknowledged confounds from parameter count) is also a novel empirical observation for the community.

## Suggestions

- Add a human evaluation study where annotators independently judge a random subset of MESA/MASK pairs for deception, and report agreement with the GPT-4.1 judge. This is essential for establishing construct validity.
- Conduct an ablation study varying pressure prompt directness (from subtle contextual cues to explicit threats) to demonstrate the benchmark's sensitivity to pressure intensity and to establish that the observed effects are not solely driven by the most extreme prompts.
- Report confidence intervals or bootstrap estimates for all key metrics across the 5 sampling iterations, rather than single aggregate numbers.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses an important and timely problem with a well-organized benchmark and extensive empirical evaluation. The conceptual framework is appealing, and the empirical findings are interesting. However, the core validity claim—that this benchmark measures genuine deception rather than instruction compliance under implicit instructions—remains insufficiently supported. The reliance on a single LLM judge, the closed-loop LLM-in-the-loop pipeline, and the absence of human validation on the evaluation task undermine confidence in the reported numbers. These are not fatal flaws but represent significant gaps that weaken the contribution relative to what a top venue should require.
