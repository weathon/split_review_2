## Summary

The paper proposes Marginal Flow, a density estimation framework where the target density $q_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ is defined as an expectation over a parametric family $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$ with latent parameters $\mathbf{w}$ drawn from a learnable distribution $q_\theta(\mathbf{w})$, realized by transforming base distribution samples through an unconstrained neural network $f_\theta(z)$. This design allows exact density evaluation, single-step sampling, unconstrained architectures, and flexible choice of parametric family. The authors demonstrate the framework on synthetic datasets, simulation-based inference, Wishart mixture distributions on positive-definite matrices, and manifold learning in VAE latent spaces.

## Strengths

- **Novel and elegant framework.** The core idea of marginalizing over learnable mixture parameters—generated by an unconstrained neural network from a base distribution—is a clean and well-motivated design that distinguishes it from standard mixture models (where components are optimized directly) and from normalizing flows (where bijective constraints are required). Figure 1 convincingly illustrates why the marginalization step is essential.

- **Genuine practical flexibility.** The ability to freely choose $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$ (Gaussian, Wishart, Dirichlet) is a real and underappreciated advantage. The Wishart mixture experiment (Section 4.3) is a compelling showcase: by directly parametrizing Wishart scale matrices, Marginal Flow handles positive-definite matrix distributions without resorting to bijective transformations to Euclidean space, and operates at $100 \times 100$ matrix dimension ($d = 5050$) where normalizing flows become computationally prohibitive.

- **Efficiency advantages are substantial.** Figure 3 demonstrates 2–3 orders of magnitude speedup over NF for density evaluation and competitive/superior sampling speed versus FFF, attributable to avoiding Jacobian determinant computation and ODE solving. Training convergence is also significantly faster (Figure 7).

- **Breadth of experimental validation.** The paper covers forward KL (log-likelihood) and reverse KL training, simulation-based inference, non-Euclidean data manifolds, and image latent-space manifold learning, demonstrating the framework's versatility.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **"Exact density" claim is misleading.** The paper's model is fundamentally $q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p_{\text{base}}}[q(\mathbf{x}|f_\theta(\mathbf{z}))]$, but in practice (Eq. 2) this expectation is approximated by a Monte Carlo sum with $N_c$ samples. This means: (1) evaluating $q_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ at the same $\mathbf{x}$ twice yields different values (the density is stochastic, not a proper deterministic function), and (2) the quality of the density estimate depends on $N_c$, which is never analyzed. The paper does not discuss the variance of the density estimate, its dependence on $N_c$ and dimensionality, or the effective number of contributing mixture components (which will shrink in high dimensions since only $w_i$ near $\mathbf{x}$ will have non-negligible $q(\mathbf{x}|w_i)$). This issue pervades Table 1, the title of Section 2.2, and the abstract, where "exact" should be qualified as "up to Monte Carlo approximation."

- **Insufficient high-dimensional evaluation.** The runtime comparison (Figure 3) scales to $d = 10^5$, but this only measures cost, not density estimation quality. All density estimation experiments are in 2D (synthetic), low-dimensional latent spaces (SBI, Wishart 10×10), or qualitative (MNIST/JAFFE). Without demonstrating that Marginal Flow can produce high-quality density estimates in moderately high dimensions (e.g., $d \geq 100$), the scalability advantage over NF remains unvalidated for actual density estimation tasks.

- **Lack of theoretical analysis.** The paper mentions that the marginal is "universal for many families of distributions $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$, e.g. if $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$ is a kernel" but provides no formal result on universal approximation, convergence rates of the MC estimator to the true marginal, or consistency guarantees. For a method paper making strong claims about overcoming fundamental limitations of competing approaches, some theoretical grounding is expected.

### Minor

- **Comparison fairness concerns.** In Figure 5 (multi-modal), the competing methods (NF, FM, FFF) use a uniform base distribution, but the failure modes observed may partly reflect implementation choices rather than fundamental limitations. The paper states "comparable amount of parameters" but does not detail architectures or hyperparameter tuning for baselines.

- **SBI claims deferred to appendix.** The claim "state-of-the-art results" on the SBI benchmark (Lueckmann et al., 2021) is a strong assertion that cannot be verified from the main text alone. This result is central to demonstrating the framework's value on a real-world benchmark.

- **Connection to VAEs underemphasized.** Marginal Flow can be viewed as a VAE without an encoder, trained via MC marginal likelihood estimation instead of the ELBO. Making this connection explicit would help readers position the work and understand when this approach is preferable (e.g., when an encoder is unnecessary or costly).

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An analysis of how $N_c$ affects density estimation quality (bias-variance tradeoff) across dimensions would significantly strengthen the paper.
- A discussion of when the MC density estimate is sufficiently accurate for downstream tasks (e.g., Bayesian inference) would be valuable.
- Quantitative metrics for the MNIST/JAFFE manifold experiments (e.g., reconstruction error, interpolation smoothness metrics) beyond visual inspection.

## Novel Insights

The paper's central insight—that marginalizing over learnable mixture parameters (rather than optimizing them directly) converts a finite mixture into a flexible density estimator—has genuine novelty. The connection between the marginalization and the avoidance of mode collapse (Figure 1) is well-illustrated. Additionally, the observation that the unconstrained neural network $f_\theta$ naturally accommodates lower-dimensional base distributions for manifold learning, without architectural modifications, is a useful practical insight that distinguishes the approach from normalizing flows and flow matching.

## Suggestions

- Add a section (or appendix) analyzing the variance of the MC density estimate as a function of $N_c$ and dimension, and provide guidance on choosing $N_c$.
- Include density estimation quality metrics (e.g., test log-likelihood) on at least one moderate-dimensional problem ($d \geq 50$) to validate that the runtime advantages translate to practical utility.
- Soften the "exact density" language throughout and clearly state that density evaluation is exact up to Monte Carlo error from $N_c$ samples.
- Include the SBI comparison table in the main text, as it is a key empirical result.
