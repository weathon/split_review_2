## Summary
The paper presents O-Forge, a tool that couples frontier LLMs with Mathematica's `Resolve` function to prove asymptotic inequalities. The LLM proposes domain decompositions (or series decompositions), and Mathematica's quantifier elimination rigorously verifies the inequality on each subdomain. Two case studies from Terry Tao's work are demonstrated, and the authors claim this is among the first AI-powered tools useful for research-level mathematics.

## Strengths
- **Compelling motivation and practical vision.** The core idea—LLMs handle the creative step of guessing decompositions while a CAS handles rigorous verification—is well-motivated and clearly positioned relative to Tao's stated desiderata for AI math tools. The divide-and-conquer framing for asymptotic analysis is insightful.
- **Well-reasoned engineering choices.** The paper provides a thorough justification for choosing Mathematica's `Resolve` over Lean tactics and SMT solvers (Z3, CVC5, MetiTarski), with concrete examples of where alternatives fail (e.g., transcendental functions). This is a genuine strength of the system design.
- **Accessible deployment.** The availability of a web interface at o-forge.com lowers the barrier to entry, which is valuable for mathematicians without coding experience. This increases potential real-world adoption.

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Essentially no quantitative evaluation.** Section 5 mentions "around 40-50 easier problems" but reports zero concrete results—no success rates, no tables, no metrics, no failure analysis. For a tool paper, this is a critical gap. The reader cannot assess how often the LLM finds a correct decomposition, how often `Resolve` succeeds or fails, or how the tool performs across problem difficulty levels.
- **No baseline comparisons.** There is no systematic comparison against: (a) LLMs alone (without CAS verification), (b) other CAS approaches (e.g., Maple's `QuantifierElimination`, SageMath), (c) Lean-based autoformalization, or (d) SMT solvers with different encodings. Without baselines, the claimed superiority of the approach is unsubstantiated.
- **Claims exceed evidence.** The paper asserts this is "one of the first AI-powered tools that is useful for research-level mathematics today" and claims it can save mathematicians "several hours and days." However, only two hand-picked case studies are shown in detail (both from Tao), and these involve inequalities with short proofs once the decomposition is known. The gap between demonstrating two examples and establishing broad research utility is large.
- **LLM reliability is unquantified.** The LLM's decomposition proposal is described as the "bottleneck," yet the paper provides no data on how reliably frontier LLMs find correct decompositions. How many attempts does it take? What happens on failure? This is the central technical question and it remains unanswered.

### Minor
- **Prompt template redacted.** The structured prompt shown in Section 4 is mostly replaced with "-" placeholders, making it impossible to assess or reproduce the LLM interaction design.
- **The two case studies are partly human-authored proofs.** Case Study 1's "extremely short proof" is written by the authors, not generated by the tool. The paper does not show the actual tool output (e.g., the LLM's raw decomposition proposal, the Mathematica verification log) for either case study, making it difficult to evaluate the automated pipeline.
- **Inconsistent website references.** The paper mentions both o-forge.com and o-forge.net.

### Trivial
- Minor formatting issues from PDF parsing (e.g., fragmented code blocks) which are parser artifacts, not paper flaws.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A table cataloging all tested problems with decomposition proposed, success/failure, number of subdomains, and verification time.
- Analysis of failure modes: when does the LLM propose incorrect decompositions? When does `Resolve` time out or fail?
- Discussion of how the constant C is found (the paper mentions searching over a grid but doesn't discuss efficiency).

## Novel Insights
The observation that Mathematica's `Resolve` function, through quantifier elimination over the reals, is substantially more capable than SMT solvers and Lean tactics for proving inequalities involving transcendental functions is a genuinely useful engineering insight for the AI-for-math community. The argument that LLMs should be restricted to proposing decompositions (rather than full proofs) and that verification should be entirely delegated to a CAS is a sound design principle, though not entirely novel given AlphaGeometry's precedent.

## Suggestions
- Add a quantitative evaluation section with a table of at least 20-30 problems showing: problem statement, LLM decomposition (success/fail), number of subdomains, Resolve outcome, and total time. This is the single most important improvement.
- Report LLM decomposition success rates (e.g., over 5 independent runs per problem) to characterize reliability.
- Include at least one comparison against a baseline (e.g., LLM alone producing a full proof, or a naive decomposition strategy).
- Show the actual tool output for the two Tao case studies to demonstrate the end-to-end pipeline.

## Score and Decision
The paper presents a sound and well-motivated idea, but the evaluation is far too thin for a top venue. With no quantitative results, no baselines, and only two illustrative case studies, the reader cannot assess the tool's reliability, generality, or practical value. The strong claims about research utility are not substantiated by the evidence presented. This reads more as a system demonstration or workshop paper than a complete research contribution.
