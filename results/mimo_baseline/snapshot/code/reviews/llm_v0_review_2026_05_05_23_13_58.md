## Summary

This position paper argues that over-alignment—the tendency of LLMs to give excessively cautious responses to health-related queries—is itself harmful, unethical, and dangerous, particularly for users with anxiety or OCD. The paper draws on literature from clinical psychology (cyberchondria, health anxiety), AI alignment (value pluralism), and philosophical ethics (utilitarianism, Kantianism, virtue theory) to argue that "better safe than sorry" strategies can reinforce pathological anxiety cycles. It illustrates the phenomenon with case studies of three models (Gemini, GPT-5, Qwen-3) and a small 21-query quantitative evaluation, and addresses counterarguments in a rebuttal section.

## Strengths

- **Clear, important, and discussable position**: The paper identifies a genuine blind spot in AI safety discourse—the overwhelming focus on under-cautious behavior—and argues that over-caution can itself cause harm to vulnerable populations. This is a concrete, falsifiable, and debatable claim that invites productive disagreement, exactly what a position paper should do.

- **Strong engagement with counterarguments (Section 6)**: The rebuttal to "people with anxiety/OCD should not use LLMs for reassurance" is the paper's most compelling argument, grounding the ethical case in empirical evidence: OCD diagnosis takes 5–15+ years (Bey et al., 2025; Ziegler et al., 2021), fewer than 42.7% of OCD patients ever access mental health services (Mack et al., 2014), and fewer than 4% of online health seekers recognize reassurance-seeking as harmful (Mohammed et al., 2019). This makes the argument that responsibility cannot rest solely on users concrete and empirically grounded.

- **Effective cross-disciplinary synthesis**: The paper connects clinical psychology (cyberchondria, health anxiety, OCD literature), philosophical ethics, and AI alignment research in a way that is genuinely integrative rather than merely additive. The observation that 30–50% of health-related searchers already have clinically relevant health anxiety (Wetzel et al., 2024; Mohammed et al., 2019) directly reframes the question from "how should models serve typical users?" to "how should models serve a user population that may be disproportionately anxious?"

- **Concrete, illustrative case studies**: The rabies, chest pain, and Naegleriasis cases effectively demonstrate the spectrum of over-caution—from completely failing to recognize near-zero risk (Gemini on rabies) to acknowledging low risk but still triggering symptom-monitoring anxiety (Gemini on Naegleriasis).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Core tension between reassurance-seeking and over-caution remains unresolved**: The paper's central argument is that LLMs should provide less over-cautious, more contextually appropriate responses—yet it also acknowledges that "OCD and anxiety patients should avoid seeking reassurance from LLMs" (Section 9) and that reassurance-seeking is therapeutically counterproductive (Section 6). The preferred responses in the case studies (e.g., GPT stating "the emergency risk was low" and Qwen affirming "strong evidence against life-threatening issues") are themselves forms of reassurance that could reinforce compulsive checking. Without a principled account of when reassurance is therapeutically appropriate versus when it enables pathological behavior, the paper critiques "over-alignment" without clearly specifying what "appropriate alignment" looks like for the very populations it centers. This is not a peripheral concern—it is at the heart of the problem the paper identifies. The paper would be substantially stronger with even a rough framework (e.g., drawing on clinical communication literature about graded exposure versus reassurance) that delineates what a better response pattern would be.

- **Distinct phenomena conflated under "over-alignment"**: The paper groups together over-refusal (Gemini refusing to assess near-zero rabies risk), poor risk calibration (Gemini stating extremely low risk but still recommending immediate medical attention), failure to recognize anxiety cues (GPT/Qwen providing reasonable risk assessments without recognizing possible OCD), and corporate liability-driven conservatism—treating all of these as manifestations of "over-alignment." These are not obviously the same failure mode, and lumping them together makes the diagnosis less precise and the prescription less targeted. Over-refusal may require different interventions than poor calibration or failure to detect user distress signals.

### Minor

- **Assertion of corporate intent is inadequately supported**: The claim that over-cautious responses are "designed primarily to protect companies rather than users' actual safety and well-being" (lines 165–167) is a strong factual assertion about developer intent rather than an argument that invites debate. The more defensible claim—that over-cautious responses *have the effect* of protecting companies more than helping vulnerable users—is already supported by the paper's reasoning and does not require attributing intent.

- **Harm analogy from symptom checkers is extrapolative but not established**: The paper argues that "LLMs could have similar effects as online symptom checkers" (Section 3) in causing or worsening anxiety. This extrapolation from the symptom-checker literature to LLM interactions is plausible but not directly established. The paper would benefit from acknowledging this inferential gap more explicitly rather than treating the analogy as established.

- **Quantitative results section adds little and may distract**: The 21-query experiment with single-author labeling, low inter-rater reliability, and unclear inclusion criteria (IoU and kappa thresholds that are hard to interpret from the description) does not materially strengthen the position. The paper explicitly frames itself as "qualitative-first," and the quantitative section's thinness risks undermining the paper's credibility more than it bolsters the argument.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A framework—even a rough one—drawing on clinical communication or exposure therapy literature that distinguishes therapeutic reassurance from reassurance that reinforces compulsive behavior would significantly strengthen the paper's contribution.
- The paper could specify whether it is arguing for (a) personalized responses that detect user anxiety, (b) improved contextual reasoning without personalization, or (c) domain-specific health models. It gestures at all three (Appendix B, Section 6) but does not commit, which leaves the prescriptive part underdeveloped.
- Distinguishing the distinct failure modes (over-refusal vs. poor calibration vs. lack of anxiety detection) and discussing their potentially different remedies would sharpen the argument.

## Novel Insights

The most novel insight is the reframing of the "better safe than sorry" heuristic as actively harmful for a substantial subpopulation of health-seeking users (30–50% of whom already have clinically relevant health anxiety, per Wetzel et al., 2024). This transforms over-alignment from a mere inconvenience into a design failure with disproportionate impact on vulnerable users who are precisely the ones most likely to interact with LLMs for health information. The empirical observation about diagnosis delays (5–15+ years) and low treatment access (<42.7% ever accessing services) further strengthens the case that "users should just avoid LLMs" is not a viable design response.

## Suggestions

- Add a brief framework (even 1–2 paragraphs) drawing on clinical literature about the difference between therapeutic reassurance (which reduces anxiety through graded, scaffolded information) and pathological reassurance (which enables compulsive checking), and explain how LLM responses could distinguish these. This would address the core tension.
- Separate the discussion into distinct failure modes (over-refusal, poor risk calibration, failure to detect anxiety) and discuss what different technical or design interventions each would require.
- Consider removing or substantially condensing the quantitative results section; it currently reads as a methodological liability for what is otherwise a qualitative position paper, and the main argument does not depend on it.
