## Summary

This paper presents the first systematic empirical study of whether Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) benefit from prompt optimization and whether they serve as superior prompt optimizers, using event extraction (ACE05) as the primary testbed with two additional tasks for generalization. Using a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) framework, the authors compare two LRMs (DeepSeek-R1, o1) and two LLMs (GPT-4.5, GPT-4o) in both task-model and optimizer roles, finding that LRMs benefit substantially more from prompt optimization and produce higher-quality prompts that generalize across tasks and models.

---

## Strengths

- **Large, consistent quantitative gains for LRMs across multiple conditions.** Table 1 (dev set) shows DeepSeek-R1 improving from a baseline of 16.45 to 44.26 AC (+27.81%) under full MCTS on ACE_med, and o1 from 13.94 to 39.81 (+25.87%), while LLMs top out at ~+21%. This is reproduced on the test set (DS-R1: 16.00 → 43.75), and the findings hold across low- and medium-resource settings and under both shallow and full search.

- **LRMs as superior prompt optimizers, demonstrated across all task models.** In the low-resource setting (ACE_low, depth 1), DS-R1 as optimizer consistently yields the highest AC for every task model (+7.47 for GPT-4o, +5.84 for GPT-4.5, +7.98 for o1, +8.21 for itself vs. best LLM optimizer). The convergence analysis (Fig. 4) further shows that DS-R1-optimized trajectories converge faster and with lower variance than GPT-4.5-optimized ones.

- **Qualitative mechanism via Table 2.** The side-by-side comparison of prompts generated by different optimizers for the same task model (DS-R1 as M_task) provides concrete evidence that LRM-generated prompts contain more actionable extraction rules, exception handling, and span normalization examples than LLM-generated prompts, giving a mechanistic explanation beyond raw scores.

- **Multi-faceted experimental design.** The study varies training set size (ACE_low vs. ACE_med), search depth (1 vs. 5), and model roles, which isolates different factors. The additional generalization experiments (Table 3) on Geometric Shapes and NCBI Disease NER confirm that the core LRM-as-task-model finding holds beyond schema-based tasks.

- **Error analysis linking prompt quality to specific error reduction.** Figure 5c shows quantitatively that LRM-optimized prompts reduce multiple/implicit event errors and argument overprediction, directly connecting the "why" of the qualitative analysis to observable error categories.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Internally inconsistent No Opt. baseline for GPT-4o in the ACE_med depth-1 section of Table 1.** The No Opt. value for GPT-4o appears as 26.30 in the ACE_med depth-1 section but as 12.68 in both the ACE_low depth-1 and ACE_med depth-5 sections — all supposedly measuring the same zero-shot GPT-4o on the same held-out 100-example dev set. Arithmetic verification confirms the problem: the stated improvements of +14.86 (GPT-4.5) and +12.42 (DS-R1) are consistent with No Opt = 12.68, but +4.98 (GPT-4o self-opt) does not match either value, and the o1-optimized result (26.30) equals the stated baseline. The most likely cause is a misplaced cell value, with the o1-optimized result substituting the baseline. The three other models' deltas check out arithmetically in that section, so this error is isolated to GPT-4o in ACE_med depth-1. The main LRM claims are not invalidated (they rest on DS-R1 and o1 rows, which are internally consistent), but this error must be corrected and the true ACE_med No Opt value for GPT-4o disclosed with explanation.

### Minor

- **DeepSeek-R1 is run at 2.5-bit quantization; no evaluation of degradation on structured prediction tasks is provided.** The paper acknowledges this (Section 4.1: "we quantize DeepSeek-R1 to 2.5 bits…which has shown minimal degradation in reasoning tasks even at lower precisions") and cites benchmarks at 1.58-bit. However, span-accuracy and output-format adherence in structured prediction may be more sensitive to weight precision than the reasoning benchmarks used for validation. The paper provides no EE-specific quantization ablation. This is unlikely to change the qualitative direction (if full-precision DS-R1 is stronger, the LRM advantage would only grow), but the magnitude of the DS-R1 vs. o1 head-to-head is not interpretable as a fair comparison at the stated precision levels.

- **Generalization experiments (Table 3) test only self-optimization, not cross-model optimizer quality.** Table 3 covers RQ1 (LRMs benefit more from optimization) in a generalization setting, which it does well. But the paper's Conclusion states LRMs "serve as strong agents for prompt optimization across diverse tasks" — the cross-model optimizer advantage (RQ3) is not tested in Geometric Shapes or NCBI. An explicit scope qualifier would align the generalization claim with what is actually tested.

- **The 10 ACE05 event types are not identified in the main text.** Section 4.1 notes the restriction to 10 of 33 event types for prompt length reasons but does not name them. The selection criteria given ("prioritizing those with higher densities of event and argument annotations") describe training instance selection, not event type selection. Without knowing which types were included, the representativeness of the subset cannot be assessed.

### Trivial

- The paper's text at line 175 contains a typo: "full MSTC prompt optimization" should be "full MCTS."
- The motivating sources in the Introduction (Wang et al., 2024a; OpenAI, 2025; Mantaras, 2025; Together AI, 2025; Menendez et al., 2025) appear to be a mix of blog posts and informal claims. Framing these as the basis for a formal "argument" is a minor overstatement; the empirical question is real regardless of whether the claim was formally proposed.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A systematic categorization of *what types of edits* LRM vs. LLM optimizers introduce across multiple optimization steps (e.g., proportion of span-normalization rules, format instructions, illustrative examples) would substantiate the mechanistic claim beyond the single qualitative example in Table 2.
- The depth-1 vs. depth-5 finding (roughly 85% of full-MCTS gain achieved at depth 1) is practically important and could be elevated to a clearer thesis rather than reported as a secondary observation about diminishing returns.
- Extending the error analysis of Fig. 5c to the generalization tasks (Geometric Shapes, NCBI) would strengthen the mechanistic argument that LRM-optimized prompts reduce the same error categories across domains.
- A brief quantization ablation on at least one task (even on a small dev split) would address the methodological gap about DS-R1 precision, even if the results are expected to be small.

## Novel Insights

The most distinctive observation from combining both reviews is the depth-1 near-optimality finding: a single LRM refinement step captures approximately 85% of the full-MCTS improvement (DS-R1 goes from 40.00 at depth 1 to 44.26 at depth 5; LLMs gain ≤1%). This suggests that the primary source of the LRM optimizer advantage is not the multi-step MCTS structure itself but the quality of a single reasoning step — specifically, the model's ability to generate precise, exception-aware extraction rules from error feedback in one pass. This has practical implications: the cost of a full 5-depth MCTS search may not be justified when an LRM is available as a one-shot optimizer.
