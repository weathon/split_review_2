## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT), treating them as inverse tasks sharing a disentangled latent space of note-level score content and a global performance style embedding. Training uses both paired (ASAP) and large-scale unpaired (MuseScore, YouTube-transcribed) data via masked reconstruction objectives, without requiring fine-grained note-level alignment. A separate DDPM-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module is introduced to generate stylistically coherent embeddings from score content alone, enabling accessible automated rendering. The model achieves best overall MUSTER E_avg on APT and top subjective ratings on EPR, with supporting evidence for content-style disentanglement via latent-space probing.

---

## Strengths

- **Best overall APT accuracy on the holistic MUSTER metric.** Table 1 shows E_avg = 12.48, the best in the comparison. The model reduces onset deviation (16.26 vs. 17.48 of the Beyer & Dai end-to-end baseline, p<0.01) and extra notes (7.33 vs. 8.95), validating that alignment-free Seq2Seq training does not sacrifice structural transcription quality.

- **Strong EPR quality confirmed by objective and subjective evaluation.** Ours-Target achieves the lowest velocity KL divergence (1.76) and MAE (10.33) in Table 2, with variance values closest to human references. Subjective ratings in Figure 2 place both Ours-Target and Ours-PSR above both alignment-based baselines across all four dimensions (dynamics, tempo, style, overall).

- **Effective content-style disentanglement demonstrated through probing classifiers.** Style embeddings z_s achieve 77.46% composer identification accuracy and 42.07% performer accuracy (Table 4), while content representations z_c yield 29.99% and 9.94% — large separations that confirm the two representations encode fundamentally different information. Figure 3's qualitative clustering by composer and performer visually corroborates this.

- **Successful style transfer without quality degradation.** The style transfer listening test (Figure 5) shows that transferring a target performer's style increases similarity ratings (Samples 1 and 3) without a corresponding drop in overall quality, and that linear interpolation in style space produces smooth, natural outputs — evidence for a well-structured latent style manifold.

- **Principled alignment-free formulation.** Using sequence-aligned rather than note-aligned supervision removes dependence on brittle alignment preprocessing, enables use of large unpaired corpora, and still produces competitive or superior results on alignment, insertion, and missing rates (Table 3).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
*None.*

### Major

- **The core "joint training is mutually beneficial" claim is evidentially underspecified due to a confounded ablation.** §5.1 states: *"the consistent improvement of Ours-Target over the EPR-Only variant indicates that joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data, leads to better EPR performance."* EPR-Only is trained solely on ASAP; Ours-Target adds the APT objective **and** 75,913 unpaired MuseScore files **and** a YouTube-transcribed MIDI corpus. Two variables change simultaneously. The paper itself names both factors in the same sentence but attributes the result jointly to both, making it impossible to determine whether the APT loss contributes anything beyond the benefit of the additional data. Since "leveraging the duality between the two tasks for mutual supervision" is the first-listed contribution (§1), this confound is a material evidential gap, not a peripheral one. A control condition — EPR-only trained on the full data mixture without the APT objective — would resolve this.

### Minor

- **The PSR module's core claim (score-appropriate style generation) is evaluated only via indirect proxies.** The paper claims PSR "generates style embeddings directly from score content" that are "stylistically appropriate." The evidence is (a) Figure 4, a 2D projection showing era-level clustering similar to real-performance embeddings, and (b) Ours-PSR achieving high overall naturalness ratings in Figure 2. Neither directly tests whether the generated style is *appropriate to the input score's specific content* rather than generically expressive. The era clustering in Figure 4 is qualitative and uses metadata-assigned labels from GPT-4o mini rather than a quantitative distributional measure. A cluster purity or inter-era KL divergence comparing real-vs.-generated style distributions would more rigorously support the claim.

- **The pitch error E_p deserves more forthright acknowledgment.** Table 1 shows E_p = 3.08 for Ours versus 2.02 for Liu et al. (2022) and 2.09 for Shibata et al. (2021) — a 50–52% relative increase. The paper mentions this briefly in §5.1 but frames the overall result as "comparable to state-of-the-art," which is accurate for E_avg but not for pitch error specifically. Readers focused on pitch accuracy should be alerted more explicitly that this is where the alignment-free approach gives ground.

- **Tang et al. (2023), cited as a directly comparable transformer-based EPR system in §2.1 and §4.3, is absent from the EPR comparison tables without explanation.** Its exclusion creates a gap in the EPR baseline landscape that the paper does not justify.

### Trivial

- **Identification results in Table 4 would benefit from stating class counts.** §4.1 mentions 49 pianists and 25 composers, but Table 4 does not reference these numbers. Stating chance levels (≈2% for performer, ≈4% for composer) would help readers immediately interpret the absolute accuracy values.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Clean ablation separating data quantity from joint-task effects.** Training an EPR-only model on the same total data mixture (unpaired + paired, with masked reconstruction) but without the APT loss would cleanly isolate whether the APT objective itself contributes. If it does, the core contribution is strengthened; if not, the paper's other contributions (PSR, Seq2Seq formulation, disentanglement) still stand on their own.

- **Quantitative evaluation of PSR style-score compatibility.** A cluster purity metric or inter-era distributional distance comparing real-performance vs. PSR-generated style embeddings would upgrade Figure 4's visual argument to a falsifiable claim.

- **A genre-appropriateness condition in the subjective evaluation.** Asking listeners specifically whether the rendered style feels *suited to the piece* (not just natural in isolation) would directly probe PSR's central purpose.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most thought-provoking observation is that the learned global style embedding z_s predominantly encodes composer identity (77.46% classification accuracy) rather than performer identity (42.07%), even though it is extracted from performance MIDI and designed to capture performer expressiveness. The paper's explanation — that professional pianists systematically adapt their expressive choices to match each composer's stylistic conventions — is a musicologically interesting claim that, if true, has implications for style transfer research: transferring a "performer style" also implicitly transfers composer-style information, which could produce unexpected cross-genre artifacts when style is applied to a score from a different compositional tradition. This motivates the PSR module's era-conditioned behavior and suggests that style and genre are empirically coupled in the professional performance domain in ways that the field has not fully formalized.

## Suggestions

1. **Run the missing ablation control**: Train EPR-only on the full data mixture (ASAP + unpaired MuseScore + YouTube MIDI) with masked reconstruction but without the APT objective, and report its Table 2 metrics. This single row resolves the paper's primary evidential gap.
2. **Quantify Figure 4 era alignment**: Report inter-era centroid distances or cluster purity for both the real-performance embeddings and PSR-generated embeddings. If the two distributions are quantitatively close, the PSR evaluation argument becomes rigorous.
3. **Explain the Tang et al. (2023) omission**: Either include it as a baseline or add a sentence explaining why it was excluded (e.g., incompatible evaluation format, unavailable code).
4. **State chance-level baselines for Table 4**: Add a footnote with number of classes (49 performers, 25 composers) and corresponding chance accuracy so readers can calibrate the identification results without consulting §4.1.
5. **Rephrase §5.1 to acknowledge the pitch error gap more directly**: Current framing of "comparable to the state-of-the-art" holds for E_avg but not E_p; a brief acknowledgment that alignment-free formulation trades pitch precision for flexibility in one-to-many contexts is warranted.

## Evaluation on Key Axes

**Originality:** The joint EPR+APT disentangled framework is a genuine novelty in MIR. Treating two inverse music tasks as dual paths through a shared latent space, combined with a diffusion-based PSR module, is not a routine combination of prior work.

**Importance:** Both tasks (expressive rendering and transcription) are core MIR problems with practical applications. Unifying them under a shared representation while eliminating alignment preprocessing is a useful step toward scalable, accessible piano AI systems.

**Claims supported:** The empirical results substantiate competitive performance on both tasks. The main claim that joint training is *mutually beneficial* is plausible but not cleanly demonstrated due to the confounded ablation. PSR's style-score compatibility claim rests on suggestive rather than definitive evidence.

**Soundness of experiments:** The experimental design is generally careful — multiple metrics, Wilcoxon significance testing, human listening study, OOD disentanglement probing — but the single ablation that matters most for the paper's core claim is not fully controlled.

**Clarity:** The paper is well-written and the architecture is clearly described. The relationship between tasks (Figure 1) is presented effectively.

**Community value:** The alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation, the PSR module, and the content-style disentanglement framework are all reusable ideas with transfer potential to other music domains (as the authors note in future work). The demo site is a practical contribution.
