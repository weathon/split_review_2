## Summary
This paper conducts a systematic empirical evaluation of nine open-source mid-sized LLMs (CodeLlama, Llama2, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen2.5, Qwen3, and others) on two binary reverse engineering tasks: name recovery (function and variable) and type inference from stripped binaries. The study spans four architectures, four compiler optimization levels, decompiled and assembly inputs, and includes LoRA fine-tuning experiments, finding that all models perform poorly (F1 < 0.1 in most settings) and that fine-tuning produces inconsistent gains.

---

## Strengths

- **Broad, multi-axis evaluation design eliminates narrow-setup confounds.** Nine models are evaluated across four ISAs (x86-64, x86-32, ARM 32-bit, MIPS 32-bit) and four optimization levels (O0–O3) on 40,000 unique test functions (§4.1), ensuring that uniformly low performance is not an artifact of a single architecture or compiler flag.

- **Fine-tuning experiments reveal instability, not just improvement.** Table 3 shows that while WizardCoder improves from 0.01 to 0.33 on variable name recovery at O0, DeepSeek-V2 regresses under O1 (from 0.10 to 0.05); Table 4 shows that multiple models drop to exactly 0.00 after fine-tuning on type inference. These directional inconsistencies are the paper's most informative empirical finding, directly undercutting simple "fine-tuning helps" narratives.

- **Model size ablation on CodeLlama is a clean, properly controlled experiment.** Figure 2 holds architecture and training pipeline constant and varies only parameter count (7B, 13B, 34B), showing negligible differences in F1 across all tasks. This is the correct experimental design for the model-size claim and it is executed well.

- **Automated pipeline with an auxiliary extraction LLM is a reusable methodological contribution.** Section 3.2 describes a post-processing step that normalizes heterogeneous LLM outputs into a uniform schema, enabling fair comparison across models that vary widely in format compliance. This addresses a real practical barrier to large-scale LLM benchmarking.

- **Real-world firmware evaluation extends lab findings.** Table 6 evaluates all nine models on actual stripped firmware under both Ghidra and IDA Pro pseudocode, consistently finding near-zero F1, and additionally documents a systematic IDA Pro > Ghidra quality difference. This confirms that the controlled-dataset results generalize.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The headline quantitative claim in the introduction is unsupported by any result table in the paper.** Section 1 states: "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." No such value appears in any main-body table. Table 3 (Variable Name Recovery, x86-64) shows DeepSeek-R1 fine-tuned reaching a maximum of 0.17 at O0—the highest value for that model. The best fine-tuned variable name recovery across all models in Table 3 is 0.33, achieved by WizardCoder, Qwen2.5, and Phi4, not DeepSeek-R1. The value 0.37 may reside in the stripped appendix for another architecture, but even so, presenting it as the sole quantitative headline in the introduction—without naming the task, architecture, or optimization level—is materially misleading and cannot be verified from the paper as published. This directly undermines the reliability of the reported findings.

### Major

- **Apparent inconsistency between the CodeWordNet-based Figure 1 values and the main result Tables 2.** Section 3.4 designates CodeWordNet semantic F1 as the evaluation metric for all name recovery experiments. Figure 1 (described explicitly as "F1 Score on Function Name Recovery with and without CodeWordNet in x86-64 Architecture via Decompiled Code") shows CodeLlama achieving approximately 0.15 F1 *with* CodeWordNet at O0—yet Table 2 reports CodeLlama Original at 0.04 for the same architecture, optimization level, and task. If Table 2 also uses CodeWordNet (as the metric section implies), these numbers should match. If Table 2 does *not* use CodeWordNet, then the main results are reported under a stricter metric than the one the paper formally adopts, and the implication of Section 3.4 is false. The paper never reconciles this gap, leaving the metric governing the primary results ambiguous. (Note: the Figure 1 values are extracted from an image and may be approximations; nevertheless, the factor-of-3+ difference is too large to dismiss as extraction imprecision alone, and the paper offers no clarification.)

- **No comparison to existing specialized tools despite those tools being the paper's central motivation.** Section 2 (Motivation) criticizes prior work—SYMGEN (Jiang et al.), ReSYM (Xie et al., 2024), VARBERT (Pal et al., 2024), SYMLM (Jin et al., 2022)—for arbitrary model selection. The paper's own experiment setup explicitly says it "follows prior work such as VARBERT and ReSYM" (§4.3). Yet none of these systems are evaluated as baselines on the paper's test set, and the fine-tuned models are never benchmarked against them. Without knowing whether a fine-tuned LLM at F1 = 0.33 (variable names, Table 3) beats, matches, or falls far short of VARBERT or ReSYM on the same data, the paper cannot support its central interpretive claim that "current LLMs are not yet ready." It establishes only that LLMs score low in absolute terms, which is a weaker and less actionable conclusion.

- **Only 200 fine-tuning steps are used across all models with no ablation or justification.** Table 7 shows that 200 steps cost 13–52 GPU-hours depending on the model; this choice is mentioned but never justified. The paper's Findings 1 and 2—that fine-tuning is inconsistent and has "limited impact on type inference"—rest entirely on this setting. Different models may not have converged; type inference's persistent zero scores after fine-tuning (Table 4: DeepSeek-V2, Qwen2.5, Qwen3, WizardCoder all at 0.00) could reflect premature stopping rather than a fundamental task ceiling. Without a learning curve or step-ablation, these conclusions are premature.

### Minor

- **Cross-model conclusions about training quality are confounded.** Finding 5 states that "training data quality significantly impacts performance, even among models with the same architecture," based on the comparison of DeepSeek-R1 vs. Qwen2.5. However, these models differ not only in training data but also in instruction-tuning procedure, RLHF data, and inference behavior (DeepSeek-R1 uses chain-of-thought suppression, §4.5). The paper cannot isolate the training-data variable. The *within-family* CodeLlama size experiment (Figure 2) is a legitimate controlled result; extending its lesson cross-family is not supported.

- **No statistical treatment of near-zero results.** Most model comparisons in Tables 2 and 4 occur in the F1 range 0.00–0.05. The paper draws substantive conclusions from these differences (e.g., "Qwen3 achieves the best function name recovery" at 0.06 vs. Phi4 at 0.05 in Table 2) without any confidence intervals or significance estimates. Given acknowledged output stochasticity (§4.5) and the auxiliary-LLM extraction step (§3.2), single-point estimates in this range may not be reproducibly ordered.

- **DeepSeek-R1 is tested with reasoning suppressed, making its zero-shot scores incomparable to other models.** Section 4.5 discloses that the standard DeepSeek-R1 prompt was modified to suppress chain-of-thought because the model lacks a native toggle. This means DeepSeek-R1 is not evaluated in its intended operating mode. The paper does not report what happens under full reasoning (even on a small subset), leaving unanswered whether chain-of-thought reasoning helps or hurts structured prediction in this domain—a question with direct practical relevance.

### Trivial

- Table 1 lists "Phi4 Unsloto" (likely a parser artifact for "Phi4 + Unsloth"), and the paper alternates between "Deepseek-R1" and "DeepSeek-R1-Qwen" in Table 1 vs. the rest of the text.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Resolve the CodeWordNet metric ambiguity by reporting results both with and without CodeWordNet consistently in all main tables.** Figure 1 already shows this split for four models; extending it to all nine and reporting both clearly would let readers calibrate the results under different strictness assumptions.

- **A learning curve (F1 vs. fine-tuning steps) for at least one representative model** would allow readers to determine whether 200 steps is a reasonable stopping point or an underfit regime, directly strengthening the fine-tuning conclusions.

- **Analysis of type failures by type category (primitive vs. user-defined).** The paper notes that user-defined types complicate type inference (§4.4), but never shows the proportion of failures attributable to each category. This breakdown would clarify whether poor type inference is fundamental to LLMs or an evaluation artifact of strict matching on types with many possible spellings.

- **Test-set contamination analysis.** The test set is drawn exclusively from GNU software (gnu, 2025), which is well-represented in LLM pretraining corpora. An analysis of whether test function names appear in pretraining data would help separate genuine generalization from memorization in the zero-shot results.

- **Comparison of fine-tuned models against at least one specialized tool** (e.g., running the paper's fine-tuned best model on VARBERT's or ReSYM's published test splits) would transform the finding from "F1 = 0.33 in absolute terms" into a directly actionable comparison for practitioners.

## Removed Points

*These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.*

- **Auxiliary LLM extraction accuracy (Harsh Critic §3.2):** The critic suggests that auxiliary-LLM extraction errors are an uncontrolled confounder. While not validated against manual annotation, this pipeline is applied uniformly to all models, so its errors affect all models equally and are unlikely to change rankings systematically. Downgraded from weakness to acknowledged limitation.

- **Claim that "LLMs are not yet ready" is unsupported as a comparative claim (Harsh Critic):** Retained in the Major section as "No comparison to specialized tools," but reframed—this is a methodological gap rather than a fatal invalidation, since the paper's absolute findings are internally consistent even if the comparative claim is weak.

- **DeepSeek-R1 reasoning suppression as a "fatal" methodological flaw:** Demoted to Minor. The prompt modification is disclosed, and the resulting results are still informative as a data point; the concern is about incomparability rather than invalidity.

- **Strength: "Training quality more decisive than parameter count" (Strength Finder #3):** Partially retained but qualified—the within-family CodeLlama size experiment supports this; the cross-family generalization is a weakness. The strength is kept only for the controlled within-family finding.

- **Generic strength about "addressing an important problem" (Strength Finder):** Removed per filtering rules.

## Novel Insights

The juxtaposition in Table 3 of anomalously high O0 performance (WizardCoder/Qwen2.5/Phi4 reaching 0.33 under O0, dropping sharply to 0.07–0.10 at O1–O3) raises an interesting hypothesis not discussed by the authors: at O0, decompiled code is structurally closest to source code, potentially containing library call patterns (e.g., `_stack_chk_fail`, `__libc_start_main`) whose names appear verbatim in LLM pretraining data. If this memorization effect accounts for the O0 spike, the practical utility of fine-tuned LLMs for real stripped binaries (which are routinely compiled at O2–O3) would be even lower than the headline numbers suggest. The sharp O0 → O1 degradation in Table 3 is consistent with this interpretation and is more informative than the authors acknowledge.

## Suggestions

1. **Correct or qualify the 0.37 F1 claim in the introduction.** If this result appears in the appendix for a specific architecture/task/optimization level, state exactly where it comes from. If it is an error, revise the introduction accordingly.

2. **Clarify the metric applied in Tables 2–3 vs. Figure 1.** Either explicitly state "Tables 2–3 use CodeWordNet F1" and reconcile the numerical gap with Figure 1, or acknowledge that the tables use exact-match F1 and rename the metrics accordingly.

3. **Add even one specialized-system baseline** (ReSYM or VARBERT) evaluated on the paper's test functions under the same metric. This single addition would substantially strengthen the central conclusion.

4. **Run a step-count ablation** (e.g., 50/200/500/1000 steps for one model on variable name recovery) to justify the 200-step stopping criterion.

5. **Report the O0 vs. O1–O3 gap prominently** and discuss the memorization/structural-proximity hypothesis—this is the paper's most interesting and underexplored empirical signal.

## Evaluation on Core Axes

- **Originality:** Moderate. Systematic LLM benchmarking applied to binary reverse engineering is a real gap, but the tasks, models, and metrics are all drawn from prior work; the novelty is in the combination and scale.
- **Importance:** Moderate. The community genuinely needs principled model-selection guidance; however, the results as reported have factual and metric-clarity problems that limit their trustworthiness.
- **Claims supported:** Weak. The headline claim (0.37 for DeepSeek-R1) is unsupported; the metric applied in main tables is ambiguous; cross-model training-quality conclusions are confounded.
- **Soundness of experiments:** Weak-to-moderate. The experimental design (multi-axis, large dataset, LoRA fine-tuning) is well-conceived, but the 200-step fine-tuning with no ablation and the unresolved metric inconsistency undermine soundness.
- **Clarity:** Weak. Key inconsistencies (0.37 claim, CodeWordNet application) are left unexplained; the relationship between Figure 1 and the main tables is never stated.
- **Value to research community:** Moderate potential, currently limited. The dataset (200,000 functions across four ISAs) and the automated evaluation pipeline are genuine contributions; the reported results need correction before they can be relied upon.
