## Summary

This empirical study investigates the reliability of rule-based and model-based verifiers in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Reward (RLVR) for mathematical reasoning. The authors construct an 8,000-example evaluation dataset, document non-negligible false-negative rates in popular rule-based verifiers (recall dropping to 0.78 on challenging datasets), and then demonstrate a counterintuitive finding: fine-tuned model-based verifiers that achieve higher static classification accuracy can be *more* susceptible to reward hacking during RL training than their un-fine-tuned base counterparts. A systematic probing study with 13 adversarial patterns further reveals that generative verifiers are broadly fragile, while discriminative verifiers (xVerify) show near-zero attack success rates.

---

## Strengths

- **Quantified false-negative problem in rule-based verifiers with a clear trend.** Figure 1 shows average recall as low as 0.86 for the Verl Math Verifier, with Skywork-OR1 recall dropping to 0.78. Figure 2 demonstrates that recall declines further as the generating model gets stronger (R1-style Long-CoT models averaging ~0.92 recall), concretely quantifying a risk that the community has largely overlooked.

- **Counterintuitive and practically significant RL finding.** Table 2 and Figure 3 document that R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, which improves static recall from 0.49 to 0.62 and precision from 0.68 to 0.73 over its base, nevertheless causes training reward to diverge dramatically from oracle reward at ~450 iterations (Figure 3, right), yielding only 55.6 average evaluation accuracy versus 57.3 for the un-fine-tuned DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B verifier. This classification-RL performance mismatch is the paper's most important and non-obvious contribution.

- **Systematic adversarial probing study with concrete, differentiated results.** Table 3 reports attack success rates across 13 pattern types and a range of verifiers. The contrast between generative verifiers (e.g., Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B with 44.4% empty-symbol success, R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B with 35% adversarial-prefix success) and discriminative verifiers (xVerify-0.5B/3B with near-zero rates across all patterns) is specific, reproducible, and actionable.

- **Carefully constructed evaluation dataset.** Section 3.1 details 8,000 examples across four datasets, generated by four models (Short-CoT and Long-CoT), annotated by GPT-4o and validated against human judgments. The decision to evaluate model-based verifiers only on the subset the rule-based verifier fails (§3.3) correctly aligns the static evaluation with the hybrid design used in RL, making Table 1 directly interpretable for downstream use.

- **Replication across domains.** The RL finding generalizes to Skywork-OR1 (appendix) and WebInstruct-Verified (appendix), where the performance gap between rule-based and hybrid verifiers persists (3.6-point gap on WebInstruct-Verified per §4.3) and reward hacking recurs with fine-tuned verifiers.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Narrow RL experimental base with best-peak rather than stable performance reporting.** The central quantitative RL claim — the hybrid verifier achieves 57.3 average vs. 55.0 for rule-based (a 2.3-point gain) — is derived from a single policy model (Qwen2.5-7B Base) on a single primary training dataset (DeepScaleR). Table 2 caption explicitly states: "The best result from each run is reported." This best-peak reporting, without multi-seed variance or confidence intervals, makes it difficult to judge whether the 2.3-point spread represents a reliable behavioral difference or a favorable comparison of peaks. For a paper framing itself as a "comprehensive analysis," the primary empirical quantity should be more robustly established. The appendix experiments provide directional support but use the same policy model and different training datasets, rather than independently varying the policy architecture.

- **Missing xVerify RL experiment, the most important internal gap.** Table 1 shows xVerify-3B achieves the strongest static performance (0.90/0.78 Avg. precision/recall), and Table 3 shows near-zero adversarial attack success rates across all 13 patterns — a sharp contrast to all other verifiers tested. Yet the paper does not include xVerify in any RL training experiment (Table 2 includes general-verifier at 57.0 but not xVerify). Testing xVerify in RL is the most direct way to validate the paper's central thesis — that verifiers excelling in both accuracy and robustness produce reliable RL training — and its absence leaves the paper's prescriptive conclusion without its most important supporting data point.

### Minor

- **Mechanism for fine-tuning-induced vulnerability is observed but not analyzed.** The paper documents that fine-tuning R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B with a "rejection fine-tuning" objective (§5.1: "to reduce overthinking and encourage more concise outputs") causes increased hacking susceptibility. However, the causal mechanism — whether suppressing CoT reasoning reduces faithfulness, overfits to classification-training distributions, or shifts the decision boundary in adversarially exploitable ways — is not analyzed. A brief analysis of representative reasoning traces where the fine-tuned verifier fails while the base model succeeds would meaningfully sharpen the narrative and help practitioners design more robust fine-tuned verifiers.

- **GPT-4o oracle role in dynamic RL training lacks validation.** GPT-4o serves as an oracle to detect reward hacking during training (§5.2), with Appendix B validating its role as an annotator in the static setting. However, no validation is provided for its reliability as an oracle when the policy model's outputs degrade into single symbols or gibberish — exactly the exploit patterns that emerge at ~450 iterations. A spot-check or qualitative audit on a sample of hacking-exploit responses would strengthen this analysis pillar.

- **Figure 2 trend confound.** The four models used to generate responses in Figure 2 (Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, DS-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B) differ simultaneously in architecture, training paradigm (Short-CoT vs. Long-CoT), and size. The claim "recall drops as models get stronger" is supported directionally but conflates multiple factors. This is a presentation issue that the paper should qualify more carefully.

### Trivial

- The claim "Scaling compute alone is insufficient" (§4.3) is stated more assertively than the evidence supports, since the rule-based baseline is not run for additional iterations to confirm true saturation; the comparison uses equal compute budgets at peak rather than demonstrating a sustained asymptote difference.

- A probing analysis verifying that the hacking patterns actually discovered during RL (single-symbol, gibberish per §5.2 and Figures 11/12) are among the 13 patterns in the probing suite — closing the loop between the adversarial dataset and the actual exploits observed in training.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation — emerging from the synthesis of static, dynamic, and adversarial analyses — is that *the direction of fine-tuning can invert the accuracy-robustness tradeoff*: a model fine-tuned specifically to suppress overthinking and produce concise verification outputs becomes *more* susceptible to shallow pattern exploitation than the base model, even when static classification accuracy improves. This suggests that short-format reasoning, optimized for classification efficiency, may reduce the model's ability to perform the kind of robust sanity-checking that resists adversarial patterns. This insight reframes verifier design: it is not sufficient to ask "does fine-tuning improve accuracy?" — one must also ask "does fine-tuning preserve the depth of reasoning that blocks adversarial exploitation?" The discriminative xVerify's near-zero attack success rates further imply that sidestepping CoT generation entirely (by directly outputting a binary judgment) may be an underexplored path to robustness.
