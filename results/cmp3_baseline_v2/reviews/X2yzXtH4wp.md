## Summary

This paper introduces Ambig-SWE, an underspecified variant of SWE-Bench Verified designed to evaluate how LLM-based agents handle incomplete instructions in software engineering tasks. The authors systematically evaluate proprietary and open-weight models across three key capabilities: detecting underspecificity, asking targeted clarification questions, and leveraging interaction to improve performance. Their experiments reveal that models default to non-interactive behavior, struggle to distinguish well-specified from underspecified instructions, but can recover up to 74% of performance when interaction is enabled, with Claude Sonnet 4 achieving the best results.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated and practically important problem**: The paper addresses a critical real-world issue—how AI agents handle underspecified instructions—which has direct implications for safety, reliability, and user alignment in deployed systems. The motivation is clearly articulated with concrete examples.

- **Principled decomposition of the problem**: Breaking down underspecification resolution into three distinct capacities (detection, questioning, integration) is a valuable analytical contribution. This decomposition enables targeted improvements and provides a diagnostic framework that goes beyond simple pass/fail evaluation.

- **Rigorous experimental design with multiple settings**: The three-setting design (Full, Hidden, Interaction) allows clean causal attribution of interaction effects. The use of Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, controlled user proxy with conservative response behavior, and analysis of navigational vs. informational details demonstrates methodological care.

- **Actionable empirical insights**: The findings about exploration-first strategies, question quality vs. quantity tradeoffs, and the disconnect between information extraction and integration provide concrete guidance for model and agent design. The observation that Qwen 3 Coder rigidly follows protocols despite explicit corrective prompting is a specific, actionable finding.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Synthetic underspecification may not reflect real-world ambiguity patterns**: The underspecified variants are generated by GPT-4o removing details from fully specified issues. The authors acknowledge this limitation but the distributional analysis only shows that generated issues lack certain features of natural underspecified issues—it does not validate that the synthetic underspecification captures the same *types* of ambiguity that arise in practice. Real underspecification often involves implicit assumptions, domain-specific jargon, or context-dependent omissions that may be fundamentally different from "aggressive information removal." This limits external validity.

- **User proxy limitations are under-discussed**: Using GPT-4o as a simulated user who only responds with information explicitly in the full issue is a reasonable design choice, but the paper does not adequately address how this differs from real users. Real users may provide partial, incorrect, or misleading information; may not know what information is relevant; or may become frustrated with excessive questioning. The proxy's perfect knowledge and cooperative behavior likely overestimates the benefits of interaction in practice.

- **The detection experiment conflates multiple factors**: In RQ2, the model must simultaneously (a) detect underspecificity, (b) decide to interact, and (c) actually initiate interaction. The accuracy metric conflates all three. A model that correctly detects underspecificity but fails to act on it (e.g., Qwen 3 Coder) is scored as having 0% detection ability, when the failure may be in action execution rather than detection. A cleaner experimental design would separate detection (e.g., asking the model to classify) from action (deciding to interact).

### Minor

- **Limited model diversity for drawing general conclusions**: The paper evaluates only 6 models, with 3 from the Claude family. Claims about "proprietary vs. open-weight" differences are based on very few data points. The inclusion of only one model from the Llama family and one from Deepseek limits the generality of findings about open-weight models.

- **The 30 vs. 100 turn allocation difference is a confound**: Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder get 100 turns while others get 30. The paper justifies this by their greater reasoning capacity, but this makes direct comparisons of interaction efficiency and effectiveness difficult. Higher resolve rates for these models could partially reflect more opportunities rather than better interaction.

- **Cosine distance as an information gain metric is questionable**: Using embedding cosine distance between pre- and post-interaction task representations assumes that all information gain is equally valuable and that embedding space distances correspond to task-relevant information. The paper's own finding that Qwen 3 Coder achieves the highest cosine distance but not the best performance suggests this metric has limited validity.

### Trivial

- The paper states "Claude Sonnet 4 is evaluated on a subset of 100/500 instances in the Hidden setting" but does not specify how this subset was selected or whether it is representative.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A human evaluation study comparing the synthetic underspecified issues to real underspecified GitHub issues would significantly strengthen external validity claims.
- Analysis of what types of missing information (e.g., file locations, expected behavior, error context) are most impactful for task completion would provide more actionable guidance.
- Training or fine-tuning experiments that explicitly optimize for interaction behavior would demonstrate whether the identified gaps can be addressed.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, a genuinely novel insight is the finding that **information extraction capability and information integration capability are dissociable**—models can ask good questions and extract relevant information (high cosine distance, high LLM-judge scores) yet fail to effectively use that information (low resolve rates). This suggests that current training paradigms may optimize for question-asking without teaching models to appropriately modify their behavior based on answers. The observation that Qwen 3 Coder rigidly follows predetermined protocols even after receiving corrective information is particularly striking, as it reveals a fundamental limitation in how current LLMs handle interactive feedback: they may "hear" the information without "listening" to it in a way that changes their subsequent actions.

## Suggestions

- Separate the detection and action components in RQ2 by adding a classification-only condition where models are explicitly asked "Is this task description missing information?" without being given the option to interact. This would isolate detection ability from action execution.
- Conduct an ablation study where the user proxy provides varying levels of information quality (e.g., partially correct, vague, or irrelevant responses) to test robustness of interaction benefits under more realistic conditions.
- Include a baseline where the underspecified issue is given to the model with the missing information explicitly appended (without interaction) to quantify the pure information value vs. the interaction process value.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a solid contribution to an important and understudied problem. The decomposition of underspecification handling into detection, questioning, and integration is valuable, and the empirical findings are informative despite some methodological limitations. The paper is clearly written and the experiments are well-designed within their scope. However, the synthetic nature of the underspecification and the limited model diversity temper the strength of the conclusions. The paper would benefit from addressing the major weaknesses, particularly the conflation of detection and action in RQ2 and the external validity concerns.
