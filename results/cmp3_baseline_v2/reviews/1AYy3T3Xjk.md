## Summary
This paper presents CREDO (Creativity–Reality Evaluation with Decoupled Ontology), a process-level evaluation framework for assessing creativity in LLM-assisted learning. The framework introduces an Innovation Tracing Atlas (ITA) to attribute student vs. LLM contributions in multi-turn dialogues and an instruction-tuned evaluator (DeepSeek-32B fine-tuned with LoRA) that scores four creativity dimensions tailored to human-AI collaboration (Interdisciplinary Innovation, Problem Reframing, Risk-Driven Innovation, Resource Integration Efficiency) with textual rationales. On a dataset of 1,273 cleaned dialogues from 81 undergraduates across multiple STEM domains, the fine-tuned model achieves a Quadratic Weighted Kappa of 0.728 (near the human expert ceiling of 0.81) and demonstrates strong attribution accuracy (macro F1 = 0.84), supporting the feasibility of interpretable, process-oriented creativity assessment in human-AI interactions.

## Strengths
- **Timely and relevant problem formulation**: The paper tackles an increasingly important issue—how to assess student creativity when LLMs are used as co-creators—that lacks established methodologies. The focus on process-level attribution rather than outcome-only evaluation directly addresses a practical pain point in education.
- **Well-designed evaluation dimensions**: The CREDO dimensions (Interdisciplinary Innovation, Problem Reframing, Risk-Driven Innovation, Resource Integration Efficiency) are thoughtfully adapted from classical creativity theory to the human-AI collaboration context. The theoretical grounding in Bloom’s Taxonomy, PISA 2022 framework, and ICAP provides construct validity, and the operational definitions in Table 1 clearly differentiate CREDO from classical TTCT dimensions.
- **Strong empirical results with rigorous methodology**: The fine-tuned model significantly outperforms both GPT-4 (QWK 0.513) and untuned DeepSeek-32B (QWK 0.342), achieving QWK 0.728—~90% of the human expert ceiling (0.81). The attribution validation experiment (Table 3) provides concrete quantitative evidence that the model can distinguish learner-initiated ideas from LLM scaffolding, directly supporting the core claim. Inter-rater reliability (weighted κ=0.81) and internal consistency (α=0.86) are strong.
- **Interpretability and auditability**: The joint prediction of scores + rationales (50-word explanations) is a practical design choice that supports formative assessment and human review, addressing opacity concerns that plague many LLM-based evaluation systems. The ITA visualizations provide an additional layer of traceability.

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Circularity in performance evaluation**: The human-level performance ceiling (QWK 0.81) is derived from the same expert annotations used to train the model. The model is then shown to approach this ceiling. While this demonstrates successful supervised learning, it does not independently validate that the model captures human-like creativity judgment—it only shows that the model can mimic the training experts. A more convincing evaluation would require testing on a held-out set annotated by a different expert panel, or a cross-domain generalization experiment where the model is evaluated on tasks/topics completely absent from training.
2. **Disconnect between "process-level" framing and model architecture**: The paper repeatedly emphasizes process-level assessment, and the ITA decomposition is used during expert annotation. However, the fine-tuned evaluator receives the full raw dialogue as input and produces dialogue-level scores—it does not explicitly model the temporal or causal structure of the dialogue. The attribution capability is evaluated on a separate utterance-level classification task, but the primary scoring model does not incorporate ITA structure internally. This raises the question of whether the evaluator truly captures process dynamics or merely learns to associate dialogue features with expert holistic scores. The claim of "process-level" evaluation would be stronger if the model operated on turn-level intermediate representations or if the ITA decomposition were part of the model input.

### Minor
1. **Limited sample diversity**: The dataset is restricted to 81 undergraduates from two research-intensive universities, predominantly STEM contexts (cell biology, cancer therapy, etc.). Although the paper acknowledges this limitation, it substantially constrains the generality of both the CREDO dimensions (which may not transfer to arts, design, or humanities) and the empirical results. Claims about alignment with educational theories require validation across more diverse learner populations and disciplines.
2. **Unclear role of ITA in the evaluator pipeline**: The paper describes ITA as a tool for annotation, but it is not clear whether the ITA decomposition is provided as input to the fine-tuned model or used only during expert labeling. If the model operates on raw dialogue text without ITA, the "process-level" characterization relies entirely on the training labels rather than on actual process modeling. The paper should clarify the input format to the evaluator.
3. **Missing details about training and ablation**: The ablation studies (w/o LoRA, w/o KD, Scores-only) and the iterative manual refinement (17 samples) are mentioned but only referenced to Appendix A, which is not available in the provided content. Without these results, the contribution of each design component cannot be fully assessed. Additionally, the rationale generation loss coefficient (λ_rat) and KD loss coefficient (λ_KD) are not specified.
4. **Terminology inconsistency**: The model is referred to as an "instruction-tuned evaluator" but it is fine-tuned for a specific output format (scores + rationales) rather than instruction-tuned in the standard sense (e.g., via RLHF on varied instructions). This is a minor imprecision but could confuse readers.

### Trivial
- The radar chart (Figure 2) includes BERTScore as a metric, but BERTScore is not defined or motivated in the main text.
- The ITA visualization (Figure 3) is dense and difficult to interpret from the text description alone; a clearer explanation of node/edge semantics would help.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Cross-domain validation: testing the model on a small set of dialogues from humanities/arts tasks to assess generalization.
- Ablation of the dialogue input length (e.g., does the model still perform well on short dialogues with few turns?).
- User study with educators evaluating the usefulness and trustworthiness of the generated rationales.
- Analysis of error patterns: on which dialogues/students does the model disagree most with experts, and why?

## Novel Insights
Beyond the paper's own contributions, the most novel insight is that attribution-based evaluation (distinguishing student-initiated vs. LLM-generated ideational moves) can be automated to near-human accuracy using a moderately-sized fine-tuned model, and that this attribution capability directly supports dialogue-level scoring on dimensions specifically designed for human-AI collaboration. This suggests that process-level creativity assessment in LLM-assisted learning is feasible without requiring full cognitive modeling of the learner—a pragmatic insight for deploying evaluation tools in real educational settings.

## Suggestions
1. **Clarify the evaluator's input and ITA integration**: Explicitly state whether the ITA decomposition is part of the model input (e.g., as structured annotations) or only used during training label construction. If it is not part of the input, consider modifying the approach (or the language) to avoid over-claiming process-level modeling.
2. **Add an independent validation set**: Annotate a small set of dialogues with a different group of expert raters (e.g., from an external institution) and report the model's performance against this set. This would address the circularity concern and demonstrate generalization across annotation teams.
3. **Report per-dimension performance**: The overall QWK is 0.728, but the paper notes earlier that Risk-Driven Innovation had lower consistency. Reporting QWK (or MSE) separately for each of the four CREDO dimensions would provide a clearer picture of where the model excels and where it needs improvement.
4. **Explore a truly process-aware model variant**: As an extension, consider a model that processes dialogue turn-by-turn and produces attribution labels (Original Student Idea, Developed Student Idea, Restated Student Idea) as intermediate outputs before computing the final four scores. This would more directly instantiate the "process-level" claim and improve traceability.

## Score and Decision
The paper addresses an important and under-explored problem with a well-structured methodology and convincing quantitative results. The major weaknesses (evaluation circularity and disconnect between process-level claims and actual model architecture) are significant but not fatal; they can be addressed with additional experiments and clearer framing. The contributions—a new evaluation framework tailored to human-AI collaboration, an attribution protocol, and a fine-tuned evaluator achieving near-human agreement—are valuable to the ICLR community, particularly researchers working on LLM evaluation, AI in education, and creativity assessment. I recommend acceptance with the expectation that the authors address the major concerns during discussion.
