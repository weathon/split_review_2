## Summary

This paper proposes Critique-RL, a two-stage reinforcement learning approach for training language models to critique and provide feedback on model outputs, without requiring stronger supervisors or oracle verifiers at test time. The key insight is that existing RL methods using only indirect reward signals from actor refinement (e.g., correctness of subsequent response) fail to properly optimize the critic's discriminability—the ability to judge whether a response is correct—leading to conservative or aggressive behavior. Critique-RL addresses this by first optimizing discriminability via direct rule-based rewards, then optimizing helpfulness via actor refinement rewards while preserving discriminability through regularization. Experiments on math reasoning tasks across multiple model families show consistent and substantial improvements over SFT, STaR, and prior RL methods (Retroformer, CTRL), with gains on both in-domain and out-of-domain tasks.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem and diagnosis**. The paper clearly identifies and demonstrates a genuine failure mode in existing RL-based critic training: indirect reward signals (refinement correctness) fail to properly incentivize discriminability, causing critics to become overly conservative (refuse to change correct answers but fail to fix wrong ones) or overly aggressive (fix wrong answers but break correct ones). The training dynamics visualizations in Figure 3 convincingly illustrate this phenomenon across multiple reward designs.

- **Clean and principled two-stage solution**. The proposed decomposition—Stage I optimizes discriminability with direct rule-based rewards, Stage II optimizes helpfulness with actor refinement rewards while maintaining discriminability via explicit regularization—is simple, intuitive, and well-justified by the analysis. The ablation study (Table 3) rigorously validates the necessity of both stages and each component (discrimination reward, KL regularization to Stage-I model), confirming that the design is tightly connected to the identified problem.

- **Strong and consistent empirical results**. Critique-RL outperforms all baselines (SFT, STaR, Retroformer, CTRL) across three in-domain tasks and two out-of-domain tasks, for both Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-7B. The gains are substantial (e.g., 9.02% improvement on in-domain tasks for 7B), and the method consistently improves both final accuracy and discriminability (Acc@Dis) simultaneously. The method also benefits from iterative training and inference-time compute scaling, further demonstrating its reliability.

- **Well-designed evaluation metrics**. The paper uses a comprehensive set of metrics (Acc@Refine, Δ, Δ^{c→i}, Δ^{i→c}, Acc@Dis) that capture different aspects of critic quality, making the analysis thorough and the claims about improved discriminability and helpfulness well-supported.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Limited domain scope and reliance on verifiable correctness**. The method requires a rule-based oracle reward function (\(r_{\text{oracle}}\)) that can automatically verify answer correctness, which inherently limits the approach to tasks where such verifiers exist (e.g., math with known answers, programming with unit tests). While the authors briefly experiment with summarization using an LLM-as-judge (Appendix G), the primary contribution and all main results are on math reasoning. This restricts the immediate applicability of the method, though the problem of scalable oversight for verifiable tasks is itself important.

- **SFT initialization still requires labeled critique data**. The paper claims "without stronger labeling," but Stage I starts from an SFT-initialized critic trained on critique data generated by prompting Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and filtered by refinement correctness. While this avoids expensive annotations from GPT-4 or humans, it still requires a curation pipeline and the quality of the SFT initialization may affect RL convergence. The impact of SFT data quality and quantity on final performance is not systematically studied.

- **Sensitivity analysis of hyperparameters is limited**. The method introduces several hyperparameters (\(\beta_1\), \(\beta_2\), \(\beta\), and the KL coefficients). The paper reports specific values (e.g., \(\beta_1=0.2\)) but does not provide ablation over these choices. Given that Stage II involves a trade-off between discrimination regularization and helpfulness reward, understanding sensitivity to \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\) would strengthen the paper.

### Minor

- **Baseline implementation details could be clarified**. The paper uses Retroformer and CTRL as RL baselines but adapts them to the math reasoning setting. Some implementation decisions (e.g., whether Retroformer's PPO is replaced with RLOO for consistency, how CTRL's GRPO is configured for the critique setting) are briefly mentioned but not fully justified. The STAR baseline adaptation—applying iterative self-training on critique data filtered by refinement correctness—is reasonable but its connection to the original STAR algorithm for rationale generation is loose.

- **The oracle verifier availability experiment (Figure 5) conflates two factors**. When the oracle verifier is available, the critic's discrimination ability is bypassed and only helpfulness matters. The finding that Critique-RL still outperforms baselines in this setting suggests discrimination training implicitly improves helpfulness. However, this could also partially reflect that Stage I's direct reward optimization biases the critic toward more useful feedback structures. A more controlled analysis of how Stage I representations influence Stage II learning would be insightful.

### Trivial

- Figure 1 (right) legend contains a duplicate entry ("w/o Critique-RL (3B)" appears twice) and the x-axis labels are inconsistently formatted (\(2^1\) vs \(2\ \text{sup}\ 1\)).

- The abstract formatting has a superscript "1" and "2" that are not explained inline in the main text (though footnotes appear).

## Nice-to-Haves

- Analysis on other types of reasoning tasks (e.g., coding, formal theorem proving) would broaden the paper's impact. The summarization experiment is a step in this direction but uses an LLM-as-judge reward rather than a rule-based verifier, introducing additional complexity.
- Investigation of the effect of actor model quality on critic training would be valuable—the paper fixes the actor throughout, but having a weaker or stronger actor may change the difficulty of the critic's job.
- The paper could discuss how the two-stage approach might generalize to cases where the discrimination reward itself is learned or noisy (e.g., using process reward models) rather than rule-based.

## Novel Insights

The paper's core insight is that discriminability and helpfulness in critique models are partially independent capabilities, and that optimizing only the latter via indirect actor-level rewards leads to optimization pathologies (conservative or aggressive bias) because the RL signal cannot properly incentivize the critic to make accurate judgments about response quality. This is a genuinely novel observation that extends earlier work (Saunders et al., 2022) which distinguished these two functions but did not study the RL optimization dynamics. The finding that direct discrimination rewards in Stage I not only improve accuracy but also enable more effective helpfulness optimization in Stage II (even when discrimination is no longer needed, as in the oracle-verifier setting) is an interesting emergent property. The regularization technique—keeping the Stage II model close (via KL) to the discrimination-optimized Stage I model—provides a practical recipe for balancing competing objectives in online RL for language models.

## Suggestions

- Add an ablation on the KL coefficient \(\beta_2\) (regularization toward Stage-I model) to understand how sensitive the Stage II helpfulness optimization is to this constraint. A simple sweep over a few values (e.g., 0.01, 0.05, 0.1) would help.
- Clarify in the main text how the discrimination judgment \(f(x,y,c)\) is extracted from the critic's natural language output (e.g., whether a fixed parsing rule is used, and what happens when parsing fails).
- Include a brief discussion of failure cases or qualitative examples where Critique-RL still makes mistakes, to provide a balanced view of the method's limitations.
- Provide explicit comparison with Self-Refine and Critic-CoT in the main table (currently in Appendix E) since these are well-known baselines in the critique/self-correction literature.
