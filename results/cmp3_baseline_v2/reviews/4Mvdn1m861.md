## Summary

The paper proposes TokenCount, a training-free object counting framework built entirely on the Segment Anything Model (SAM) without additional models or fine-tuning. It introduces two key components: (1) a probabilistic prompt generation stage that iteratively samples prompts based on a probability distribution derived from exemplar-image similarity, and (2) an output token-based verification stage that uses SAM’s decoder output tokens with a TS-SS similarity metric to distinguish positive from negative instances. Experiments on FSC-147 and CARPK show competitive performance, with particularly strong results on CARPK where it outperforms all supervised methods.

## Strengths

- **Novel use of SAM’s output tokens for verification**: The paper is the first to directly leverage SAM’s output tokens (rather than image embeddings or mask-based post-processing) for object counting. This is a clean and efficient approach that avoids additional models or costly similarity computations.
- **Strong empirical results on CARPK**: The method achieves MAE 4.68 and RMSE 6.13 on CARPK, outperforming all training-based methods (e.g., SAFECOUNT 5.33, BMNet+ 5.76) and most training-free methods, demonstrating practical value in a real-world scenario.
- **Well-designed ablation studies**: The ablations systematically validate the contribution of each component—verification metric, iterative sampling parameters, and output token space vs. image embedding space—providing clear evidence for design choices.
- **Training-free and computationally efficient**: The method uses only SAM without additional encoders or training, and reports 1.69 seconds per image on FSC-147, which is competitive with or faster than methods that rely on extra models (e.g., TFCAC).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Overclaiming in the abstract**: The abstract states the method “outperforms existing training-based and training-free counting methods” with MAE 16.25. However, on FSC-147, TFCAC (a training-free method using an additional DINO encoder) achieves MAE 12.26, which is substantially better. The claim is only true when restricting to “SAM only” methods, but the abstract does not make this qualification. This misrepresentation weakens the paper’s credibility.
- **Marginal improvement of TS-SS over simpler metrics**: The ablation in Table 3 shows TS-SS (MAE 16.25) is only marginally better than cosine similarity (16.48) and L2 distance (16.53). The paper argues that a hybrid metric is necessary, but the empirical gain is small and may not justify the added complexity. A statistical significance test (e.g., paired bootstrap) would strengthen the claim.

### Minor
- **Limited analysis of failure cases**: The discussion mentions performance degradation on dense, small instances due to SAM’s mask quality, but no quantitative analysis or examples are provided. Understanding when and why the method fails would help practitioners assess its applicability.
- **One-shot counting results are less competitive**: On FSC-147 one-shot (Table 2), the method achieves MAE 18.57, which is worse than training-based LOCA (12.53) and training-free TFCAC (15.15). The paper acknowledges this but does not discuss why the method struggles in the one-shot setting compared to the few-shot setting.

### Trivial
- The paper uses “output token” and “prompt token” somewhat interchangeably in places; clarifying the distinction between the exemplar output token and candidate output tokens would improve readability.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison of inference time (seconds per image) for all methods in Table 1 would strengthen the efficiency argument, especially against TFCAC which is claimed to be slower.
- An analysis of how the number of iterations and prompts per iteration affect the trade-off between accuracy and runtime (beyond the mean prompts column in Table 4) would be useful for practitioners.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper’s own contributions, the key insight is that SAM’s output tokens, which integrate both semantic and positional information through bidirectional attention, provide a more discriminative representation for object verification than the raw image embeddings. The PCA visualization in Figure 5 supports this: output tokens form separable clusters for positive vs. negative instances, while image embeddings overlap. This suggests that the decoder’s token-to-image attention mechanism naturally aligns token representations with the prompted object, making them well-suited for counting without additional training.

## Suggestions

- Revise the abstract to accurately reflect the comparison scope (e.g., “outperforms existing training-free methods that use only SAM” or “achieves competitive accuracy among training-free methods”).
- Add a statistical significance test (e.g., paired bootstrap) for the TS-SS vs. cosine similarity comparison in Table 3 to justify the adoption of the more complex metric.
- Include a quantitative breakdown of failure cases (e.g., performance vs. object size or density) to better characterize the method’s limitations.

## Score and Decision

The paper presents a novel, well-motivated training-free counting framework with strong results on CARPK and solid ablations. However, the overclaiming in the abstract and the marginal benefit of the core verification metric temper the overall impact. The contribution is valuable but not exceptional.
