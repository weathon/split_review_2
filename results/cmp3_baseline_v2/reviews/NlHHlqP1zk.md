## Summary

This paper proposes the *Fast and Slow Effect* (FSE) framework to automatically evaluate the sufficiency of concept-class annotations generated by LLMs/VLMs for explainable AI. The framework structures annotation into five progressive stages (from fast visual inference to slow concept-driven reasoning) and introduces the *Class Representation Index* (CRI) to measure how well accumulated concepts support accurate classification. Experiments across fine-grained and general datasets reveal that current automated annotations often lack sufficient semantic coverage, with the slow mode underperforming the fast mode by over 25% on average, and that strong downstream task performance does not guarantee annotation quality.

## Strengths

- **Important and timely problem**: The paper addresses a critical gap in concept-based XAI—the lack of systematic, automatic validation of annotation sufficiency. This is highly relevant as the community increasingly relies on LLM-generated annotations.
- **Novel evaluation framework**: The FSE framework and CRI metric are well-motivated and provide a principled, human-free way to assess whether concept annotations capture discriminative semantics. The fast vs. slow mode comparison is a clever operationalization of dual-process theory.
- **Comprehensive empirical study**: Experiments cover six LLMs, five datasets (including fine-grained and general), and two annotation paradigms (post-hoc and visual-grounded). The results consistently show that current annotations are insufficient for fine-grained tasks, and the utility-as-proxy assumption is challenged with concrete evidence.
- **Counterintuitive findings**: The observation that slow mode (concept-based reasoning) underperforms fast mode (direct visual inference) is striking and raises important questions about how LLMs externalize knowledge. The fused-mode experiment further demonstrates that high downstream accuracy can coexist with poor annotation quality.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Self-evaluation confound**: The CRI metric measures how well the *same model* that generated the concepts can classify using only those concepts. Low CRI could reflect either insufficient annotations or the model's limited ability to reason from its own textual outputs. The paper does not disentangle these factors. For example, the model might generate perfectly discriminative concepts but fail to use them correctly due to reasoning biases (as hinted in Figure 1). This weakens the claim that annotations themselves are insufficient.
- **Arbitrary five-stage design**: The hierarchical concept-gathering process (Background, Superclass, Salient Features, Detailed Features, Auxiliary Features) is presented as a natural extension of prior work, but no ablation or validation justifies why five stages are optimal or whether the ordering matters. The prompts (deferred to appendix) are not visible in the main text, making it hard to assess the quality of the concept elicitation.
- **Limited grounding of "sufficiency"**: Definition 3.1 defines sufficiency as the ability to infer the class from concepts alone, which is exactly what CRI measures. This creates a circular validation: the framework tests whether concepts are sufficient by checking if they enable classification, but classification success depends on the model's reasoning capability. A more rigorous evaluation would compare against human judgments or use held-out concept sets.

### Minor

- **Distractor selection relies on a pretrained model**: The semantically related distractors are chosen using ResNet-18 predictions. This introduces a dependency on a specific visual encoder and may not generalize across datasets or models. The paper does not analyze sensitivity to this choice.
- **No comparison to human annotation quality**: While the paper argues that human evaluation is costly, a small-scale human study on a subset would strengthen the claim that the FSE framework captures meaningful annotation insufficiency. Without it, the metric's validity rests entirely on internal consistency.
- **The utility-as-proxy experiment is unsurprising**: That fused (visual + concept) mode performs well while concept-only mode performs poorly is expected, as the model can rely on visual features. The paper could strengthen this by showing cases where fused mode *misleads* (e.g., high accuracy despite wrong concepts), but it only shows the discrepancy.

### Trivial

- The paper uses "LLMs" to refer to multimodal models (VLMs), which is slightly imprecise but clarified early on.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation study varying the number of concept-gathering stages (e.g., 3, 5, 7) to justify the five-stage choice.
- A small human evaluation where annotators rate concept sufficiency for a subset of samples, to correlate with CRI scores.
- Analysis of whether the CRI gap between fast and slow modes correlates with model size or family, which could inform design of better annotators.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the key insight is that LLMs exhibit a "knowledge externalization gap": they can correctly classify images from visual input but fail to produce textual concepts that would support the same classification. This suggests that the internal representations used for visual recognition are not fully accessible via language generation, even for the same model. This has implications not only for XAI but also for understanding the alignment between visual and textual modalities in VLMs.

## Suggestions

- To address the self-evaluation confound, consider using a separate model (or a different prompt) for concept generation and classification, or evaluate whether human readers can classify from the generated concepts.
- Provide a clear ablation of the concept-gathering stages, showing the marginal contribution of each stage to CRI.
- Include a discussion of when the FSE framework might give misleading results (e.g., if the model's text reasoning is systematically flawed).
