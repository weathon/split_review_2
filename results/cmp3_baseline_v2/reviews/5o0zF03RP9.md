## Summary

The paper proposes **IncentRL**, a reinforcement learning (RL) framework that augments the standard reward with a KL-divergence penalty between a predicted outcome distribution and a preferred outcome distribution.  The central claimed novelty is a Bayesian adaptation scheme for the trade-off weight  $\beta$ , intended to eliminate manual tuning.  Experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid show that small positive  $\beta$ values can improve sample efficiency and final performance over a vanilla RL baseline.

## Strengths

- The idea of adaptively controlling the influence of an intrinsic shaping term via Bayesian inference is conceptually appealing and, if properly implemented, could address a known limitation of many intrinsic motivation methods.
- The paper draws connections to cognitively motivated ideas (dopamine RPE, Free Energy Principle), which may inspire interdisciplinary discussion.
- The experimental environments (MiniGrid, MountainCar) are standard sparse-reward benchmarks, making the evaluation context clear.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

1. **The Bayesian adaptation mechanism is not specified.** The paper repeatedly claims that  $\beta$  is treated as a Bayesian random variable and updated online, but it never defines the prior, the likelihood, the update rule, or how  $\beta$  is sampled during training.  The algorithm description (Section 5.1) only shows a fixed  $\beta$  in the shaped reward; the Bayesian update is completely absent from the pseudocode and the implementation details.  Without this specification, the central novelty of the paper is not reproducible, and the claimed contribution ("Bayesian adaptation of preference gaps removes the need for manual trade-off tuning") is unsubstantiated.

### Major

2. **The KL shaping term is not operationalized.** The method requires a predicted outcome distribution  $p(o|s,a)$  and a preferred outcome distribution  $q(o|s)$ .  The paper does not explain how  $p(o|s,a)$  is obtained in practice—is it the true transition model, a learned forward model, or something else?  In MountainCar and MiniGrid, it is unclear whether the agent has access to the true dynamics or must learn  $p$  from data.  Without this detail, the algorithm is not well-defined for the claimed experimental settings.

3. **The experiments lack comparison to standard intrinsic motivation baselines.** The baseline is only vanilla RL ( β=0 ).  There is no comparison to curiosity-driven exploration (e.g., ICM, RND), empowerment, or information-gain methods.  Given that the paper positions IncentRL as an intrinsic motivation approach, the lack of such comparisons makes it impossible to assess whether the proposed method offers any advantage over existing techniques, even in these simple domains.

4. **The claimed benefit of Bayesian adaptation is not empirically validated online.** Figures 3 and 4 show posterior concentration over "rounds" after training, but there is no experiment that compares the *online performance* of the Bayesian  β  adaptation against the best fixed  β  (or against a manual tuning baseline).  The MiniGrid experiment uses a fixed  β=0.01 ; the Bayesian method is only mentioned in passing ("In additional runs (not shown)").  The claim that "Bayesian adaptation can match the best fixed choice without manual tuning" is therefore not supported by the presented results.

5. **The theoretical analysis is trivial.** Proposition 1 (small  β  preserves optimality) and Proposition 2 ( β→∞  ignores external reward) are essentially continuity arguments that hold for any finite MDP with bounded perturbation.  These provide no new insight into the behavior or convergence of IncentRL, nor do they guide the choice of  β .

### Minor

- The algorithm description is extremely generic ("IncentRL can be applied to any RL algorithm") and does not explain how the KL term is computed or integrated with the policy update, especially when  p  must be learned.
- The Bayesian  β  adaptation is presented as a central contribution, yet it appears only in the MiniGrid experiment and without any performance comparison.
- The paper uses "preference-prediction shaping" but does not distinguish this from standard reward shaping (Ng et al.) or potential-based shaping, which preserve optimality.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A clear specification of the Bayesian model (prior, likelihood, update step) and how  β  is sampled and integrated into the RL loop.
- Experiments comparing IncentRL to representative intrinsic motivation methods (curiosity, RND, ICM) on the same benchmarks.
- An ablation study directly comparing online performance of Bayesian  β  adaptation vs. fixed  β  sweeps, alongside computational overhead.
- Details on how  p(o|s,a)  is learned (architecture, training procedure) in complex environments like MiniGrid.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions

1. Provide a full algorithmic description of the Bayesian  β  adaptation, including prior, likelihood, and update rule, or remove the claim of Bayesian adaptation if it is not a core part of the method.
2. Specify how  p(o|s,a)  is obtained in each environment. If it must be learned, describe the learning process and how it interacts with the RL objective.
3. Add comparisons to standard intrinsic motivation baselines (e.g., ICM, RND) on at least the MiniGrid and MountainCar tasks.
4. Include an online comparison of the Bayesian  β  method vs. the best fixed  β  (e.g., learning curves) to support the claim that manual tuning is eliminated.
