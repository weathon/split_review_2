## Summary
This paper introduces VisualPRM400K, the first large-scale multimodal process supervision dataset (~400K samples) constructed via an automatic Monte Carlo sampling pipeline. Using this dataset, the authors train VisualPRM, an 8B multimodal Process Reward Model (PRM) that estimates step-level correctness in reasoning chains. They also present VisualProcessBench, a human-annotated benchmark (2,866 samples, 26,950 step labels) for evaluating step-wise error detection. Experiments show that VisualPRM consistently improves Best-of-N reasoning performance across multiple MLLM families and scales (e.g., +8.4 points for InternVL2.5-8B, +5.9 for InternVL2.5-78B over seven benchmarks), outperforming Outcome Reward Models and Self-Consistency. The dataset, model, and benchmark will be released.

## Strengths
- **First multimodal PRM dataset and model.** To my knowledge, this is the first work to construct a large-scale process supervision dataset for multimodal reasoning and train a dedicated multimodal PRM. This fills a clear gap in the literature.
- **Thorough and convincing experimental evaluation.** The paper evaluates BoN performance across seven diverse multimodal reasoning benchmarks, four policy model families (MiniCPM, QwenVL, InternVL2.5 at multiple scales), and compares against ORM and Self-Consistency. The improvements are consistent and substantial (3.7–8.9 points overall). The ablation studies on modeling choices (value vs. advantage, early stop, aggregation methods) are informative.
- **Well-constructed benchmark.** VisualProcessBench is carefully designed: it collects questions from five representative multimodal reasoning benchmarks, generates solutions from five different MLLMs for diversity, and employs human experts with quality control. The use of macro F1 and the requirement to detect all errors (not just the first) is a thoughtful improvement over prior benchmarks.
- **Clear and well-structured presentation.** The paper is easy to follow, with clear definitions of the data pipeline, PRM modeling, and evaluation settings. Figures and tables are informative.
- **Open-source release.** The commitment to release data, model, and benchmark will significantly benefit the community and enable future research.

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Noise in automatic data pipeline.** The process supervision labels are generated by Monte Carlo sampling with only 16 continuations per step, and a step is labeled correct if *any* continuation leads to a correct answer (mc_i > 0). This threshold is very permissive and likely introduces substantial label noise (e.g., a step that is actually wrong but happens to lead to a correct completion by chance). The paper acknowledges trying a higher threshold but says it hurt performance (Section B, not provided). The impact of this noise on PRM quality is not deeply analyzed. While the empirical results are positive, the noise could limit the ceiling of performance and may cause the PRM to learn spurious correlations.
2. **Lack of training loss specification.** The paper describes the multi-turn chat formulation and that the model predicts correctness tokens, but does not specify the training loss (presumably cross-entropy on the token prediction). The inference method (using generation probabilities of "+" and "-" as scores) is described, but the equivalence to a standard PRM with a scalar output head is not discussed. This omission affects reproducibility.
3. **Incomplete details for VisualProcessBench evaluation.** For MLLM evaluation, the prompt template used to ask models to judge each step is not provided. For PRM evaluation, the threshold on probability difference to decide correctness is not specified. These details are important for reproducibility and fair comparison.
4. **Limited exploration of PRM architecture/training.** The paper acknowledges this as a limitation, but the PRM is essentially a fine-tuned MLLM with a multi-turn chat format. Alternative approaches (e.g., adding a separate value head, using a smaller dedicated model) are not explored. The paper would be stronger with some analysis of design choices beyond the value vs. advantage comparison.

### Minor
- The dataset size is given as "about 400K" but the exact number is not stated.
- The paper states that "about 10% of steps are incorrect" in VisualPRM400K, but the benchmark statistics show 28.5% incorrect steps (7691/26950). This discrepancy is not explained.
- The text-only experiments (Table 5) are interesting but the paper does not discuss why a multimodal PRM works well on text-only inputs. A brief explanation would be helpful.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- An analysis of the noise in the automatic pipeline (e.g., human evaluation of a subset of labels) would strengthen confidence in the dataset quality.
- A comparison with a PRM trained using a separate value head (rather than token prediction) would be informative.
- Providing the exact prompt used for MLLM-as-judge evaluation in VisualProcessBench.

## Novel Insights
Beyond the paper's own contributions, the key insight is that process-level supervision can be effectively generated for multimodal reasoning via Monte Carlo sampling, and that a PRM trained on such noisy data still yields substantial gains in Best-of-N selection. The finding that value-based PRMs outperform advantage-based PRMs under noisy supervision is practically useful. The benchmark results starkly reveal that even strong open-source MLLMs (e.g., InternVL2.5-78B) perform near random guessing on step-level correctness detection, underscoring the need for dedicated PRMs.

## Suggestions
- Specify the training loss and inference threshold for PRM evaluation in the main text or appendix.
- Provide the prompt template used for MLLM evaluation on VisualProcessBench.
- Include a small-scale human validation of the automatic pipeline labels to quantify noise.
- Discuss why the PRM transfers to text-only tasks (e.g., shared reasoning structure).
