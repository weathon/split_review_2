## Summary

IncentRL augments the standard RL reward with a KL divergence between the agent's predicted outcome distribution \(p(o|s,a)\) and a preferred outcome distribution \(q(o|s)\), weighted by a coefficient \(\beta\). The paper claims as its **central novelty** that \(\beta\) is treated as a Bayesian random variable adapted online to eliminate manual trade-off tuning. Experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 compare a fixed-\(\beta\) version of IncentRL against standard Q-learning/DQN (i.e., \(\beta=0\)).

## Strengths

- **Clean formulation of preference-prediction shaping (Eq. 1).** The idea of penalizing \(\text{KL}(p(o|s,a)\|q(o|s))\) to align predicted outcomes with preferred ones is conceptually clear and distinguishes IncentRL from curiosity-based intrinsic motivation (which rewards prediction error) or empowerment (which rewards information gain). The objective is specified precisely in Section 3.2.

- **MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 delivers concrete, non-trivial improvements.** Figure 2 shows that a small fixed \(\beta=0.01\) achieves 98% final success rate vs. 90.5% for \(\beta=0\), and more notably reduces mean episode length from 75 to 29 steps—a ~2.6× efficiency gain in a sparse-reward task.

- **Honest reporting of failure modes.** Table 2 documents that \(\beta=0.3\) and \(\beta=1.0\) degrade MountainCar performance by 52.8% and 75.0%, respectively. This negative result is reported clearly and provides useful information about the method's sensitivity to \(\beta\).

- **Toy-domain experiment with adequate statistics.** The 2-state MDP experiment uses 7 seeds and reports means with standard deviations, cleanly isolating the effect of the KL shaping term in a controlled setting.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The claimed central contribution—Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\)—is never described in the paper.** The abstract states that "the central novelty is treating the incentive weight \(\beta\) as a Bayesian random variable, updated online." The contributions list calls it "a Bayesian scheme for adapting the incentive weight \(\beta\)." Yet the paper contains **no specification of the Bayesian inference procedure anywhere**: no prior over \(\beta\), no likelihood function, no update rule, no description of how posterior samples are generated or used during training. Section 3 (Methods) describes only the KL shaping term. The algorithm listing in Section 5.1 (lines 149–155) describes how the shaped reward is computed but says nothing about \(\beta\) updates. The "Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation" subsection (lines 230–247) shows posterior means and samples (Figs. 3–4) and Table 3 gives summary statistics, but never states what data \(\beta\) conditions on or what generative model produces these posterior quantities. Even the notion of "rounds" is left undefined. A reader cannot tell whether this is Bayesian optimization, Thompson sampling, empirical Bayes, or something else entirely. **A paper cannot be evaluated on a claim it does not specify.** This is not a missing implementation detail—it is the absence of the paper's first-listed contribution.

### Major

- **The main experiments do not actually use the Bayesian \(\beta\) adaptation.** All reported results in Figures 1–2 and Tables 1–2 use fixed, manually-swept \(\beta\) values. The Bayesian version appears only in a single sentence: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (line 228). Figures 3–4 show posterior evolution, but it is ambiguous whether these \(\beta\) values were used during training or computed post-hoc. This means the paper's central claim has no direct experimental support as an online learning mechanism.

- **No comparisons against existing intrinsic motivation methods.** The paper motivates itself by claiming that existing approaches rely on fixed trade-offs, yet the experiments compare only against the base RL algorithm (Q-learning/DQN) without the shaping term. There are no comparisons against curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., 2017; Burda et al., 2018), RND, ICM, empowerment, or any other standard intrinsic motivation baseline. Without these, there is no evidence that IncentRL improves upon existing approaches at all, let alone that its \(\beta\) adaptation addresses a real limitation.

- **The method for obtaining \(p(o|s,a)\) is underspecified.** The predicted outcome distribution is central to the method, but for MountainCar and MiniGrid the paper never describes how \(p(o|s,a)\) is obtained—no architecture, training procedure, loss function, or update schedule. The paper mentions "a forward model" as a possibility but provides no implementation details. For MountainCar, \(q(o|s)\) is described as "assigning all probability to the goal (\(x \ge 0.5\))," but it is unclear how this defines a valid distribution over a continuous outcome space. These omissions make the experiments difficult to reproduce.

- **Thin empirical base.** The evaluation consists of three environments: (i) a trivial 2-state MDP where any reward shaping toward the goal mechanically helps, (ii) MountainCar-v0 (3 seeds), where only one of three \(\beta\) values shows a modest 18% improvement, and (iii) MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 (3 seeds), where a single \(\beta=0.01\) is compared against \(\beta=0\). Three environments with 3 seeds on the non-trivial tasks do not constitute a convincing evaluation for claims about "general-purpose RL agents."

### Minor

- **The theoretical analysis (Propositions 1–2) is too weak to be a contribution.** Proposition 1 states that for sufficiently small \(\beta\) the optimal policy remains optimal under the shaped objective—a generic continuity result that holds for any bounded reward perturbation in a finite MDP. Proposition 2 states that as \(\beta \to \infty\) the KL term dominates—a trivial limit observation. These are not incorrect, but they provide no insight specific to IncentRL beyond what follows from the definition of the objective.

### Trivial

- None beyond what is noted above.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison against at least one alternative intrinsic motivation method (curiosity, RND, or empowerment) on the MiniGrid Doorkey task would strengthen the empirical claims.
- Reporting statistical significance or confidence intervals for the main comparisons would help assess result reliability.
- Clarifying whether the posterior evolution in Figures 3–4 was computed online during training or post-hoc from fixed-\(\beta\) runs is essential for interpreting the Bayesian claims.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The gap between the paper's framing (Bayesian adaptation as central novelty) and its content (no specification of the Bayesian mechanism, no experimental evidence of it working in training) is the core finding of this review, but it is an observation about the paper's structure rather than a novel insight about the subject matter.

## Suggestions

1. **Specify the Bayesian adaptation mechanism.** This is a prerequisite for the paper to be about what it claims to be about. The prior over \(\beta\), the likelihood function, the inference procedure, and the sampling schedule must be fully described.
2. **Run the Bayesian \(\beta\) as the actual training procedure on at least one benchmark** and compare its performance against both the best manually-tuned fixed \(\beta\) and standard RL. Figures 3–4 should come with a clear statement of whether the shown \(\beta\) samples were used during training.
3. **Add comparisons against at least one existing intrinsic motivation method** (e.g., curiosity-driven exploration, RND) to establish that IncentRL offers advantages beyond standard RL.
4. **Specify how \(p(o|s,a)\) is obtained** for each non-trivial experiment—architecture, learning procedure, and how it interacts with the RL update.
