## Summary

TokenCount proposes a training-free object counting framework that works with SAM without architectural modifications. It operates in two stages: (1) probabilistic prompt generation via iterative multinomial sampling from a softmax-normalized similarity map, and (2) output-token-based verification using the TS-SS geometric similarity metric on SAM's decoder output tokens. On FSC-147 it achieves MAE 16.25 (best among SAM-only training-free methods) and on CARPK it achieves MAE 4.68 (beating all listed training-based methods).

## Strengths

- **First to directly repurpose SAM's output tokens as a verification criterion for counting.** The idea is well-motivated by SAM's architecture — output tokens encode combined semantic+positional information through bidirectional attention with the image embedding. Figure 5 provides PCA evidence that output tokens form clearly separable positive/negative clusters whereas image embeddings do not, lending concrete support to the approach's rationale.

- **Best-performing SAM-only training-free method on FSC-147.** Table 1 shows MAE 16.25 vs. TFOC (19.95), Count-Anything (27.97), and vanilla SAM (42.48). On CARPK, MAE 4.68 beats every listed training-based method (FamNet 18.19, BMNet+ 5.76, SAFECOUNT 5.33, LOCA 9.97).

- **Three controlled ablation studies cleanly isolate each proposed component.** Tables 3, 4, and 5 separately test (a) the verification metric (TS-SS vs. dot product, cosine, L2), (b) the iteration/prompt configuration, and (c) the choice of vector space (output tokens vs. image embeddings). This makes it clear which design decisions drive performance.

- **The TS-SS metric is well-chosen and ablated against alternatives.** Table 3 shows TS-SS (16.25 MAE) beats dot product (19.58), cosine similarity (16.48), and L2 distance (16.53), with a clear rationale about why standard metrics are inadequate for output tokens that jointly encode semantic (angular) and positional (magnitude) information.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Abstract overclaim contradicted by the paper's own results.** The abstract states "our method achieves superior accuracy with an MAE of 16.25, outperforming existing training-based and training-free counting methods." Table 1 directly contradicts this: BMNet+ (14.62), SAFECOUNT (14.32), PseCo (13.05), LOCA (10.79), and TFCAC (12.26) all achieve better MAE on FSC-147. The method is competitive among SAM-only approaches (which is a fair claim) but does not broadly outperform the field. This is the paper's headline claim and it is not supported by the evidence presented.

- **Extremely high RMSE/MAE ratio reveals severe reliability issues.** On FSC-147, the method achieves MAE=16.25 but RMSE=133.11 — an RMSE/MAE ratio of approximately 8.2. All other methods in Table 1 have substantially lower ratios (TFCAC ~4.6, LOCA ~5.3, FamNet ~4.5, OmniCount ~6.1, Counting-DETR ~7.4). This is the worst ratio in the entire table, indicating that on a nontrivial fraction of images the method produces errors an order of magnitude larger than its typical error. The Discussion (Section 5) briefly mentions "performance degradation in scenarios with dense distributions of very small instances" but provides no quantitative analysis of when, why, or how often these catastrophic failures occur. For a counting method, reliability on individual images is critical, and this issue is not adequately acknowledged or analyzed.

- **No uncertainty quantification despite an inherently stochastic procedure.** The prompt generation stage explicitly samples from a multinomial distribution (Eq. 1, line 134: "prompts are sampled from a multinomial distribution"). This means the method is fundamentally stochastic — running it twice with different random seeds will produce different prompt locations and potentially different counts. Yet no error bars, standard deviations, or confidence intervals are reported anywhere. Every number in every table is a single point estimate. Without understanding the method's variance, it is impossible to assess whether the modest improvements claimed in the ablation studies (TS-SS over cosine: 0.23 MAE; output token space over image embedding: 0.69 MAE) are meaningful or within the method's own stochastic variation. This is the single most important missing piece in the experimental evaluation.

### Minor

- **Claimed component improvements are very small and not tested for significance.** The TS-SS metric (16.25) barely outperforms cosine similarity (16.48) — a 0.23 MAE difference (~1.4%). The output token space (16.25) barely outperforms image embedding space (16.94) — a 0.69 MAE difference (~4%). Meanwhile, changing the iteration count from 16 to 32 (with 128 prompts) changes MAE from 16.25 to 16.65 — a larger swing than either claimed component improvement. Without error bars or statistical tests, these differences cannot be distinguished from noise, and the paper's language describing each component as "critical" is disproportionate to the evidence.

- **No sensitivity analysis for key hyperparameters.** The temperature parameter (τ = 0.066) and verification threshold (300) are reported as single values with no ablation showing how performance varies as they change. The threshold of 300 is especially opaque since TS-SS is an unbounded product of two terms (triangle area and sector area) with no natural scale, making it unclear how this value was determined or how robust performance is to its choice.

- **Computational efficiency claims lack baseline comparisons.** The paper states its method is "more than twice as fast" as TFCAC but never reports TFCAC's inference time, making this impossible to verify or contextualize. The absolute inference time of 1.69s/image on FSC-147 is reported but not compared against other methods under identical hardware.

- **No quantitative failure case analysis.** The method's extreme RMSE/MAE ratio (worst in Table 1) strongly suggests systematic failures on certain image types (e.g., dense small instances as mentioned in the Discussion). No analysis of these failure modes is provided — no characterization of which images fail, by how much, and under what conditions.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- The exemplar selection process for CARPK ("randomly selected 12 objects from the training set") should be seeded and run multiple times to report variance, since different exemplar selections will change results.
- The one-shot results (Table 2: MAE 18.57) are noticeably worse than few-shot results (MAE 16.25), with a gap (2.32 MAE) larger than the improvement from either claimed component. A discussion of why the method benefits substantially from multiple exemplars would strengthen the analysis.

## Removed Points

These points were removed during the filtering process; treat them with caution.

- "Computational cost scales with prompts — same limitation as criticized prior work": Removed because the paper's criticism targets methods that compute *additional* similarity operations between image embeddings per prompt. TokenCount uses SAM's existing output tokens (generated during the decoder forward pass), which has a meaningfully different cost structure.
- "Exemplar requirement means it doesn't eliminate the annotation bottleneck": Removed because this mischaracterizes the training-free paradigm — exemplars are test-time inputs, not training annotations, and this is standard for class-agnostic counting.
- "Uses similarity for prompt generation despite claiming to bypass it": Removed because the paper clearly distinguishes prompt generation (which uses similarity) from verification (which uses output tokens). This is a fair distinction presented clearly in Sections 3.1 and 3.2.
- "One-shot results being noticeably worse than few-shot": Removed because this is expected behavior and the results are contextualized in the paper.
- "CARPK claim misleading because PseCo/Counting-DETR/CFOCNet+ are listed as '—'": Removed because the claim "outperforming all supervised methods" refers to the supervised methods actually evaluated on CARPK, which is factually correct.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The core observation — that SAM's output tokens encode both semantic and positional information that can serve as a verification signal — is the paper's own contribution, not an insight that emerges from the review process.

## Suggestions

1. **Fix the framing throughout.** The paper should present itself as the best-performing SAM-only training-free method, not as a method that "outperforms existing training-based and training-free counting methods." The honest story — showing that SAM's output tokens can serve as an effective verification signal, achieving competitive results without auxiliary models — is still a solid contribution.
2. **Add thorough variance analysis.** Run all experiments multiple times with different random seeds and report means with standard deviations. This would (a) establish whether the TS-SS and output-token improvements are statistically reliable, and (b) give practitioners a realistic sense of the method's stability.
3. **Analyze the high-RMSE failure cases.** Identify which images cause catastrophic errors, characterize them quantitatively (object size, density, category), and explain the failure mechanism. This would turn a weakness into a genuine contribution to understanding when SAM-based counting works and when it doesn't.
4. **Add hyperparameter sensitivity analysis** for τ and the verification threshold.
5. **Report inference times for baseline methods** (especially TFCAC) on identical hardware.
