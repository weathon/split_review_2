## Summary

This paper presents VisualPRM400K, the first large-scale multimodal process supervision dataset (~400K samples, ~2M step-level annotations), along with VisualPRM (an 8B multimodal Process Reward Model) and VisualProcessBench (a human-annotated benchmark with 2,866 samples and 26,950 step-wise labels). Under Best-of-N evaluation, VisualPRM improves reasoning performance across six policy models (three families, four scales) on seven multimodal reasoning benchmarks, with overall gains of +3.7 to +8.9 points. The paper further shows that PRMs consistently outperform Outcome Reward Models and Self-Consistency at all values of N up to 128.

## Strengths

1. **First multimodal PRM dataset filling a clear gap.** The paper correctly identifies that while text-only PRMs are well-studied (PRM800K, Math-Shepherd, OmegaPRM), multimodal process supervision has been absent. VisualPRM400K addresses this gap at scale (~400K samples), and VisualProcessBench provides the first dedicated benchmark for multimodal step-wise error detection.

2. **Broad and systematic BoN evaluation.** Table 2 evaluates VisualPRM as a critic for 6 policy models spanning 3 families (MiniCPM, QwenVL, InternVL) and 4 parameter scales (7B–78B). Every model improves under BoN with VisualPRM, and the gains are substantial (e.g., InternVL2.5-26B +8.9 overall, DynaMath worst-case accuracy improvements of +8.6 to +12.1 points). The cross-family gains on MiniCPM-V2.6 (+8.0) and Qwen2.5-VL-7B (+3.7) demonstrate that the PRM is not overfitted to the InternVL family.

3. **PRM consistently outperforms ORM and SC across all N.** Figure 4 reports BoN at N = 1, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128. The PRM exceeds both SC and ORM at every N, and the gap widens as N grows (e.g., +2.4 over SC at N=8 vs. +3.1 at N=128 for InternVL2.5-8B). This directly supports the paper's central claim that PRMs are the superior critic for test-time scaling.

4. **VisualProcessBench defines a more realistic evaluation setting.** Requiring models to detect *all* erroneous steps (rather than only the first) avoids false negatives when models exhibit reflection/self-correction. The annotation pipeline is concretely documented (13 experts, 39 person-days, 10% author review per split, $37/person-day), giving confidence in label quality.

5. **Informative ablations.** The comparison of value-based vs. advantage-based PRMs, early stopping, and aggregation methods (Section 4.3, Table 4) provides practical guidance. The finding that average aggregation outperforms selecting the maximum step score is non-trivial and explained by the distribution of errors across reasoning steps.

## Weaknesses

### Major
None.

### Minor

1. **No human validation of the automated data pipeline's labels.** The paper uses an `mc_i > 0` threshold (a step is "correct" if any of 16 Monte Carlo continuations reaches the correct answer), but provides no human agreement study to validate these labels. A step with only 1/16 correct continuations receives the same "correct" label as a flawless step. While the paper mentions trying a higher threshold and finding it hurt performance (Section B, in the stripped appendix), a direct measurement of precision/recall against human judgments on a sample of 100–200 steps would substantially strengthen confidence in data quality. The downstream BoN gains provide indirect validation, but this does not substitute for a direct quality check.

2. **All training data solutions are generated by InternVL2.5 models, creating an uncontrolled confound.** The step-by-step solutions in VisualPRM400K are exclusively generated by InternVL2.5-series models (line 130: "the step-by-step solutions S are sampled using InternVL2.5 series models"). This means the PRM learns what constitutes correct/incorrect steps from a single solution-generation style. The cross-family BoN gains (MiniCPM, Qwen2.5-VL) partially alleviate this concern, but the paper does not test whether a PRM trained on InternVL2.5-generated solutions transfers less well to a held-out policy model whose solution style differs sharply.

3. **No variance or statistical significance reporting.** All BoN results in Table 2 are point estimates from a single evaluation run. With N=8 candidates and temperature=0.7 sampling, there is non-negligible variance across seeds. Reporting standard deviations over multiple seeds (or at least for a subset of key results) would increase confidence, especially for the smaller gains (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-7B +3.7 overall).

4. **"Overall" score is a simple average across seven heterogeneous benchmarks.** The paper reports "Overall" as the arithmetic mean of scores on MMMU, MathVista, MathVision, MathVerse, DynaMath, WeMath, and LogicVista. These benchmarks differ substantially in sample size and difficulty. A weighted average or per-benchmark reporting without aggregation would be more informative.

5. **Ablations limited to two policy models.** The ablation study (Section 4.3, Figure 4) only uses InternVL2.5-8B and MiniCPM-V2.6 as policy models. Including Qwen2.5-VL-7B would make this more comprehensive.

6. **No qualitative analysis of what the PRM learns.** The paper shows *that* VisualPRM selects better responses but provides no analysis of *what* it looks for in individual steps. A qualitative examination of high-scoring vs. low-scoring steps (e.g., identifying whether it detects arithmetic errors vs. reasoning gaps) would be instructive.

### Trivial

- The text says "Section B" (referring to the threshold analysis) and "Section D" (step distribution) — these sections are in the stripped appendix, so readers cannot verify claims made about them. This is a limitation of the parsing but should be considered for camera-ready where the appendix is available.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A sensitivity analysis varying the `mc_i > 0` labeling threshold (the paper mentions trying this in the appendix, but the analysis could be more prominently presented).
- A small-scale experiment generating solutions with a different MLLM (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL or GPT-4o) and comparing PRM performance on those held-out solution styles to directly test the InternVL2.5-specific bias concern.
- Reporting weighted averages across benchmarks rather than simple averages.

## Removed Points

These points were flagged during consolidation and removed with justification:

1. **Figure 1 / Table 2 discrepancy (from Harsh Critic).** The OCR-extracted table in Figure 1 shows garbled text with repeated model names and values that don't match Table 2. This is a parser-induced formatting artifact, not an author error. Per rules, formatting artifacts from PDF extraction are removed.

2. **"Abstract/Introduction claim is thin" criticism.** The claim that "existing open-source MLLMs struggle to serve as critic models" is supported by Figure 1 (InternVL2.5-8B as critic underperforms baseline) and by Table 4's ablation showing InternVL2.5-8B and 78B as critics provide marginal gains. This is adequate support for a motivation statement.

3. **"Text-only section feels extraneous" criticism.** Showing that VisualPRM transfers to text-only reasoning (Table 5: +6.1 on MATH-500, +5.0 on GPQA-Diamond for Qwen2.5-7B) strengthens the paper by demonstrating the model captures general reasoning quality. This is a legitimate supporting result.

4. **"Missing analysis of neutral step exclusion" criticism.** The paper explicitly states neutral steps are excluded when computing F1 scores and reports their count (2,674 out of 26,950, ~10%). The paper acknowledges this and excluding them for the F1 calculation (which focuses on binary correct/incorrect classification) is standard practice.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The core observation — that a straightforward extension of Monte Carlo-based process supervision (Math-Shepherd) from text to multimodal settings works effectively across multiple model families — is itself the paper's main contribution. No reviewer surfaced an unexpected interpretation or counterintuitive finding not already present in the paper.

## Suggestions

1. **Add a human validation study of the automated pipeline** (100–200 randomly sampled steps) to measure precision/recall of the `mc_i > 0` criterion against human judgment. This would directly address the most significant concern about data quality and is the single highest-leverage improvement.
2. **Report variance** for at least the key BoN results (e.g., run 3 seeds for each setting and report mean ± std).
3. **Diversify the solution-generation model** in the data pipeline. Even a small-scale comparison (generating solutions with Qwen2.5-VL or GPT-4o and testing the resulting PRM on held-out solution styles) would strengthen claims about generality.
4. **Explicitly discuss** the InternVL2.5-only solution generation confound and why the cross-family BoN results mitigate it.

**Initial bracket:** 5.5–7.0. The paper is stronger than PRM800K (5.5) due to multimodal novelty and broader evaluation, but weaker than top-tier benchmarks (7.0+) due to automated labels without human validation and the InternVL2.5-only confound.

**Round 2 narrowing:**
- OpenPRM (6.0, accepted): Text-only PRM for open-domain tasks. Similar in nature (extending PRMs to a new domain) and evaluation style. The current paper has more comprehensive BoN evaluation (6 models, 7 benchmarks) and a dedicated benchmark, but its automated labels are weaker than OpenPRM's human-verified data. Comparable.
- RMB (6.0, accepted): Text-only RM benchmark. Similar contribution level (dataset+benchmark), but text-only. The current paper's multimodal aspect adds novelty.
- ToolComp (5.4, rejected): Process supervision benchmark but small scale (485 prompts). The current paper is substantially larger and better evaluated.

**Final score: 6.0.** The paper is comparable to accepted papers like OpenPRM and RMB. It brings clear novelty (first multimodal PRM dataset), solid empirical evidence, and a useful benchmark, but the lack of direct human validation of the automated pipeline and the InternVL2.5-only confound prevent it from reaching the 7+ tier.
