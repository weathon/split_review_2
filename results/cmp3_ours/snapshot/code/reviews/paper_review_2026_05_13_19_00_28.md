## Summary

This paper identifies a critical blind spot in the evaluation of frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems: pixel-level reconstruction metrics (PSNR, SSIM) do not capture identity-level leakage. The authors present FaceLinkGen, a deliberately simple distillation pipeline that extracts identity-discriminative embeddings from protected templates and uses them for linkage and face regeneration attacks. Across three recent PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), the attack achieves >98.5% linkage accuracy and >96% regeneration success, even with minimal training resources (256 images, <50 seconds). The paper further demonstrates vulnerability under constrained attacker assumptions, soft-biometric leakage, and preliminary transfer to de-identification systems.

## Strengths

- **Concrete, falsifiable counterexample to the prevailing evaluation paradigm**: Table 1 directly shows that high pixel-level similarity (SSIM=0.841, PSNR=26.81 for a CanFG-protected face) corresponds to near-zero identity similarity (FS=0.008), while two arbitrary photos of the same person have low pixel similarity (SSIM=0.235) yet meaningful identity similarity (FS=0.586). This pair of numbers alone exposes the fundamental disconnect between pixel-level and identity-level privacy.

- **Near-total attack success across all three target systems under the intended threat model**: Table 2 reports regeneration Success@5 ranging from 97.9% to 100% across three datasets and three PPFR methods. Table 6 directly contrasts FracFace's claimed protection rates (0.68–1.0 under its own frequency-channel metric) with near-zero protection under FaceLinkGen's identity-centric evaluation (0.000–0.008). This is a devastating empirical demonstration.

- **The simplicity of the attack is a deliberate strength, not a weakness**: The distillation pipeline is standard (cosine similarity loss, ArcFace teacher/student, no adversarial optimization). Training completes in under two hours on a single consumer GPU. Section 5.1 shows the attack works with only 256 training images and under 50 seconds of training (90.5% regeneration success, 98.7% linkage accuracy). This makes the argument that the vulnerability is structural — inherent to the representations themselves, not an artifact of a clever attack.

- **Threat model discussion properly grounds the evaluation**: Section 2 provides a principled return to the insider (service-provider) adversary originally motivating PPFR, showing that the oracle-access assumption is both realistic and less restrictive than assumptions in prior work (e.g., MinusFace assumes knowledge of conversion architecture, PartialFace assumes knowledge of conversion process). The argument that a locally running conversion model can be reverse-engineered by the service provider is well-reasoned.

- **Constrained-assumption experiment demonstrates robustness of the finding**: Section 6 shows that even without oracle access, a generic Gaussian-blur high-pass filter proxy achieves >92% 1-to-1 matching and >94% regeneration Success@5 (Face++) across all three methods using a single trained model. This rules out the possibility that the attack depends on specific knowledge of the conversion process.

- **Soft-biometric leakage quantification challenges prior defensive claims**: Table 9 shows that race, gender, and age can be inferred from protected templates at nearly original-image accuracy (e.g., PartialFace gender accuracy 0.932 vs. original 0.949), directly contradicting FracFace authors' public rebuttal claims of successful obfuscation.

- **Multi-API cross-validation avoids evaluation self-dealing**: Results are validated with both Face++ and Amazon Rekognition (Tables 2, 5, 7), and embedding-space analysis (Section 7) confirms identity extraction is independent of any single verification system or generative model.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

### Minor

- **TIP-IM experiment uses the same ArcFace model for both attack and the FR system TIP-IM targets**: In Section 8, the teacher model f_t (ArcFace) used to train the student is also the model that TIP-IM's adversarial masks are designed to fool. The paper frames this as recovery "by a student aligned to a different space," but the student was trained via cosine similarity loss against f_t embeddings, so the spaces are closely aligned. This limits how much the experiment demonstrates about de-identification more broadly. The paper appropriately hedges by calling these "pilot evaluations" with "preliminary results," and the core contribution targets PPFR (not de-identification), so this does not threaten the main claims.

- **Amazon API results in the constrained-attack setting show a sharp drop (44–57%) relative to Face++ (94–96%)**: The paper acknowledges this (attributing it to Amazon being "more strict or sensitive to AI-generated images") but does not investigate whether the drop reflects genuinely weaker identity recovery or merely synthetic-image rejection. A simple control (e.g., comparing Amazon scores on real-vs-real image pairs) would clarify the source of the gap. This affects the constrained-attack narrative but not the main oracle-access results, which also have Amazon cross-validation in Table 5.

### Trivial

- Student model architecture details beyond "prepend a 3×3 Conv2D to Antelopev2" are deferred to appendix. For a method whose simplicity is central, the main paper should at minimum state whether the student backbone is identical to the teacher's. This is a one-sentence fix.

- The threat model section (Section 2) is thorough but somewhat longer than necessary; some of the critique of external-attacker framings could be condensed.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Open-set linkage evaluation with distractor identities and false-positive rates would strengthen the practical leakage claim beyond the current closed-set top-1 recall.

- A deeper investigation of the Face++/Amazon discrepancy in the constrained setting (e.g., control experiments comparing scores on real-vs-real portraits) would clarify whether the gap reflects weaker identity recovery or metric confounding.

- Including a panel of regeneration failure cases would give readers intuition about the attack's boundary conditions.

- Testing the regeneration pipeline with a different generative model (e.g., StyleGAN-based) would further rule out dependence on Arc2Face-specific properties, though the embedding-space analysis in Section 7 already addresses this concern to a degree.

## Novel Insights

The most striking insight emerging from this work is that the attack's simplicity is itself the strongest evidence. Prior work in this space often equated stronger attacks with more sophisticated methodology, but FaceLinkGen demonstrates the reverse: if a standard distillation pipeline with 256 training images and 50 seconds of training can defeat three independently designed PPFR systems, the vulnerability is not in the attack design but in the fundamental assumption that scrambling pixels in the frequency domain scrambles identity. This reframes the evaluation burden: the question is no longer whether an attack is clever enough, but whether the representation retains any identity-discriminative structure at all — and the paper shows that utility-preserving PPFR templates, by design, must.
