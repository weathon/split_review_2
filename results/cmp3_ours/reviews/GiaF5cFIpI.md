## Summary

This paper proposes an integrated streaming pipeline for real-time adaptive neural stimulation in latent spaces. The system combines online dimensionality reduction (proSVD, sjPCA, mmICA), dynamical modeling (Kalman filter, VJF, Bubblewrap), a kernel-regression stimulus-response mapping (Ŝ), and constrained optimization to design high-dimensional stimulation patterns that push latent dynamics in a desired direction. The method is validated on a toy model and on real neural datasets (calcium imaging, electrophysiology) where stimulation effects are synthetically injected via an AR(1) process. Runtime benchmarking confirms end-to-end latencies <10 ms on average.

## Strengths

- **Complete end-to-end pipeline for a difficult problem.** The paper integrates streaming latent space construction, dynamical modeling, kernel-regression stimulus-response learning, and constrained optimization into a single real-time system. While individual components are not novel in isolation, their integration into a closed-loop stimulation framework is new and addresses a genuine gap — prior work has addressed these subproblems separately.
- **Credible runtime evidence.** End-to-end runtimes averaging <10 ms and consistently <100 ms are reported, which is concrete evidence of practical viability for closed-loop neuroscience experiments at typical data rates.
- **Well-motivated problem.** Real-time design of high-dimensional neural stimulations to manipulate latent dynamics is an important open challenge for causal testing of manifold hypotheses in neuroscience. The paper clearly articulates why this is hard (combinatorial stimulation space, state-dependent responses, non-negativity and sparsity constraints).

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Validation of stimulus-response mapping on "real neural data" uses entirely synthetic stimulation effects.** In Section 4.1, the paper states: "For each of the real datasets, we simulated stimulations using an autoregressive function to model a fast rise in neural activity… $y_t = r_t + a_t$, $a_t = 0.8 \cdot a_{t-1} + u_t$." The effects of stimulation on real neural data are generated by a hand-designed AR(1) process — i.e., the method is tested on whether it can learn a simple synthetic function injected by the experimenters, not on whether it can model actual neural responses to optogenetic or electrical stimulation. The paper acknowledges this in the Discussion ("our real data experiments were performed offline"), but this understates the issue: the problem is not offline-vs-online, but that the ground-truth stimulus-response mapping on real data is an experimenter-created AR(1) process. The claim of having demonstrated the approach on "real neural data" for stimulation-response modeling is therefore not supported by the evidence presented. The toy model experiments provide clean verification against a known $S$, but the leap from that to a claim of real-world validity is unbridged.

2. **No comparison against existing stimulation design methods.** The paper cites Wagenmaker et al. (2024) on active learning, Minai et al. (2024) on Bayesian optimization, and Yang et al. (2021) on input-output dynamical modeling, yet never benchmarks against any of these methods — even on the toy model where such a comparison would be straightforward. The baselines used (random single neurons, random groups, shuffled stimuli, and a "blind" method that ignores stimulation times) are useful sanity checks but do not establish the method's advantages over prior work. The paper shows the method is better than doing nothing; it does not show it is better than existing approaches.

### Minor

3. **Abstract overclaims a capability that is not demonstrated.** The abstract promises "adaptive selection of stimulations to best distinguish amongst neural subspace hypotheses" and "a novel streaming estimator to determine which representation is most predictive." In Section 2.2, this estimator is simply comparing prediction errors across models at each timepoint — an online model selection procedure, not a fundamentally novel estimator. Moreover, no experiment actually uses this capability for adaptive switching between representations or for designing stimulations to disambiguate subspace hypotheses. Figure 1c shows only descriptive heatmaps of which space predicts best where.

4. **No ablation studies isolating component contributions.** The system has at least four major components: (a) streaming latent space construction (three options), (b) dynamical modeling (three options), (c) kernel regression for Ŝ with three separate kernels ($K_1, K_2, K_3$), and (d) constrained optimization. There are no ablations that isolate the contribution of any component. How much does the location-dependent kernel ($K_1$) matter vs. a location-independent response model? How much does the temporal discounting ($K_3$) matter vs. a static model? How much does streaming latent space help vs. a periodically recomputed fixed space? Without these, a reader cannot tell which parts of the system are essential.

5. **Optimization objective is under-specified.** The term $\|u\|_0^{\max}$ in Equation 8 is never clearly defined (it appears to be the maximum allowed number of non-zero elements, but this is only inferable from context). The optimization solver is not specified (Algorithm 1 line 19 says only "Solve with box constraints"), and sensitivity to initialization is not discussed. These are fixable clarity issues.

6. **Toy model is quite simple.** The toy model (Section 3, Eq. 9) has binary stimulation ($u_t \in \{0,1\}$) with a deterministic effect on only the third latent dimension. This is a forgiving test case that does not stress-test the method on the noise, nonlinearity, and high-dimensional coupling characteristic of real optogenetic responses.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Ablation studies separating the roles of $K_1$ (location dependence), $K_2$ (stimulus dependence), and $K_3$ (temporal discounting) would substantially strengthen the paper.
- Specification of the optimization solver and discussion of sensitivity to initialization.
- Systematic analysis of how the temporal kernel length scale affects the trade-off between adaptation speed and estimation variance.
- Comparison against at least one existing stimulation design method (e.g., Bayesian optimization as in Minai et al., 2024) on the toy model.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed; treat them with caution.

- *"The 'novel' contribution of sjPCA is a relatively minor modification"* — This is an editorial opinion about novelty level, not a specific verifiable weakness. The Orthogonal Procrustes stabilization is a concrete algorithmic addition.
- *"Statistical rigor: many claims stated without formal quantification"* — The paper does report standard deviations and $N=10$ runs in Figures 1a and 2e. The criticism was too generic to keep as a standalone weakness.
- *"Hyperparameters not analyzed"* — The paper notes that kernel length scales are "optionally tuned by stochastic coordinate descent at each new observation" (Section 2.3), which is a reasonable design choice for a system paper. Systematic hyperparameter sweeps across all components would be nice-to-have but not a required weakness.
- *"The paper has a decisive validation gap" framed as structural/fatal* — Downgraded to Major. The toy model validates core algorithms against a known $S$, and the runtime evidence is solid. What is missing is the final validation step, not a proof that the method cannot work.
- *Section-by-section editorial notes* (e.g., "the framing overpromises", "sjPCA adds a minor modification") — These are observations, not discrete weaknesses.

## Novel Insights

The reviews do not surface any insight beyond the paper's own contributions. The key concern is consistent across reviewers: the real-data validation is not real validation, because the stimulation-response function being learned is a synthetic AR(1) process. No reviewer identifies a flaw in the algorithmic design itself or a reason the method would fundamentally fail — the gap is evidentiary, not architectural.

## Suggestions

The single highest-leverage improvement is to validate the stimulus-response mapping against actual neural responses to stimulation. This could take the form of: (a) using a publicly available dataset with paired optogenetic stimulation and recording, (b) running closed-loop in simulation with a biophysically realistic spiking network model, or (c) even a limited proof-of-concept with a small number of real stimulation trials. Without this, the paper's central claim remains unsubstantiated.

Second, adding at least one baseline comparison against an existing stimulation design method (e.g., Bayesian optimization) on the toy model would significantly strengthen the evidential position of the paper.
