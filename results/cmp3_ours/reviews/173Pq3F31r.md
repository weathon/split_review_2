## Summary
This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework for joint Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR: scores → performances) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT: performances → scores), leveraging disentangled note-level score content and global performance style representations. It introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, and demonstrates that EPR can be formulated as a Seq2Seq task without note-level alignment. The model achieves competitive results on both tasks across objective metrics, subjective listening tests, and representation analysis.

## Strengths
- **Seq2Seq EPR without note-level alignment is a genuine practical advance.** The paper demonstrates competitive EPR performance using only sequence-level supervision, matching or exceeding alignment-dependent baselines (Tables 2–3). Ours(PSR) achieves the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) in Table 3. This removes a significant practical bottleneck.
- **The PSR module addresses a real limitation of prior work.** Existing EPR models require composer labels or manual parameter control; generating style embeddings from score content alone (Section 3.4) via diffusion is technically sound and novel. Figure 4 shows similar era-wise clustering between PSR-generated and real-performance style embeddings, providing visual evidence of stylistic appropriateness.
- **Competitive results across both tasks.** On APT (Table 1), Ours achieves the best composite MUSTER E_avg (12.48) with statistically significant improvements on several sub-metrics. On EPR (Table 2), Ours(Target) achieves the best MAE for duration (0.37) and velocity (10.33) among all methods, as well as best KL for velocity (1.76).
- **Broad evaluation covering multiple dimensions.** The evaluation spans objective APT metrics (MUSTER, ScoreSimilarity), objective EPR metrics (variance, KL, MAE, alignment/insertion/missing rates), subjective listening tests (Figure 2), performer/composer classification (Table 4), style transfer listening tests (Figure 5), and latent space visualizations (Figures 3–4).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **The "shared content space" between z_x and z_y is not explicitly enforced, creating a gap between the conceptual claim and the architecture.** The paper states (Section 3.2, line 79) that score and performance domains "share a latent content space Z_c," and the Figure 1 caption says z_x and z_y "are encouraged to align." However, examining the training objective (Equation 6: L_total = L_EPR + L_APT + L_rec,X + L_rec,Y + L_KL), there is no direct loss that aligns or compares z_x and z_y. They are trained through entirely separate pathways—z_x from scores via L_EPR and L_rec,X; z_y from performances via L_APT and L_rec,Y—with no explicit similarity constraint. The implicit alignment from shared decoder parameters and task objectives is plausible but unmeasured. The paper's headline claim about joint modeling via shared content representations would be more accurately described as multi-task learning with separate content encoders. Adding an explicit alignment loss (cosine similarity, L2, or adversarial domain confusion) would close this gap.

- **The benefit of joint training is confounded with increased data scale.** Ours(Target) is compared against EPR-Only (Section 5.1, line 235) to argue that joint modeling improves EPR. These two conditions differ simultaneously in: (a) joint EPR+APT vs. EPR-only training, and (b) paired ASAP + large unpaired data (75,913 MuseScore scores + YouTube transcriptions) vs. ASAP only. The paper acknowledges this confounding (line 235: "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data") but does not resolve it. A controlled ablation—e.g., joint model trained only on paired ASAP data, or EPR-Only augmented with unpaired data—is needed to attribute improvements to joint modeling per se rather than to data scale.

### Minor
- **Disentanglement evidence is confounded by an architectural asymmetry.** Table 4 shows that style embeddings (z_s, a global vector) encode performer/composer information far better than content embeddings (z_c, a note-level sequence). The paper acknowledges (line 257) that "as a global embedding, z_s is better suited for capturing high-level stylistic features than the note-level z_c," which means the classification gap could partially reflect the classifier's ease of processing a single vector vs. a variable-length sequence. A controlled comparison using a pooled version of z_c (e.g., mean, attention-pooled) would isolate whether the gap is due to disentanglement or to architectural convenience. Additionally, no intervention experiment (e.g., swapping z_s between pieces and verifying style transfer with objective metrics) is conducted.

- **Style transfer listening test has interpretability issues.** In Figure 5, the "Original" condition (same piece, same style) receives systematically lower overall quality ratings than the "Mean" (interpolated) condition across all three samples. If style transfer only modifies style while preserving content, "Original" should maintain high quality. This pattern suggests either that the style manipulation affects more than style, or that with only 3 samples and 11 participants, the results are unreliable. The paper should discuss this more transparently.

- **The unpaired performance dataset quality is uncharacterized.** The unpaired performance data comes from YouTube piano covers transcribed by an audio-to-MIDI model (Section 4.1). Transcription errors could propagate to the joint model, but no quality analysis (comparison with ground truth, manual inspection, or error characterization) is provided.

## Suggestions
1. **Add explicit alignment between content encoders.** Either introduce a direct loss (cosine similarity, L2) between z_x and z_y, or revise the paper's claims to accurately describe the architecture as multi-task learning with separate content encoders rather than a "shared content space."
2. **Run controlled ablations.** Isolate the effect of joint training from the effect of unpaired data by either (a) training the joint model on only paired ASAP data, or (b) augmenting EPR-Only with unpaired data via some mechanism.
3. **Strengthen disentanglement evaluation.** Pool z_c to a global vector before classifier input to control for the global-vs.-sequential confound. Add a quantitative intervention experiment (swapping z_s between pieces with objective metrics on what is preserved and what changes).
4. **Characterize unpaired data quality.** Report transcription error rates or show a manual inspection sample of the YouTube-derived performance MIDI.
