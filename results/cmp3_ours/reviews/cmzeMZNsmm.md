## Summary

This paper studies whether Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) such as DeepSeek-R1 and o1 still benefit from prompt optimization, using event extraction as a case study within a Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) framework. The authors compare two LRMs (DeepSeek-R1, o1) and two LLMs (GPT-4.5, GPT-4o) as both task models and prompt optimizers, finding that LRMs benefit more from optimization and produce higher-quality prompts. Generalization experiments on Geometric Shapes and NCBI Disease NER extend the findings beyond event extraction.

## Strengths

1. **Qualitative analysis of optimized prompts (Table 2).** The side-by-side comparison of prompts generated by different optimizers is the paper's strongest contribution. It shows that LRM optimizers (DeepSeek-R1, o1) produce prompts rich in concrete extraction rules and exception handling, while LLM optimizers focus on formatting instructions. This is a genuine empirical observation that survives the quantitative concerns.

2. **Generalization experiments beyond event extraction.** The inclusion of Geometric Shapes (symbolic reasoning) and NCBI Disease NER (biomedical NER) in Section 4.2 (RQ5, Table 3) shows the findings are not specific to event extraction, strengthening the external validity of the core observation that prompt optimization helps LRMs.

3. **Timely research question.** The paper asks whether LRMs, with their strong built-in reasoning capabilities, still benefit from prompt optimization — a question of genuine practical and scientific interest, grounded in recent discussions cited in Section 1.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **DeepSeek-R1 evaluated at 2.5-bit quantization while all other models run at full precision.** The paper states (Section 4.1): "Due to policy restrictions, we were not allowed to access DeepSeek-R1 through API calls and thus deployed it locally on our own server. Because of our compute limit, we quantize DeepSeek-R1 to 2.5 bits using the UnSloth framework." DeepSeek-R1 is one of only two LRMs and features prominently — it achieves the highest scores in many configurations (Table 1), drives the convergence analysis (Fig. 4), and anchors the survival analysis (Fig. 5a). The cited evidence for "minimal degradation" is the UnSloth framework's own documentation, not an independent peer-reviewed benchmark. This asymmetry creates a fundamental confound: we cannot determine whether DeepSeek-R1's larger gains from optimization reflect an LRM advantage or are artifacts of a degraded model with more room to improve. While o1 (full precision via API) shows broadly similar trends, the paper's strongest quantitative claims rely heavily on DeepSeek-R1.

2. **Numerical inconsistency in Table 1.** In the "MCTS at depth 1 trained on ACE_med" block, GPT-4o's No Opt baseline is reported as 26.30, but the improvement deltas are inconsistent with this base value: 22.32−4.98=17.34 (not 26.30), 27.54−14.86=12.68 (not 26.30), 25.10−12.42=12.68 (not 26.30). The value 12.68 consistently appears as GPT-4o's zero-shot score in every other block of the same table (ACE_low depth 1, ACE_med depth 5), strongly suggesting a data entry error. Since the paper draws aggregate conclusions (e.g., "LRMs gain more than LLMs" [Insight 1]), errors in individual cells undermine confidence in the reported data.

### Minor

3. **No variance estimates or multiple runs.** All results in Table 1 appear to come from single runs without reported standard deviations or confidence intervals. For an empirical study whose conclusions sometimes rest on small differences (e.g., +1% AC between optimizers), the absence of variance estimates makes it impossible to assess which comparisons are reliable. The MCTS process involves randomness, and single-run results could reflect unlucky search trajectories.

4. **No-optimization baselines lack contextualization.** The zero-shot AC F1 scores range from 12.68 to 16.47. The paper does not provide external reference points (prior published zero-shot EE results on ACE05 or a well-constructed alternative prompt) to help the reader assess whether these low scores reflect genuine task difficulty or a suboptimal initial prompt format. While this does not invalidate the relative comparisons (all models start from the same prompt), it leaves ambiguity about whether the gains represent meaningful optimization or recovery from a poor starting point.

5. **Risk of overfitting with very small training sets.** ACE_low contains only 15 training examples, and the MCTS process iteratively refines prompts based on errors sampled from this set. With such limited data, optimization could overfit to idiosyncrasies of specific samples. The paper does not analyze the gap between dev and test performance to rule out this concern.

6. **Conclusion omits the quantization limitation.** Given the paper's strong comparative claims, the absence of any mention of the DeepSeek-R1 quantization issue in the conclusion (Section 6) is a notable omission that should be addressed.
