## Summary

This paper proposes the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework, an evaluation framework for assessing whether concept-class annotations generated by LLMs/VLMs are "sufficient"—i.e., whether the generated textual concepts alone enable accurate class identification. The framework comprises (1) a five-stage progressive concept-gathering pipeline (Background → Superclass → Salient Features → Detailed Features → Auxiliary Features) that transitions from "fast mode" (visual-only inference) to "slow mode" (concept-only multi-step inference), and (2) the Class Representation Index (CRI) metric. Experiments across 6 models and 5 datasets show that slow mode underperforms fast mode by ~25% on fine-grained datasets, revealing that current automated annotations struggle to capture fine-grained discriminative semantics.

## Strengths

1. **The motivating problem is real and well-illustrated.** Figure 1's dialogue showing a model correctly identifying a bird visually but failing when forced to reason from its own textual concepts effectively crystallizes why annotation sufficiency matters, grounding the work in a concrete failure mode.

2. **Five-stage refinement is well-grounded in prior work.** The progressive concept-gathering pipeline extends prior hierarchical extraction practices (Oikarinen et al.'s three-tier, Sun et al.'s two-level) in a sensible way for testing annotation sufficiency at increasing granularity.

3. **Thorough model coverage.** Six models across three families (GPT-4o, QwenVL2, Llama-3.2-vision) at two sizes each, plus preliminary results on DeepSeek-R1, provide good generalization evidence that the observed patterns are not model-specific.

4. **The distractor strategy preliminary experiment (Section 5.3) is well-executed.** The finding that semantically related distractors produce substantially higher contradiction rates (34–45%) than random ones (14–20%) provides a principled basis for constructing challenging candidate sets.

5. **The fine-grained vs. general dataset comparison (Table 3) is genuinely informative.** The result that slow mode achieves >90% CRI on CIFAR-100 and Caltech-101 but struggles on fine-grained datasets is the most compelling empirical finding. It shows the issue is about specificity required in fine-grained domains rather than a general failure of the framework, and it partially addresses the concern that the fast-slow gap reflects only limited text-reasoning ability (since reasoning works well on general datasets).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Central claim about annotation insufficiency is confounded with reasoning ability.** The CRI measures whether a model can correctly classify when given *its own generated concepts* as input. The paper's headline claim—"current annotation methods fail to provide sufficient semantic coverage" (abstract)—attributes the fast-to-slow gap primarily to the annotations. However, the gap could equally reflect the model's limited ability to *reason from textual concepts*, even when those concepts are actually informative. Since the same model generates, evaluates, and provides the fast-mode baseline, the two explanations are not separated. The fine-grained vs. general comparison (Table 3) partially mitigates this (if reasoning were the sole bottleneck, slow mode would be poor everywhere), but it does not resolve it fully: the model's text-reasoning capability may simply degrade as concepts become more precise. A control experiment with human-provided concepts would cleanly resolve this ambiguity. As written, the paper's central conclusion is stronger than the evidence uniquely supports.

2. **No calibration of what CRI threshold constitutes "sufficiency."** Definition 3.1 sets an intuitively reasonable standard (concepts alone enable accurate inference), but the paper never establishes what CRI score qualifies as "sufficient" nor what CRI human-generated concepts would achieve. If human annotators also produced concepts yielding only ~60% CRI on fine-grained datasets, then finding LLM concepts at ~60% CRI would suggest *good* annotation quality, not insufficiency. Without this reference point, the paper's critique of current annotations as "insufficient" is uncalibrated. Stating that the framework operates "without human supervision" explains why such a baseline is absent but does not eliminate the need for external grounding when making absolute insufficiency claims.

### Minor

3. **CRI formula (Equation 2) contains a clear typographical error.** The formula writes CRI = (1/t) Σ_{i=1}^t 𝟙[y_i^t = y_i], using the annotation step t as both the step index and the upper bound of the summation over test cases. This would produce division by zero at t=0 (fast mode, for which CRI values are reported) and would sum over only t test cases rather than all l cases. Since the reported results are clearly computed correctly, this is a presentation error, but it is concerning for a paper whose core contribution is a metric—a reader cannot verify the exact quantity computed from the formula as written.

4. **The utility-as-proxy experiment (Table 4) has limited force.** The "fused mode" gives the model both the image and the text concepts simultaneously. The finding that fused ≈ fast mode simply shows the model relies on its higher-bandwidth visual modality when both are available—an expected outcome. This does not demonstrate that the concepts are insufficient; it demonstrates that the fusion protocol does not force reliance on the concepts. A genuine test of the utility-as-proxy assumption would require a bottleneck architecture where predictions must pass through the concept representation.

5. **Distractor selection confounds fine-grained vs. general comparison.** The semantically related distractors are selected using a ResNet-18's top-4 predictions. For fine-grained datasets (CUB-200, Cars-196), ResNet-18 confusions are among near-identical classes, yielding true near-distractors. For general datasets (CIFAR-100, Caltech-101), confusion patterns span coarser categories. The finding that slow mode works better on general datasets could partially reflect that the distractors are easier to distinguish via text at coarse granularity. The paper does not report or control for the semantic distance between correct class and distractors in either setting.

6. **Missing key experimental details for reproducibility.** The paper does not state the number of test cases (l) used for CRI computation in the main experiments, nor report standard deviations in the tabular CRI results (Tables 2–4). (Figure 3 mentions three runs with error bars, but visual trends alone are insufficient for independent verification of the tabular values.)

## Novel Insights
The reviews surface a central confound that the paper does not adequately address: the degree to which the fast-to-slow CRI gap reflects annotation insufficiency versus the model's limited ability to reason from textual concepts. The fine-grained vs. general comparison (Table 3) partially speaks to this, but a human-concept control experiment would be needed to fully resolve it. Beyond this, no genuinely novel insight emerges beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions
1. Run a human-concept control experiment. Feed human-written concepts to the same LLMs for classification. If human concepts yield substantially higher CRI, the paper's claim about annotation insufficiency is strongly supported. If CRI remains low, the bottleneck is in the models' text-reasoning capability, and the paper should be reframed accordingly.
2. Fix the CRI formula typo in Equation 2 (change 1/t Σ_{i=1}^t to 1/l Σ_{i=1}^l).
3. Either add a human-calibration baseline on a subset, or explicitly frame the findings as comparative (slow vs. fast, fine-grained vs. general) rather than making absolute insufficiency claims.
4. Report the number of test cases used and include standard deviations in tabular results.
5. Control for distractor difficulty across fine-grained vs. general settings by measuring and reporting the semantic distance between class and distractors.
