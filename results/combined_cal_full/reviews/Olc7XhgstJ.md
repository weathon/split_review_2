## Summary

This paper addresses the "under-thinking" phenomenon in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs)—excessive switching between reasoning thoughts that abandons promising lines of reasoning before they reach completion. The authors propose **Steady Thought (ST)**, a three-stage framework: (1) entropy-based thought segmentation, (2) generating "committed" completions via logit suppression of switch-trigger tokens, and (3) thought-level preference optimization (STPO) that trains the model to prefer deeper commitment over switching. Experiments across three model sizes (1.5B, 8B, 14B) and four benchmarks show consistent accuracy improvements (up to 5.3%) with substantial token reductions (up to 39.3%), including generalization to an out-of-distribution coding dataset.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem with concrete evidence (Figure 1).** The under-thinking phenomenon is clearly articulated and empirically grounded: Figures 1a and 1b show that the first correct thought typically appears early, yet the model continues generating many additional thoughts. This observation is useful and could be of independent interest.

- **Clean pipeline with sensible design decisions.** The three-stage framework (segmentation → completion → preference optimization) is logically structured. The choice of SimPO over DPO is well-justified (Section 4.4.4): the rejected trajectory (switching) is typically much longer than the chosen one, so length normalization is essential. The ablation in Table 4 confirms DPO underperforms STPO in this setting, which is a valuable empirical finding.

- **Consistent accuracy-efficiency improvements across multiple model sizes and benchmarks.** In Table 1, ST improves or maintains accuracy on every model-dataset combination where the baseline is reasonable while substantially reducing tokens. Compared to SEAL—the strongest test-time baseline—ST achieves better accuracy *and* fewer tokens on Qwen3-8B (83.35% vs 82.58%, 4558 vs 6940 tokens) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B (57.33% vs 54.30%, 5016 vs 5295 tokens).

- **OOD generalization is demonstrated.** ST is trained only on math data (omni-math) but shows accuracy gains on LiveCode (coding), with Qwen3-8B going from 71.8% to 77.1% while reducing tokens by 19.0%. This mitigates the concern that ST simply learns shorter response patterns specific to math.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Central claim about selective commitment is not supported, and a key statement is contradicted by the paper's own data.** The paper claims ST teaches models to "selectively commit to promising thoughts while preserving exploration" and states that "the final thought consistently accounted for a larger proportion of the total response" (line 219). However, the data in Figure 2 shows a clear counterexample: for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B on AIME2024, the proportion of last thought *decreased* from 18.96% to 15.66%, while the number of thoughts *increased* from 12.87 to 18.21. The paper acknowledges the increase in thoughts but not the decrease in last-thought proportion, which directly contradicts the "deeper commitment" narrative for this setting. Moreover, Table 2's reduction in correct intermediate thoughts is equally consistent with the model simply suppressing switching globally. The paper does not provide evidence that the model has learned *selective* commitment rather than uniform switch suppression.

### Minor

- **Comparison against test-time baselines is structurally asymmetric.** ST is a training-based method (fine-tunes model parameters on task-specific data), while the baselines (NoThink, NOWAIT, SEAL) are inference-time interventions with no training. The paper does not acknowledge this asymmetry. The absence of a training-based baseline in the main results table (beyond the SFT row in Table 4, which is an ablation) makes it difficult to isolate the contribution of preference optimization from the general benefits of training on the task.

- **Statistical significance is not reported for key results.** AIME 2024 has only 30 problems. For the 1.5B model, ST goes from 27.5% (~8.25/30) to 31.2% (~9.36/30)—roughly 1 more correct answer. The paper reports averaging "eight test runs" but provides no variance, standard deviation, or confidence intervals anywhere. Without error bars, the reader cannot assess the reliability of the reported improvements, especially on small-N benchmarks.

- **Data construction pipeline has underspecified reproducibility details.** The paper does not report the number of training problems sampled, number of preference pairs generated, completion success rate, or which model generated training data for each experimental condition (e.g., whether the 14B model's training data was generated by the 14B model itself or a smaller model).

- **"Chosen" trajectories generated under artificial constraints.** The completions used as preferred responses are generated with switch-trigger tokens suppressed via logit manipulation—a constraint that does not exist at inference time. While the correctness filter partially addresses quality concerns, the paper does not discuss potential artifacts from this suppression mechanism being learned by the model.
