## Summary

This paper addresses a training-inference mismatch in tree-based speculative decoding (SpD). It proposes two techniques: TALF, a tree-aware loss function that aggregates cross-entropy loss over dynamic tree nodes during draft model training, and SALF, a stopping criterion that halts tree construction when the expected probability gain from further expansion falls below a threshold. Experiments on 3 target LLMs, 5 benchmarks, and 2 temperatures show consistent speedups over EAGLE-2 and HASS.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated, precisely identified training-inference mismatch.** The diagnostic in Section 3.1 (Figure 2) concretely shows that prior methods (EAGLE, HASS) improve accuracy and calibration on 1st-ranked tokens but show marginal or negative gains on lower-ranked tokens—yet lower-ranked tokens constitute ~12% of the draft tree. This is a crisp empirical demonstration of a gap that prior work missed.

- **TALF is a natural and principled solution.** Given the diagnosed problem, aggregating cross-entropy loss over dynamically generated tree nodes (Algorithm 1) is the right approach. The training procedure is simple, uses tree attention for efficiency, and cleanly extends HASS's feature-level alignment to the tree structure.

- **The ablation design (Table 2) cleanly isolates the two contributions.** The 3×3 grid (loss × tree construction method) produces 9 data points for DeepSeek, letting the reader see the individual and joint contributions of TALF and SALF separately. This is stronger evidence than most SpD papers provide.

- **Consistently positive end-to-end results.** Across 3 target LLMs, 5 benchmarks, and 2 sampling temperatures (Table 1), SALF & TALF consistently outperform both EAGLE-2 and HASS with non-trivial margins (15.6–39.4% over EAGLE-2 and 6.5–24.4% over HASS).

- **SALF provides a practical stopping criterion with a provable monotonicity guarantee (Theorem 1).** The speedup from SALF over optimal tree search (Table 2: ~14–19% wall-clock improvement) confirms the practical value of trading tree optimality for lower drafting cost.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Training epoch confound in the primary comparison (Table 1).** For Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B, the EAGLE-2 baseline draft model is trained for 10 epochs while HASS and TALF models receive 10+3=13 epochs (line 196: "we first trained the draft model for ten epochs using the original EAGLE loss... Then, we performed additional training... for three epochs"). This means the headline "15.6–39.4% improvement over EAGLE-2" partially conflates additional training with a better loss function. The comparison between HASS and TALF is fair (both get 10+3), and the DeepSeek experiment controls for total training time, but the abstract/conclusion present the unqualified 15.6–39.4% range as if it were purely attributable to the method. This does not invalidate the paper (the controlled DeepSeek experiment and Table 2 support the core claims), but the framing is misleading.

- **No variance or significance reporting.** All speedup numbers across all tables are reported as point estimates with no confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. LLM inference is stochastic (especially at temperature=1) and wall-clock times are sensitive to hardware state. Without any measure of variance, the reader cannot assess whether the observed differences are robust or could arise from a single run. The paper needs at minimum a statement that results are averaged over N runs with standard deviations.

### Minor

- **SALF threshold (*th*) selection is under-supported.** Table 4 shows sensitivity data for DeepSeek only (where *th*=0.5 gives 2.62× vs. *th*=0.6 giving 2.59×), but the paper chooses *th*=0.6 as default claiming "more consistent performance improvements for the tested target LLMs" (plural, line 264). The supporting data for Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B is not shown, making this claim unverifiable from the presented evidence.

- **TALF drops the regression loss without ablation.** All prior methods (EAGLE, HASS) use a regression loss for feature alignment, but TALF discards it (line 114: "training solely on the token probability distributions across multiple nodes was sufficient"). No ablation is shown with regression loss added back to TALF, so it is unclear whether gains come from tree-awareness, from removing a potentially harmful loss term, or both.

- **Residual training-inference mismatch in TALF.** The tree structure used during TALF training is generated by the target model and fixed across epochs (lines 110–112), reflecting the target's distribution rather than the draft model's evolving distribution. Since the draft model generates its own trees at inference (using its own probabilities), a second-order mismatch remains that the paper does not discuss. This does not invalidate the method (the results show it works), but the narrative about "aligning" training and inference is slightly overstated.
