## Summary

This paper proposes RADAR, a neural framework that augments existing constructive neural VRP solvers with two components to handle asymmetric distance matrices: (1) an SVD-based node embedding initialization that captures static directional structure from the asymmetric distance matrix, and (2) Sinkhorn normalization replacing softmax in attention to provide bidirectional neighborhood context during encoding. The paper evaluates RADAR on synthetic ATSP/ACVRP (training on size 100, testing up to 1000), 16 multitask VRP variants, and 3 real-world benchmarks, consistently outperforming learning-based baselines with strong generalization.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem, clearly scoped.** The paper correctly identifies that real-world routing involves asymmetric costs (one-way streets, traffic directionality) and that standard coordinate-based neural VRP pipelines cannot handle cases where only asymmetric pairwise distance matrices are available. (Section 1)

- **Clean conceptual decomposition into static and dynamic asymmetry.** The framing directly motivates the two architectural components: SVD initialization captures static directional discrepancies in the input matrix, while Sinkhorn normalization helps attention capture bidirectional neighborhood context. (Sections 4.1, 4.2)

- **Strong empirical results across a wide range of settings with impressive generalization.** RADAR outperforms all learning-based baselines on ATSP and ACVRP (Table 1), on 16 multi-task variants (Table 2), and on real-world benchmarks (Table 3). The zero-shot generalization from training size 100 to test sizes 200, 500, 1000 is particularly compelling — e.g., on ATSP, RADAR's gap grows from 0.72% (size 100) to only 2.13% (size 500), while the best competitor (ReLD) goes from 1.64% to 13.39%.

- **Principled ablation.** Table 6 cleanly isolates the contribution of each component across four instance sizes. Both SVD initialization and Sinkhorn normalization contribute monotonic and consistent gains. The paper also ablates the number of singular values k and compares against alternative decomposition methods (EVD, MDS, QR). (Section 6)

- **Theoretical grounding for the SVD embedding.** Definition 1 formalizes asymmetry-aware embeddings, and Eq. (5) shows constructively that the SVD-based embedding can reconstruct the distance matrix via linear projections matching the form of attention QK^T, providing a clear justification for the initialization design. (Section 4.1)

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None.

### Minor

- **The paper overstates what Definition 1 establishes.** The paper claims "the model is theoretically capturing static asymmetry through a single embedding matrix" (Section 4.1). Definition 1 and Eq. (5) show that there *exist* linear projections W₁, W₂ such that XW₁(XW₂)^T ≈ D. This guarantees that the embedding space has the **capacity** to encode asymmetric information, not that the learned model will realize this during training (since Q and K projections are learned from scratch and can diverge arbitrarily). The paper would benefit from wording that distinguishes representational capacity from learned behavior.

- **The multitask evaluation compares against adapted RouteFinder variants without clarifying the relationship to the original system.** The paper compares RADAR against two adapted variants (RF and RF-NN) that replace RouteFinder's encoder attention with MatNet attention and modify the initialization. While the adaptations are described, it is not stated whether the original RouteFinder (designed for symmetric Euclidean VRPs) can be directly applied to asymmetric inputs, or how RF/RF-NN's performance relates to RouteFinder's original reported numbers. This makes it somewhat harder to assess whether RADAR advances the state of the art or compares against weakened proxies.

- **The ACVRP results show more modest margins at larger sizes.** On ACVRP500, RADAR has a 3.39% gap while HGS-Short achieves -1.68% gap (outperforming all neural methods). On ACVRP500, ReLD achieves -0.05% (though it spikes to 47.40% at size 1000, suggesting instability). This context tempers the strongest claims of universal superiority in the ACVRP setting and warrants discussion.

- **The synthetic asymmetry injection (Section 5.5) uses a specific noise model that may not match real-world patterns.** Asymmetry is generated by multiplying Euclidean distances with noise from N(1, σ²). While informative as a controlled study, the paper does not discuss how well this synthetic pattern approximates real-world asymmetry structures.

### Trivial

- **No confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests are reported.** Given that gaps are small in some settings (e.g., 0.72% on ATSP100), it is unclear whether improvements over the second-best method are stable. Reporting variance over multiple seeds would strengthen the claims.
