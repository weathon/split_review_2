## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) — two naturally inverse tasks — by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. It introduces a Seq2Seq formulation of EPR that removes the need for note-level alignment, and a diffusion-based performance style recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The framework is trained on both paired and unpaired data. Experiments on the ASAP and ATEPP datasets show competitive APT results, strong disentanglement evidence, and convincing subjective EPR ratings.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated joint modeling of inverse tasks.** The core idea — unifying EPR and APT via disentangled content and style representations — is genuine and underexplored in prior work. The formalization in §§3.2–3.3 cleanly articulates the one-to-many (EPR) vs. many-to-one (APT) mapping distinction, and the ASR/TTS analogy provides effective motivation.

- **Seq2Seq EPR without note-level alignment is a practical advance.** Most existing EPR systems (VirtuosoNet, DExter) require alignment tools that struggle with ornaments like trills and mordents. The proposed formulation removes this requirement while achieving the best alignment rate among EPR methods (92.27% for Ours-PSR, Table 3).

- **The PSR module is a genuinely novel architectural contribution.** The diffusion-based module generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automatic style selection without requiring composer/performer labels or manual parameter control. The era-consistent clustering in Figure 4 provides qualitative support for its effectiveness.

- **Strong disentanglement evidence.** Table 4 convincingly shows that style and content embeddings capture different information: classifiers using style embeddings achieve 42.07% performer accuracy vs. 9.94% for content, and 77.46% composer accuracy vs. 29.99% for content. The 2D visualizations in Figure 3 further support this.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Style transfer evaluation is too thin to support the claim of "reliable style transfer" (Abstract, §5.2).** The subjective test (Figure 5) uses only 3 samples. Sample 2 shows a contradictory result: the Target condition scores *lower* than the Mean condition on both style similarity (2.4 vs. 3.0) and quality (2.4 vs. 3.1), which the paper does not acknowledge. With 3 samples, no cross-validated statistics, and one of three samples contradicting the narrative, "reliable" style transfer is not demonstrated. This directly undercuts one of the three claimed contributions.

### Minor

- **The EPR ablation confounds two factors.** Ours-Target (joint model trained on ASAP + unpaired data) is compared against EPR-Only (EPR components trained on ASAP only). The paper attributes improvement to "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data" (§5.1), but these factors are not isolated. An ablation training the joint model on *only* ASAP paired data (without unpaired data) is needed to separate the contribution of joint supervision from that of additional data.

- **Overclaim on velocity variance in Table 2.** The paper claims "our models exhibit duration and velocity variances that more closely match those of human performances compared with other baselines." This is accurate for duration (Ours-Target 0.58 vs. Human 1.72 vs. DExter 4.15) but not for velocity, where DExter (238.86) is substantially closer to Human (241.04) than Ours-Target (151.03). The subjective results clearly favor Ours, so this is a minor inaccuracy that should be corrected rather than a fatal flaw.

- **Underspecified unpaired performance data pipeline (§4.1).** The YouTube transcription pipeline uses "a state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" selected via "a pilot study demonstrating strong accuracy," but no model name, accuracy numbers, or error analysis is provided. Since the style encoder is trained partly on these transcribed performances, unknown transcription errors could propagate into learned representations. Acknowledging this limitation would substantially improve credibility.

### Trivial

- **No limitations section.** The paper lacks a discussion of its own limitations. The conclusion (§6) does not address the unpaired data quality concern, the pitch accuracy trade-off in APT (E_p = 3.08 is the worst in Table 1), the small subjective sample sizes, or the restriction to classical piano. Adding a limitations section would improve the paper's balance and self-awareness.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An objective metric for the PSR evaluation (e.g., Wasserstein distance between real and generated style embedding distributions per era, or era-classifier accuracy on generated vs. real embeddings) would strengthen the claim beyond the qualitative 2D projections in Figure 4.
- Reporting inter-annotator agreement for the subjective listening tests would help establish rating reliability.
