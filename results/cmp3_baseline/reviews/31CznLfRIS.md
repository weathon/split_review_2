## Summary

The paper introduces **VideoJudge**, a bootstrapping framework that automatically generates training data for MLLM-based evaluators specialized for video understanding. By iteratively producing candidate responses across a 1–5 rating scale with a generator–evaluator pipeline and retaining only aligned examples, the method creates over 100K training examples without human annotation. Fine-tuned VideoJudge models (3B and 7B) match or outperform much larger baselines like Qwen2.5-VL-72B on multiple pointwise and pairwise meta-evaluation benchmarks, and also learn to generate instance-specific evaluation rubrics that improve interpretability and performance.

## Strengths

- **Novel bootstrapping methodology for a timely problem**: The paper addresses the lack of scalable, principled evaluation for video understanding by proposing a generator–evaluator loop that synthesizes high-quality training data autonomous of human annotation. This fills an important gap in the literature and the approach is general enough to extend to other modalities.
- **Comprehensive and rigorous evaluation**: The paper evaluates both pointwise and pairwise judge performance across multiple benchmarks (constructed and human-annotated), with detailed analyses of temperature robustness, frame count sensitivity, and rubric quality from both human and LLM judges. The inclusion of independent human-annotated benchmarks (VateX-Eval, LongVideoBench, VideoAutoArena) mitigates concerns about closed-loop effects.
- **Instance-specific rubric generation is a clear contribution**: Training a compact 3B model to generate evaluation rubrics at inference time not only improves performance (comparable to 72B models) but also provides interpretability. The human and LLM-as-a-Judge evaluations confirm that these rubrics are indeed preferred, showing the method produces meaningful, context-grounded criteria.
- **Open-source release of data, models, and benchmarks**: The paper commits to releasing trained models, bootstrapped datasets, and meta-evaluation benchmarks, which will support reproducible research and accelerate progress in video understanding evaluation.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Closed-loop bias in constructed benchmarks and training data**: Both the bootstrapped training data and two of the meta-evaluation benchmarks (VideoJudgeLLaVA, VideoJudgeVCG) are generated by the same generator–evaluator pipeline. While the paper acknowledges this and evaluates on independent human-annotated benchmarks, the constructed benchmarks may encode the pipeline’s own biases, and the strongest performance gains are often observed on those benchmarks. This makes it difficult to fully disentangle whether VideoJudge learns generalizable human-aligned judgment or merely fits the pipeline’s preferences.
- **Claim of outperforming much larger models is not uniformly supported**: The paper claims VideoJudge-7B outperforms larger models “across three out of four meta-evaluation benchmarks.” However, examining Table 1 shows that on VideoJudgeVCG, Qwen2.5-VL-72B achieves higher Spearman (0.77 vs. 0.74) and Pearson (0.77 vs. 0.76); on LongVidB, Qwen2.5-VL-32B obtains higher PSUP (0.73 vs. 0.66). The advantage is clearer on VideoJudgeLLaVA and on some metrics (e.g., MAE, Δ(C−D)), but the headline claim should be more conditional.

### Minor

- **Rubric quality human evaluation is limited**: The human evaluation for rubric quality (300 pairs per model) shows extremely high win rates for VideoJudgeR-3B against base models (96–98%), which is expected given the trained model is specifically optimized for rubric generation. The comparison against GPT-4o-mini (53.4% win) is more telling but still close; the paper’s conclusion that rubrics are “substantially higher-quality” is overstated given this narrow margin.
- **Error analysis reveals systematic overestimation bias**: The analysis shows a strong overestimation bias (14.8% over-score vs. 1.5% under-score by ≥2 points) and poor calibration for ratings 3 and 4. While acknowledged, this weakness is only briefly discussed and not addressed methodologically. Future work directions are mentioned but the impact on the current model’s trustworthiness is understated.

### Trivial

- The paper sometimes uses “MLLM” and “MLLM-as-a-Judge” interchangeably; consistency would improve readability.

## Nice-to-Haves

- An ablation study varying the generator model strength would strengthen the claim that the bootstrapping pipeline is robust to generator quality.
- A comparison with other judge training methods (e.g., distillation from GPT-4, self-consistency) in the video domain would better contextualize the advantages of bootstrapping.
- Analysis of how many refinement iterations (T) are typically needed per candidate would help understand the computational overhead.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper’s own contributions.

The key novel insight is that bootstrapped supervision from a generator–evaluator pipeline, paired with fine-tuning on Qwen2.5-VL, enables small multimodal models to act as reliable video judges, often matching models over 10× larger. The finding that LLM judges (Qwen3) underperform MLLM judges and that long chain-of-thought reasoning does not improve evaluation reinforces the importance of direct video inputs. Additionally, the demonstration that rubric-guided training makes judge models robust to decoding temperature is a practical insight for deployment.

## Suggestions

- Tone down the claim about “outperforming larger models across three of four benchmarks” to reflect the mixed results on VideoJudgeVCG and LongVidB. For example, state that VideoJudge achieves competitive or superior performance on several benchmarks and metrics.
- Expand the error analysis section to discuss how overestimation bias could be mitigated in future iterations (e.g., adding hard negatives, reweighting high-rating examples). Consider including a calibration plot or confidence analysis.
- Add a brief discussion on the computational cost of the bootstrapping pipeline (including generator inference and refinement) relative to the cost of human annotation, to better justify the scalability claim.
