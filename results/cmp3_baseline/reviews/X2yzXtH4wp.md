## Summary

This paper introduces Ambig-SWE, an underspecified variant of SWE-Bench Verified designed to evaluate how LLM-based agents handle incomplete instructions in software engineering tasks. The authors systematically evaluate proprietary and open-weight models across three key capabilities: detecting underspecificity, asking targeted clarification questions, and leveraging interaction to improve performance. Their experiments reveal that while interaction can recover up to 74% of performance lost to underspecificity, most models default to non-interactive behavior, struggle to distinguish well-specified from underspecified inputs, and vary significantly in question quality and integration strategies.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated and practically important problem**: The paper addresses a critical gap in current LLM agent evaluation—most benchmarks assume perfectly specified instructions, while real-world usage involves underspecified prompts that can lead to safety risks, wasted computation, and misaligned solutions. This is a timely and impactful research direction.

- **Principled decomposition of the interaction capability**: Breaking down underspecification resolution into three distinct capacities (detection, clarification, integration) is a valuable analytical contribution. This decomposition enables targeted improvements and provides a diagnostic framework that goes beyond simple pass/fail metrics.

- **Rigorous experimental design with multiple settings**: The three-setting design (Full, Hidden, Interaction) allows clean causal attribution of interaction effects. The use of a simulated user proxy with conservative response behavior (refusing to hallucinate missing information) is a thoughtful methodological choice that isolates the agent's capabilities.

- **Rich qualitative analysis of interaction strategies**: The paper goes beyond aggregate metrics to analyze different question-asking strategies (exploration-first vs. immediate questioning, navigational vs. informational queries), providing actionable insights for model and agent design.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Synthetic underspecification may not reflect real-world ambiguity**: The underspecified variants are generated by GPT-4o removing details from fully specified issues. While the authors acknowledge this limitation and provide distributional analysis, the synthetic nature raises questions about ecological validity. Real-world underspecification often involves implicit assumptions, domain-specific jargon, and conversational context that may not be captured by simple information removal. The paper would benefit from at least a small-scale validation on naturally occurring underspecified issues.

- **Single user proxy model (GPT-4o) limits generalizability**: All experiments use GPT-4o as the simulated user. Different user proxy behaviors (e.g., more verbose, more impatient, less knowledgeable) could significantly affect agent performance and interaction patterns. The paper's claims about "interaction effectiveness" are therefore contingent on this specific proxy design.

- **Limited analysis of why interaction fails to close the gap**: The paper shows that Interaction setting performance is significantly below Full setting for all models, but the analysis of *why* interaction fails is relatively shallow. Is it because agents ask the wrong questions? Because they fail to integrate answers? Because they run out of turns? A more detailed error analysis would strengthen the contribution.

- **Uneven evaluation of Claude Sonnet 4 in Hidden setting**: The paper notes that Claude Sonnet 4 was evaluated on only 100/500 instances in the Hidden setting due to cost, yet this model's performance is compared directly with others. While statistical significance is claimed, the reduced sample size and different evaluation conditions weaken the comparison.

### Minor

- **The "question quality" analysis conflates information gain with task relevance**: Cosine distance measures semantic change in the agent's knowledge state, but not all information gain is equally useful for task completion. The paper acknowledges this but does not provide a more targeted analysis of whether the acquired information is actually *necessary* for solving the specific issue.

- **Interaction turn limits differ across models**: Claude Sonnet 4 and Qwen 3 Coder receive up to 100 turns while others get 30. This confounds the comparison of interaction efficiency and could advantage models with more turns even if their per-turn effectiveness is lower.

- **The detection experiment (RQ2) measures interaction initiation, not genuine detection**: The paper operationalizes "detection of underspecificity" as whether the model chooses to interact. However, a model might detect missing information but still choose not to interact (e.g., due to training biases or prompt phrasing). This conflates detection ability with interaction propensity.

### Trivial

- Table 2 would benefit from confidence intervals or error bars to assess the reliability of the reported accuracies, especially for models with extreme FPR/FNR values.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A small-scale human evaluation of the synthetic underspecified issues to validate that they are indeed perceived as underspecified by human developers.
- Analysis of how different user proxy models (e.g., more helpful vs. less helpful) affect the relative ranking of agent models.
- A breakdown of failure modes in the Interaction setting (e.g., what fraction of failures are due to wrong questions vs. poor integration vs. insufficient turns).

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, a genuinely novel observation is the discovery of a "rigid protocol" behavior in Qwen 3 Coder, where the model acknowledges user-provided navigational information but then redundantly re-explores the codebase to discover the same information independently. This suggests that current training paradigms may inadvertently reward self-contained problem-solving over adaptive integration of external input, even when the external input is explicitly provided. This insight has implications for how we train agents to be genuinely interactive rather than merely tolerating interaction.

## Suggestions

- Conduct a targeted analysis of interaction failures: for each model, categorize the proportion of failures attributable to (a) not asking, (b) asking wrong questions, (c) getting correct information but failing to integrate it, and (d) getting insufficient information. This would provide more actionable guidance than aggregate resolve rates.

- Validate the synthetic underspecification approach by testing a subset of models on naturally underspecified SWE-Bench issues (even without paired ground truth) to check whether the relative model rankings and interaction patterns hold.

- Consider adding a "forced interaction" baseline where the agent is required to ask at least one question before proceeding, to disentangle the effect of interaction itself from the model's decision to interact.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a solid contribution to an important and understudied problem. The experimental design is thoughtful, the decomposition of interaction capabilities is valuable, and the findings (especially regarding model-specific interaction patterns and the limitations of prompt engineering) are informative for the community. However, the synthetic nature of the underspecification and the reliance on a single user proxy model limit the strength of the conclusions. The paper is a clear accept at a top venue but falls short of the transformative impact that would warrant a 10.
