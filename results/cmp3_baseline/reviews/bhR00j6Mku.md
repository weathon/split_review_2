## Summary
This paper presents the first systematic study of benchmark contamination in Large Reasoning Models (LRMs), examining two realistic contamination scenarios. The authors find that: (1) when contamination occurs during SFT before RL training (Stage I), subsequent GRPO/RL training effectively conceals contamination signals from existing detection methods, with theoretical analysis and ablation studies showing that PPO-style importance sampling and clipping are the root cause; (2) when contamination with Chain-of-Thought (CoT) data is applied as a final SFT step on advanced LRMs (Stage II), detection methods perform near random guesses because LRMs generalize to non-member samples, undermining the memorization-based assumptions of current detectors. The paper provides extensive empirical evidence across multiple base models, detection methods, and benchmarks, and offers theoretical justification for why RL training conceals contamination.

## Strengths
- **First systematic study of contamination in LRMs**: The paper identifies and addresses a crucial gap in the literature—how reasoning models' unique training pipeline (SFT+RL, long CoT) creates new vulnerabilities for benchmark contamination detection. This is timely and important given the prominence of LRMs in current leaderboards.
- **Rigorous experimental design**: The evaluation is comprehensive, covering 10 detection methods across 5 categories (generation-based, perturbation-based, reference-based, reference-free, and embedding-based), multiple base models (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), and 6 reasoning benchmarks. The contamination simulation is realistic, especially the distinction between two stages of contamination.
- **Theoretical analysis with empirical validation**: The paper derives a decomposition showing that PPO-style importance sampling and clipping drive the contraction of member/non-member NLL gap. The theory is directly supported by ablation studies (Table 3) comparing RAFT, RAFT++, and GRPO with and without clipping, which cleanly isolate the mechanism. This moves beyond mere observation to causal explanation.
- **Clear, actionable findings**: The paper demonstrates that the concealment effect scales with more RL steps (Figure 2), that it is not simply due to forgetting (performance inflation persists), and that removing clipping largely restores detection performance. These are strong, falsifiable claims with practical implications.

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Reference-based methods (LiRA) still show non-trivial performance in Stage I after RL**: While the paper correctly highlights the degradation, LiRA achieves ~80% AUROC after GRPO with clean data and ~75% with clean&mem data (Table 2). This is still well above random chance and may be practically useful for detection, especially with more samples. The paper's claim that detection methods are "fragile" and contamination can be "concealed" is somewhat overstated for LiRA, which is arguably the strongest detector in their set. The narrative would benefit from more nuanced discussion of which methods are most and least affected.

2. **Limited exploration of detection methods that might work**: The paper identifies that existing methods fail but proposes only high-level directions (release checkpoints, move beyond memorization) rather than testing even simple baselines that might resist the concealment. For example, methods that use multiple LRMs for comparison, or that examine per-token NLL patterns rather than average NLL, could be discussed or preliminarily evaluated. The paper's contribution is primarily demonstrating failure modes, which is valuable, but the lack of constructive evaluation is a missed opportunity.

3. **Potential confounding in Stage II experiments**: In Stage II, contamination is applied as a final SFT step using benchmark questions with CoT responses derived from advanced LRMs. However, the *same advanced LRMs are used for detection evaluation* (i.e., the DS-R1-Distill models). If the CoT responses used for contamination are generated by the same model family, the model may already have high confidence on member data before contamination. The paper shows that log-prob increases for both members and non-members after contamination (Figure 4), but it does not rigorously control for the possibility that the pre-contamination model already exhibits similar log-prob distributions due to similar training data. A cleaner experiment would use CoT data from a different source or demonstrate that the effect is not present when CoT responses are from a sufficiently different distribution.

### Minor
1. **The theoretical analysis in Section 3.2 relies on a tabular setting and several simplifying assumptions** (e.g., small step size, neglecting higher-order terms, idealized advantage computation for GRPO). While the analysis is reasonable and consistent with empirical results, the formal justification that RAFT++ and GRPO contract the gap while RAFT does not is based on sign arguments of covariance terms rather than rigorous bounds. The empirical ablation (Table 3) is the stronger evidence; the theory is suggestive but not tightly coupled to the empirical magnitude.

2. **The paper uses "SFT contamination" and "RL contamination" without always clearly distinguishing which training data includes benchmark questions vs. clean data.** Table 1 is somewhat dense and could benefit from clearer row labeling. Additionally, the term "extensive contamination with CoT" in Stage II is never precisely defined in terms of number of epochs or training steps beyond what is in the appendix.

3. **Figure 2 plots AUROC for a subset of methods on a subset of benchmarks** but the description in the caption claims "bar chart showing... trends across five benchmarks" while showing only one average value per method. The figure is still informative but the caption is slightly misleading.

### Trivial
- The paper's own proposed directions (release checkpoints, new detection assumptions) are reasonable but not novel in the sense that similar calls have been made for earlier LLM contamination detection challenges.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Testing whether ensemble-based detection (e.g., combining multiple individual detectors) provides any robustness against RL concealment.
- Evaluating detection methods that use the *entire generated response* (including CoT tokens) for NLL computation rather than just the final answer, since the paper notes that training CoT is unavailable to detectors.
- A quantitative analysis showing the correlation between the covariance terms predicted by theory (β_M, β_N) and actual detection degradation across different algorithms.

## Novel Insights
Beyond the paper's own contributions, a genuinely novel insight emerging from this work is that **RL training objectives, which are designed to optimize for reward, can inadvertently serve as a "contamination eraser" that decouples benchmark performance inflation from the statistical traces that detection methods exploit.** This means that the very algorithmic progress that makes LRMs powerful (PPO-style RL with clipping) simultaneously undermines the most widely used tools for verifying evaluation integrity. The paper's theoretical decomposition shows that clipping, typically viewed as a training stabilizer, is the main driver of this effect. This finding suggests a fundamental tension between RL optimization and transparent evaluation that extends beyond LRMs to any domain where RL is applied to models evaluated on fixed benchmarks.

## Suggestions
- Consider adding a small experiment in Stage II where contamination uses CoT from a *different* model or a synthetic process, to rule out the possibility that the pre-contamination model already has member/non-member indistinguishability due to similar training trajectories.
- For the Stage I results with LiRA (AUROC ~80%), include a discussion or a simple calculation showing what sample size or additional detection features would be needed to make this still practically effective, to give a more balanced view.
- To strengthen the theory in Section 3.2, consider adding a simulation in a simple tabular MDP that reproduces the observed concealment pattern with a tractable number of states/actions, which would make the theoretical argument more concrete.

## Score and Decision
This is a strong paper with clear contributions: it identifies a previously underexplored vulnerability in LRM evaluation, provides extensive empirical evidence, offers theoretical insight into the mechanism, and does so across multiple models and detection methods. The weaknesses—primarily that some methods (LiRA) retain moderate performance, the lack of constructive proposals beyond diagnosis, and potential confounds in Stage II—are not fatal and can be addressed in discussion or follow-up work. The paper is highly timely, methodologically sound, and likely to influence how the community thinks about LRM evaluation integrity.
