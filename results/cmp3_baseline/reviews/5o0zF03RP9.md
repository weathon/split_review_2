## Summary

The paper proposes IncentRL, a reinforcement learning framework that augments extrinsic rewards with a KL divergence between a predicted outcome distribution and a preferred outcome distribution. The central claimed novelty is treating the incentive weight \(\beta\) as a Bayesian random variable that is updated online to balance exploration and exploitation without manual tuning. The approach is motivated by cognitive principles including dopamine reward prediction error and the Free Energy Principle. Empirical results are reported on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey.

## Strengths

- **Problem relevance:** Addressing the need to tune trade-offs between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards is a practical concern in reinforcement learning.
- **Cognitively inspired framing:** Connecting preference-prediction alignment to dopamine RPE and the Free Energy Principle gives the work an interesting conceptual angle.
- **Simple and interpretable objective:** The KL-based shaping term is well motivated and easy to state.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **Central method (Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\)) is not specified.** The paper repeatedly claims that \(\beta\) is treated as a Bayesian random variable adapted online, yet it never describes the prior, likelihood function, update rule, or how \(\beta\) interacts with the RL training loop. Figures 3 and 4 show posterior quantities, but the underlying algorithm is a black box. This makes the core contribution irreproducible and invalidates the paper’s main claim.

### Major

1.  **Empirical validation of the Bayesian adaptation is insufficient and unconvincing.** The adaptation results are shown only on a single environment (MiniGrid) and compared only against \(\beta=0.0\), not against the fixed \(\beta=0.1\) that the sweep already identified as effective. The paper admits that “the Bayesian adaptation of \(\beta\) achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value” but provides no head-to-head comparison. The advantage of adaptation over a fixed small \(\beta\) is therefore not demonstrated.

2.  **No comparison to existing intrinsic motivation methods.** The paper does not compare IncentRL against curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., 2017; Burda et al., 2018), information gain (Houthooft et al., 2016), or other state-of-the-art exploration techniques. Without such comparisons, it is impossible to know whether IncentRL offers any practical advantage over established approaches.

3.  **Theoretical contributions are trivial.** Propositions 1 and 2 state that for very small \(\beta\) the optimal policy does not change and for very large \(\beta\) the KL term dominates. These are straightforward continuity arguments that provide no insight into the behavior of the Bayesian adaptation or the challenges of KL-based shaping in realistic settings.

4.  **Missing algorithmic details for general environments.** The paper does not explain how the predicted outcome distribution \(p(o|s,a)\) is learned or estimated in practice (except in the toy MDP where the true model is used). In MountainCar and MiniGrid, this is a crucial missing component that affects reproducibility and practical feasibility.

### Minor

- The number of random seeds is low (3 for MountainCar and MiniGrid), and many results have large variance; no statistical significance tests are reported.
- The connection to dopamine reward prediction error is claimed but remains at the level of analogy; the proposed KL term does not naturally reduce to a scalar RPE.
- The toy 2-state experiment is too simple to support the paper’s general claims about sample efficiency and exploration.

### Trivial

- The paper states “In additional runs (not shown)” without providing any details, which is unhelpful.
- Figures 3 and 4 use the term “rounds” without defining what a round is in terms of training steps or policy updates.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Provide a complete algorithmic description of the Bayesian \(\beta\) update, including prior, likelihood, and update schedule.
- Compare the adaptive \(\beta\) against a simple fixed \(\beta\) that is known to work (e.g., \(\beta=0.1\) in MiniGrid) and against a meta-gradient or annealing baseline.
- Compare against at least one standard intrinsic motivation method (e.g., ICM, RND) on the same benchmarks.
- Increase the number of seeds and include confidence intervals or statistical tests.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper’s own contributions, because the core mechanism (Bayesian adaptation) is not specified and the remaining components (KL-based shaping) are straightforward extensions of existing ideas.

## Suggestions

- Fully specify the Bayesian adaptation procedure, including the prior, the likelihood, and the update rule. Without this, the paper’s main contribution cannot be evaluated or built upon.
- Provide a direct comparison between the Bayesian adapted \(\beta\) and the best fixed \(\beta\) (e.g., \(\beta=0.1\)) in the same environment to support the claim that adaptation removes the need for manual tuning.
- Include at least one baseline from the intrinsic motivation literature (curiosity, RND, etc.) to contextualize the performance gains.
- Clarify how \(p(o|s,a)\) is obtained in each experiment (true model, learned forward model, or other) and discuss computational cost.

## Score and Decision

The core contribution of the paper—Bayesian adaptation of the incentive weight—is not specified, making the method irreproducible and the main claim unverifiable. The empirical evaluation is weak, lacks critical comparisons, and does not convincingly show that the adaptation adds value over a simple fixed \(\beta\). The theoretical analysis is trivial. Given these issues, the paper does not meet the bar for acceptance.
