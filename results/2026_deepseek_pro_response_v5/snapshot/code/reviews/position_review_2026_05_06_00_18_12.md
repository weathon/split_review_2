## Summary

This position paper argues that LLMs' over-cautious health responses — where models overstate risk or use alarming framing even while answering queries — constitute a genuine safety harm to vulnerable users, particularly those with OCD and health anxiety. The argument proceeds in two layers: first, that LLMs are systematically prone to over-cautiousness due to misaligned safety incentives (value pluralism, corporate liability); second, that this over-cautiousness causally harms OCD/anxiety patients by reinforcing maladaptive thought patterns (reassurance-seeking, threat overestimation, symptom checking). The paper introduces OCD-Eval, a 225-query evaluation set of OCD-patterned health queries validated by GPs, demonstrating that current models exhibit over-cautious rates of 20–55% on low-risk queries, and engages with four counterarguments.

## Strengths

- **Reframes over-caution from a helpfulness problem to a safety problem.** The paper's central conceptual contribution is distinguishing over-cautiousness from mere over-refusal and arguing it constitutes direct harm to vulnerable users — a category error in current alignment thinking, which typically treats over-cautiousness as a user experience annoyance rather than a safety concern (lines 12–19: prior work "fram[es] its drawbacks as reduced helpfulness... rather than as a safety concern in its own right"). This reframing is novel and consequential for the alignment community.

- **Empirical grounding through OCD-Eval with rigorous annotation.** The paper constructs a 225-query evaluation set specifically targeting OCD-triggered health anxiety, validated by 7 GPs with Gwet's AC2 of 0.93 and estimated annotation noise upper bound of 0.16 for OCR (lines 215–228). The dataset demonstrates that the phenomenon exists at scale: current models show OCR of 20–55% on low-risk queries. This transforms the argument from purely theoretical into one with concrete, measurable evidence.

- **Identifies specific, mechanism-linked harmful response patterns.** The paper goes beyond generic "models are too cautious" to identify concrete patterns like "symptom checking" (telling users to "keep an eye on" symptoms, creating a nocebo effect) and "reassurance undermining" (acknowledging low risk but then listing dangers that OCD users fixate on) in Table 1. These map directly to established OCD thought patterns (intolerance of uncertainty, threat overestimation), making the harm claim specific and falsifiable.

- **Clear differentiation from HealthBench.** The paper distinguishes its contribution by noting that HealthBench's over-caution category uses general health queries that become "awkward or implausibly anxious" when rewritten for OCD contexts (lines 192–194), and that HealthBench only covers emergency/non-emergency triage, not the broader anxiety landscape. Figure 1(b) illustrates this concretely.

- **Productive "over-alignment" framing via overfitting analogy.** The analogy to overfitting (line 21) provides a technically grounded conceptual anchor that invites ML practitioners to think about alignment as a calibration problem rather than a maximization problem — a frame that naturally invites productive disagreement.

- **Well-grounded empirical rebuttals to some counterarguments.** The response to "vulnerable users shouldn't use LLMs" effectively cites that only ~4% of health searchers know reassurance-seeking is counterproductive, and OCD diagnosis delays average 5–12+ years with only 17.5% service utilization in 12 months (lines 315–319), making self-regulation empirically unrealistic.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The "minimizes harm at scale" counterargument is the strongest objection and receives the weakest engagement.** Lines 343–358 present the argument that over-cautiousness at scale may prevent more harm than under-cautiousness would cause. The paper's response calls this "inhuman and unfair" (line 346) and cites 30–50% health anxiety prevalence among online health searchers. While the ethical critique and prevalence data are relevant, the paper misses the opportunity to use its own data constructively: Figure 2 shows models like Gemini 2.5 Flash achieving both low OCR (~17%) and high emergency recognition (~90%), while some high-OCR models (GPT-5.5 at 58%) also maintain high emergency recognition. Whether this correlation is genuinely weak or negative in the data is something the paper should have analyzed rather than leaving to the reader's visual inspection. Dismissing the most important counterargument primarily as "inhuman" weakens the paper's credibility with readers who hold precisely this distributional concern — these readers would be far more persuaded by a data-driven argument that over-cautiousness and under-cautiousness are not zero-sum.

- **The severity of claimed harms outpaces what the documented over-cautiousness supports.** The paper links over-cautiousness to extreme outcomes including "death from suicide and accidents" (lines 162–163). However, the empirical data shows that over-cautiousness is primarily minor: OCR is driven by one-level elevations (negligible → low), and SOCR (two+ level elevations) is consistently low across models. The paper acknowledges the chain is indirect ("LLMs likely will not directly suggest these behaviors; however, the reinforced anxiety might lead users to them as a form of secondary harm," lines 160–161), and the mechanism is plausible via established OCD literature. But the inferential bridge from "model says 'low risk' instead of 'negligible risk'" to "suicidal ideation" relies on extrapolating from pre-LLM cyberchondria research (symptom checkers, search engines) to conversational AI with fundamentally different interaction patterns, while the paper's own data shows the documented over-cautiousness is mostly at the mild end. The position would be stronger if the severity of claimed outcomes better matched the magnitude of the documented phenomenon.

### Minor

- **The subjective alarming tone (dimension 2) is under-developed relative to its importance in the argument.** The paper defines over-cautiousness with two traits: objective risk overstatement and subjective alarming tone (lines 18–19). The evaluation (OCR, SOCR) focuses almost entirely on dimension (1). The paper's most distinctive observations — about statistical vs. everyday "significance," reassurance undermining, cherry-picking studies, and how information is presented (lines 101–112) — concern dimension (2), yet this receives only descriptive tag frequencies in Table 1 without systematic analysis. For a position paper this is not fatal, but the strongest part of the position receives the least support.

- **Title scope exceeds evidence scope.** The title promises harm to "vulnerable users" broadly, but the evidence and argument focus almost exclusively on OCD and health anxiety. Either the framing should be narrowed to match the evidence, or the paper should discuss how the argument generalizes to other vulnerabilities (e.g., depressive rumination, eating disorder communities).

- **The over-alignment terminology may create confusion.** In ML, "over-alignment" could be interpreted as alignment too narrow to training preferences, which is related but distinct from the paper's meaning (over-weighting one objective at the expense of others). The overfitting analogy is intuitive but imprecise — overfitting concerns fitting noise in training data, while the paper describes over-weighting a safety objective.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A more systematic analysis of the subjective alarming tone dimension (dimension 2), e.g., correlating Table 1 tag frequencies with OCR across models, to provide a richer picture of the problem landscape.
- Direct evidence linking LLM response framing to anxiety outcomes in vulnerable users, even from a small user study, would dramatically strengthen the causal claim.
- Discussion of how personalization for OCD/anxiety users could be implemented without creating privacy risks or misidentification problems — the recommendations in Section 5 don't address this practical challenge.
- A practical recommendation for how LLM developers should navigate the tension between corporate liability and calibrated risk communication, beyond the Kantian ethical critique.

## Novel Insights

The paper's distinction between *objective risk overstatement* and *subjective alarming tone* as two independent dimensions of over-cautiousness is an underexplored conceptual separation with significant implications: most over-refusal/over-caution research focuses exclusively on whether the model answers (refusal vs. compliance), but this paper identifies a neglected middle ground — the model answers, but frames the answer in a way that is technically correct yet psychologically harmful. This framing problem (the "how" vs. the "what") may be the more consequential and harder-to-fix issue, because it cannot be addressed by simply making models less cautious; it requires models to understand the *pragmatics* of risk communication for different user cognitive profiles.

## Suggestions

- Analyze the relationship between OCR and emergency recognition in Figure 2 quantitatively — even a simple correlation analysis would transform the "minimizes harm at scale" counterargument response from purely ethical to data-driven, which would be far more persuasive.
- Narrow the title to match the evidence (e.g., "LLMs Are Too Cautious About Health, and It Is Hurting Users with OCD and Anxiety") or add a paragraph discussing generalization to other vulnerable populations.
- Develop the subjective alarming tone analysis in Table 1 beyond descriptive frequencies — e.g., examine whether some models achieve low OCR but high rates of reassurance undermining, which would reveal models that appear calibrated on risk ratings but are harmful in framing.
