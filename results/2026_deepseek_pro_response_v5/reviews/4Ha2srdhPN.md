## Summary

GRAID is a framework for generating spatial-reasoning VQA data using only 2D bounding box geometry from object detectors. Applied to BDD100k, NuImages, and Waymo, it generates 8.5M+ QA pairs across 22 templates spanning spatial relations, counting, ranking, and size comparisons. A human evaluation finds 91.16% of GRAID-generated pairs valid vs. 57.6% for a community SpatialVLM implementation. Fine-tuning experiments show cross-dataset generalization (+29.1pp on unseen NuImages), transfer from 6 trained question types to 10+ held-out types (+47.5pp), and gains on external benchmarks (BLINK, A-OKVQA).

## Strengths

- **Human evaluation validates the 2D-geometry approach:** 91.16% of GRAID-generated VQA pairs are valid vs. 57.6% for the community SpatialVLM implementation. The evaluation includes bounding-box overlays for verification, difficulty ratings, and random sampling with per-evaluator seeds, directly supporting the paper's core claim that 2D-geometry-based generation produces higher-quality data than 3D-reconstruction-based alternatives.

- **Cross-dataset generalization (RQ1) provides strong evidence of transferable spatial reasoning:** Fine-tuning Llama 3.2 11B on only 10% of GRAID-BDD and evaluating on the entirely unseen GRAID-NuImages dataset yields a +29.1pp improvement (38% → 67.1%). This meaningfully distinguishes the contribution from dataset-specific memorization.

- **Primitive-to-complex transfer (RQ2) demonstrates composable spatial concept learning:** Training on only 6 question types (LeftOf, RightOf, HowMany, AreMore, LargestAppearance, IsObjectCentered) from GRAID-BDD yields accuracy improvements across 10+ held-out types, including an entirely new cognitive category (Size & Aspect), with +47.5pp on BDD and +38.0pp on NuImages. This strongly supports the claim that the data teaches fundamental spatial reasoning primitives.

- **SPARQ acceleration is quantitatively characterized with concrete measurements:** In the RightOf template, predicates complete in 5.17ms vs. 46.95ms for full realization (9× speedup). On LargestAppearance, predicates achieve over 1400× speedup, and predicates imply realization success 78.8% of the time (Section 3.2). These measurements credibly support the scalability claim for generating 8.5M+ pairs.

- **Detector-agnostic and architecture-agnostic design:** GRAID supports Detectron2, MMDetection, and Ultralytics via a standard interface, requires no VLM architectural changes, and needs no region-based prompting — a practical differentiator from methods like SpatialRGPT that require both (Table 1).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The headline comparison (91.16% vs. 57.6%) is against a community reimplementation, not the original SpatialVLM authors' dataset.** The paper does disclose "community implementation" in Sections 2 and 4 (lines 82, 182, 202), but the abstract refers to "a dataset produced by a current training data generation pipeline" and "a dataset generated by recent work" without this qualifier. The introduction similarly uses "a dataset generated by a current method" (line 53). This framing risks overclaiming: the comparison evaluates the publicly available community implementation, not the method as originally published by Chen et al. (2024a). The comparison remains informative (it evaluates what practitioners can actually access), but the paper should consistently qualify throughout what is being compared, particularly in the abstract.

- **The "2D-only" framing is imprecise for the full framework.** Four of the 22 question templates (Closer, Farther, and their variants) require depth estimation — which is single-view 3D reconstruction. The paper separates these as "a demonstration of GRAID's extensibility" (line 157) and the core human evaluation (91.16%) uses only the no-depth variant. However, blanket statements that GRAID requires "no 3D reconstruction" (line 53) and "avoids single-view 3D reconstruction" (Table 1) are misleading for the full framework. The paper should clearly distinguish the core 2D-only contribution from the depth-dependent extension in all such claims.

### Minor

- **Algorithm 1 omits the "similar planes" check described in the text.** Section 3.2 (line 138) states that the RightOf realizer checks that objects "lie on similar planes" to avoid ambiguous left/right judgments (e.g., an object appearing left in image space but being on a different depth plane). Algorithm 1 (lines 110–134) implements only an x-coordinate comparison and an IoU check — the plane condition is entirely absent. This discrepancy matters because it directly affects the correctness guarantee for spatial-relation questions. Either the algorithm as published is incomplete, or the text overstates what is computed.

- **Dataset quality is evaluated under ground-truth labels, not detector outputs.** The paper explicitly uses ground-truth bounding boxes "to evaluate GRAID's effectiveness in isolation" (line 155). While this is a defensible scoping choice for isolating QA generation quality, the 91.16% validity figure assumes perfect detections — a condition no real user experiences. The practical reliability under detector noise (missed objects, false positives, misclassifications) remains uncharacterized.

- **No dedicated limitations section.** Important failure modes go unacknowledged: 2D left/right in image space does not equal 3D left/right in world space; pixel-area-based size comparisons conflate object distance with actual size; question diversity is bounded by the detector's class vocabulary; the domain-agnosticism claim is evaluated only on driving scenes (which have favorable properties: dominant ground plane, consistent camera perspective).

- **Section 3.1's discussion of interpretability methods (Saliency Maps, Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++, Score-CAM, SuperPixels — lines 94) is irrelevant** to GRAID's contribution. These are tools for analyzing why a detector made a prediction, not for generating spatial reasoning data. This paragraph should be tightened or removed.

### Trivial

- The abstract's "91.16% human-validated accuracy" conflates validity and accuracy. The actual breakdown from Section 4: 95.58% question validity, 93.69% answer validity, and ~91.16% unique-instance validity (28 out of 317 pairs had some issue). The number itself is correct, but "accuracy" is imprecise terminology — "validity" or "overall validity rate" would be more accurate.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Run the full pipeline with a real object detector (e.g., YOLOv8) on a held-out set to characterize how detector noise affects QA quality. A simulation (randomly dropping some fraction of ground-truth boxes and measuring QA correctness changes) would give readers a practical sense of robustness without requiring full detector integration.
- Report human evaluation results for the depth-including variant, since those questions rely on estimated 3D information and are most vulnerable to error.
- Clarify what fraction of generated questions might be affected by the 2D-vs-3D left/right ambiguity and whether the "similar planes" check exists or is planned for future release.
- The Waymo variant adds little (16.4k pairs from ~1,000 images) and could be dropped or substantially expanded; in its current form it dilutes the "8.5M+" number while contributing negligible training data.
