## Summary

This paper conducts a systematic benchmarking study evaluating nine mid-sized open-source LLMs (CodeLlama, Llama2, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen2.5/3, WizardCoder, Phi4, Gemma3) on two reverse engineering tasks: name recovery and type inference from decompiled and assembly code. Using 40,000 function samples drawn from GNU software compiled across four architectures and four optimization levels, the paper finds that all models achieve very low zero-shot F1 scores and that fine-tuning yields inconsistent, modest improvements. The paper's core contribution is a large-scale empirical characterization of where current LLMs stand on these specific binary analysis tasks.

## Strengths

- **Comprehensive multi-axis benchmarking**: Tables 2–4 report F1 scores for all nine models across O0–O3 optimization levels on x86-64 for function name recovery, variable name recovery, and type inference, covering both original and fine-tuned variants. The evaluation spans 40,000 unique functions and a 160,000-sample fine-tuning set, giving the negative result statistical weight rather than being anecdotal.

- **Controlled CodeWordNet ablation**: Figure 1 and its accompanying data (lines 331–348) quantify the impact of semantic vs. strict string matching on name recovery evaluation. The ~3× score drop without CodeWordNet directly supports the claim that LLMs often generate semantically reasonable but lexically inexact names, and that fair evaluation requires relaxed matching.

- **Model-size comparison demonstrating size-invariance**: Figure 2 compares CodeLlama variants at 7B, 13B, and 34B parameters and finds effectively flat performance across scales. This provides concrete evidence for the claim (Finding 6) that training data quality and domain specialization matter more than raw parameter count for these reverse engineering tasks.

- **Real-world firmware evaluation with two decompilers**: Table 6 reports F1 scores on real stripped firmware binaries decompiled with both Ghidra and IDA Pro, showing consistently low scores and the systematic IDA Pro advantage. This adds ecological validity beyond the controlled GNU dataset.

- **Qualitative failure analysis**: Table 8 presents side-by-side comparisons of original vs. fine-tuned Qwen2.5 predictions against ground truth (e.g., recovering `_stack_chk_fail` vs. generating `error hand ling`), providing diagnostic depth that helps readers understand why performance is low rather than merely that it is low.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None verifiable from the paper as written.

### Major

- **Unverifiable headline result in abstract and introduction**: The abstract and introduction (line 19) prominently claim that "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." No table in the main body shows DeepSeek-R1 achieving 0.37; the highest fine-tuned F1 for this model in the main-body tables is 0.17 (Table 3, variable name recovery, O0). While the paper notes that results for non-x86-64 architectures are in the appendix (lines 217–218), featuring an appendix-only result as the abstract's most prominent quantitative claim without any qualification about its source is a significant reporting failure for an empirical benchmarking paper. The reader cannot verify the paper's headline number from the evidence presented.

- **Type inference degradation after fine-tuning is under-analyzed**: Table 4 shows that after fine-tuning, the majority of models degrade on type inference — DeepSeek-V2 drops from 0.01 to 0.00 across all optimization levels, Gemma3 from 0.02 to 0.00, and similar patterns hold for CodeLlama, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen2.5, and Phi4 in multiple settings. The paper's sole analysis is a single sentence: "Fine-tuning has limited impact on type inference. Improving performance may require richer contextual information and more flexible evaluation metrics" (Finding 2). Whether this degradation reflects a genuine finding (models overfit or learn degenerate outputs) or an artifact of the training setup is unexplored. Either way, the paper's treatment is insufficient for a result this striking.

- **No non-LLM baselines to contextualize performance**: The paper cites DIRT, DEBIN, SYMLM, VARBERT, and other specialized reverse engineering tools but never benchmarks any of them. The conclusion that "current LLMs remain fundamentally limited in their ability to perform reverse engineering tasks" (§5) presupposes knowledge of what "limited" means relative to alternatives. Without at least one specialized-tool baseline, the reported F1 scores cannot be interpreted as evidence about LLM-specific capabilities versus inherent task difficulty. While the paper's stated scope is LLM benchmarking, the strength of its conclusions depends on this context.

### Minor

- **CodeWordNet metric is unvalidated for this domain**: Figure 1 shows that CodeWordNet-based matching produces F1 scores approximately 3× higher than strict string matching. While the SYMLM method is adopted from prior work (Jin et al., 2022), the paper provides no validation of whether CodeWordNet subword-level cosine similarity actually captures naming equivalence in the C/compiler domain. Qualitative examples of what partial credit looks like, or a small-scale validation against human judgments, would strengthen confidence in the name recovery results.

- **Fine-tuning setup is ambiguously described**: The paper states models are fine-tuned for "200 steps" (line 394, Table 7) on 160,000 functions. It is unclear whether these are 200 gradient update steps (and at what batch size) or 200 epochs. If the former with a small batch size, the effective number of training examples seen would be very small relative to the dataset size, which could partially explain the weak fine-tuning results. This hampers reproducibility.

- **Auxiliary LLM for response extraction is unidentified and unvalidated**: Section 3.2 describes using "an auxiliary LLM" to normalize heterogeneous model outputs, but never names which model or reports any validation of extraction accuracy. If the auxiliary LLM misparses responses, that noise propagates into F1 scores across all experiments.

### Trivial

- Table 5 rounds all assembly-code F1 scores to 0.00; the paper notes models "occasionally generate correct predictions" (line 306) but rounding hides this variation. Reporting to three decimal places or providing raw counts would be more informative.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Variance reporting**: LLM outputs are stochastic. Reporting variance across multiple sampling runs or seeds would help distinguish signal from noise in the small score differences between models (e.g., F1 0.04 vs. 0.05 in Table 2).
- **Analysis of recoverable function types**: The paper does not investigate whether certain function categories (e.g., simple library wrappers, error handlers) are systematically easier or harder to recover. This kind of analysis would make the negative result more diagnostically useful.
- **A single larger-model experiment**: While the paper justifies excluding 70B+ and API-based models on cost/GPU grounds, one experiment with a larger model would substantially strengthen the claim that current LLMs are "fundamentally limited."

## Suggestions

- Move the experiment behind the 0.37 claim into the main body with full context (architecture, optimization level, task, metric), or correct the abstract and introduction to reflect only results verifiable from the main body.
- Add at least one diagnostic ablation for the type inference degradation: check whether fine-tuned models are outputting a single default type, garbled tokens, or empty responses. Distinguish metric strictness from genuine model failure.
- Run one non-LLM baseline (e.g., DIRT for type inference or SYMLM for name recovery) on the same dataset to provide an interpretable reference point.
- Validate the CodeWordNet metric on a small sample (50–100 name pairs) against human judgments to establish that semantic similarity scores correspond to human notions of naming equivalence in this domain.
- Specify the batch size used during fine-tuning and clarify whether "200 steps" means 200 gradient updates.
