## Summary
This paper proposes an adaptive correction mechanism for enforcing conservation laws (linear and quadratic) in neural operator outputs. The method introduces lightweight learnable operators that adjust predictions to exactly satisfy conservation constraints while preserving the original model architecture. Theoretical analysis shows the approach can achieve lower reconstruction loss than constrained optimization alternatives, and experiments across multiple neural operator architectures (UNet, GTNO, FNO) and PDE benchmarks demonstrate improved accuracy and stability compared to original models, loss-based methods, and projection-based methods.

## Strengths
- **Novel and practical approach**: The adaptive correction mechanism is a clever combination of mathematical structure (ensuring exact conservation) with learnable components (providing flexibility), addressing a genuine limitation of existing hard-constraint methods that rely on fixed, hand-crafted post-processing.
- **Theoretical grounding**: Theorem 1 provides a formal guarantee that the adaptive correction approach can achieve lower reconstruction loss than direct constrained optimization, which gives principled justification for the method beyond empirical observation.
- **Comprehensive empirical evaluation**: The paper tests across three neural operator architectures (UNet, GTNO, FNO) and six PDE benchmarks covering both linear and quadratic conservation laws, with clear comparisons against loss-based and projection-based baselines, including ablation studies.

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Limited scope of conservation laws**: The method is restricted to linear and quadratic conservation laws. While these cover important cases (mass, momentum, energy/norm), many physical systems involve higher-order or more complex conserved quantities. The paper acknowledges this as a limitation but does not discuss how the framework might generalize, which limits its impact.
- **Theoretical claim vs. practical significance**: Theorem 1 compares against an extreme case (λ=∞ in soft constraints), but the practical baselines use finite λ. The comparison with projection methods (which also achieve exact conservation) shows the adaptive method outperforms them, but the theoretical result does not directly explain why the adaptive method outperforms projection methods, which is the more relevant comparison.
- **Computational cost analysis missing**: The paper claims the method is "lightweight" but provides no analysis of additional parameters, training time, or inference overhead compared to baselines. Given that the projection method is criticized for computational cost, a quantitative comparison would strengthen the claims.

### Minor
- **Ablation study could be stronger**: The ablation compares against appending an MLP without conservation constraints, which shows the conservation structure matters. However, it would be more informative to also compare against a version where the correction coefficients are fixed (non-learnable) to isolate the benefit of adaptivity.
- **Implementation details for learnable coefficients**: The paper states that A can be generated by a lightweight network but does not specify the exact architecture choices (e.g., number of parameters, input features) for each baseline, making reproducibility harder.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Extending the framework to handle multiple simultaneous conservation laws would significantly broaden applicability.
- A discussion of how the method might extend to non-linear, non-quadratic conservation laws (e.g., via Taylor expansion or other approximations) would be valuable.
- Analysis of the trade-off between correction strength and prediction accuracy when the conservation law is only approximately satisfied in the data (e.g., due to numerical errors in ground truth).

## Novel Insights
The key insight is that conservation enforcement can be formulated as a learnable convex combination of local correction operators (for linear laws) or a structured rescaling (for quadratic laws), which preserves exact conservation while allowing the model to adapt the correction to different inputs. This bridges the gap between rigid post-processing methods (which are exact but inflexible) and soft constraints (which are flexible but inexact). The theoretical result showing that this approach can outperform constrained optimization is a nice formalization of the intuition that separating conservation enforcement from the main model training avoids the optimization difficulties of balancing multiple loss terms.

## Suggestions
- Add a computational cost comparison (parameters, training time, inference time) between the adaptive method, projection method, and loss-based method.
- Include an ablation where the correction coefficients are fixed (e.g., uniform α_i = 1/N) to isolate the benefit of learnability.
- Discuss how the method could be extended to conservation laws that are not purely linear or quadratic (e.g., energy conservation in nonlinear systems).

## Score and Decision
The paper presents a well-motivated, theoretically grounded, and empirically validated method for enforcing conservation laws in neural operators. The approach is novel, practical, and clearly demonstrated across multiple architectures and benchmarks. The main limitations are the restriction to linear/quadratic laws and the lack of computational cost analysis, but these do not invalidate the core contribution. The paper represents a solid contribution to the field of physics-informed machine learning.
