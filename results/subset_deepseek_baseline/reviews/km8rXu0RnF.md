## Summary

This paper proposes LOGIT, a federated learning framework that addresses intermittent client unavailability by learning to generate surrogate gradients on the server side. The method introduces a Gradient Generation Network (GGN) that captures client-specific gradient trajectories and produces missing updates when clients drop out, conditioned on their gradient history and updates from available clients. The authors provide convergence analysis showing O(1/√T) rate and validate their approach on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and IMAGENETTE, demonstrating accuracy improvements of up to 4.98% over baselines.

## Strengths

- **Novel application of generative approach to client unavailability**: The idea of learning to generate surrogate gradients for unavailable clients using a learned generator is a creative and meaningful contribution to the FL straggler problem. The coordinatewise parameterization is practical for high-dimensional models.

- **Solid theoretical analysis**: The paper provides convergence bounds with O(1/√T) rate and derives meaningful insights about the relationship between gradient staleness, participation probabilities, and convergence. The special case analysis (Theorem 2) gives clean practical guidance about prioritizing the least reliable clients.

- **Comprehensive experimental evaluation**: The experiments cover multiple datasets (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, IMAGENETTE), different levels of data heterogeneity, and ablation studies on transmission success probability, staleness, and number of clients. The communication efficiency analysis (Fig. 2) is practically relevant.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Unrealistic assumption about surrogate gradient unbiasedness (Assumption 3)**: Assumption 3 states that surrogate gradients are unbiased with bounded variance. However, the surrogate gradients are generated by a learned model and there is no mechanism ensuring they are unbiased estimators of the true client gradients. This assumption essentially assumes away the core difficulty the paper claims to address. The convergence proof is thus built on an assumption that may not hold in practice.

2. **Missing key experimental details and comparison fairness**: The GGN is trained using both the client's own gradient and other available clients' gradients, meaning available clients incur additional training overhead that baselines don't have. The paper doesn't discuss the computational and memory cost of maintaining and training N separate LSTM models on the server. Additionally, the baselines (FedAvg, MIFA, WS) do not have access to the same generative capability, so the comparison may not be apples-to-apples.

3. **Limited baseline selection**: The paper compares against only three baselines, none of which are recent generative or learned approaches for client unavailability. There is no comparison with more sophisticated methods like SCAFFOLD, FedProx, or buffered asynchronous aggregation methods. The WS baseline is from 2025 but the comparison seems unfair given WS uses a simple weighted sum formula while LOGIT uses a learned LSTM.

4. **Scalability concerns not adequately addressed**: The paper claims client-specific GGNs with coordinatewise parameterization, but having N separate generators (even if lightweight) raises questions about memory and computational costs as the number of clients scales to hundreds or thousands. The ablation on N only goes to 50 clients, which is small for FL.

### Minor

- The term "arbitrary client unavailability" in the title is somewhat misleading since the theoretical analysis assumes independent Bernoulli participation with fixed probabilities.
- The improvement over baselines in low-heterogeneity settings (α=0.3) is modest (less than 1% on CIFAR-100), suggesting the benefits may be more situational than the paper suggests.
- The paper does not discuss the sensitivity to the hyperparameter λ_n (the weight balancing MSE and alignment loss).

### Trivial

- Figure 1 is mentioned twice in the text but is not clearly labeled.
- The "Top-3 accuracy" label in Figure 3 doesn't match the text (which says "average top-1 accuracy" in the ablation discussion).

## Nice-to-Haves

- An analysis of when the generative approach might fail (e.g., under extreme distribution shift or when a client has never been observed) would strengthen the paper.
- A comparison with asynchronous FL methods that handle delays rather than dropouts would be informative.
- Discussion of communication overhead of maintaining gradient history on the server.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions

1. Relax or justify Assumption 3 more carefully. Consider providing an analysis that bounds the bias introduced by the generator rather than assuming unbiasedness.
2. Include comparisons with additional baselines such as SCAFFOLD, FedProx, and buffered asynchronous aggregation to better situate the method.
3. Provide a computational cost analysis (server-side memory and compute per round) for maintaining N separate GGNs, especially for large-scale FL with hundreds of clients.
4. Add sensitivity analysis for the hyperparameter λ_n, which controls the trade-off between local reconstruction and global alignment.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses an important practical problem in FL with a novel and theoretically grounded approach. The core idea of learning to generate surrogate gradients is interesting, and the convergence analysis is a strength. However, the unrealistic assumption about unbiasedness of surrogate gradients undermines the theoretical guarantees, and the experimental setup may not fully demonstrate the method's advantages over a broader set of baselines. The paper has merit but requires stronger theoretical foundations and more comprehensive evaluation.
