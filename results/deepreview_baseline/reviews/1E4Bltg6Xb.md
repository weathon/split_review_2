## Summary
This paper proposes a Dynamics Feature Representation (DFR) framework for reinforcement learning-based dynamic path planning in urban road networks. DFR hierarchically refines global traffic dynamics into compact, decision-relevant features using a pre-trained policy attention mechanism (to select top-\(k\) shortest paths and build a subgraph) and an \(n\)-hop neighborhood method (to extract node-local dynamics). Experiments on three real-world urban subgraphs show that DFR improves planning performance (lower GAP, higher success rate) and reduces computational overhead compared to using all dynamics, and ablation studies characterize the effects of parameters \(k\) and \(n\).

## Strengths
- **Addresses a practical problem**: The trade-off between completeness and efficiency in state representation for RL-based dynamic path planning is a relevant and under-explored challenge in intelligent transportation.
- **Intuitive hierarchical design**: The two-stage refinement (task-level policy attention followed by agent-centric \(n\)-hop neighborhoods) is conceptually clear and aligns well with the intuition that an agent needs both a global task context and local traffic awareness.
- **Empirical demonstration on real-world networks**: The framework is evaluated on three urban road networks extracted from OpenStreetMap, adding practical relevance to the work.

## Weaknesses
### Fatal
None.

### Major
1. **Lack of statistical rigor** – All experimental results appear to be from a single run (no error bars, no standard deviations reported). RL training is high-variance; without multiple random seeds it is impossible to assess whether observed improvements are statistically significant. This weakness undermines the reliability of the core claims.

2. **Insufficient baselines** – The only baseline is using all dynamics (AD). The paper does not compare against simpler alternatives such as a fixed local neighborhood without policy attention, a randomly sampled subgraph, or an attention-based aggregator (e.g., GAT). The ablation study shows that the \(n\)-hop neighborhoods alone (\(k=-1\)) often achieve competitive results (e.g., SR 0.901 at \(n=3\)), making it unclear how much value the policy attention module adds.

3. **Overclaimed theoretical grounding** – The paper invokes Predictive State Representations (PSR) to argue that DFR yields a Markovian and theoretically sufficient state, but it provides no formal proof or even a rigorous argument. The statement “Grounding DFR in PSR principles thus guarantees…” is unsupported and misleading. This section should either be substantiated with actual guarantees or removed.

4. **Limited scale and realism** – Experiments are conducted on extracted subgraphs (likely a few hundred nodes) with dynamics generated by a simple congestion factor \(\beta\) that is i.i.d. sampled per step (no temporal correlation, no realistic traffic model). The paper does not test on larger city-wide graphs or with realistic time-series traffic data, leaving scalability and practical relevance unverified.

### Minor
- The notation for time indices is confusing: \(t\) is used for both the decision step (in MDP) and the physical time in the dynamics sequence (e.g., \(W_t\)). The mapping between the two is not clearly explained.
- The description of the “dynamic Dijkstra algorithm” used to compute ground-truth optimal paths is vague. It is unclear whether it assumes full knowledge of future dynamics or replans step-by-step.
- The ablation heatmaps are informative but the paper does not report sensitivity of results to the random seed; the values may be just one seed.

### Trivial
- The metric “Mean GAP” is not explicitly defined in the main text; a precise definition would improve readability.
- Some figure captions (e.g., Figure 2) are overly long and attempt to describe every element, making them hard to scan.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Report results with at least 5 random seeds and include mean ± std for all metrics.
- Add baselines: (i) local neighborhood only (no policy attention, as a control), (ii) random subgraph of same size as DFR, (iii) attention-based feature selection (e.g., using a GAT to weight edges).
- Test on larger graphs (e.g., full city network with >10k nodes) and with realistic, temporally-correlated traffic simulations.
- Provide a formal analysis (or at least a counterexample) about when DFR preserves or fails to preserve the Markov property.

## Novel Insights
None beyond the paper’s own contributions. The combination of a static pre-trained distance policy for subgraph selection with dynamic local features is a sensible engineering heuristic, but the paper does not yield new theoretical understanding of state representation in dynamic environments.

## Suggestions
- Run all experiments with at least 5 random seeds and report mean and standard deviation; include statistical significance tests where appropriate.
- Add a baseline that uses only the \(n\)-hop neighborhoods (with \(k=-1\)) to isolate the contribution of policy attention.
- Clarify how dynamics are generated: are the congestion factors \(\beta\) sampled i.i.d. at each time step? Do they follow a stochastic process with temporal correlation? This is essential for reproducibility and for understanding the difficulty of the task.
- Either remove the PSR discussion or replace it with a formal statement about when the constructed state is sufficient (e.g., by proving that the Bellman optimality equation holds given the DFR state under certain conditions).
- Provide guidance on tuning \(k\) and \(n\) for larger graphs, or propose an adaptive selection mechanism (as mentioned in the conclusion) and include preliminary results.

## Score and Decision
The paper addresses a relevant problem and proposes a reasonable heuristic, but the experimental validation is insufficient (no multiple seeds, limited baselines) and the theoretical grounding is overclaimed. The contribution is incremental and not convincingly demonstrated at the rigor expected for a top venue. I recommend rejection.
