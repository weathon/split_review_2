## Summary

This paper proposes the *Fast and Slow Effect* (FSE) framework to automatically evaluate the sufficiency of concept-class annotations generated by LLMs/VLMs for explainable AI. The framework introduces a multi-step annotation process (fast mode: direct visual inference; slow mode: stepwise concept gathering) and a metric called Class Representation Index (CRI) to quantify how well accumulated concepts support accurate classification. Experiments across fine-grained and general datasets reveal that current annotation methods often fail to provide sufficient semantic coverage, with the slow mode underperforming the fast mode, and that the widely used utility-as-proxy assumption does not reliably reflect annotation quality.

## Strengths

- **Addresses an important and underexplored problem**: The paper tackles the critical issue of validating automated concept annotations for XAI, which is essential for trustworthiness but has received limited systematic attention.
- **Novel evaluation framework**: The FSE framework is fully automatic, requiring no human supervision, and provides a principled way to assess annotation sufficiency through the proposed CRI metric and the fast/slow mode comparison.
- **Reveals surprising and impactful findings**: The empirical results showing that slow mode (concept-based reasoning) underperforms fast mode (direct visual inference) by over 25% on fine-grained datasets, and that high downstream utility does not imply annotation sufficiency, are both novel and practically important.
- **Comprehensive experiments**: The paper evaluates six LLMs across five datasets (three fine-grained, two general), covering both post-hoc and visual-grounded annotation scenarios, with careful distractor selection and multiple runs.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Limited validation of the CRI metric**: The CRI is essentially classification accuracy on a small candidate set (ground truth + 4 distractors). While this is a reasonable proxy for sufficiency, the paper does not validate whether CRI correlates with human judgments of annotation quality or with other interpretability metrics. Without such validation, it is unclear whether CRI captures the intended notion of sufficiency beyond a simple classification test.
- **Arbitrariness of the five-stage annotation process**: The paper adopts a five-stage refinement (Background, Superclass, Salient Features, Detailed Features, Auxiliary Features) but provides limited justification for why five stages are optimal or how this choice affects results. The stages are motivated by prior work, but the specific number and ordering could influence the observed CRI trends, and the paper does not ablate this design choice.
- **Lack of comparison to human annotations**: The paper evaluates only LLM-generated annotations. Without a human-annotated baseline, it is difficult to interpret the absolute CRI scores (e.g., is 60% CRI good or bad?). A comparison to human concept annotations would strengthen the claim that current methods are insufficient.
- **No analysis of why slow mode fails**: The paper attributes the slow mode underperformance to LLMs struggling to "conceptualize their knowledge," but does not provide deeper analysis (e.g., error analysis, qualitative examination of generated concepts) to support this explanation. Understanding the failure modes is crucial for improving annotation strategies.

### Minor

- **Distractor selection uses supervised pretraining**: The semantically related distractor selection relies on a pretrained ResNet-18, which introduces a form of supervision. While this is not human supervision, it slightly weakens the claim of a "fully autonomous" framework.
- **Candidate set size is small**: Using only 5 classes (1 ground truth + 4 distractors) makes the classification task relatively easy. In real-world settings with hundreds of classes, the CRI might be much lower, and the observed trends might not generalize.
- **Limited exploration of Chain-of-Thought reasoning**: The paper mentions that FSE can evaluate CoT performance (e.g., DeepSeek-R1) but only briefly discusses this in an appendix. Given the relevance to reasoning models, this could have been a stronger part of the contribution.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Validate CRI against human expert annotations on a subset of data to establish its reliability as a sufficiency metric.
- Ablate the number of annotation stages (e.g., 3 vs. 5 vs. 7) to show robustness of the framework.
- Provide qualitative examples of generated concepts that lead to correct vs. incorrect slow-mode predictions, to illustrate failure modes.
- Extend the candidate set to include more classes (e.g., 10 or 20) to test whether the findings hold under more realistic conditions.

## Novel Insights

The paper's key insight is that LLMs' ability to generate plausible concepts does not guarantee that those concepts are sufficient for accurate class discrimination, especially in fine-grained domains. The finding that slow, concept-based reasoning underperforms fast, opaque visual inference challenges the assumption that more explicit reasoning always improves interpretability. Furthermore, the demonstration that high downstream utility (fused mode) can coexist with poor annotation sufficiency (slow mode alone) is a critical caution for the XAI community: performance gains in multimodal pipelines may mask inadequate conceptual supervision.

## Suggestions

- Conduct a human evaluation study to validate CRI against human ratings of annotation sufficiency, even on a small scale.
- Perform an error analysis to categorize why slow mode fails (e.g., missing discriminative concepts, overly generic concepts, reasoning errors).
- Test the framework with a larger candidate set (e.g., 10–20 classes) to assess scalability.
- Consider using a more unsupervised method for distractor selection (e.g., based on LLM embeddings) to maintain full autonomy.

## Score and Decision

The paper makes a solid contribution by proposing a novel, automatic evaluation framework for concept annotation sufficiency and revealing important empirical findings. However, the lack of validation of the CRI metric against human judgments and the limited analysis of failure modes temper the strength of the conclusions. The paper is well-written and addresses a timely problem, but the evaluation framework itself requires further validation to be fully convincing.
