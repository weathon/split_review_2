## Summary

This paper studies the problem of covert misinformation injection in Large Language Model-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS). It introduces MISINFOTASK, a dataset of 108 realistic tasks designed to benchmark MAS robustness against misinformation, and proposes ARGUS, a two-stage training-free defense framework that uses adaptive localization of critical communication channels and goal-aware reasoning for corrective intervention. Experiments across four LLMs and three attack types show that ARGUS reduces misinformation toxicity by roughly 28% and improves task success rate by about 10% on average.

## Strengths

- **Timely and important problem.** Addressing covert misinformation in MAS is a practically relevant challenge that goes beyond overt jailbreak or malicious content, and the paper draws useful attention to this distinction.
- **End-to-end system with dataset + defense.** Providing a dedicated dataset and a complete defense pipeline (localization, monitoring, correction) is a service to the community and makes the work self-contained.
- **Comprehensive evaluation setup.** Experiments cover four different core LLMs, three injection methods, five MAS topologies, and include ablation studies and temporal analyses. This gives a reasonable picture of where the method does and does not work.
- **Training-free and modular.** The ARGUS framework does not require fine-tuning, making it practical for deployment with black-box LLMs. The modular design allows each component to be analyzed independently.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Limited dataset scale and construction.** The dataset contains only 108 tasks, all generated by an LLM with manual filtering. The small size and synthetic origin limit confidence in the generalizability of the reported results. More tasks with broader domain coverage and human-authored ground truth would be necessary for a convincing benchmark.

2. **Evaluation relies entirely on an LLM judge (GPT-4o) for both MT and TSR.** No human evaluation, inter-annotator agreement, or calibration against gold-standard labels is provided. Since the judge might share systematic biases with the agent LLMs, the measured improvements could be inflated or misaligned with true task success.

3. **Baselines are weak and not state-of-the-art.** Self-Check is a trivial self-reflection baseline, and G-Safeguard also shows limited effectiveness. The paper does not compare with more recent and stronger defense methods such as multi-agent debate (Chern et al. 2024), graph pruning (AgentPrune, Zhang et al. 2024b), or other consensus-based techniques. Missing these comparisons makes it hard to assess the relative value of ARGUS.

4. **Limited novelty of the defense approach.** The core components—edge-betweenness centrality for topology, embedding similarity for relevance, CoT-based detection and correction—are all standard techniques applied in a straightforward pipeline. The “goal-aware reasoning” is essentially prompt engineering with CoT. While the integration is clean, the individual ideas are not new, and the overall contribution is incremental for a top venue like ICLR.

### Minor

5. **Modest and inconsistent performance gains.** The average TSR improvement is about 7–10%, but for some models (e.g., Gemini-2.0-flash) the gain is only ~4.4%, and for DeepSeek-V3 it is already high without defense. The claim of “significant efficacy” is somewhat overstated given the variability.

6. **The distinction between misinformation and malicious information is not crisply operationalized.** In practice, the boundary can be blurry, and the defense may need to handle mixed cases that the paper does not evaluate. The threat model assumes a single compromised agent; more complex attack scenarios (e.g., multiple colluding agents) are not considered.

7. **Ablation results show that the adaptive localization contributes only modestly.** For example, removing dynamic localization still yields MT = 4.55 (vs. 3.50 with full ARGUS), which is still much lower than attack-only (4.88). The main benefit appears to come from having any monitoring/rectification agent, not from the sophisticated localization. The hyperparameter sensitivity (Table 3) also suggests the method is not critically dependent on the adaptive component.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Human evaluation of a subset of outputs to validate the LLM judge.
- Larger and more diverse dataset with multiple domain splits.
- Comparison with additional defense methods such as multi-agent debate, AgentPrune, or consensus verification.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper’s own contributions. The observation that misinformation toxicity increases over rounds in undefended MAS and can be curbed by a monitoring agent is a straightforward confirmation of expected dynamics.

## Suggestions

- Enlarge the dataset with human-verified examples and publicly release it to encourage follow-up work.
- Include a human evaluation study or at least a calibration of the LLM judge against human judgments.
- Add comparisons with at least two stronger baselines (e.g., AgentPrune, multi-agent debate) to better contextualize ARGUS.
- Provide open-source code for reproducibility of the main results.
