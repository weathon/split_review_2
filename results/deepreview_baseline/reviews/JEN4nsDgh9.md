## Summary
The paper introduces a benchmark for Taxonomy Image Generation, evaluating how well 12 text-to-image (TTI) models can generate images for WordNet synsets and LLM-predicted concepts. It proposes 9 metrics, including taxonomy-specific similarity scores derived from CLIP, reward-model scores, ELO rankings from both human and GPT-4 pairwise comparisons, along with FID and IS. The main finding is that Playground-v2 and FLUX consistently outperform other models, retrieval-based methods perform poorly, and model rankings differ substantially from standard T2I leaderboards.

## Strengths
- **Timely and practical problem statement.** Automating visual enrichment of taxonomies (e.g., WordNet) is a worthwhile goal that could reduce manual annotation effort. The paper correctly identifies that ImageNet covers only 6.5% of WordNet synsets, motivating the work.
- **Large-scale comparative study.** The benchmark covers 12 models across multiple datasets (ground-truth synsets and LLM-predicted nodes) and uses both human experts (4 annotators, 3370 pairwise judgments) and GPT-4 as evaluators, providing a rich set of observations.
- **Release of generated images and data.** The authors commit to publishing the full set of generated images for WordNet-3.0, which is a valuable resource for the community to reproduce and extend the work.
- **Interesting divergence from standard T2I rankings.** The finding that SDXL-turbo dominates CLIP-based similarity metrics despite being a distilled model, while performing poorly in preference evaluations, is a non-trivial observation that could drive further analysis.

## Weaknesses

### Major
- **Novelty and methodological contribution are overstated.**  
  The claim of introducing “9 novel taxonomy-related text-to-image metrics” is misleading. Lemma similarity is a standard CLIP score (Hessel et al., 2021). Hypernym and cohyponym similarities are averages of CLIP scores over related nodes, which is a straightforward extension. Specificity is the ratio of two such averages. The only genuinely new metric is the pairwise GPT-4 preference evaluation, but GPT-4-as-a-judge for images has already been explored (Chen et al., 2024; Cui et al., 2024). The paper does not clearly differentiate its metrics from prior work (e.g., ISP from Baryshnikov & Ryabinin, 2023, which it claims to generalize but does not provide a direct comparison).

- **GPT-4 evaluation reliability is insufficiently validated.**  
  The paper reports high Spearman correlation (0.88–0.92) between GPT-4 and human *rankings*, but explicitly states that “we found no correlation between raw scores for individual battles” due to a strong first-option bias (Figure 5). This undermines the trustworthiness of GPT-4 as a judge for individual pairwise decisions. The paper treats GPT-4 as one of nine metrics, but then draws heavy conclusions from its ELO scores (e.g., “Playground ranks first in all preference-based evaluations”). If the raw battle decisions are unreliable, the resulting ELO scores are questionable. A deeper analysis of this bias and its mitigation is needed.

- **Reward model evaluation conflates with GPT-4 preferences.**  
  The reward model (Xu et al., 2024) is described as a separate metric, but it was itself trained on human preferences and outputs scores based on the Bradley-Terry model (same underlying framework as ELO). This creates a dependency: the reward model’s ranking (Playground first) aligns suspiciously well with GPT-4’s ranking, but the paper does not control for potential overlap in training data or methodology. The moderate correlation (0.59) between reward model and GPT-4 does not rule out circularity.

- **Dataset construction has several issues.**  
  - The “Easy Concepts” dataset (483 entities) is small and may not be representative.  
  - The random split from WordNet uses a biased sampling procedure (0.1/0.1/0.8 for sampling, but then different probabilities in the test set: 1×10⁻⁵, 0.05, 0.1). The mismatch between sampling and test probabilities is confusing and not well justified. Why not simply sample uniformly from each relation type?  
  - The LLM predictions dataset is generated by TaxoLLaMA-3.1, but the paper does not release this model or its training details, limiting reproducibility. The use of GPT-4 to generate definitions for these predicted synsets introduces an additional dependency without ablating the effect.

- **Results presentation is opaque and lacks statistical rigor.**  
  Table 2 shows the top-1 model for each metric/subset but does not indicate whether differences are significant (except for a vague “*” note about confidence intervals). Many comparisons may be within noise. Figures 4 and 8 show ELO scores with confidence intervals, but the paper does not discuss whether the top models are statistically separable. The paper claims “Playground and FLUX are among the top models across different metrics,” but a proper significance test is missing.

- **Retrieval baseline is poorly specified.**  
  The retrieval model (Wikimedia Commons) is described in one sentence. How were the images retrieved? Using the synset lemma as a search query? Was there any filtering? The retrieval approach is dismissed as “poorly performing,” but without a clear explanation, it is hard to assess whether the downstream comparison is fair. The example in Figure 2 (a golden Buddha statue for “cigar lighter”) suggests the retrieval was not concept-aware, which is not surprising.

### Minor
- **FID calculation is unusual.**  
  The paper computes FID “based on retrieved images,” meaning the reference distribution is the set of retrieved Wikimedia images. This makes FID a measure of “closeness to retrieved images” rather than a measure of visual quality relative to natural images. This is a deliberate choice, but it contradicts the standard interpretation of FID and makes the results hard to compare with other T2I benchmarks. The paper should have justified this more clearly or used an external reference set.

- **Definition-based prompts are treated inconsistently.**  
  The paper argues that adding definitions “does not turn the task into standard instruction following” and is an additional diagnostic. However, the exact prompt template (“An image of <CONCEPT> (<DEFINITION>)”) is still a form of augmented instruction. The paper does not analyze how much the definition helps vs. hurts each model, beyond brief statements. A per-model ablation on a sample would clarify the effect.

- **Human evaluation details are sparse.**  
  Only four expert annotators (all in computational linguistics) were used. The instructions they received, the interface, and inter-annotator agreement metrics (beyond overall Spearman) are not provided. The paper mentions a Spearman of 0.8 between annotators, which is reasonable but not high; possible disagreements could affect ELO rankings.

### Trivial
- The acronym “TTI” is used interchangeably with “T2I”; standardizing would improve clarity.
- Figure captions are overly long and repeat text from the body.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Provide per-metric significance tests (e.g., bootstrap confidence intervals for top-model differences).
- Ablate the effect of definition inclusion for each model individually.
- Compare the proposed taxonomy-specific metrics (hypernym/cohyponym similarity) against vanilla CLIP score to demonstrate added value.
- Release the TaxoLLaMA-3.1 fine-tuned model or its training configuration for reproducibility.

## Novel Insights
The paper demonstrates that TTI model rankings for taxonomy concepts differ from standard T2I benchmarks, with Playground-v2 and FLUX leading despite not always being top in general T2I evaluations. This suggests that concept-level abstraction and taxonomic accuracy place unique demands on models. The observation that SDXL-turbo, a distilled model, achieves the highest CLIP-based similarity scores while failing in preference-based evaluations indicates a divergence between text-image alignment and human aesthetic/semantic judgment—a finding worth further study. However, the core methodological insights (use of CLIP, pairwise ELO ranking, GPT-4 evaluation) are largely adapted from prior work rather than being novel to this paper.

## Suggestions
1. **Tighten the novelty claims.** Rephrase “9 novel metrics” to “9 metrics, of which X are newly proposed (specificity, pairwise GPT-4 evaluation) and Y are adapted from prior work (CLIP similarities, FID, IS).”
2. **Address GPT-4 bias explicitly.** Analyze whether removing biased pairs changes rankings, or apply a debiasing method (e.g., randomizing presentation order and discarding confident first-choice cases).
3. **Provide significance tests for top models.** Use bootstrap or permutation tests to compare the top-1 and top-2 models across metrics.
4. **Clarify the retrieval baseline.** State the query formulation, retrieval algorithm, and any post-processing. Show results with and without retrieval to confirm the gap.
5. **Simplify result tables.** Instead of showing top-1 per cell, show model rankings with significance groups (e.g., letters from pairwise tests) to improve interpretability.

## Score and Decision
Score: 5.0 – borderline reject. The paper addresses an interesting problem and provides a large-scale comparison, but the claimed novelty of the metrics is overstated, the GPT-4 evaluation methodology has unresolved reliability issues, and the results lack statistical rigor. With major revisions addressing the above points, the paper could become a solid contribution, but in its current form it is not ready for acceptance.
