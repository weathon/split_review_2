## Summary

This paper systematically evaluates nine open-source mid-sized LLMs (CodeLlama, Llama 2, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen, etc.) on two core reverse engineering tasks: name recovery (function and variable names) and type inference from stripped binaries. The authors develop an automated evaluation pipeline, use decompiled and assembly code inputs, and assess both zero-shot and fine-tuned (LoRA) performance. Their main finding is that without fine-tuning, all models achieve very low F1 scores (often below 0.1), and while fine-tuning provides some improvements, performance remains fundamentally limited—none of the models achieve reliable reverse engineering capability.

## Strengths

- **Timely and well-motivated research question.** The paper addresses a genuine gap in the literature: the lack of systematic, principled model selection for LLM-based reverse engineering. The motivation (Section 2) clearly articulates why arbitrary model choices in prior work (e.g., SYMGEN using CodeLlama-34B, RESYM using StarCoder-3B without justification) are problematic, and the paper provides a much-needed benchmarking effort.

- **Comprehensive evaluation scope.** The study covers nine models across multiple architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS), multiple optimization levels (O0-O3), two input representations (decompiled and assembly code), and two decompilers (Ghidra and IDA Pro). The dataset of 40,000 unique functions is reasonably large and diverse, drawn from real open-source projects (GNU software).

- **Honest and clear reporting of negative results.** The paper does not oversell its findings. It transparently reports that all models perform poorly (F1 scores mostly below 0.1), that fine-tuning gains are inconsistent, and that type inference is especially challenging. This is valuable for the community as it sets realistic expectations and identifies where future work is needed.

- **Useful methodological contributions.** The automated post-processing pipeline using an auxiliary LLM to normalize heterogeneous outputs is a practical solution to a real evaluation challenge. The use of CodeWordNet for semantic name matching (rather than strict string matching) is appropriate and well-justified.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The paper's core claim is not surprising and the contribution is incremental.** The finding that "LLMs perform poorly on reverse engineering tasks without fine-tuning" is already well-known in the community. Prior work (e.g., Jin et al. 2023 on ChatGPT/GPT-4 for binary code summarization, Xu et al. 2023 on symbol name recovery) has already demonstrated that LLMs require fine-tuning for these tasks. The paper's main contribution—a systematic benchmark confirming this—is useful but does not constitute a significant advance. The paper would be stronger if it provided deeper insights into *why* models fail, or proposed concrete improvements.

- **The fine-tuning setup is weak and the results are not properly contextualized.** The paper uses only 200 LoRA fine-tuning steps (Table 7) across all models, which is a very small amount of training. The training set is 160,000 functions, but only 200 steps means the models are barely trained. The paper does not report whether training converged, what the loss curves looked like, or whether more steps would yield better results. The finding that "fine-tuning has limited impact" may simply reflect insufficient training rather than a fundamental limitation of LLMs. This is a critical methodological weakness that undermines one of the paper's main conclusions.

- **The evaluation metrics for name recovery are not sufficiently justified.** The paper uses CodeWordNet for semantic matching, which is reasonable, but it does not report human evaluation or user studies to validate whether the metric correlates with actual usefulness for reverse engineers. An F1 score of 0.3 might be practically useful if the names are semantically meaningful, or it might be useless if they are only superficially similar. The paper does not address this.

- **The paper lacks a clear baseline comparison.** The authors compare LLMs against each other but do not compare against traditional non-LLM approaches (e.g., pattern matching, statistical models, or even simple heuristics). Without such baselines, it is unclear whether the poor LLM performance is a reflection of the difficulty of the task itself or a specific limitation of LLMs. The paper mentions prior work on function name recovery (Jin et al. 2022, David et al. 2020) but does not compare against their reported results.

### Minor

- **The paper's title is somewhat misleading.** The title "LLMs as Reverse Engineers? Not Yet on Types and Names" suggests a definitive negative answer, but the paper's own results show that fine-tuning can achieve F1 scores of 0.33 on variable name recovery (WizardCoder, Table 3), which is not negligible. The title overstates the negativity of the findings.

- **The paper does not analyze failure modes in sufficient depth.** Section 4.5 provides some qualitative examples (Table 8) and observations about formatting issues, but a more systematic analysis of *why* models fail (e.g., confusion with specific type patterns, inability to handle long-range dependencies in decompiled code, sensitivity to specific decompiler artifacts) would be more informative.

- **The paper's claim about "training data quality" (Findings 4 and 5) is speculative.** The paper attributes performance differences to "training data quality" without actually analyzing the training data of the models. This is an unsupported claim. The observed differences could be due to many other factors (architecture details, tokenization, pre-training objectives, etc.).

### Trivial
- The paper states it evaluates "nine" models but Table 1 lists nine models plus an empty cell, which is slightly confusing.
- The paper mentions "DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37" in the abstract, but this specific number does not appear in any of the result tables (the highest F1 in the tables is 0.33 for WizardCoder on variable name recovery). This inconsistency should be resolved.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison against traditional machine learning approaches (e.g., random forests, SVMs) on the same tasks would strengthen the paper's claims about LLM limitations.
- An analysis of whether longer fine-tuning (more steps, full fine-tuning) would change the conclusions would be valuable.
- A user study or expert evaluation to assess whether the recovered names/types are practically useful despite low F1 scores would add practical relevance.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The paper's main insight—that current LLMs perform poorly on reverse engineering tasks without substantial fine-tuning—is already known from prior work. The paper's systematic benchmarking is useful but does not produce a genuinely novel insight about why this is the case or how to fix it.

## Suggestions

1. **Increase fine-tuning duration and report convergence.** The paper should fine-tune models until convergence (or at least for a substantially larger number of steps) and report loss curves. This would make the fine-tuning results more meaningful and address the concern that the observed "limited impact" is due to insufficient training.

2. **Add non-LLM baselines.** Compare against at least one traditional approach (e.g., a simple n-gram model or a small neural network trained from scratch) to contextualize the LLM results.

3. **Provide a more nuanced analysis of failure modes.** Instead of just reporting F1 scores, categorize the types of errors (e.g., semantic similarity vs. complete misses, type confusion patterns, sensitivity to specific decompiler constructs) to guide future research.

4. **Clarify the abstract's claim about DeepSeek-R1's F1 score of 0.37.** Either provide the specific experiment that produced this result or correct the abstract to match the reported tables.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses a well-motivated question and provides a reasonably comprehensive benchmark. However, the core findings are not surprising, the fine-tuning setup is too weak to support the paper's conclusions about LLM limitations, and the paper lacks critical baselines. The contribution is incremental and the methodological weaknesses are significant enough to warrant rejection.
