## Summary

This paper introduces PELICAN, a two-stage adaptive tutoring framework that combines collaborative cognitive diagnosis with dynamic instructional adaptation. In the first stage, the system assesses a student's cognitive state through hierarchical knowledge point diagnosis using a successor-first strategy and an expert-assistant-verifier pipeline. In the second stage, it employs a dual-system theory approach (fast and slow thinking) to select teaching strategies from a predefined pool, continuously monitoring student responses to adapt instruction. Experiments on the Gaokao dataset and a human study with 169 students show improvements in critical thinking stimulation and task completion rates compared to several baselines.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem and clear framing**: The paper clearly articulates a genuine limitation of current LLM-based tutoring systems—their tendency to generate one-size-fits-all responses that fail to account for individual cognitive states. The illustrative example in Figure 1 effectively demonstrates this issue.

- **Novel integration of cognitive diagnosis with adaptive strategy selection**: The two-stage framework that first diagnoses cognitive state and then uses that diagnosis to guide strategy selection is a sensible and well-structured approach. The successor-first diagnostic strategy that leverages hierarchical knowledge dependencies is a practical contribution.

- **Comprehensive evaluation with both automated and human studies**: The paper includes GPT-based evaluation across multiple dimensions, ablation studies, analysis by cognitive level, strategy distribution analysis, and a real-world human experiment with 169 students. This multi-faceted evaluation strengthens the empirical claims.

- **The slow-thinking simulation approach is interesting**: Using a simulated teaching tree to evaluate candidate strategies by projecting their future dialogue paths is a creative application of planning/rollout methods to the tutoring domain.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The simulated student in the slow-thinking module raises circularity concerns**: The slow-thinking module evaluates strategies by simulating a virtual student's responses (Equation 4). However, the simulated student's behavior is generated by the same LLM that is being evaluated. This creates a potential circularity: the system may select strategies that work well for the LLM's own model of a student rather than for real students. The paper does not discuss or address this fundamental limitation. The human evaluation results mitigate this concern somewhat, but the mechanism by which slow thinking improves performance remains unclear—it could simply be that more tokens/computation leads to better responses regardless of the simulation.

- **The human evaluation has significant confounds**: The real-world experiment involved 169 high school students submitting 1335 reports (average 7.90 per student). However, the paper does not specify: (1) how students were assigned to conditions, (2) whether the same students interacted with multiple systems (carryover effects), (3) how the "student role" simulation (Appendix G) relates to the human experiment, or (4) whether students were aware of which system they were using. The success rates in Table 6 are surprisingly high across all methods (80-87%), suggesting the problems may have been relatively easy or that the evaluation setup favors all methods similarly.

- **The Gaokao dataset is small and limited**: The dataset contains only 184 high school exam questions. This is a very small corpus for evaluating a tutoring system. The paper does not report how many unique knowledge points are covered, how many students were simulated in the main experiments, or how many diagnostic/tutoring sessions were conducted. The small scale raises questions about generalizability.

- **Critical implementation details are missing**: The paper states that slow thinking is activated after M=1 rounds, meaning it kicks in after just one round of difficulty. This seems extremely aggressive—essentially every time a student struggles, the system immediately switches to the expensive simulation mode. The paper also does not explain how the threshold M was chosen or whether it was tuned on the test set. Additionally, the strategy pool of ten strategies is mentioned but only described in Appendix E (which is not provided in the reviewable content).

### Minor

- **The baseline comparisons are somewhat weak**: Several baselines (Free-Prompt, Stepwise) are simple prompting approaches that would not be expected to perform well. The strongest baselines (Socratic, Bridge-Based) are from 2024-2025, which is reasonable, but the paper does not include any non-LLM cognitive diagnosis baselines (e.g., NeuralCDM, IRT-based methods) for the first stage.

- **The ablation study results are confusing**: In Table 3, removing both diagnosis and slow thinking actually improves "Inspiration" (4.56 vs 4.30) and "Reliability" (4.21 vs 4.44 is close). The paper claims these results "emphasize the key roles" of the modules, but the improvements in some metrics when removing modules suggest the story is more nuanced.

- **The backbone model ablation shows inconsistent results**: In Table 4, Qwen-max achieves higher R_coverage (64.41) than GPT-4o (54.84), yet the paper claims GPT-4o "excels" across the board. The paper does not discuss why a smaller/cheaper model might achieve better coverage.

### Trivial
- The paper uses "Sepwise" in Table 6 and Figure 5 but "Stepwise" in Table 2—likely a typo.
- The score formula uses both λ and φ as the penalty parameter in different places.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison with non-LLM cognitive diagnosis methods (e.g., NeuralCDM, MIRT) would strengthen the claim that the collaborative diagnostic approach is superior.
- An analysis of when slow thinking actually changes the strategy selection vs. when it agrees with fast thinking would provide insight into the module's value.
- Error analysis showing which types of students or problems the system struggles with would be valuable.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions.

## Suggestions

1. Address the circularity concern in the slow-thinking module by either (a) using a separately trained student model for simulation, (b) providing evidence that the simulated student responses correlate with real student behavior, or (c) ablating the simulation component to show it adds value beyond simply using more computation.

2. Provide more details on the human evaluation: random assignment procedures, whether students were blinded to condition, how many sessions per condition, and whether the same students saw multiple systems.

3. Report results on a larger or more diverse dataset, or at minimum provide per-question statistics to show the 184 questions are not dominated by a few knowledge areas.

4. Clarify why M=1 was chosen and whether results are sensitive to this hyperparameter. A sensitivity analysis would be helpful.

5. Discuss the inconsistency in the ablation study where removing modules sometimes improves certain metrics.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses an important problem and presents a well-structured framework with reasonable empirical support. However, the major concerns about the circularity of the slow-thinking simulation, the limited scale of the evaluation, and the confounds in the human study prevent this from being a strong accept. The core ideas are solid and the two-stage approach is sensible, but the evidence that the specific technical contributions (particularly the simulated teaching tree) drive the improvements is not fully convincing.
