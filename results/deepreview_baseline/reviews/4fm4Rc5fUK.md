## Summary

This paper proposes Autoformalizer with Tool Feedback (ATF), a framework that integrates Lean 4 compiler feedback for syntax checking and a multi-LLM-as-judge approach for semantic consistency validation directly into the autoformalization generation process. The training pipeline consists of three stages—cold-start on synthetic tool-calling data, expert iteration to improve formalization, and DPO to reduce ineffective revisions. Experiments across three benchmarks show ATF substantially outperforms existing formalizers, with particularly large gains on the out‑of‑distribution CombiBench, and the authors release a 750K formal statement dataset.

## Strengths

- **Novel integration of tool feedback during generation.** Prior work trains a formalizer offline, then uses it as a black‑box generator. ATF instead lets the model invoke syntax and consistency tools during inference and adaptively refine its output, which is a principled way to compensate for the model’s limited knowledge of formal language details.
- **Strong and consistent empirical results.** ATF-32B beats the best baseline (Goedel‑V2‑Formalizer‑32B) by 9–29% in semantic consistency Pass@1 across all three benchmarks. The gains are largest on the difficult out‑of‑distribution CombiBench, demonstrating genuine generalization. The human evaluation further confirms the reliability of the consistency check tool (Pearson R = 0.746).
- **Thorough ablation and analysis.** Ablations cleanly separate the contribution of each training stage and each tool. The scaling analysis (revision scaling, Pass@K scaling) and tool‑usage breakdown are informative and add depth to the paper.
- **Open‑source dataset.** The release of Numina‑ATF (750K formal statements) is a valuable resource that will facilitate future work on autoformalization and automated theorem proving.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Reliance on a proprietary model for cold‑start data.** The cold‑start trajectories are generated by Claude‑4‑Sonnet, a closed, expensive model. This makes the pipeline difficult to reproduce and raises questions about whether the method would work as well with only open‑source models. While the authors show that ATF-8B‑Distilled works, the core synthetic data still depends on an API that may change or become unavailable.
- **Circularity and potential bias in the consistency check.** The same type of model (Qwen3‑32B) appears both as the base model being trained and as one of the judges in the multi‑LLM ensemble. Although the ensemble also uses QWQ‑32B, the judge models are not independent of the training distribution. The benchmark used to validate the judge is constructed by perturbing statements with Gemini‑2.5‑Pro and filtering by character similarity (>0.95); this may not capture all realistic inconsistencies. The paper does not evaluate how well the judge generalizes to other families of judges or to fully adversarial examples.

### Minor

- **DPO contribution is marginal.** Table 4 shows that adding DPO on top of expert iteration yields only small improvements (e.g., +0.09% SC and +0.36% CC on FormalMath‑Lite). The narrative around DPO as a key component feels slightly over‑claimed given these numbers.
- **Complexity of the training pipeline.** Three separate training stages, each with its own data generation and hyper‑parameters, make the method somewhat heavy to adopt. A sensitivity analysis of hyper‑parameters (e.g., the number of expert iterations, the revision cap) is missing.

### Trivial
- None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Explore alternative consistency checks (e.g., embedding‑based semantic similarity or formal verification via a proof assistant) to reduce dependence on LLM judges.
- Provide an analysis of the computational cost (total GPU hours, inference latency) of the tool feedback loop to help practitioners assess trade‑offs.
- Since the cold‑start data is generated by a proprietary model, switching to an open‑source model (e.g., DeepSeek‑R1, Qwen3) for this step would strengthen the claim that the method is fully reproducible with open resources.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper’s own contributions, the most interesting observation is that the success rate of the consistency check decays sharply with revision attempts (from 69.5% on attempt 1 to 8.8% on attempt 8). This suggests that the model quickly exhausts its “confident” repair strategies, and that further revisions become increasingly uncertain. This provides a concrete lens into the limitations of tool‑integrated self‑correction: while tool feedback helps, the model’s ability to exploit it has diminishing returns, and interventions that enrich the revision strategy (e.g., retrieving exemplars or using search over candidate revisions) might be needed to push performance further.

## Suggestions

1. **Address the reproducibility concern regarding the cold‑start data.** Consider experimenting with an open‑source model (e.g., DeepSeek‑R1 or the same Qwen3 family) to generate the synthetic tool‑calling trajectories and report whether the final ATF performance remains competitive.
2. **Validate the consistency check judge more thoroughly.** Report the judge’s performance on a hand‑crafted set of subtle inconsistencies, or compute the correlation between judge decisions and human judgments on the exact evaluation sets rather than a separate sanity benchmark.
3. **Provide a more nuanced analysis of the DPO stage.** Show examples where DPO actually changes the revision behavior (e.g., reduces unnecessary tool invocations) and quantify the average number of revisions before and after DPO.
