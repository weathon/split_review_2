## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. The framework uses a Seq2Seq architecture that can be trained with sequence-aligned data (no note-level alignment needed), and introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings directly from score content, enabling automated stylistically appropriate rendering. Experimental results on ASAP and ATEPP datasets show competitive performance on both tasks, effective disentanglement, and meaningful style generation.

## Strengths

- **Novel unified formulation of inverse tasks**: Jointly modeling EPR and APT under a shared disentangled representation framework is a natural and well-motivated idea. The paper convincingly demonstrates that joint training with unpaired data improves EPR performance (Ours-Target vs. EPR-Only), validating the mutual benefit hypothesis.
- **Practical removal of note-level alignment**: Formulating EPR as a Seq2Seq problem without requiring fine-grained note alignment is a meaningful practical advance that simplifies data preprocessing and handles expressive ornaments more flexibly. The approach achieves competitive alignment rates (92.27% for PSR variant).
- **Comprehensive evaluation with both objective and subjective metrics**: The paper uses multiple objective metrics (MUSTER, ScoreSimilarity, variance, KL, MAE, alignment/insertion/miss rates) and includes a well-designed listening test with 11 musically trained participants across four dimensions. The style transfer and era-clustering visualizations further support the disentanglement claims.
- **The PSR module is a genuinely useful addition**: Generating style embeddings directly from score content using a diffusion model is creative and practically valuable, especially for non-expert users. The visualization in Figure 4 showing that PSR-generated styles mirror real performance styles in era-wise clustering is compelling.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **Subjective evaluation is underpowered**: Only 11 participants and 5 test pieces. For a subjective claim about "human-likeness" and style quality, this sample is too small to draw statistically robust conclusions. The error bars in Figure 2 are not reported (only bar heights), making it impossible to assess variability. A larger-scale listening test or a formal ABX test would strengthen the claims.
2. **PSR module evaluation lacks quantitative grounding**: The main evidence for PSR quality is visual similarity in 2D projections (Figure 4) and the subjective ratings in Figure 2. No quantitative metric (e.g., Fréchet Inception Distance, cosine similarity between PSR-generated and actual style vectors) is provided to measure how well the generated styles match real performance styles. The subjective comparison for PSR (Figure 2) shows it often lags behind the target-style variant, but the gap is not analyzed statistically.
3. **Ablation confounds joint training with additional data**: The EPR-Only baseline is trained only on ASAP (967 pieces), while Ours-Target uses additional unpaired data. The observed improvement from joint modeling could partly be due to simply having more training data. An ablation that trains the joint model *without* unpaired data on the same data as EPR-Only would cleanly isolate the benefit of the joint APT objective.
4. **APT performance is not state-of-the-art**: While competitive, the model's pitch error ($E_p=3.08$) is worse than several baselines (e.g., 2.02 for Liu et al., 2.11 for Shibata). The paper frames this as a trade-off for alignment-free training, but the practical benefit over simpler alignment-based methods is unclear for users who already have aligned data. The MUSTER average is best, but the individual sub-metrics show mixed results.

### Minor

1. **Small paired dataset**: The core paired training data is only 967 performances from ASAP. While unpaired data helps, the limited paired data may constrain the quality of joint representation learning, especially for rare expressive patterns.
2. **Style transfer evaluation is narrow**: Only 3 samples are used for the subjective style transfer test (Figure 5). This is too few to generalize about style transfer capability across diverse genres.
3. **No failure case analysis**: The paper does not discuss when PSR might produce inappropriate styles (e.g., for atypical score structures) or when the joint model's disentanglement breaks down. This would improve understanding of limitations.

### Trivial

None.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A quantitative metric for PSR quality (e.g., cosine distance between generated and ground-truth style vectors on a held-out set) would strengthen the results.
- Larger-scale subjective evaluation with more participants and pieces, possibly including an ABX preference test.
- An ablation where the joint model is trained *without* unpaired data to isolate the effect of the APT objective on EPR performance.

## Novel Insights

Beyond the paper's own contributions, the finding that *composer* identity is more strongly encoded in the style embedding than *performer* identity (77.46% vs. 42.07% accuracy) is an interesting empirical observation. This supports the PSR module's motivation—that style is strongly tied to compositional characteristics—and suggests that the learned style space captures aspects of musical tradition and genre beyond individual pianistic gestures. The smooth interpolation in the style space (Mean condition performing well in style transfer) is also a nice property that could enable creative control.

## Suggestions

- Add a quantitative comparison between PSR-generated style vectors and ground-truth style vectors on a held-out set (e.g., average cosine similarity or FID in style space).
- Include a controlled ablation that trains the joint model on the same data as EPR-Only (i.e., without unpaired data) to cleanly measure the benefit of joint APT supervision.
- Expand the subjective listening test to at least 20 participants and 10–15 pieces for more robust conclusions, or use a crowdsourced ABX framework.
- Report the subjective results with error bars or confidence intervals to allow readers to assess variability.
