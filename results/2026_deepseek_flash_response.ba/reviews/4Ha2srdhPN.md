## Summary

GRAID is a framework for generating spatial VQA training data by determining qualitative spatial relationships from 2D bounding box geometry alone, entirely avoiding the 3D reconstruction errors and generative hallucinations that plague prior pipelines. Applied to BDD100k, NuImages, and Waymo, it produces 8.5M+ VQA pairs spanning 22 question templates and includes SPARQ, a predicate-based efficiency mechanism. Fine-tuning experiments demonstrate that VLMs trained on GRAID data improve spatial reasoning on held-out question types, cross-dataset scenarios, and several existing benchmarks.

## Strengths

- **Genuine non-obvious insight with clean instantiation.** The core idea — that qualitative spatial relationships (left/right, counting, size comparisons) can be reliably determined from 2D bounding boxes without 3D reconstruction — is sound, well-motivated, and cleanly implemented. This avoids the cascading errors that plague depth-estimation-based pipelines (SpatialVLM's 50-200% acceptance threshold being a telling data point). The paper correctly identifies the source of the problem (Section 3.1).

- **RQ2 provides compelling evidence of concept learning.** Training on only 6 of 22 question types yields accuracy gains of +47.5pp on BDD100k and +38.0pp on NuImages across over 10 held-out types (Figure 3). The cross-dataset transfer (GRAID-BDD → GRAID-NuImages, with different cities and scenes) is a genuine and well-designed test that shows the model learned spatial primitives, not template-specific patterns. This is stronger evidence than typical in-distribution fine-tuning results.

- **SPARQ is a well-engineered practical contribution.** The predicate-based early rejection system addresses a genuine quadratic-scaling bottleneck. Measured timing data (5.17ms predicate vs 46.95ms realization for RightOf, 9× speedup) are concrete. The 78.8% predicate-success-to-realization-success rate for LargestAppearance shows the predicates are well-calibrated.

- **Scale and extensibility.** Generating 8.5M+ VQA pairs from three real-world datasets with 22 templates demonstrates the framework's practical value. The modular template interface means the framework can be extended as new detection/segmentation models emerge.

- **No architectural changes required.** Unlike SpatialRGPT, GRAID works with any off-the-shelf VLM, lowering the adoption barrier.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The headline 91.16% vs 57.6% comparison is not a controlled comparison, yet the paper presents it as direct evidence of superiority.** The 57.6% figure comes from evaluating OpenSpaces (a community reimplementation of SpatialVLM) and the 91.16% from GRAID-BDD. These differ in: (a) source images (different datasets/scenes), (b) question types (qualitative binary for GRAID vs metric estimation for SpatialVLM), and (c) evaluation conditions (GRAID evaluators could see bounding boxes; SpatialVLM evaluators could not use region-based prompting). The paper states this comparison in the abstract, introduction (line 53: "our human study finds that over 91.16% of GRAID generated VQA pairs are valid as compared to under 58% of a dataset generated by a current method"), and conclusion without qualifying these confounds. The individual numbers are informative, but the gap is not attributable to GRAID's quality alone — question-format difficulty, evaluator ability to verify answers, and source-data differences all contribute. This overclaiming undermines the paper's most prominent quantitative claim.

2. **All experiments use ground-truth annotations; the practical scenario with real object detectors is never evaluated.** Section 4 (line 155) states: "we select to directly leverage these high-quality labels in GRAID's generation rather than train our own object detectors so that we can evaluate GRAID's effectiveness in isolation." This is a deliberate scoping choice, but the paper's framing throughout (abstract: "using only 2D bounding boxes from standard object detectors"; Section 3.1: detectors are "robust enough for practical deployment") claims compatibility with practical detector outputs without ever testing that scenario. Real detectors produce false positives, false negatives, class confusions, and localization errors. How much does GRAID's data quality degrade when fed YOLO detections vs. oracle annotations? Without this experiment, the paper's practical claims are unsupported. At minimum, a sensitivity analysis comparing ground-truth vs. detector-based outputs on a subset would address this.

### Minor

3. **RQ3 comparison baseline is a community reimplementation, not the original SpatialVLM.** The paper acknowledges "OpenSpaces, a dataset generated by the community implementation of SpatialVLM" (line 202) but subsequently refers to it as "the SpatialVLM dataset" without qualification (lines 280-283). A community reimplementation may differ in data sources, depth models, question templates, or quality control. The comparison should consistently be framed as "our reimplementation of SpatialVLM" and differences from the original method should be discussed.

4. **Algorithm 1 does not implement the "similar planes" check described in the text.** The text (line 138) states that the RightOf realization checks that bounding boxes "lie on similar planes" as a necessary condition. However, Algorithm 1 (lines 110-134) checks only x-coordinate ordering and IoU=0. The plane-similarity computation is never specified. This is a reproducibility issue — how "similar planes" is determined from 2D bounding boxes alone is non-trivial and should be described.

5. **The 1407× speedup is cherry-picked; typical speedup is much smaller.** The maximum figure applies to `LargestAppearance` (predicate 0.02ms vs. full realization). For `RightOf`, the speedup is 9×. The paper should report median or representative speedup rather than leading with the extreme maximum.

6. **No discussion of limitations.** The paper never discusses what GRAID cannot do: "in front of"/"behind" relationships, occlusion reasoning, metric distance, object orientation, or any 3D spatial relation. For embodied applications (robotics, navigation), these are significant limitations. Acknowledging them would help users understand when GRAID is appropriate.

### Trivial

7. Inter-annotator agreement is not reported for the human evaluation (4 evaluators, but no Cohen's kappa or agreement rate).

8. The "similar planes" mention in Algorithm 1's text description (line 138) but absence from the pseudocode creates an inconsistency that should be resolved.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A controlled human evaluation using the same source images for both GRAID and the SpatialVLM pipeline, with matched question formats, would turn the headline comparison from suggestive to conclusive.
- An experiment with a YOLO-family detector (or similar) running inference on BDD100k, feeding detections into GRAID, and comparing the resulting data quality and downstream performance against the oracle baseline would directly address the most significant evaluation gap.
- Including a brief qualitative error analysis of the 28 problematic GRAID-BDD VQA pairs (labeling errors, unclear formulations) — are certain question types more error-prone?
