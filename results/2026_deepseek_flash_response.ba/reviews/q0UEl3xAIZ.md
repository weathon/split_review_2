## Summary

This paper applies Goal-Oriented Environment Inference (GOEI), an existing model-based RL algorithm (Takahashi et al., 2024), to the competitive card game "Hol's der Geier." GOEI compresses 15,542 possible observations down to 452 states (2.9%) while achieving a median reward rate of −0.010 against a Nash equilibrium opponent — a tiny deviation from the NE-vs-NE baseline of 0.000. The paper also provides a mutual information analysis showing which features' information is preserved in the reduced representation.

## Strengths

1. **Striking state compression with near-NE performance**: The paper demonstrates that GOEI compresses the observation space to 2.9% of its original size (452 vs. 15,542 states) while the best configuration (β=0.2, α=25) achieves a median reward rate of −0.010 against the NE opponent, close to the NE-vs-NE baseline of 0.000 (Table 1). This is a genuinely non-trivial compression ratio for a competitive game with complex dynamics, and the result is clearly presented.

2. **Information-theoretic analysis of preserved content**: Section 4.2 and Figure 3 go beyond reporting compression ratios by computing mutual information between five observable features (SD, CT, AH, OH, RT) and the learned states. The finding that CT and RT are relatively preserved at early rounds while SD is preserved only at round 4 aligns with the game's strategic structure in a plausible way. This provides some interpretability into what the compressed representation retains — a concrete advance over a pure black-box compression claim.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **Central quantitative claim ("indistinguishable from optimal") lacks statistical support**: The paper states that GOEI's performance is "indistinguishable from the optimal one (≃0)" (Section 5, line 228). The best median reward rate is −0.010 with quartiles [−0.012, −0.009] — all negative and narrowly concentrated. In a zero-sum game where individual rewards are {−1, 0, 1}, a systematic negative bias of 0.01 per game may or may not be negligible, but no statistical test (confidence interval, t-test, Bayesian analysis) is provided to justify the "indistinguishable" characterization. The claim would be stronger if quantified precisely (e.g., "within 0.01 of NE" or "not significantly different from 0, p > 0.05 with test X").

2. **Evaluation decouples inference from interaction, limiting what the results establish relative to the stated motivation**: The introduction motivates GOEI as a method for efficient online learning in environments with large observation spaces (Section 1: "much room for improvement in tasks that require online learning to adapt to opponents"). Yet the evaluation trains GOEI on a fixed dataset of games between two fixed strategies (Rand vs. NE), with the agent never taking actions during training — only observing. The training data is generated by two fixed policies (one already optimal), so the agent receives high-quality, well-distributed data. The paper honestly acknowledges this in Section 5 ("environment inference and strategy optimization may interfere with each other... should be further confirmed"), but this acknowledgment means the paper demonstrates GOEI's offline compression ability on curated data rather than its effectiveness in the interactive online setting that motivated the work. A reader cannot tell whether the impressive compression figures would survive under interactive learning where the agent's own changing policy determines what data it sees.

### Minor

1. **Mutual information analysis has an unresolved tension**: Section 4.2 reports that Agent Hand (AH) and Opponent Hand (OH) information is "almost completely reduced" (very low mutual information with states), but then claims these features "are likely to be crucial for learning a near-optimal strategy" and speculates the information is preserved in "complex combinations of all the features." This is a plausible conjecture but is unsupported — the analysis only measures pairwise MI, not higher-order interactions, and the paper provides no evidence (e.g., feature ablation showing that removing AH or OH degrades performance) to back the "crucial" claim.

2. **Reward rate averaged across all 3,000 epochs**: The reported reward rate averages over all epochs including early learning, not just the converged phase (line 132). This means early poor performance drags down the average. The learning curves in Figure 2A partially address this, but a separate converged-phase metric (e.g., last 500 epochs) would be more informative and would likely show a reward rate even closer to 0.

3. **Q-learning comparison lacks implementation details relevant to the offline setting**: The paper does not specify how tabular Q-learning was applied to observational data generated by two fixed policies. Standard Q-learning requires the agent to take actions; if run off-policy on fixed data, the paper should describe the target policy, behavior policy, and whether any correction for off-policyness was applied. Without this, it is difficult to fully assess the comparison (though this does not affect the paper's main contribution about GOEI's state compression).
