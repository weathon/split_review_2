## Summary

This paper presents a systematic benchmarking study evaluating nine open-source LLMs on two reverse engineering tasks — name recovery (function and variable names) and type inference from stripped binaries. The evaluation spans 4 architectures, 4 optimization levels, two input formats (decompiled and assembly), and includes both zero-shot and fine-tuned (200-step LoRA) settings on a 40,000-function dataset from GNU software. The paper finds that all models achieve low F1 scores (mostly below 0.1), with fine-tuning yielding modest and inconsistent gains.

## Strengths

1. **Addresses a genuine gap in model selection rationale.** The paper correctly identifies that prior work (SYMGEN using CodeLlama-34B, RESYM using StarCoder-3B) selects models without principled justification, and provides the first broad side-by-side comparison on both name recovery and type inference (§2, lines 87–91).

2. **Multi-dimensional evaluation scope.** The study spans 9 models, 4 architectures (x86-64, x86-32, ARM, MIPS), 4 optimization levels (O0–O3), two input formats, and two task types, using a 40,000-function dataset. This breadth is the paper's main asset (§4.1, line 139).

3. **Automated response normalization pipeline.** The use of an auxiliary LLM to standardize heterogeneous model outputs is a practical contribution, since formatting inconsistency is a real obstacle to fair comparison in this setting (§3.2, lines 95–107).

4. **Controlled architectural comparisons.** The paper compares models with identical architectures (DeepSeek-R1 vs. Qwen2.5, CodeLlama vs. Llama2) to isolate the effect of training data quality over model size, yielding actionable findings (§4.4, Finding 5, lines 360–362; Finding 4, lines 356–357).

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Introduction claims an unsupported quantitative result.** Line 19 states that "after fine-tuning, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates a notable improvement, reaching an F1 score of 0.37." The highest F1 score for DeepSeek-R1 visible in the main paper's tables is **0.17** (Table 3, variable name recovery, O0, fine-tuned). The body text discussing top performers mentions WizardCoder reaching 0.33 (line 265), not DeepSeek-R1 at 0.37. The paper notes (line 217) that results for other architectures are in the appendix, but the introduction presents 0.37 as a headline finding without any table reference, and the main results section never contextualizes it. A reader cannot determine whether this is a typo, a result from an unreported experimental condition, or an error. This disconnect between the paper's self-presentation and its evidence damages credibility.

2. **Auxiliary LLM for response extraction is unnamed and unvalidated.** The evaluation pipeline (§3.2, line 107) uses an auxiliary LLM to normalize outputs, but never identifies which model served this role, reports its extraction accuracy, or tests whether extraction quality varies systematically across target models. Since DeepSeek-R1 produces extended chain-of-thought reasoning (line 412) that is harder to parse, this could introduce systematic bias against certain models. Without validation, the reported F1 scores conflate target model performance with extraction accuracy, making both absolute and relative scores uninterpretable.

3. **Fine-tuning protocol lacks convergence guarantees and risks data overlap.** The paper fine-tunes for exactly 200 LoRA steps with no convergence check, no learning curves, and no report of the effective number of epochs (§3.3, lines 111–125). Given that a central finding is that fine-tuning yields "modest and inconsistent gains," the possibility that 200 steps is simply insufficient training cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, training data (160K functions) and test data (40K) are independently sampled from the same GNU projects (line 186); despite deduplication, near-duplicate functions from the same projects could inflate fine-tuning gains.

### Minor

4. **No non-LLM baselines.** The paper evaluates LLMs only against other LLMs. Without comparison to traditional ML approaches (VARBERT, Debin) or simple baselines (decompiler default naming, vocabulary-based guessing), the conclusion that "current LLMs remain fundamentally limited in their ability to perform reverse engineering tasks" (conclusion, line 426) is too broad — the observed F1 scores may be competitive with existing specialized methods.

5. **No variance or confidence intervals.** All tables report point estimates of F1 without any measure of variance. Given that LLM generation is stochastic, readers cannot assess whether differences between models (e.g., Qwen3 at 0.06 vs. Phi4 at 0.07 in Table 2) are meaningful.

6. **Precision and recall components of F1 are never shown.** The paper defines F1 and states it uses precision and recall (line 139), but only F1 aggregates are reported. Without the components, low F1 could be driven by low precision (many wrong predictions) or low recall (missing correct predictions), with very different implications.

### Trivial

7. The exact prompt template (referenced as Figure 4a) is not visible in the extracted text, making it difficult to assess prompt quality or reproduce the work.

## Suggestions

1. Correct or substantiate the introduction's 0.37 claim about DeepSeek-R1 with a specific table reference, or remove it.
2. Report which model was used as the auxiliary LLM and provide a validation experiment measuring its extraction accuracy per target model on a manually labeled subset.
3. Add learning curves showing validation F1 over training steps for at least a representative subset of models, and report effective epochs.
4. Include at least one non-LLM baseline (decompiler default naming, simple vocabulary-based prediction) to contextualize the absolute F1 scores.
5. Report precision and recall alongside F1 in at least one representative table.

## Score and Decision

The paper addresses a genuine gap and has commendable breadth, but it suffers from two major methodological weaknesses that prevent confidence in the reported results: (1) the auxiliary LLM used for response extraction is never validated, making every F1 score a confounded measure; and (2) the introduction makes an unsupported quantitative claim (DeepSeek-R1 reaching 0.37) that cannot be verified from the main paper's tables. These issues, combined with a shallow fine-tuning protocol and absence of non-LLM baselines, make the evidence insufficient to support the paper's conclusions. Score: **3.5**.
