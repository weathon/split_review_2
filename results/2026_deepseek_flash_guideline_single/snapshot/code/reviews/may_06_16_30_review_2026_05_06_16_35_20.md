# Review of may_06_16_30.md

## Summary
The paper argues that health-oriented LLMs can be overly cautious in ways that may be harmful for people with OCD or health anxiety, and introduces OCD-Eval, a 225-query benchmark of OCD-triggered health-anxiety prompts. It evaluates 22 LLMs by comparing model-produced risk labels against GP risk labels, adds response-pattern tags via an LLM judge, and compares over-caution against emergency-recognition performance on HealthBench.

## Strengths
- **The paper identifies a concrete and underexplored failure mode distinct from standard over-refusal.** The introduction explicitly defines over-cautiousness as both objective risk overstatement and subjectively alarming tone, rather than simply refusal to answer (lines 49–50), and the paper focuses on cases where the model *does* answer but frames risk too conservatively.
- **OCD-Eval is targeted at a vulnerable-user scenario that existing general health benchmarks may miss.** The dataset consists of 225 OCD-triggered health-anxiety queries sourced from authors with OCD experience and human-validated (lines 260–266), and the paper explicitly motivates why HealthBench’s general non-emergency questions may not capture emotionally loaded reassurance-seeking prompts (lines 254–261).
- **The risk labels have some clinical anchoring and a meaningful reliability check.** The paper uses GP risk labels, gives GPs the user’s OCD context (lines 268–270), reports the label distribution (lines 282–283), and re-labels 50 retained questions with a second group of five GPs, reporting exact agreement 0.70, quadratic weighted Gwet’s AC2 0.93, linear weighted AC2 0.83, MAE 0.3, and no retained item reclassified as high risk (lines 286–291).
- **The empirical evaluation covers a broad model set rather than isolated anecdotes.** The abstract reports evaluation of 22 LLMs (lines 24–27), and Section 4.2 discusses differences across model generations and sizes, including low OCR for some recent models and high OCR for earlier/smaller ones (lines 342–351).
- **The OCR/SOCR distinction is useful.** The paper separates any upward risk-label mismatch from two-or-more-level exaggeration (lines 306–309), allowing the authors to show that many models may not severely exaggerate risk but still elevate negligible-risk situations to low-risk language (lines 347–351).
- **The paper tests a simple mitigation rather than assuming user disclosure solves the problem.** Section 4.4 evaluates six models when explicitly given the user’s OCD/health-anxiety context and finds inconsistent effects, including degraded emergency recognition for some models and persistent over-caution for others (lines 386–398).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The paper contains a real contribution: a targeted benchmark and empirical evidence that many models assign higher explicit risk categories than GPs on these prompts. However, the central claims are substantially stronger than what the evidence supports.

### Major
- **The main metric has limited construct validity for the paper’s stated phenomenon of “over-cautiousness.”** The paper defines over-cautiousness as both risk overstatement and fearful/alarming tone (lines 49–50), but the headline OCR metric is simply whether a forced model risk label exceeds the GP label (lines 301–309). This supports the narrower claim that models often choose more conservative explicit risk categories than GPs, but it does not by itself establish that responses are clinically inappropriate, anxiety-provoking, harmful, or over-cautious in the richer sense the paper motivates. A response could use a “low risk” label while being calm and appropriate, or a “negligible risk” label while still listing frightening caveats. The paper does add LLM-as-judge tags and an anxiety index, but these are secondary and weakly validated relative to the strength of the central claim.
- **The evaluation protocol may induce the behavior being measured by forcing models to append explicit risk ratings.** The paper states that evaluated LLMs are required to append a final risk rating using the same categories as GPs (lines 301–303). This is not necessarily how users normally ask health questions or how models normally answer them. Explicit categorical medical risk assignment is a high-liability framing and may push models toward hedging or avoiding “negligible/no risk” labels. Therefore, interpreting forced labels as evidence of natural over-cautious health communication is not fully justified without a parallel naturalistic evaluation.
- **The paper repeatedly claims harm to vulnerable users, but does not empirically measure harm in vulnerable users.** The abstract says over-cautious responses have “negative consequences” and can cause “potential harm” (lines 19–30), the position section lists downstream harms such as intensified anxiety, compulsive behavior, unnecessary visits, tests, avoidance, and diet changes (lines 222–234), and the conclusion says the paper demonstrates that over-cautiousness “poses real harm” (lines 571–573). The empirical evidence is indirect: GP risk labels, model risk labels, LLM tags, and a small anxiety-index calibration. There is no user study with OCD/health-anxiety participants, no measurement of anxiety change after exposure, and no behavioral outcomes. The harm hypothesis is plausible and important, but the paper should present it as a motivating risk pathway rather than an established empirical finding.
- **Clinical suitability for OCD users is not established by the annotation setup.** The authors explicitly state they did not use GP labels about suitability for OCD patients because GP OCD-suitability judgments were almost identical to general-population suitability labels and “may not fully recognize the nuances specific to OCD patients” (lines 270–275). This is a reasonable admission, but it undercuts later interpretations that a one-level shift from negligible to low is clinically harmful for OCD users. The current evidence supports “models often assign higher risk labels than GPs,” not “models respond in ways clinically harmful for OCD patients.”
- **The dataset is narrow and under-characterized relative to the broad claims.** OCD-Eval contains 225 queries sourced from two authors with past/current OCD experiences and filtered by the authors/human validator (lines 260–266). This is useful as a seed stress test, but the paper does not sufficiently characterize representativeness across OCD subtypes, health anxiety, contamination fears, reassurance seeking, cyberchondria, or broader vulnerable populations. The abstract and conclusion describe over-cautiousness as “widespread” and “significant” (lines 26–28, 571–573), but the dataset construction only supports a more cautious claim about this particular hand-built benchmark.
- **The trade-off claim about over-caution and emergency recognition is over-interpreted.** Figure 2 and Section 4.2 compare different models on OCR and HealthBench emergency recognition, and the counterargument section concludes that reducing over-cautiousness and maintaining emergency recognition “are not in tension” and “both can be achieved simultaneously” (lines 453–464). The evidence shows that some current models occupy better points on both metrics, which is useful. But this is a cross-model observational comparison: models differ in capability, training, policies, instruction following, and calibration. It does not show that tuning a given model to reduce OCR would preserve emergency recognition.

### Minor
- **The anxiety index is too weak to support strong claims about “intense anxiety.”** Section 4.3 samples 42 questions, obtains ratings from one mental health professional, reports Pearson \(r=0.693\) with DeepSeek predictions, and then applies linear calibration (lines 373–382). This is a reasonable exploratory analysis, but a single rater and small calibration sample cannot establish absolute validity, reliability across clinicians, or generalization to OCD users. The claim that models “can still cause intense anxiety” should be softened.
- **The second-round GP relabeling is useful but the “upper bound” interpretation is too strong.** The paper says the 0.16 fraction of second labels exceeding first labels can be interpreted as an upper bound on random disagreement affecting OCR (lines 291–296). This is better viewed as a rough sensitivity check on a 50-item subset, not a principled upper bound on OCR noise across the full dataset and all risk levels.
- **The response-pattern tag analysis is promising but under-validated.** The paper uses DeepSeek V4 Pro to tag six predefined answer patterns, arguing that this is “straightforward pattern matching” (lines 325–330), and then interprets symptom checking and reassurance-undermining as anxiety-heightening patterns (lines 354–362). This is plausible, but the clinical meaning of these tags is not independently validated. In health advice, “monitor symptoms” can be medically appropriate; whether it reinforces compulsive checking depends on wording and user context.
- **The mitigation experiment with OCD context is useful but should not be generalized too far.** Section 4.4 shows that one simple instruction—telling the model the user has OCD/health anxiety and to avoid triggering anxiety—does not reliably reduce OCR and can hurt emergency recognition (lines 386–398). This supports the narrow conclusion that this prompt is insufficient. It does not establish that contextual personalization in general is unreliable.
- **The causal explanation in terms of alignment/legal/PR pressure is speculative.** The position section attributes over-caution partly to universalizing alignment and legal/public-relations pressure (lines 193–210), and later says the behavior arises from “alignment-induced artifacts” rather than knowledge deficits (lines 561–563). The paper does not directly test these mechanisms. The evidence shows a behavioral pattern, not its training or policy cause.

### Trivial
None worth weighing in the decision.

## Nice-to-Haves
- A naturalistic version of the evaluation where models answer normally, without required final risk labels, and independent raters assess risk communication, escalation advice, tone, and OCD-sensitive appropriateness.
- Ratings from OCD/health-anxiety clinicians, not only GPs, on whether responses reinforce reassurance-seeking, checking, avoidance, or compulsive behaviors while preserving medical safety.
- A small human-subjects study or ethically designed vignette study with people high in health anxiety/OCD traits, measuring anxiety, checking urge, reassurance-seeking urge, escalation intention, and perceived helpfulness after reading responses.
- Controlled within-model interventions to test whether reducing over-cautiousness preserves emergency recognition, rather than relying only on cross-model comparisons.
- A clearer positive target for “good” OCD-sensitive health communication: when to reassure, when to redirect to therapy/ERP principles, when to recommend professional medical care, and when to escalate urgently.

## Novel Insights
The most important synthesis is that the paper’s empirical contribution is stronger than its causal and clinical claims: OCD-Eval plausibly measures a narrow but useful phenomenon—models choosing more conservative explicit risk labels than GPs on OCD-flavored low-risk health prompts—while the paper frames this as evidence of actual harm to OCD users. This distinction matters because a benchmark that rewards “negligible risk” labels too aggressively could itself create unsafe under-triage unless it is paired with independent axes for medical risk, escalation appropriateness, tone, uncertainty communication, and OCD-sensitive support.

## Suggestions
- Reframe the main empirical claim as: “Models often assign higher explicit risk labels than GPs on OCD-Eval, especially for negligible-risk prompts,” rather than “LLMs are hurting vulnerable users.”
- Add a naturalistic-response evaluation without forced risk labels, then compare whether OCR correlates with human/clinician ratings of alarmism and OCD-sensitive appropriateness.
- Replace the single headline OCR with a multi-axis rubric: medical risk calibration, escalation advice, emotional tone, symptom-checking encouragement, uncertainty framing, and mental-health redirection.
- Expand annotation to include OCD/health-anxiety clinicians and people with lived experience, while keeping GPs for medical risk ground truth.
- Treat the anxiety index as exploratory unless validated with multiple clinicians and/or affected users.
- Present Figure 2 as evidence that some models perform better on both evaluated metrics, not as evidence that there is no intervention-level trade-off.
- Characterize OCD-Eval more fully: source pool, inclusion/exclusion criteria, themes/subtypes represented, number of candidates discarded, and examples by category.

## Score and Decision
**Originality:** Good. The framing of over-caution as a possible safety failure for OCD/health-anxiety users is novel and valuable, and the benchmark targets a real gap between generic medical benchmarks and vulnerable-user communication.

**Importance:** High. Health advice from LLMs is a safety-critical setting, and the paper correctly highlights that “erring on the safe side” can have costs for anxiety-prone users.

**Support for claims:** Weak-to-moderate. The narrow benchmark result is supported; the broader claims about harm, clinical unsuitability for OCD users, causal alignment mechanisms, and absence of an emergency-safety trade-off are not adequately supported.

**Experimental soundness:** Mixed. The 22-model evaluation and GP risk labels are useful, but the forced risk-label protocol, narrow dataset construction, limited OCD-specific expertise, and weak anxiety validation substantially limit the conclusions.

**Clarity:** Generally readable and conceptually clear, but overclaims throughout the abstract, position, counterargument, and conclusion.

**Value to community:** Potentially valuable as a preliminary benchmark/position paper, but not yet strong enough as a conclusive empirical demonstration.
