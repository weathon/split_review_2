## Summary

CHATS proposes a discrete-token system that transforms written dialogues into spoken dialogues by conditioning a dual-tower Transformer (extended from dGSLM) on phoneme sequences and speaker IDs. The system generates speaker-side speech from text while simultaneously producing listener-side phenomena (backchannels, laughter) without transcription, and incorporates a turn-taking mechanism (TTM) that handles silence durations and overlapping speech. Evaluation includes utterance-level PER, dialogue-level backchannel and turn-taking statistics, speaker-level analyses, and a human evaluation showing statistically significant improvement in Dialogue Naturalness over a TTS baseline.

## Strengths

- **Text-conditioned dual-channel generation is a clean architectural contribution**: Extending the dGSLM dual-tower architecture with phoneme and pitch streams to enable text-conditioned speech on the speaker channel while preserving untranscribed listener phenomena is conceptually well-motivated and fills a real gap between turn-based TTS and textless dialogue models. The human evaluation (Table 4) confirms that the Proposed system improves Dialogue Naturalness over the TTS baseline (3.30 vs 3.01, p=0.038) while maintaining comparable Meaningfulness (3.58 vs 3.43).

- **Turn-taking mechanism (TTM) is simple and demonstrably effective**: The TTM modifies utterance boundary times during training to teach the model appropriate silence durations and overlap handling. Table 3 shows that Proposed w/o TTM consistently degrades MAE across IPU (0.34 vs 0.24), Pause (0.16 vs 0.08), and Gap (0.12 vs 0.08) metrics, and Figure 4 shows the TTM brings pause/gap distributions substantially closer to Ground Truth.

- **Data augmentation by context reduction is practically important**: Table 1 shows that removing this augmentation catastrophically degrades PER from 13.03 to 59.35, demonstrating that the technique effectively addresses the train-inference context mismatch at dialogue starts.

- **Comprehensive evaluation across multiple axes**: The paper evaluates utterance-level intelligibility (PER), dialogue-level backchannel frequency/duration (Table 2), speaker-level backchannel characteristics (Table 2 bottom), turn-taking event distributions and speaker-level statistics (Figure 4, Table 3), and human judgments (Table 4). This multi-faceted evaluation probes the system's behavior more thoroughly than is typical.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Backchannel evaluation methodology is insufficiently described**: The dialogue-level evaluation (Section 4.3.1, Tables 2–3) reports backchannel counts and durations in generated dialogues, but the paper never specifies how backchannels are identified in the synthetic waveforms. The natural candidate is the IPU classifier from Section 2, which was trained on *real* speech segments from the training set. Its accuracy is never reported, and its performance on synthetic speech from the various systems is unchecked. Since the entire listener-event evaluation (backchannel frequency, duration, speaker-level backchannel distributions) depends on this detection pipeline, the reported statistics cannot be fully trusted without validation of the classifier on generated speech. This does not invalidate the human evaluation (which circumvents automated backchannel detection), but it weakens the automated metrics that constitute a large portion of the paper's evidence.

- **Limited baseline comparisons for the central claim**: The paper claims CHATS produces spoken dialogues that are "more interactive and fluid" than a TTS baseline (abstract). The Baseline is a single-channel TTS variant that fills the listener channel with silence — it cannot generate backchannels, laughter, or handle overlaps at all. While outperforming this baseline demonstrates that the proposed features *do something*, the paper does not compare against any system that also attempts to model listener-side events or turn-taking from text. A comparison against, e.g., a pipeline that inserts predicted backchannels into the written dialogue before feeding it to a two-channel system, or an existing conversational TTS adapted to two channels, would substantially strengthen the evidence that CHATS's design choices confer a genuine advantage over simpler alternatives. The comparison against dGSLM is informative but dGSLM operates in a fundamentally different (textless) setting.

### Minor

- **Turn-taking mechanism description could be clearer**: Section 3.2 states that in the overlap case, modified boundaries become $\hat{b}_n = \hat{a}_{n+1} = b_n$. The paper then claims the uLM "must generate the first $b_n - a_{n+1}$ seconds of the $n+1$th utterance concurrently with the $n$th utterance." A reader may question how the model learns to generate overlapping speech when the modified training timeline removes the temporal overlap. The mechanism is in fact coherent — the model generates discrete units for both channels in parallel, and the TTM teaches it to predict appropriate unit durations — but the exposition would benefit from a concrete walk-through with an illustrated unit-level timeline showing how overlap is realized at inference from training examples that lack explicit temporal overlap.

- **IPU classifier used for data preparation is unvalidated**: Section 2 describes a hybrid rule-based/ML approach for identifying and removing listener IPUs to create written dialogues. The binary IPU classifier's precision and recall are never reported. While this is not central to the generation architecture, errors in this data-preparation step could propagate into training data quality. Reporting classifier performance on a held-out set would be valuable.

### Trivial

- The paper could benefit from spectrograms or timing diagrams of generated dialogues showing overlaps, backchannels, and turn-taking dynamics, which would complement the aggregate statistics in Figures 4–5 and Tables 2–3.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A comparison against a baseline that also attempts listener-side generation (e.g., a backchannel predictor feeding into CHATS's own uLM with TTM disabled) would strengthen the contribution, though the current baselines are sufficient to support the paper's stated claims.
- Validation of the backchannel detection pipeline on synthetic speech, or alternatively, a description of how backchannel counting was performed that clarifies whether the IPU classifier was applied to s2u-extracted units from generated audio.
- Scaling experiments to test the hypothesis (mentioned in Section 4.4) that dGSLM's poor performance is due to the 74h dataset size.

## Removed Points

These points are flagged to be removed, treat them with caution:

- **"The turn-taking mechanism contains a contradiction — training data no longer contains overlapping regions"**: Removed. The critic's claimed contradiction is a misunderstanding. The TTM modifies time boundaries but does not remove content. The uLM generates discrete units for both channels simultaneously; the overlap is realized through unit-level generation. The model does not need explicit temporal overlap in training because it learns to produce the content of utterance n+1 (including its early portion that overlaps with utterance n) conditioned on next-utterance phonemes. The mechanism is sound but under-described.

- **"The human evaluation uses too few raters (24)"**: Removed. 24 raters with 25 samples each (600 total ratings) is standard for speech/dialogue human evaluation studies and adequate for detecting the reported effect (p=0.038).

- **"Meaningfulness scores are almost identical to the baseline (3.43 vs 3.58), which suggests text-conditioning does not yield a practically meaningful improvement"**: Removed as a criticism. Both Proposed and Baseline are text-conditioned, so similar Meaningfulness scores are expected and validate that adding dual-channel generation does not degrade content fidelity. The contribution is in Dialogue Naturalness, not Meaningfulness vs. Baseline.

- **"The dGSLM evaluation requires dividing by three because of length mismatch, introducing a scaling assumption"**: Removed. The paper explicitly states this normalization (line 312: "we divided the results... by three") and dGSLM is not the primary comparator — it serves as a textless reference point. This normalization is a reasonable heuristic for a secondary comparison.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most interesting insight is the effectiveness of conditioning on *next-utterance phonemes* for handling overlapping speech. Table 1 shows that removing next-sentence conditioning marginally improves standalone PER (12.79 vs 13.03) but the paper reports it "often generated unnatural or meaningless content as overlapping segment" in the dialogue setting. This suggests that next-utterance phoneme conditioning serves a qualitatively different purpose from main-utterance conditioning — it guides the model on *what to say* when speech overlaps, not on the current utterance's content. This separation of roles (current phonemes for content, next phonemes for overlap management) is a non-obvious design choice that the paper validates implicitly through the qualitative observation, though a more systematic evaluation of overlap-segment quality would strengthen this finding.

## Suggestions

- Clarify the backchannel detection methodology for generated dialogues. If the IPU classifier from Section 2 was used, report its performance on synthetic speech. If a different method was used (e.g., directly analyzing generated unit sequences), describe it explicitly.
- Add a brief walk-through of the TTM with a concrete example showing unit-level timelines for both channels in the overlap case during training and inference.
- Report precision/recall of the IPU classifier from Section 2 on a held-out set of manually labeled IPUs to support the data preparation pipeline.
- Consider including a simple pipeline baseline (backchannel predictor + two-channel TTS) even if it performs worse, to contextualize the relative contribution of end-to-end learning vs. the architecture itself.
