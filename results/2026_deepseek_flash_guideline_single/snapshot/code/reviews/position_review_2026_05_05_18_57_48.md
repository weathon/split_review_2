## Summary

The paper argues that LLMs' over-cautious health responses—overstating risk and creating alarming tones—are actively harmful to vulnerable users with OCD and health anxiety, not merely unhelpful. It coins "over-alignment" (by analogy to overfitting) to describe this phenomenon and illustrates it through OCD-Eval, a 225-query evaluation showing that 20+ models exhibit over-caution rates of 20–58% on negligible-risk queries while maintaining >90% emergency recognition. The position calls for new evaluation standards, more health professionals in alignment, and user education.

## Strengths

- **Novel reframing of over-cautiousness as a safety problem rather than a helpfulness problem.** Prior work on over-refusal (OR-Bench, etc.) frames drawbacks as reduced utility; this paper reframes overly cautious health responses as actively harmful to specific populations (lines 13–15). This is the paper's most distinctive conceptual contribution and opens a genuinely new dimension for alignment research.

- **Empirical demonstration via OCD-Eval with rigorous annotation.** The 225-query evaluation, sourced from authors with lived OCD experience and annotated by 7 GPs, shows over-caution rates of 20–58% across 20+ models. The inter-annotator agreement (Gwet's AC2 of 0.93; lines 221–222) and noise-bound analysis (OCR > 0.16 cannot be attributed to annotation noise; lines 227–229) ground claims in defensible methodology.

- **Identification of specific anxiety-amplifying response patterns.** The tag-based analysis identifies concrete failure modes—particularly "reassurance undermining" (Table 1, tag 4), where models acknowledge low risk but then hedge and list potential dangers—and "symptom checking" (tag 1), where telling users to "keep an eye on" symptoms communicates that something might go wrong. These observable, measurable patterns go beyond abstract calls for "less caution."

- **Strong rebuttal of the "vulnerable users shouldn't use LLMs" counterargument.** The paper concedes this is "technically correct" (line 306) but counters with compelling epidemiological data: OCD diagnosis delays of 5–12 years (lines 316–317), only 42.7% lifetime service use (lines 318–319), and <4% awareness that reassurance-seeking is disadvantageous (lines 314–315). This effectively shifts responsibility to system designers.

- **The "low vs. negligible" distinction for OCD users.** Section 4.1 notes that even labeling negligible-risk scenarios as "low risk" can heighten anxiety in OCD patients due to "intolerance of uncertainty, perfectionism, and overestimation of threat." This granular insight highlights a blind spot in current evaluation rubrics that treat these as functionally equivalent.

- **Productive distinction from HealthBench.** The paper correctly identifies that HealthBench focuses on emergency/non-emergency classification with neutral-tone prompts, missing the anxiety-reinforcement landscape that OCD-specific queries reveal (Section 2.3, lines 72–80).

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The error asymmetry counterargument receives moral condemnation rather than analytical rebuttal.** The most important objection—that under-cautious responses to genuine emergencies can be immediately fatal while over-cautious responses cause incremental psychological harm—is rebutted by calling it "inhuman and unfair to those who are vulnerable" (lines 345–346). While the paper partially supplements this with prevalence data (30–50% of health searchers have health anxiety; lines 351–354), it provides no analysis of relative harm magnitudes, no estimation of what fraction of queries involve genuine emergencies, and no consideration of whether calibration improvements could reduce both error types simultaneously. The paper's own Figure 2 actually contains evidence relevant to this counterargument—most models achieve >90% emergency recognition while varying widely on OCR—yet the paper never makes the explicit argument that the trade-off is not zero-sum. For a position paper whose evaluation criterion includes inviting productive disagreement, dismissing the strongest objection primarily on moral grounds forecloses the very discussion it should be opening. (Section 6, lines 343–358)

- **Unresolved tension with evidence-based OCD treatment.** The gold-standard treatment for OCD (Exposure and Response Prevention therapy) deliberately withholds reassurance—the patient learns to tolerate uncertainty without seeking external confirmation. The paper's recommended "calibrated response" (e.g., "it's highly unlikely you need a rabies shot" in Figure 1) is literally reassurance, which clinical practice considers counter-therapeutic. If the paper recommends accurate risk communication, this may function as reassurance for OCD patients and reinforce reassurance-seeking; if it recommends withholding reassurance (consistent with ERP), that response would likely feel under-cautious to patients. This clinical paradox is unaddressed and creates ambiguity about what the paper actually recommends: accurate risk assessment (which OCD patients may still seek repetitively as reassurance) or therapeutic intervention (recognizing OCD patterns and redirecting to professional help). These are very different recommendations with different risk profiles and implementation requirements. (Section 3, Figure 1, Section 5)

### Minor

- **The causal chain from model behavior to user harm is inferential rather than demonstrated.** The paper establishes that LLMs overstate risk but the step from "model says 'low' when GP says 'negligible'" to "increased user anxiety" to "reinforced OCD patterns" to "physical/psychological harm" is constructed through analogy with symptom checkers (Doherty-Torstrick et al., 2016) and general OCD epidemiology, not validated specifically for LLM interactions. The paper's title and thesis ("it is hurting vulnerable users") assert harm, while the evidence demonstrates existence of over-cautiousness. For a position paper, reasoning from analogy is legitimate, but the strength of the central claim ("hurting") exceeds what the inferential chain supports. (Section 3, lines 153–166; Section 4)

- **Metrics primarily operationalize only one of the paper's two stated traits of over-cautiousness.** The paper defines over-cautiousness through objective risk overstatement and subjective alarming tone (lines 18–19), but OCR/SOCR measure only objective risk overstatement. The tag analysis partially captures subjective mechanisms (reassurance undermining, symptom checking), but these are categorical frequencies rather than calibrated metrics. A model could achieve zero OCR while still producing subjectively alarming responses through hedging or tone, and a one-level OCR violation (saying "low" instead of "negligible") may reflect clinically reasonable language rather than anxiety-amplifying communication. (Section 4.1, metrics definition)

- **Solutions section does not address the core hard problem of dual-user calibration.** The three proposals (industrial standards, more professionals in alignment, user education) are reasonable but generic. None addresses how to calibrate responses differently for OCD/anxiety users without reducing caution for general users, or how to detect when a user has these conditions. The "User and Public Education" section briefly suggests detecting "repeated low-risk health queries" (lines 293–295) as a signal, but does not discuss the error profile or implementation challenges. (Section 5)

### Trivial

- **The "over-alignment" analogy to overfitting is imprecise but explicitly flagged as analogy.** Overfitting involves learning noise; the paper describes faithful optimization of a mis-specified objective. The paper says "in analogy to overfitting" (line 21), acknowledging the metaphorical relationship rather than asserting identity. Readers may find "mis-specified safety alignment" more precise, but the analogy serves its rhetorical purpose.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A user study (even small-scale, 20–30 participants with clinical or subclinical health anxiety reading over-cautious vs. calibrated responses) would dramatically strengthen the paper's central harm claim, though it is not standard to demand empirical user validation for a position paper's argument.

- Concrete specification of what a calibrated response looks like for the non-trivial OCD queries in OCD-Eval (beyond the trivial rabies example in Figure 1) would help operationalize the paper's recommendations.

- Explicit argumentation from Figure 2 that high emergency recognition and high OCR can coexist—suggesting the error-asymmetry trade-off is not necessarily zero-sum—would strengthen the error asymmetry rebuttal and turn the paper's own data into an analytical (rather than purely moral) response.

However, this is a position paper whose primary method is reasoning and argumentation. Requesting the same standard of empirical validation as a medical research paper (user studies demonstrating harm) is scope creep into standard research criteria. Moved to Nice-to-Have.

The paper explicitly says "in analogy to overfitting" (line 21). This is a presentation precision issue, not a substantive flaw. Moved to Trivial.

- **The required risk-rating prompt constraint is "unusual."** The critic flags that requiring models to append a final risk rating changes natural output distribution. This is a methodological choice that enables OCR/SOCR computation—it can be debated but does not undermine the position. Not a substantive weakness for a position paper.

- **Kantian argument is "generic."** The critic notes the Kantian argument could apply to any cautious design decision. This is a fair observation about the argument's specificity but doesn't undermine the core position. Omitted as minor quibble.

- **"Anecdotal observations" about LLMs treating papers as gold standard are "not systematic."** The paper itself presents these as anecdotal (line 110). Flagging a limitation the authors acknowledge isn't adding a new weakness.

## Novel Insights

The OCD treatment paradox identified by the harsh reviewer—where ERP therapy withholds reassurance while the paper recommends "calibrated" responses that function as reassurance—is a genuinely novel and productive tension that neither the paper nor the mental health ML literature appears to have addressed. Resolving whether LLMs should provide accurate risk communication (which may reinforce reassurance-seeking) or therapeutic-aware responses (which may feel dismissive or under-cautious) would advance both the alignment and clinical communities. Additionally, the observation that reasoning mode increases OCR for some models (GPT-5.5:thinking's OCR rises from 0.47 to 0.58) suggests the problem may worsen with reasoning capabilities—a counterintuitive finding that deserves attention from the reasoning-model research community.
