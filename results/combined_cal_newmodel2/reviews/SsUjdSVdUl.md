## Summary

This paper proposes Critique-RL, a two-stage reinforcement learning approach for training critique language models (models that assess and provide feedback on other models' outputs) without relying on stronger supervisors. The key insight is that indirect reward signals from actor refinement alone fail to optimize the critic's discriminability (ability to judge correctness), leading to conservative or aggressive critique behavior. Stage I optimizes discriminability via direct reward signals, and Stage II optimizes helpfulness while maintaining discriminability through regularization. The method is evaluated on MATH, GSM8K, and AQuA (in-domain) and SVAMP and TheoremQA (out-of-domain) with Qwen2.5-3B and 7B, consistently outperforming baselines including SFT, STaR, Retroformer, and CTRL.

## Strengths

- **The diagnosis of the failure mode of indirect reward signals is well-supported.** Section 4.1 and Figure 3 provide a clear empirical decomposition: optimizing only on refinement-correctness rewards (r_refine, r_Δ, r_correction) leads to either conservative or aggressive critique behavior because discriminability is not explicitly optimized. This analysis isolates the mechanism rather than just providing motivating observations.

- **The two-stage design cleanly addresses the diagnosed problem.** Stage I (optimize discriminability via r_dis directly) and Stage II (optimize helpfulness via r_refine while regularizing toward Stage I's discriminability via KL and retaining r_dis) are a natural decomposition of the identified challenge. The ablation in Table 3 confirms that both stages contribute and that removing discrimination-related regularization in Stage II causes a drop — exactly what the paper's thesis predicts.

- **The main results are consistent and nontrivial across MATH, GSM8K, and AQuA for both Qwen2.5-3B and 7B.** Critique-RL outperforms all baselines on Acc@Refine, Δ, and Acc@Dis with substantial margins on MATH and GSM8K (e.g., +4.54 accuracy points over CTRL on MATH with 7B, +6.36 discrimination points on GSM8K with 7B).

- **The study generalizes beyond in-domain tasks.** Table 4 shows OOD gains on SVAMP and TheoremQA that, while modest in absolute terms on TheoremQA, are consistent across both model sizes and evaluation metrics.

- **The paper does not assume stronger supervision.** The SFT critique data is generated by Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (not GPT-4o), and RL training uses ground-truth answer as the oracle signal, which is a standard and self-sufficient setup without requiring a larger model's annotations.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **The comparison to prior RL baselines is confounded by different RL algorithms.** Retroformer uses PPO, CTRL uses GRPO, and Critique-RL uses RLOO. The paper attributes the performance advantage entirely to its two-stage reward design, but the headline comparison (Table 1) mixes differences in both reward structure AND RL algorithm. While the ablations in Table 3 provide internal validity for the two-stage design (varying rewards within RLOO), the external comparison to Retroformer/CTRL is weakened. The paper should either re-implement baselines with the same RL algorithm, or explicitly acknowledge and bound this confound. The core contribution survives this issue, but it limits the conclusiveness of the "our method beats existing RL methods" claim.

### Minor

- **No variance or statistical significance is reported.** Every numerical result (Tables 1, 2, 3, 4) is a single number with no standard deviation, confidence interval, or indication of multiple seeds. While evaluation uses temperature 0 (deterministic), the RL training is stochastic, and metrics like Pass@10 involve sampling. This is especially problematic for small-margin comparisons (AQuA 7B: +0.79 over CTRL; TheoremQA 7B: +0.3 over CTRL in accuracy) where readers cannot judge whether the differences are meaningful.

- **On AQuA with Qwen2.5-7B, the results are substantially weaker than the paper's narrative suggests.** Critique-RL's Δ is only +2.36 (vs +12.66 on MATH and +12.05 on GSM8K), and the improvement over CTRL is just +0.79 accuracy points. SFT and STaR actually hurt performance on this dataset (negative Δ). The paper's claim of "significant positive improvements" is overstated for this setting, and no analysis is offered for why AQuA differs qualitatively from MATH and GSM8K.

- **The compute-efficiency claim in §6/Figure 1 is undersupported.** The paper states that K critique-refinement samples are more compute-efficient than 3K direct samples, but provides no token-cost analysis. A critique-refinement cycle generates a critique AND a refinement response, roughly doubling the token cost per sample. The paper should clarify whether the efficiency claim is about sample efficiency, token efficiency, or both.

- **The function f(·) that extracts the critic's correctness judgment from natural language critique text is not described.** The paper defines r_dis(x,y,c) = 𝟙(f(x,y,c) = r_oracle(x,y)) but does not explain how f operates — whether via regex parsing, prompt-based extraction, or another method. This is needed for reproducibility.

- **The motivating analysis (Section 4.1, Figure 3) is conducted only on GSM8K with Qwen2.5-3B.** While the method is later validated across settings, the paper's core diagnosis — that indirect RL signals fail to optimize discriminability — is derived from a single dataset/model configuration. This limitation should be explicitly noted.

### Trivial

- The abstract's claim of "a 9.02% gain" lacks explicit specification of the baseline (relative to what? averaged how?). The framing reads more impressively than the fine-grained per-dataset numbers.
