## Summary

This paper introduces SpatialBoost, a framework that enhances pre-trained vision encoders' spatial awareness by injecting 3D spatial knowledge expressed in linguistic form. It uses a multi-turn Chain-of-Thought reasoning dataset (pixel→object→scene level), constructed by applying off-the-shelf models (Depth Pro, SAM, VGGT) to generate QA pairs, then fine-tunes the vision encoder via an LLM with a dual-channel attention mechanism to preserve pre-trained knowledge. The method is evaluated on depth estimation, semantic segmentation, 3D scene understanding, robot control, image classification, and image retrieval across four backbone architectures (OpenCLIP, SigLIPv2, DINOv2, DINOv3).

## Strengths

- **Broad and systematic evaluation across multiple task families** (Tables 1–5 covering depth, segmentation, 3D tasks, robot learning, classification, retrieval). This breadth is rare and strengthens the case that the method improves representations generally, not just on one narrow benchmark.

- **Consistent improvements across four different backbone architectures** (OpenCLIP, SigLIPv2, DINOv2, DINOv3) across nearly every task. Gains hold for both language-aligned encoders and SSL encoders, suggesting the framework is architecture-agnostic.

- **Meaningful gains on tasks where spatial knowledge is not directly injected** — ImageNet linear probing improves from 88.4% to 90.2% (DINOv3), and robot learning on CortexBench improves from 72.8 to 80.8 average (DINOv3). These tasks were not sources of training data, so the improvements represent genuine positive transfer.

- **Ablation studies that isolate key design choices** (Table 7: multi-turn order, single-vs-multi-view data; Table 8: simple post-training vs SpatialBoost). The forward hierarchical ordering outperforming shuffled/reversed orders is a genuinely informative and non-obvious result.

- **Dataset scalability analysis** (Figure 5) showing consistent improvements with increased data size, supporting the method's practical potential for scaling.

## Weaknesses

### Major

**1. ScanNet data overlap between training and evaluation (Table 3).** The multi-view training data (used in both Stage 2 alignment and Stage 3 spatial reasoning) includes ScanNet scenes (Dai et al., 2017, cited on line 162), and the 3D-centric evaluation in Table 3 is explicitly performed on ScanNet scenes (line 134). While the training and evaluation tasks have different formats (VQA vs. task-specific benchmarks), the visual domain, scenes, and objects are shared. The paper does not disclose whether evaluation scenes are held out from training, nor does it acknowledge this overlap. This is particularly relevant because the largest relative gains in the paper appear in Table 3 (e.g., SigLIPv2 3D semantic segmentation mIoU: 9.2 → 55.5; OpenCLIP: 6.9 → 54.9). The paper would be stronger if it either confirmed that scenes are held out or re-evaluated on a different 3D dataset.

**2. Distillation-circularity in the evaluation framing.** The spatial reasoning training data is generated by running task-specific models (Depth Pro, SAM, VGGT) on images and converting their outputs into QA pairs. The vision encoder is then evaluated on the same task families (depth estimation, segmentation, 3D understanding). This is effectively a knowledge-distillation pipeline, yet the paper frames the contribution as "enhancing spatial awareness" without discussing the circular relationship between training data sources and evaluation tasks. The improvements on tasks not directly corresponding to the teacher models (robot learning, image classification) partially mitigate this concern, but the framing should be explicitly acknowledged.

### Minor

**3. Table 6 (LLM vs. pixel-level supervision) comparison confounds multiple factors.** The LLM-based fine-tuning uses the full 3-stage pipeline (feature alignment + visual instruction tuning + spatial fine-tuning), while the pixel-level alternatives skip Stages 1–2. When the LLM method outperforms alternatives, it is unclear whether the gain comes from language-based supervision specifically or from having alignment stages plus more training. A controlled comparison where all methods receive the same number of training stages would better isolate the effect.

**4. Dual-channel attention claim is slightly overstated (Figure 6).** The paper states that dual-channel attention "uniquely preserves and even enhances pre-trained knowledge, while other approaches cause degradation." On segmentation (ADE20K), full fine-tuning achieves 49.4% mIoU vs. dual-channel's 49.2% — both improve over the pretrained baseline (47.7%). The claim of "degradation" holds on classification (79.5% full-FT vs. 86.3% pretrained) but not on segmentation, where full-FT also enhances. The overall conclusion remains valid, but the wording is imprecise.

**5. The 'Simple FT' baseline (Table 8) is underspecified.** The paper describes it as fine-tuning with "original pre-training objectives" without specifying what this means concretely for each encoder type (e.g., contrastive objectives for OpenCLIP/SigLIPv2 require paired image-text data, making it unclear how this is applied to the spatial VQA dataset). The minimal changes and occasional degradation could partly reflect an ill-defined training setup rather than a fair comparison.

**6. No limitations section or failure case analysis.** Given the breadth of claimed improvements and potential concerns about data overlap and distillation circularity, the absence of any critical self-assessment is a notable omission.
