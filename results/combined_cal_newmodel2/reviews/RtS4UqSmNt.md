## Summary
2-3 sentence summary of the paper's contribution.

The paper introduces a formal model of controlled sequential social learning where an information-mediating planner (e.g., an LLM) dynamically chooses the precision of private signals for a sequence of agents who also learn from observing predecessors' actions. The authors prove convexity of the altruistic planner's value function and fully characterize optimal policies for both altruistic and biased planners across belief regimes. LLM-based simulations are presented as empirical support for the theoretical predictions.

## Strengths

- **A genuinely novel model that fills a clear gap (Section 3).** The paper introduces the first formal model that dynamically combines a planner's control over information precision with sequential social learning among agents. Prior work either assumes two-way communication (Wei & Anastopoulos 2022), directly alters agents' choice rules (Smith et al. 2021), or fixes the information structure ex ante for the entire sequence (Arieli et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2025).

- **Convexity of the altruistic value function (Theorem 2).** The paper identifies this as a nontrivial result — the public-belief-dependent action rule breaks the linearity that would otherwise make convexity automatic. The authors stake a clear claim about the proof's difficulty, and if the proof holds up, this is a genuine technical contribution.

- **Complete characterization of optimal policies for both planner types (Theorems 3 and 5).** The paper specifies the exact form of the optimal policy across different belief regimes: three distinct phases for the altruistic case and up to five phases for the biased case, including regions where the optimal policy does not exist (handled honestly via ε-optimal policies).

- **Internal theoretical coherence.** The model is cleanly specified (Section 3), the myopic cases are solved first (Theorems 1, 4) as a warm-up, the non-myopic cases build on them, and the convexity result (Theorem 2) is used to justify the structure of the altruistic optimal policy (Theorem 3). The Markov property, public belief as sufficient statistic, and action rule all connect consistently.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **LLM model identity and experimental parameters are absent from the main text (Section 6).** The paper uses "LLM" generically and never specifies which model was used (e.g., GPT-4, Claude, Llama), API provider, deployment method, temperature setting, number of simulation runs, or seed values. This is a basic reproducibility failure for an ICLR paper that prominently features "LLM" in its title and lists "Empirical Validation and Strategic Analysis Using LLMs" as Contribution 3. No quantitative results tables appear in the main text; all claims are conveyed through figure descriptions. The claim that "deviation is less than 10% for the majority of belief states" (line 242) is presented with no definition of the deviation metric, sample size, error bars, or statistical testing. The 40-50% welfare decrease claim (line 252) similarly lacks variance information or parameter conditions. While some details may reside in the (stripped) appendix, the model identity and basic run parameters belong in the main text.

- **The claim that the LLM planner "accounts for" non-Bayesian agents is over-interpreted given the experimental design (Sections 6.2-6.3).** The paper states that LLM planners deviate from the Bayesian-optimal policy "in ways which account for the non-Bayesian nature of the LLM agents they face" (line 218) and that differences "are best understood as the planner's strategic adaptations to the specific non-Bayesian behaviors" (line 244). However, these deviations (avoiding extreme precisions, gradual tapering) are equally consistent with the LLM planner exhibiting its own cognitive biases — a possibility the paper acknowledges as "central tendency bias" (line 245) but does not control for. The hybrid comparison (optimal policy + LLM agents vs. LLM planner + LLM agents) partially addresses this, but does not distinguish between genuine strategic adaptation and shared biases producing superficially compatible behavior.

### Minor

- **The operationalization of "signal precision" in the LLM setting is underspecified (Section 6).** In the theoretical model, precision q_i has a precise statistical meaning: P(s_i = ω) = q_i. In the experiments, the oracle "generates a private signal of desired precision." The main text does not explain how a natural-language text signal generated by an LLM can have a controlled information-theoretic precision, nor how LLM agents' non-Bayesian belief updates affect the effective precision reaching them. The paper references Appendix E, but a conceptual explanation in the main text is needed.

- **The deviation metric is not defined (Section 6, line 242).** The paper states "deviation is less than 10% for the majority of belief states" without specifying what this measures: percentage deviation of what from what? The precision range [0.5, 1]? The optimal precision value? Over how many belief states? What is the tail behavior?

- **No proof sketch of Theorem 2 in the main text.** The paper acknowledges the proof "is quite involved and may be of independent interest" (line 139), but provides no sketch of why usual arguments fail or how the proof proceeds. Given the theorem's centrality, a sketch would substantially aid reader confidence.

- **The cognitive bias measurements (NB1-NB3, Figure 1b) lack methodological detail (Section 6.1).** It is unclear how "LLM belief" is measured — is it a numeric output the model was prompted to produce? What is the variance across different phrasings of the belief elicitation prompt? Are these single responses or averaged over multiple runs?
