## Summary

This paper proposes a benchmark for Taxonomy Image Generation — evaluating how well text-to-image models can generate images for WordNet synset concepts. The benchmark includes 9 metrics (taxonomy-specific similarity scores, preference-based ELO with human and GPT-4 judges, FID, IS, and a reward model), a test dataset drawn from WordNet, and a comparative evaluation of 12 T2I models. The authors release the full WordNet-3.0 set of images generated by the best-performing model. Playground-v2 and FLUX are found to be the top performers across most metrics.

## Strengths

- **Practical motivation and underexplored task.** Generating images for taxonomy concepts (WordNet synsets) addresses a real gap — only 6.5% of WordNet synsets have visual coverage in ImageNet. Section 1 and Figure 1 provide a reasonable motivation by showing how prompting for taxonomic concepts differs from typical T2I prompts.

- **Comprehensive model coverage.** The benchmark evaluates 12 publicly available models spanning diverse architectures (U-Net, DiT, retrieval) and scales (123M to 12B parameters), broader than prior taxonomy-specific image generation work (Baryshnikov & Ryabinin 2023).

- **Human evaluation with meaningful effort.** The human ELO evaluation includes 3,370 pairwise comparisons with 4 annotators and reports inter-annotator agreement (Spearman ρ = 0.8, p ≤ 0.05), providing credible ground truth for preference-based metrics.

- **High correlation between human and GPT-4 rankings.** A Spearman correlation of 0.88–0.92 (p ≤ 0.05) between model rankings from humans and GPT-4 is genuinely high and suggests the GPT-4 preference signal is useful at the ranking level.

- **Dataset release.** Publishing the full WordNet-3.0 coverage from the best-performing model extends ImageNet and is a practically useful community resource.

- **Taxonomy-specific similarity metrics with validation.** The paper proposes Lemma, Hypernym, and Cohyponym Similarity metrics grounded in a probabilistic framing (KL Divergence, Mutual Information), going beyond the existing ISP/SCS metrics. Their reported correlation with human rankings (ρ ≈ 0.91 for Hypernym, ρ ≈ 0.87 for Cohyponym) supports their validity.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Central claim asserted without evidence.** The abstract and introduction claim that *"the ranking of models differs significantly from standard T2I tasks."* This is presented as a marquee finding that justifies the benchmark's existence. However, the paper never provides this comparison. It cites Jiang et al. (2024a) (GenAI Arena) but does not reproduce or place that ranking side-by-side with the taxonomy ranking. Without this evidence, the paper's core narrative is an unsupported assertion. The authors should either provide the explicit comparison or remove the claim.

- **Overclaimed novelty across multiple dimensions.** Three specific claims overstate originality: (a) *"pioneer the use of pairwise evaluation with GPT-4 feedback for image generation"* — the paper's own related work cites Chen et al. (2024a) (MLLM-as-a-Judge) and Cui et al. (2024) which already use GPT-4 for pairwise T2I evaluation. (b) *"first to evaluate…to generate images for WordNet concepts"* — Baryshnikov & Ryabinin (2023), cited in the paper, already evaluated T2I models on WordNet using ISP and SCS metrics. (c) *"9 novel taxonomy-related text-to-image metrics"* — the three similarity metrics are CLIP cosine similarities with different pooling strategies over taxonomy relations, while ELO, Reward Model, FID, and IS are standard off-the-shelf metrics. The metrics are useful *adaptations* but calling them "novel metrics" is a stretch. Individually each point is minor; collectively they present the paper as more original than it is.

- **Retrieval baseline is underspecified and the comparison is not credible.** The sole retrieval baseline (Wikimedia Commons) is described with no methodological detail: what query was issued (raw concept name, WordNet definition, CLIP embedding)? How many candidates were considered? Without this, results from this baseline are not reproducible. Figure 2 cherry-picks a single pathological retrieval failure (a Buddha statue for "cigar lighter") to argue that *"the generation approach is significantly superior to the retrieval approach."* One example does not constitute evidence. A proper comparison would use a well-specified retrieval system (e.g., CLIP-based search over a large corpus) and report aggregate statistics. The paper would be better served by either strengthening this baseline substantially or removing it.

### Minor

- **FID reference distribution is unclear.** The paper states *"we calculate FID based on retrieved images, meaning that in this specific setting, FID reflects the 'realness' or closeness to retrieval rather than the semantic correctness of an image"* (Section 4.3). If "based on retrieved images" means the reference distribution is the set of retrieved images, then the retrieval model's outputs are drawn from that same distribution, giving it an inherent FID advantage. Yet the retrieval model never wins on FID (SD1.5 and FLUX dominate). This suggests the computation differs from what the text describes and needs clarification.

- **Internal inconsistency in a key number.** The Spearman correlation between human and GPT-4 ELO rankings is reported as **0.92** in the Figure 4 caption (line 193) and **0.88** in Section 5 (line 253), both claiming p ≤ 0.05. Since this correlation is central to validating the GPT-4 evaluation signal, the discrepancy must be resolved.

- **Sampling description for the Random Split dataset appears inconsistent.** The test set selection probabilities are stated as: Hypernymy at 1e-5, Hyponymy at 0.05, Synset Mixing at 0.1. Yet the resulting test set contains 828/1202 (69%) Hypernymy nodes, 204 Hyponymy, and 170 Mixing — heavily dominated by Hypernymy, which should be nearly absent given its 1e-5 probability. The description needs clarification.

- **The Reward Model metric shows Playground as the top model for every single subset and condition** (Table 2). While the paper reports a 0.79 correlation with human evaluation, this across-the-board uniformity is unusual and warrants investigation into whether the reward model has a systematic bias toward Playground, possibly from training data overlap.
