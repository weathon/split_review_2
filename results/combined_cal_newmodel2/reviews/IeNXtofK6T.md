Let me now write the final review.## Summary

This paper revisits the idea of using an off-the-shelf LLM to detect and remove prompt injections — an approach previously dismissed as ineffective based on older, weaker models. The authors propose PromptArmor, which prompts a strong LLM (GPT-4o, GPT-4.1) to identify injected prompts and then removes them via fuzzy matching. Evaluated on AgentDojo, Open Prompt Injection, and TensorTrust, PromptArmor achieves FPR and FNR below 1% on AgentDojo with GPT-4 class models, reducing ASR from 54.53% to below 1%. The paper is scoped as establishing a strong baseline, not as a novel algorithmic contribution, and on those terms it largely delivers.

## Strengths

- **Clean, decisive results on AgentDojo (Table 1).** GPT-4o achieves 0.07% FPR and 0.23% FNR; GPT-4.1 achieves 0.56% FPR and 0.13% FNR — both below 1%. These are unambiguous and make the paper's central point effectively.
- **Well-designed Qwen3 ablation on model size and reasoning (Section 4.4, Figure 3).** Using the Qwen3 family (reasoning/non-reasoning modes at identical model sizes) cleanly isolates the effect of reasoning capability from model scale. Shows Qwen3-0.6B cannot resolve the security-utility tradeoff regardless of reasoning mode, while Qwen3-32B approaches GPT-4.1's performance.
- **Honest presentation of weaker-model results.** The paper does not hide that GPT-3.5 underperforms (11.24% FPR, 15.74% FNR on AgentDojo) or that Qwen3-0.6B fails in either direction. Including these negative results strengthens the thesis.
- **Memorization test (Section 4.5).** Shows GPT-4.1 is unlikely to have memorized AgentDojo benchmark data (average similarity 0.34, only 3.5% above threshold), addressing a natural concern about data leakage.
- **Clearly written and well-organized.** The paper's scope — establishing a baseline rather than proposing a novel algorithm — is well-defined and the paper delivers on its own terms.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Adaptive attack evaluation (Section 4.6) does not adequately support the claimed robustness.** The adaptive attacks generated by AgentVigil against the defended system achieve only 21.46% ASR against the *undefended* system, compared to 52.73% for the non-adaptive attacks (Table 4). This means the adaptive search produced substantially *weaker* attacks, not stronger ones adapted to bypass the defense. The paper scopes its claim to "fuzzing-based adaptive attacks" (line 288), which is appropriate, but the evidence as presented does not demonstrate that PromptArmor is robust against genuinely strong adaptive attacks — it only shows robustness against a specific automated fuzzing procedure that appears to have converged to weaker attack variants. This is the paper's most significant weakness and limits the strength of its conclusions about adaptive robustness.

### Minor

- **No comparison of GPT-4.1 with a naive/trivial prompt against the engineered PromptArmor prompt.** Since most baselines (Deberta, Llama Prompt Guard 2, DataSentinel) use much smaller models (Mistral-7B etc.), the reader cannot attribute the gains to PromptArmor's prompt design vs. GPT-4.1's raw capability. The paper partially addresses this by testing GPT-3.5 with/without a definition of "prompt injection" (Section 4.3), but testing GPT-4.1 with a deliberately naive prompt would be needed to separate these factors cleanly.
- **All results reported as single point estimates with no variance information (Tables 1–4).** Even at temperature 0, LLM outputs can exhibit system-level nondeterminism. Bootstrapped confidence intervals or multiple-run estimates would strengthen the empirical contribution, especially for small differences (e.g., GPT-4o's 0.07% FPR vs. GPT-4.1's 0.56% FPR).
- **The detection prompt is adjusted per dataset (line 199) without disclosure of the extent of tuning.** It is unclear whether a single prompt would generalize across benchmarks or how much effort was required to adapt the prompt. This raises questions about generalizability to new, unseen distributions.
- **No error analysis of false positives and false negatives.** With FPR and FNR below 1% on AgentDojo, these likely correspond to only a handful of samples. Characterizing these failures would help users understand when the approach is likely to fail.
