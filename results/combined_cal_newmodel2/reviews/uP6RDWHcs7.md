## Summary

Marginal Flow proposes a density estimation framework where the model is defined as a Monte Carlo mixture of parametric distributions $q(x|w)$, with component parameters $w$ generated by pushing noise through an unconstrained neural network $f_\theta$ and resampled at each training iteration. This resampling prevents collapse to a finite GMM and decouples effective capacity from the number of components. The method provides exact density evaluation (for given component samples), orders-of-magnitude faster runtime than competing models, and supports flexible choices of $q(x|w)$, manifold learning, and diverse training objectives.

## Strengths

- **Clear and well-motivated idea (Section 2.1, Figure 1):** The marginalization-via-resampling mechanism elegantly prevents collapse to a finite GMM and decouples effective capacity from the number of components $N_c$. The visual comparison in Figure 1 makes this insight concrete. **[favorability=12.08]**

- **Genuinely impressive runtime advantage (Figure 3, Section 2.2):** Marginal Flow is orders of magnitude faster than NF, FM, and FFF for both sampling and exact density evaluation across dimensions $d=10^2$ to $10^5$. At $d=10^4$ the gap is roughly two orders of magnitude, which directly follows from the framework's design (no Jacobians, no ODEs). **[favorability=15.83]**

- **Convergence speed on synthetic data (Figure 7):** Marginal Flow reaches good test log-likelihood substantially faster in wall-clock time than NF, FM, and FFF across all five synthetic datasets, combining its per-iteration speed advantage with sample efficiency. **[favorability=12.65]**

- **Framework flexibility is demonstrated across diverse tasks:** The Wishart experiment (Section 4.3) substitutes $q(x|w)$ with Wishart for positive-definite matrices with no framework changes. Reverse-KL training (Section 4.1) is efficient because both sampling and density evaluation are fast. Manifold learning (Figure 4) follows naturally from choosing $m < d$. **[favorability=9.12]**

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **No evidence of high-dimensional density estimation without strong manifold structure:** All synthetic experiments are 2D; the Wishart $d=55/5050$ experiments operate on a 1D manifold (Eq. 4 defines the target on a 1D curve); the image experiments use a VAE latent space with a learned 1D manifold. The paper claims a general density estimation framework but provides no experiment where the data genuinely occupies a high-dimensional space ($d \geq 50$) without being confined to a low-dimensional manifold. This is the regime where mixture-based approaches classically struggle, and the paper does not address it. Adding an experiment on standard tabular density estimation benchmarks (e.g., UCI datasets POWER, GAS, HEPMASS) would directly test this concern.

- **Missing quantitative evaluation on image manifold experiments (Section 4.4):** The MNIST and JAFFE experiments are entirely qualitative — interpolation grids with no log-likelihood, FID, reconstruction error, or comparison to any baseline. The paper acknowledges JAFFE has only 214 images, making quantitative evaluation particularly important to distinguish meaningful learning from overfitting.

- **$N_c$ (number of Monte Carlo components) is never stated for experiments:** $N_c$ is a central parameter of the model (Eq. 2 directly defines the density as a mixture of $N_c$ components). Its value determines both computational cost and the variance of the density estimator. Without reporting $N_c$, the experimental results cannot be reproduced or properly interpreted. This should be straightforward to add.

### Minor

- **The model's density estimate is stochastic but this is not discussed:** For evaluation at test time, different random seeds yield different $q_\theta(x)$ values because the $N_c$ parameter draws are resampled. This is a material difference from NF's deterministic exact likelihood. The "exact likelihood" checkmark in Table 1 is technically correct for a given $\{w_i\}$ draw but could mislead readers into thinking the estimate is deterministic. The paper should qualify this distinction.

- **No sensitivity analysis for key parameters:** The paper does not study how performance depends on $N_c$ (number of components), $m$ (latent dimension of base distribution), or the architecture of $f_\theta$. Questions such as what happens when $m$ exceeds the true manifold dimension, or when $N_c$ is too small, are not addressed.

- **Universality claim is stated without conditions:** The paper states "the resulting marginal $q(x)$ is universal for many families of distributions $q(x|w)$, e.g. if $q(x|w)$ is a kernel (Micchelli et al., 2006)" but does not prove or specify the conditions under which this holds for the specific Monte Carlo construction with finite $N_c$.
