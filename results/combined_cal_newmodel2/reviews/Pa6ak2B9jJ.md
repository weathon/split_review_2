## Summary

The paper proposes AUTO-RT, an RL-based framework for automatic jailbreak strategy exploration in LLM red-teaming. It introduces a hierarchical decomposition of attacks into high-level strategies (generated by $AM^g$) and instantiated queries (generated by $AM^r$), along with two technical components: Dynamic Strategy Pruning (DSP) for early termination of redundant exploration branches, and Progressive Reward Tracking (PRT) that uses downgrade models and a First Inverse Rate (FIR) metric to alleviate reward sparsity.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem framing (Sections 1, 2.2).** The paper draws a clear distinction between exploitability and severity in red-teaming — a conceptual point that most prior work conflates. The hierarchical decomposition into strategy generation and query instantiation is a genuinely different way to think about the problem, and the CMDP formulation in Equation 2 is more principled than flat prompt-generation approaches for covering the attack space.

- **Broad experimental scope on the white-box axis (Table 1).** The paper evaluates across 16 white-box LLMs spanning multiple families (Llama, Mistral, Yi, Gemma, Qwen, R2D2). This is substantially more comprehensive than most papers in this area and supports the claim that the method generalizes across model families.

- **Clean ablation structure (Table 2).** The ablation cleanly separates DSP and PRT and reports results on 10 models across all three metrics. Both components contribute positively relative to the RL baseline, providing clear evidence for the value of each technique.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **The main evaluation is staged against weak baselines, and the headline improvement claim is untraceable.** Table 1 compares AUTO-RT only against three self-constructed baselines (Few-Shot, Imitation Learning, plain RL). When existing methods are included (Table 3, against AutoDAN), AUTO-RT's primary metric ASR_st is 38.38% vs. AutoDAN's 55.23%. The paper categorizes AutoDAN as a "human-based approach" (even though it uses genetic algorithms), which distances the method from its strongest competitor. The abstract claims AUTO-RT "significantly improves success rates (by up to 16.63%)" — but the string "16.63" appears only in the abstract and introduction and is not traceable to any specific result in any table. This combination of weak baseline selection and an unsupported numerical claim undermines the paper's central empirical assertion.

2. **The SeD (semantic diversity) value for AUTO-RT is missing from Table 3 with no explanation.** The table reports SeD for AutoDAN (0.86), Human Template (0.36), and Past-Tense (0.52), but the AUTO-RT column is blank. The paper does not acknowledge or discuss this omission, preventing a complete diversity comparison against these baselines.

3. **The DeD (Defense Generalization Diversity) metric is critically underspecified.** The paper states it involves "constructing defenses based on the successful attacks" (Section 3.1) but provides no details about what defense is constructed, how it is trained, or what data it uses. This creates a confound: a method with lower first-round ASR produces fewer successful attacks to build defenses from, resulting in a weaker second-round defense and potentially inflating its DeD score. Since AUTO-RT has lower first-round ASR than AutoDAN (38.38% vs. 55.23%), this confound could explain its higher DeD (38.19% vs. 17.88%) rather than genuine diversity. Without describing the defense construction, DeD cannot be interpreted as a clean measure of diversity.

### Minor

1. **FIR selection protocol lacks a practical criterion.** The paper selects "the last model before a sharp increase of FIR" but provides no criterion for what constitutes a "sharp increase." The selection appears qualitative and validated post-hoc, weakening the practical value of FIR as a design tool.

2. **No statistical significance or variance reporting for Table 1.** Given that some margins are very close (e.g., Gemma 2 9b: RL=44.85 vs. AUTO-RT=44.80), the reader cannot assess whether differences are meaningful.

3. **R2D2 failure case is dismissed.** AUTO-RT's ASR_st of 12.45% on R2D2 is below both Few-Shot (27.18%) and Imitation Learning (24.24%). The paper dismisses this in one sentence as "highlight[ing] the robustness of R2D2's defense mechanism" without substantive analysis.

4. **Efficiency comparison (Figure 3) only against RL.** The efficiency violin plots compare AUTO-RT against RL only — the weakest-performing baseline from Table 1 — limiting informativeness.

5. **Characterization of prior work as "fixed templates" is overstated.** The abstract and introduction describe prior methods (AutoDAN, PAIR) as relying on "fixed attack templates," though AutoDAN uses genetic algorithms and PAIR uses iterative LLM-based refinement. The Related Work section provides more accurate descriptions, creating a tension with the introduction's framing.

6. **Cost of constructing downgrade models not discussed.** The paper generates up to 6 intermediate models per target model for 16 models, which is a substantial computational burden that is not acknowledged as a limitation.
