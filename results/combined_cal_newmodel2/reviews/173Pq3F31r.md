## Summary

This paper proposes a unified framework for expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) that jointly models these inverse tasks using disentangled score content and performance style representations. The framework uses a transformer Seq2Seq architecture trained without note-level alignment, plus a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. Experiments include objective metrics on the ASAP dataset, subjective listening tests, and style transfer evaluations.

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated joint modeling approach.** The paper clearly articulates why EPR and APT are inverse tasks (Section 1, Figure 1) and should benefit from joint modeling, drawing a valid analogy to ASR/TTS in speech. This is a genuine gap in MIR literature.

- **Convincing disentanglement evidence.** Table 4 shows a stark gap between Style→Comp (77.46%) and Cont→Comp (29.99%), and an even wider gap for performer identification (42.07% vs 9.94%), providing genuine evidence that the learned representations separate style from content. Visualizations in Figure 3 corroborate this.

- **Competitive APT results.** On the ASAP benchmark (Table 1), the model achieves the best or second-best on multiple MUSTER and ScoreSimilarity sub-metrics including E_onset (16.26), E_offset (27.30), E_dur (51.75), and E_pitch (6.24), with many improvements being statistically significant. This demonstrates that the joint framework does not harm — and in some areas improves — transcription quality.

- **PSR module addresses a practical need.** Existing EPR systems require manual control of expressive parameters or explicit composer/performer labels (Section 2.1). Automating style selection from score content alone addresses a real usability limitation, and the DDPM formulation is a reasonable technical choice.

- **Well-structured subjective evaluation.** Using 11 musically trained participants across multiple composers and rating multiple dimensions (dynamics, tempo, style, overall) goes beyond n-gram metrics. The style-transfer listening test (Figure 5) directly targets the disentanglement claim.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **'Ours (Target)' is an oracle condition presented as a primary result.** The `Ours (Target)` variant uses the ground-truth style embedding extracted from the target performance via the style encoder — an information advantage no baseline has. The paper presents this alongside the fair-condition `Ours (PSR)` in Table 2 and repeatedly highlights it in Section 5.1 ("our models achieve lower KL and MAE scores than most baselines (especially Ours-Target)") without clearly labeling it as an oracle/upper bound. The fair-condition results (Ours (PSR)) tell a more mixed story: on KL(D), DExter scores 1.48 vs Ours(PSR) 6.19 (DExter >4× better), and on MAE(V), Ours(PSR) scores 15.24 vs the ablation EPR-Only 10.65. The paper should cleanly separate oracle and non-oracle evaluations and base its primary EPR claims on Ours(PSR).

2. **Degradation of the full framework relative to the EPR-Only ablation is not discussed.** On several key metrics, `Ours (PSR)` underperforms `EPR-Only`: KL(V) 2.67 vs 2.05, MAE(V) 15.24 vs 10.65. This gap exists despite the EPR-Only variant being trained on less data (only ASAP, without the joint APT objective or unpaired data). The paper claims "joint modeling... leads to better EPR performance" but does not analyze or even acknowledge this degradation. (Note: EPR-Only likely benefits from ground-truth style access like Ours-Target, making the comparison to PSR not strictly apples-to-apples, but the paper should still address this.)

### Minor

3. **Dismissal of DExter's KL(D) advantage lacks evidence.** The paper claims DExter's much lower KL(D) score (1.48 vs Ours(PSR) 6.19) reflects "unstable dynamics rather than meaningful expressiveness" (Section 5.1) without providing specific evidence. Spectrograms, listening test breakdowns, or targeted quantitative analysis would be needed to substantiate this characterization.

4. **Alignment-free claim versus evaluation metrics.** The paper advertises "A Seq2Seq formulation of EPR without note-level alignment" as contribution 3, but the evaluation uses metrics that inherently require establishing note-level correspondence ("alignment rate, insertion rate, and missing rate" in Table 3, and "note-aligned MAE" in Section 4.3). While evaluating with alignment-based metrics while training without alignment is standard practice, the paper does not describe how these metrics are computed from unaligned outputs, leaving ambiguity about whether the evaluation re-introduces alignment the model was designed to avoid.

5. **Unnamed transcription pipeline.** The unpaired performance dataset is constructed using "a state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" (Section 4.1) from YouTube piano covers. The model is not named (footnote 2 appears empty in the extracted text), making the pipeline non-reproducible. No ablation measures the impact of upstream transcription errors on the joint model's training.

6. **No limitations section.** The paper has no discussion of limitations, failure cases, or boundaries of the approach. Given the mixed PSR results, the oracle-condition dependence, and the unvalidated transcription pipeline, this is a notable omission.

### Trivial

7. **Statistical significance notation is under-explained.** Tables 2-3 use letter groups (a, b, c, d, e, f) that are not fully described. For instance, Ours(PSR) and Ours(Target) are in different letter groups for KL(D) (group 'e' vs 'd'), indicating significant differences the paper does not discuss.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Add an ablation quantifying the contribution of unpaired data (training without the unlabeled performance dataset).
- Provide a nearest-neighbor or overfitting analysis for the generated performances.
- Include spectrograms or qualitative examples comparing DExter and Ours(PSR) outputs to substantiate the "unstable dynamics" claim.

## Removed Points

These points from the input review were removed after verification against the paper:

- **"Eq. 4 has information leakage (uses unmasked y for style encoder)."** This is by design — the style is a global vector extracted from the full unmasked performance while content is reconstructed from the masked version. This is part of the disentanglement framework, not a bug.
- **"Ours(Target) performs worse on alignment rate than Ours(PSR) — an anomaly."** The difference (91.55% vs 92.27%) is small and could be due to different style embeddings leading to different decoding behaviors. This is an observation, not a verified flaw.
- **"The EPR-Only vs PSR comparison is unfair because EPR-Only has ground-truth style."** This observation actually weakens the reviewer's own criticism #2 above; it has been incorporated as a caveat in the retained weakness.

## Suggestions

1. Present `Ours (Target)` clearly as an oracle/upper bound condition (e.g., rename to "Ours (GT Style)") and base primary EPR claims on `Ours (PSR)`.
2. Add an analysis section discussing where and why `Ours (PSR)` degrades relative to `EPR-Only` on certain metrics.
3. Specify the audio-to-MIDI transcription model used and report its accuracy on held-out piano data.
4. Describe how alignment rate, insertion rate, and missing rate are computed from Seq2Seq outputs.
5. Include a limitations section.
6. Add an ablation of unpaired data to measure its contribution.
7. Provide evidence for the "unstable dynamics" characterization of DExter (e.g., spectrograms or listening test breakdowns).
