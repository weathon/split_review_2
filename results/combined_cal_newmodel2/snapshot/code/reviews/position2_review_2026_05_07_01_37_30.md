## Summary

This position paper argues that strict alignment of image generation models to a universal aesthetic preference is problematic because it systematically overrides explicit user instructions for "wide-spectrum aesthetics" (deliberately unconventional imagery), narrowing artistic expression and substituting developer-centered values for user autonomy. The paper supports this position through: constructing a 300-prompt wide-spectrum aesthetics dataset from COCO captions augmented with VisionReward "bad" attribute labels; evaluating generation models across four families (Flux, SDXL, SD3.5M, Nano Banana) and reward models (HPSv3, PickScore, ImageReward, etc.); and validating findings against real artworks from the LAPIS dataset.

## Strengths

- **Clear, well-defined position with multi-layered argumentation**: The central thesis—that strict alignment to a generalized aesthetic preference conflicts with user intent and narrows expression—is stated precisely in the Introduction and developed through a chain of concerns in Section 2.4 (developer preference imposition, inherited bias, individual vs. collective preference, sanitized reality, toxic positivity). The paper draws a sharp, useful distinction between safety and aesthetic conformity in Section 5.

- **Convincing empirical evidence from reward model evaluation**: Table 4 provides the paper's strongest result: HPSv3 achieves only 0.381 accuracy at selecting the image matching a wide-spectrum aesthetics prompt (worse than random), while unaligned models BLIP-L and CLIP-L achieve 0.965 and 0.913 respectively. This cleanly demonstrates that preference-aligned reward models are systematically biased against user-intended anti-aesthetic outputs, and that the problem is alignment rather than prompt complexity.

- **Rigorous statistical comparisons across model families**: Table 1 reports Wilcoxon signed-rank and McNemar tests showing that aesthetics-aligned models (DanceFlux, Playground, SD3.5M-PickScore) produce significantly different outputs from their base counterparts, with most p-values < 10⁻⁵. This within-family comparison provides meaningful evidence that additional aesthetic alignment degrades instruction-following for anti-aesthetic prompts.

- **Thoughtful engagement with counterarguments**: Section 5 directly addresses two plausible objections—that alignment is needed for safety/user experience, and that wide-spectrum aesthetics represents technical flaws—with reasoned rebuttals. The distinction between "truly unsafe content" and "aesthetically filtered content" and the argument that clarity is often an expressive choice (with citation to photography practice) are particularly effective.

- **Validation on real artworks makes the bias tangible**: Evaluating reward models on ~10K artworks from the LAPIS dataset and showing that some famous works (e.g., "The Scream" at HPSv3 5.23 vs. typical AI-image scores of 12.1±2.98) score below 2 standard deviations from the mean powerfully illustrates that the problem extends beyond synthetic prompts to culturally significant art.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

- **Prompt construction validity is not established**: The wide-spectrum aesthetics prompts are built by combining "bad" rating descriptions from VisionReward's human labeling guidelines (e.g., "extremely blurry and fragmented… proportions warp chaotically"). The paper does not validate that these prompts correspond to how artists actually express unconventional aesthetics rather than simply requesting degraded or broken images. While Section 5 argues that most dimensions (emotion, realism, brightness) are stylistic choices, prompts like the "blurry and fragmented" food example could reasonably be interpreted by generation models as requests for low-quality output rather than intentional artistic subversion. This gap weakens the paper's framing of the problem as specifically about *artistic expression* rather than a broader instruction-following failure.

- **Causal claims outstrip the evidence**: The paper attributes reduced instruction-following specifically to "aesthetic alignment," but the evidence is post-hoc comparisons across different models with different training pipelines. The base models (e.g., Flux Dev is noted as "likely already aesthetics-aligned") are not neutral baselines. DanceFlux uses HPSv2.1 + CLIP, SD3.5M-PickScore uses PickScore, and Playground uses an unspecified aesthetic objective—these are different alignment methods and reward signals. The conclusion that aesthetic alignment *causes* suppression would require controlled experiments (same architecture, same data, varying only the alignment objective) that the paper does not provide. The observed patterns are suggestive and consistent with the paper's position, but the causal inference is partly conjectural.

### Minor

- **Limited engagement with personalized alignment as a remedy**: The paper mentions user-specific adapters (Jin & Chua, 2025) and LAPIS in Section 2.2 but does not substantively address whether user-level adaptation—where the reward model is conditioned on user identity—would resolve the tension between general preference and individual intent. Engaging with this would sharpen the paper's position and recommendations.

- **Results partially obscured by Appendix dependencies**: Key validation details—the human evaluation for LLM-as-judge ground truth, the emotional bias tests (Section 4.4), the LAPIS quantitative analysis (Section 4.3), and the mitigation LoRA details (Section 6)—are all delegated to the Appendix, which was stripped. The main text alone leaves these claims asserted rather than demonstrated, though the LLM-as-judge κ=0.80 validation is summarized.

### Trivial

- The COCO-based scenes (motorcyclists, buses, table settings) are mundane and create a tension with the paper's framing around artistic expression. Including prompts based on real artistic movements or creative use-cases would tighten the connection between the experimental design and the paper's thesis.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A controlled experiment training the same base model with and without aesthetic alignment (identical data, varying only the reward signal) would substantially strengthen the causal argument, though this is more demanding than what is standard for a position paper.
- Validation of prompt intent via artist ratings or examples of real artistic prompts would address the prompt-validity concern.
- Deeper discussion of how personalized alignment, user-controlled aesthetic strength, or opt-out mechanisms could resolve the problem without abandoning alignment entirely.

## Novel Insights

The most novel insight emerging from synthesizing these reviews is that the paper's experimental design inadvertently reveals a deeper structural problem: the alignment pipeline creates a two-level filtering system where *both* the reward model (used during training) and the generation model (at inference) independently bias outputs toward conventional aesthetics. The reward model results (Table 4) show that even if a generation model *did* produce the user-requested anti-aesthetic image, the reward signal used to train it would penalize that output. This means the problem cannot be fixed by simply improving generation-model instruction-following—the entire evaluation infrastructure (reward models, benchmarks, annotator pools) would need to be reformed. The paper's empirical setup demonstrates this double-bind concretely, though the paper itself does not fully articulate this structural diagnosis.

## Suggestions

- Add a small validation study where 2-3 artists or creative practitioners rate whether the constructed wide-spectrum prompts represent legitimate artistic intent versus mere degradation. This would directly address the most significant weakness.
- Restructure Section 5 to include a substantive engagement with personalized/user-conditioned alignment as a potential resolution, explaining why it does (or does not) fully address the problem.
- Clarify the causal claims by distinguishing between what the evidence *demonstrates* (aligned models follow anti-aesthetic instructions less well than base models) and what it *suggests* (aesthetic alignment is the causal mechanism), and note the controlled experiments that would be needed for the latter.
