## Summary
GRAID is a framework for generating large-scale, high-quality spatial reasoning VQA datasets for Vision Language Models using only 2D bounding boxes from standard object detectors. The core insight is that qualitative spatial relationships (left/right, larger/smaller, more/fewer) can be reliably determined from 2D geometry alone, thereby avoiding cascading errors from single-view 3D reconstruction and hallucinations from caption-based generative methods. Applied to BDD100k, NuImages, and Waymo, GRAID generates 8.5M VQA pairs with 91.16% human-validated correctness (versus 57.6% for the evaluated SpatialVLM baseline). The paper also contributes SPARQ, a predicate-sieve mechanism that accelerates dataset generation by up to 1400×, and demonstrates that fine-tuning on GRAID data generalizes across question types and datasets.

---

## Strengths

- **Sound central insight with clear empirical support.** The argument that qualitative spatial relationships (e.g., "is A left of B?") are more reliably answerable from 2D geometry than quantitative metric questions (e.g., "how far are A and B?") is directly validated by the human study showing 91.16% vs. 57.6% valid pairs. This is not a marginal difference—it is a decisive, reproducible quality gap.

- **Comprehensive generalization experiments.** RQ2 is particularly compelling: fine-tuning on only 6 question types from one dataset improves performance on 10+ held-out question types on a completely different dataset (GRAID-NuImages with different cities and scenes). The observed +47.5% on BDD and +37.9% on NuImages indicates the model is learning transferable spatial primitives, not memorizing templates.

- **Practical efficiency contribution (SPARQ).** The predicate-sieve mechanism yields documented speedups up to 1407× on computationally heavy templates, with reported average predicate latency of 0.02ms versus full realization. This makes the framework practically deployable at scale, and the engineering rationale is clearly explained with Algorithm 1.

- **Multi-model, multi-benchmark RQ3.** Testing four VLM architectures (Llama 3.2 11B, Gemma 3 4B, Qwen2.5 VL 3B, Qwen3 VL 8B) across five established benchmarks (A-OKVQA, RealWorldQA, BLINK, NaturalBench, VSR) spanning indoor and outdoor scenes is substantially more rigorous than single-model evaluations common in dataset papers. Consistent outperformance over SpatialVLM-finetuned models across all four backbones strengthens the claim.

- **Human evaluation design is thoughtful.** Evaluators are given images both with and without bounding boxes, asked to rate both question validity and answer correctness, and also rate question difficulty on a Likert scale. Distributing randomly across four evaluators with name-seeded sampling reduces systematic bias.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None identified.

### Major

1. **Human evaluation covers a tiny fraction of the claimed corpus.** The 91.16% quality figure is derived from 317 VQA pairs evaluated by 4 humans—all from GRAID-BDD without depth. This is less than 0.004% of the 8.5M generated pairs. No human evaluation is reported for NuImages, Waymo, or the depth-augmented variants. Given that the depth variants use a depth model—the same source of errors criticized in SpatialVLM—the absence of a quality audit for those variants is a significant gap.

2. **Comparison baseline is the community implementation of SpatialVLM, not the paper's own method.** The paper's central foil for quality (57.6% valid answers) is "OpenSpaces," described as "a dataset generated by the community implementation of SpatialVLM." This is not the same as the published SpatialVLM method. Errors in a community reimplementation may not reflect the original paper's quality, yet the paper's framing repeatedly attributes these failures to "SpatialVLM." The same concern applies to SpatialRGPT: evaluators couldn't assess the OpenSpatialDataset due to the masked region format, making the comparative quality study effectively one-sided.

3. **"Domain-agnostic" claim is empirically unsupported.** The paper states prominently that "GRAID is domain-agnostic" and attributes the driving-domain focus to data availability, not methodology. However, all source datasets are driving scenes, all 22 templates are instantiated on driving data, and the only non-driving evaluations are downstream benchmarks. No demonstration of GRAID applied to a non-driving domain (e.g., indoor scenes from ScanQA, medical images) is presented. The claim is plausible but unsubstantiated.

### Minor

1. **The +32.5% improvement on A-OKVQA (Table 4/5/6, referenced in text) is unusually large.** Gains of this magnitude typically indicate a very low baseline or a poorly calibrated evaluation. Without reporting baseline model accuracies and noting what score they start from, these numbers are difficult to contextualize. If the pre-SFT baseline is near random chance, the magnitude may be less meaningful.

2. **Template diversity is limited.** All 22 question types produce binary Yes/No or simple ordinal/categorical answers (e.g., "which is leftmost?"). This constrains the linguistic complexity of spatial reasoning that can be learned. Open-ended or compositional spatial questions (e.g., "describe the relative arrangement of the three objects") are absent.

3. **Ablation of key design choices is absent.** There is no ablation of the impact of margin\_ratio thresholds, IoU thresholds, or the choice of object detector backbone on dataset quality or downstream performance. GRAID's configurability is presented as a strength, but no sensitivity analysis is provided.

### Trivial

- The sunburst chart (Figure 2) description in text is more informative than the chart itself as parsed.
- Algorithm 1 covers only `RightOf`; the behavior of other templates is relegated to the appendix.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A cross-domain application of GRAID (e.g., applying templates to indoor detection annotations from ScanQA or COCO-annotated images) would substantiate the domain-agnostic claim.
- Ablation experiments on the SPARQ threshold parameters would strengthen reproducibility.
- Reporting pre-SFT and post-SFT absolute accuracy (not just delta) on all benchmarks in the main body would improve interpretability.
- Even a small human evaluation of GRAID-NuImages or the depth variants would substantially strengthen the quality claims.

---

## Novel Insights

The paper surfaces a genuinely useful and underappreciated principle: the precision mismatch between what depth/reconstruction models can reliably deliver and what spatial reasoning questions actually require. Metric-space questions (exact distances, dimensions) demand accurate 3D reconstruction, which is highly error-prone from single images. Qualitative-ordinal questions (A is to the left of B; X is larger than Y) require only reliable object localization via 2D bounding boxes—a far more mature capability. This reframing—from quantitative metric to qualitative ordinal—is elegant and practically impactful. The SPARQ predicate-sieve design pattern (define lightweight rejection predicates that short-circuit expensive realization) is also a transferable engineering insight applicable to any large-scale dataset generation pipeline involving combinatorial candidate enumeration.

---

## Suggestions

- Apply GRAID to at least one non-driving dataset (e.g., COCO or Open Images) and report human quality evaluation, even at small scale, to concretely validate domain-agnosticism.
- Clearly distinguish between "community implementation of SpatialVLM" and "SpatialVLM (the published method)" throughout, to avoid conflating implementation quality with methodological quality.
- Expand human evaluation coverage to GRAID-NuImages and the depth-augmented variants.
- Add a table reporting absolute (not just delta) accuracy scores on all benchmarks for all models, including pre-SFT baselines.
- Include a brief ablation on margin\_ratio to show how quality changes with threshold settings.

---

## Score and Decision

GRAID presents a clean, practical contribution: a sound methodological insight (qualitative 2D geometry over quantitative 3D reconstruction), a large high-quality dataset, and solid evidence of generalization. The experiments are more thorough than typical dataset papers, using multiple models and benchmarks. The primary weaknesses—unfair comparison baseline, small human evaluation sample, and unsupported domain-agnosticism claim—are major but not fatal; none invalidates the core contribution. The paper sits comfortably in accept territory given the concrete dataset artifact, the strong generalization results, and the practical SPARQ contribution, but falls short of strong accept due to the evidentiary gaps in the quality audit and comparison rigor.
