## Summary
This paper proposes a unified transformer-based Seq2Seq framework that jointly models two inverse tasks in music information retrieval: Expressive Performance Rendering (EPR, score→performance) and Automatic Piano Transcription (APT, performance→score). The key contributions are: (1) a disentangled latent representation separating note-level score content from global performance style, enabling mutual supervision across both tasks without requiring note-level alignment; and (2) a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings conditioned solely on score content, enabling non-expert users to obtain stylistically appropriate renderings.

---

## Strengths

- **Genuinely novel joint formulation.** Prior work treats EPR and APT as entirely separate problems despite their obvious duality. Jointly modeling them with a shared content space and mutual supervision is a clean and well-motivated research direction, analogous to ASR/TTS co-training in speech, and is supported by empirical improvements (Table 2: Ours-Target consistently outperforms the EPR-Only ablation, confirming that the joint framework helps).

- **Alignment-free training at competitive accuracy.** Formulating EPR as a Seq2Seq task—eliminating dependency on fragile note-level alignment tools—is practically significant. The approach achieves competitive MUSTER/ScoreSimilarity scores (Table 1) versus alignment-dependent baselines, while also achieving the highest alignment rate (92.27%) and lowest insertion rate (3.77%) among EPR models (Table 3). This is a meaningful result given the relaxed supervision.

- **PSR module is well-designed and empirically validated.** Using a DDPM to model the conditional distribution p(z_s | e_g) is principled and allows diverse, score-conditioned style sampling. The evaluation in Figure 4—showing that PSR-generated 2D projections closely mirror real-performance distributions in terms of clustering and era-wise separation—provides compelling evidence that the module captures musically meaningful structure.

- **Multi-faceted disentanglement analysis.** The performer/composer identification experiment (Table 4) is a thoughtful diagnostic: style representations (z_s) substantially outperform content representations (z_c) for both tasks, confirming successful disentanglement. Style transfer and latent space interpolation (Figure 5) further validate the continuity and interpretability of the style space.

- **Comprehensive evaluation.** The paper combines objective metrics (MUSTER, ScoreSimilarity, KL, MAE, alignment/insertion/missing rates), subjective listening tests across five composers/styles, disentanglement probing, and OOD generalization on ATEPP—a notably broad evaluation suite for an MIR paper.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Disentanglement is structural rather than explicitly enforced.** The framework relies on architectural granularity differences (sequential content vectors vs. a single global style vector) and task supervision to separate content from style. There is no explicit adversarial loss, mutual information minimization, or other disentanglement objective between z_c and z_s. The empirical results in Table 4 reveal an important side effect: Style→Comp accuracy is 77.46%, substantially higher than Cont→Comp (29.99%). The paper explains this by noting that pianists adapt style to composer conventions, but this means z_s entangles composer identity with expressive style. This makes the "style" space semantically ambiguous—it encodes content-related (compositional era/genre) information alongside expressive nuance. Whether this is actually desirable depends on the use case, but the paper does not critically discuss this conflation or its implications for controlled style transfer.

- **Subjective evaluation is underpowered.** The listening test uses 11 participants and only 5 test pieces (one per composer). With this sample, achieving statistical significance across conditions is unlikely, and the paper does not report significance tests for the subjective results (only for objective metrics via Wilcoxon). The choice of a single piece per composer also risks confounding piece-level familiarity effects with model quality. Results in Figure 2 show small differences between conditions that may not be reliable at this scale.

### Minor

- **Potential circularity in PSR evaluation (Figure 4).** The 2D projections used to compare PSR-generated vs. real-performance style vectors are produced by the same classifier trained on the ATEPP data in Section 5.2. This creates a possible circularity: the projection is optimized to separate composer/performer clusters using style embeddings from the joint model, so it may naturally align PSR-generated embeddings that live in the same latent space. A held-out linear probe or an independent embedding would provide stronger evidence.

- **Unpaired YouTube data quality is unverified.** The paper uses an unspecified "state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" to produce the unpaired performance dataset from YouTube recordings. The quality, error distribution, and potential genre/style bias of these transcriptions are not reported. Since unpaired performances contribute to the masked reconstruction loss, systematic errors here could affect the learned style representations in ways that are difficult to diagnose.

### Trivial

- The EPR-Only ablation (Section 5.1) only uses ASAP data, whereas Ours-Target also trains on unpaired data. The improvement observed could partially reflect the additional training data rather than the joint modeling objective alone. A cleaner ablation would train EPR-Only on the same combined dataset.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A mutual information or adversarial disentanglement term between z_c and z_s could strengthen the framework and provide cleaner separation between score-structural and purely expressive style information.
- Quantitative diversity evaluation for the PSR module (e.g., variance of generated style vectors for a fixed score, or audio-level variation across multiple samples from the same piece).
- Analysis of failure cases: what types of ornamental passages or structurally complex pieces still trip up the alignment-free Seq2Seq formulation?

---

## Novel Insights

The paper's most interesting implicit finding is that the learned style space z_s predominantly encodes *composer* identity rather than *performer* identity (77.46% vs. 48.97% accuracy in Table 4), even though z_s is extracted from *performance* MIDI. This suggests that professional pianists' expressive choices are strongly constrained by the compositional style of the piece—"composer as style prior"—rather than being idiosyncratic to individual performers. This observation motivates the PSR module's design in a non-trivial way: if style is already largely predictable from score content (because it mirrors the composer), a score-conditioned generative model is well-positioned to recover it. The PSR module's success (Figure 4) empirically supports this hypothesis. This is a genuine empirical insight about the structure of expressive performance that goes somewhat beyond prior claims in the disentangled music representation literature.

---

## Suggestions

- Add a statistical significance analysis (e.g., pairwise t-test or bootstrap) for the subjective listening test results in Figure 2.
- Expand the subjective evaluation to at least 20 participants and 10+ pieces to achieve adequate statistical power given the small effect sizes visible in Figure 2.
- Provide an ablation separating the effect of (a) joint training vs. (b) additional unpaired data by training Ours-Target on ASAP only and comparing to EPR-Only.
- Report basic statistics on the YouTube-sourced unpaired dataset (total duration, genre distribution, transcription model identity, estimated note error rate from a held-out sample).

---

## Score and Decision

The paper makes genuine and well-motivated contributions to the MIR field: the joint EPR/APT framework, the alignment-free Seq2Seq approach with competitive results, and the PSR module are all valuable. The evaluation is multi-faceted and supports the claims. The primary weaknesses—impure disentanglement and a small subjective study—weigh against the paper but do not invalidate the core results. This is a solid, publishable contribution.
