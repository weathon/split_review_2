## Summary

Critique-RL proposes a two-stage reinforcement learning framework for training language model critics that can (1) assess whether an actor's response is correct (*discriminability*) and (2) provide constructive natural language feedback (*helpfulness*). The paper first demonstrates empirically that training critics with only indirect reward signals (derived from whether the actor improved its response after critique) fails to optimize discriminability, producing either overly conservative or overly aggressive critics. To address this, Stage I explicitly optimizes discriminability using a direct, rule-based reward; Stage II then optimizes helpfulness via actor-refinement-based rewards while regularizing against loss of discriminability via a KL penalty anchored to the Stage I checkpoint.

---

## Strengths

- **Clear, reproducible failure-mode analysis.** Figure 3 and the accompanying analysis precisely characterize *why* baselines fail: `r_refine`/`r_Δ` produce conservative critics (good Δ^{c→i}, poor Δ^{i→c}) while `r_correction` produces aggressive critics (reversed pattern). This motivates the two-stage design cleanly rather than relying on intuition alone.

- **Substantial and consistent empirical gains.** Across three in-domain tasks, two OOD tasks, two model families (Qwen2.5, Llama 3.2), and multiple scales (3B, 7B, 72B), Critique-RL consistently outperforms all baselines. The discrimination gains are especially large—e.g., 85.20% vs. 71.42% (CTRL) on MATH/7B—indicating the mechanism is genuinely working rather than an artifact of tuning.

- **Principled reward decomposition in Algorithm 1.** The Stage II objective cleanly combines `r_refine` (helpfulness), `r_dis` (discriminability retention), and KL regularization against the Stage I model. The ablation in Table 3 validates each term independently, including showing that removing the discrimination-maintenance terms in Stage II causes both accuracy and Acc@Dis to drop.

- **Inference-compute scaling analysis.** Figure 1 (right) shows that K critique-refinement steps are more compute-efficient than 3K parallel response samples, a practically important result for deployment.

- **OOD generalization.** Table 4 demonstrates non-trivial transfer to SVAMP and TheoremQA without any task-specific retraining, supporting the "scalable oversight" framing.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Fixed actor throughout RL training.** The actor model is frozen during all critic training. This is a non-trivial design choice that limits applicability: the trained critic is optimized specifically for the behavior of a fixed actor checkpoint. It is unclear how the critic performs when paired with a different or improved actor. No experiment tests critic transfer to a different actor, which would be essential to validate the scalable oversight claim in a practical setting where actors evolve.

- **No statistical significance or variance reporting.** Results in Tables 1 and 4 are single point estimates. Several results show small margins that could be noise—e.g., TheoremQA for Qwen2.5-7B: CTRL 21.1 → Critique-RL 21.4 (Pass@10: 42.9 → 43.0). Without confidence intervals or multiple seeds, it is impossible to assess whether improvements in low-variance tasks are meaningful.

- **Hyperparameter sensitivity of Stage II is uncharacterized.** The Stage II objective has three hyperparameters: β₁ (set to 0.2), β₂ (the KL coefficient), and the number of RL steps per stage (fixed at 500). No ablation is provided on β₁ or β₂ values, nor on the relative number of steps per stage. Given that the balance between discriminability and helpfulness is the core contribution, the robustness of the design to these choices matters considerably.

### Minor

- **Stage I is closely related to reward model training.** Training a model to predict whether a response is correct is essentially process-reward-model or outcome-reward-model training. The distinction from PRM literature is not discussed, and the paper does not compare against using a separately trained verifier as the discriminability component.

- **Training data preparation mixes actor and critic data without ablation.** The actor is trained on 21,973 reasoning traces + 12,000 refinements, while the critic uses 6,000 SFT examples. The quality and composition of this synthetic data—generated by the same model being trained—could substantially affect results, but no sensitivity analysis is provided.

- **Motivating experiments and main experiments share the same backbone (Qwen2.5-3B/GSM8K).** The reward design in Stage II was informed by preliminary analysis on this exact setting, creating a risk of circular validation. Including a fully held-out model/task in the motivation section would strengthen the claims.

### Trivial
- Table 1 formatting inconsistency: both Critique-RL entries and second-best entries are underlined, but Critique-RL also has bold, making the legend text ("second-best is underlined") inconsistent with the actual presentation.

## Novel Insights

The central empirically-grounded insight—that indirect actor-improvement rewards systematically fail to improve discriminability, and do so in opposite failure directions (conservative vs. aggressive) depending on reward polarity—is the genuine contribution of this work. The observation is not obvious a priori and is validated with clean training dynamics across multiple reward variants. The resulting prescription (decouple discriminability optimization via direct rewards in Stage I, then add helpfulness in Stage II with discriminability-preserving regularization) is intuitive in hindsight but follows non-trivially from the diagnosis, and the empirical evidence that the KL anchor to the Stage I checkpoint is necessary (Table 3, "Stage II w/o discrimination" row) provides supporting mechanistic evidence.

---

## Suggestions

- Add error bars or multi-seed variance estimates, especially for the OOD results where margins are small.
- Test critic–actor transfer: evaluate the Stage II critic against a stronger actor to demonstrate portability.
- Report ablations over β₁ ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5} to characterize sensitivity of the discriminability–helpfulness trade-off.
- Clarify quantitatively how Stage I compares to simply training an outcome reward model and using it as a discriminator at test time.

---

## Score and Decision

The paper identifies a clear and reproducible failure mode in RL-based critic training, proposes a principled two-stage remedy, and validates it with thorough experiments. The fixed-actor constraint and absence of variance reporting are meaningful limitations, and the hyperparameter sensitivity of the core Stage II objective is not addressed. Nevertheless, the diagnostic analysis is valuable to the scalable oversight community, the empirical gains are large and consistent across models and tasks, and the method is clean and implementable. The work clears the bar for acceptance.
