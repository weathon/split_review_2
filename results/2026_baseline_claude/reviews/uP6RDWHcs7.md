## Summary

Marginal Flow is a density estimation framework that defines the model density as a finite mixture $q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_i q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i})$, where the mixture parameters $\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i} = f_\theta(z_i)$ are generated by an unconstrained neural network from a simple base distribution. Because the density is a sum over analytically tractable components (e.g., Gaussians, Wisharts), evaluation requires no Jacobian determinants or ODE solves; and because sampling requires only a forward pass plus a component draw, both operations are fast. The framework accommodates lower-dimensional manifold learning (by choosing $m < d$ for the base), conditional distributions, and multiple training objectives. Experiments span synthetic 2-D data, the SBI benchmark, Wishart mixture distributions, and manifold learning in VAE latent spaces.

---

## Strengths

- **Compelling efficiency advantage with empirical backing.** Figure 3 shows Marginal Flow is orders of magnitude faster than Flow Matching, Normalizing Flow, and Free-form Flows at both sampling and exact density evaluation across a wide range of dimensions. The speedup arises from a concrete architectural reason (no Jacobian, no ODE), making this claim credible rather than incidental.

- **Unified handling of manifold learning and density estimation.** The ability to choose $m < d$ in the base distribution lets the model simultaneously learn the intrinsic manifold and the density on it. The spiral example (Figure 4) and the Wishart setting (Figure 9, up to $100\times100$ matrices corresponding to $d=5050$) demonstrate this concretely and highlight a genuine limitation of competing methods in these settings.

- **Domain-specific flexibility via interchangeable $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$.** Swapping the Gaussian component for a Wishart to handle positive-definite matrices (Section 4.3) is a non-trivial application: Marginal Flow achieves test KL divergences roughly two orders of magnitude lower than NF on $10\times10$ matrices, and handles the $100\times100$ case where NF is computationally infeasible.

- **SBI state-of-the-art.** Results on the Lueckmann et al. benchmark show competitive or best C2ST scores, particularly in low-data regimes. This is a relevant and well-studied benchmark for conditional density estimation.

- **Diverse training objectives.** The framework can be trained with forward KL (log-likelihood) and reverse KL from an unnormalized target using the same architecture. Figure 8 shows comparable or superior reverse-KL performance versus NF on four synthetic tasks with error bars.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

**1. The "exact density" claim requires careful nuancing that the paper does not provide.**
The model is defined as $q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) := \frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{i=1}^{N_c} q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i})$ with $\mathbf{w}_{\theta,i}$ resampled at every call. This means the density is a *random function*: evaluating $q_\theta(x)$ twice in succession yields different numerical values. The underlying quantity being modelled is the marginal $\int q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})\,q_\theta(\mathbf{w})\,d\mathbf{w}$, but the finite-$N_c$ sum is only a Monte Carlo approximation to it. The paper treats the finite sum as the model definition and calls this "exact" in Table 1 and throughout the text, yet never:
(a) quantifies the variance of this estimator as a function of $N_c$;
(b) specifies whether the same $w_i$'s are fixed across test-point evaluations (Figure 7) or resampled per point—a detail critical for interpreting the reported test log-likelihoods;
(c) discusses what "exact" means for a stochastic density.

If the same $w_i$'s are shared over a batch (fixed per evaluation pass), this is a consistent if variance-prone estimate; if they are resampled per point, the reported test log-likelihoods are noisy and hard to reproduce. The reviewers cannot determine which protocol was used.

**2. No high-dimensional tabular or image benchmarks.**
The synthetic experiments are 2-D. SBI posteriors are moderate-dimensional ($\leq 10$ or so) and not the standard ICLR benchmark for density estimators. MNIST/JAFFE experiments are qualitative and performed in a 20/10-dimensional VAE latent space. For a paper positioning itself as a general density estimation framework that is "orders of magnitude faster," the omission of standard higher-dimensional benchmarks—e.g., tabular flow benchmarks (UCI datasets: POWER, GAS, HEPMASS, MINIBOONE) used routinely in NF literature—is a meaningful gap. Without these, it is difficult to assess whether the quality-efficiency trade-off holds beyond low-dimensional settings.

### Minor

**3. Approximation error analysis for $N_c$ is absent.**
The paper says $N_c$ "is not required to be fixed" but provides no guidance on how to choose it. There is no ablation on $N_c$ versus approximation quality versus runtime. Understanding how quickly the variance of the density estimator decreases with $N_c$ (e.g., it should scale as $O(1/\sqrt{N_c})$ by CLT for the Gaussian case) would directly support the efficiency vs. accuracy trade-off claims.

**4. Gradient variance during log-likelihood training is unanalyzed.**
The gradient $\nabla_\theta \log q_\theta(x)$ involves responsibility-weighted terms $\frac{q(x|w_i)}{\sum_j q(x|w_j)} \nabla_\theta \log q(x|w_i)$; when one component dominates (high-density region), variance can spike. This is a well-known challenge in mixture training, and no discussion or mitigation is offered.

**5. Convergence claims in Figure 7 could favor Marginal Flow due to fewer computations per iteration.**
The x-axis is wall-clock time rather than number of gradient steps. Since Marginal Flow's iteration is cheaper (no ODE or Jacobian), "converges faster in time" may simply mean "converges in similar gradient steps at lower cost per step." Showing both time and gradient-step convergence curves would allow readers to disentangle these effects.

### Trivial

- Table 1 marks "Efficient training" = $\checkmark$ for NF (column) while flow-based exact density training with real-valued coupling layers is not universally considered efficient. The notation is a simplification that could confuse readers.

## Novel Insights

Marginal Flow's key observation is that "resampling" latent parameters from a learned distribution yields a smoothly distributed mixture that is strictly more expressive than a finite GMM with the same $N_c$ components, because the support of the implied mixture is the entire push-forward measure $f_\theta \# p_\text{base}$ rather than $N_c$ fixed atoms. This is a genuinely useful conceptual clarification of why marginalization outperforms direct optimization of $w_i$, and the side-by-side in Figure 1 makes the argument vividly. The second novel contribution is extending this insight to domain-specific component families (Wishart) and showing that the framework naturally accommodates non-Euclidean constraints on the data space without any special architecture.

## Suggestions

- Report test log-likelihood on at least two UCI tabular datasets with the same hyperparameter budget as NF and FM to give a fair sense of quality in moderate dimensions.
- Add a short paragraph (or figure in the appendix) showing how test log-likelihood variance and mean change as $N_c \in \{10, 50, 200, 1000\}$; this directly addresses the core approximation question.
- Clarify explicitly in Section 2.2 or the experiment descriptions whether $w_i$'s are drawn once per evaluation batch and shared across all points in that batch, or redrawn per point.
- Reframe "exact density" as "exact given a fixed sample of components" or "exact in the limit $N_c \to \infty$" to avoid overstatement in Table 1.

## Score and Decision

Marginal Flow presents a clear, practically motivated idea—parameterizing mixture weights via an unconstrained neural network for efficient, flexible density estimation—backed by credible efficiency gains, diverse applications, and state-of-the-art results on the SBI benchmark. The framework's generalization to Wishart mixtures and high-dimensional positive-definite matrices is a non-trivial and convincing demonstration of value. The major weaknesses (stochastic density ambiguity, missing tabular benchmarks, absent $N_c$ ablation) are real but do not undermine the core contribution; they leave open questions that can be addressed.
