## Summary
TokenCount proposes a training-free framework for class-agnostic object counting built on SAM. It introduces two components: (1) probabilistic prompt generation that iteratively samples point prompts from a similarity-based probability distribution, and (2) output token-based verification that uses SAM's decoder output tokens with a TS-SS geometric similarity metric to distinguish positive from negative instances. The method is evaluated on FSC-147 and CARPK, showing competitive results among training-free SAM-only methods, with particularly strong performance on CARPK (MAE 4.68, outperforming all training-based methods).

## Strengths
- **Novel use of SAM output tokens for verification.** The insight that SAM's decoder output tokens encode discriminative instance-level information (semantic + positional) through bidirectional attention is genuinely interesting and well-motivated. Table 5 and Figure 5 provide evidence that output tokens offer better positive/negative separation than image embeddings — this is the paper's core technical insight and the contribution that most distinguishes it from prior work.
- **Strong CARPK results.** MAE 4.68 and RMSE 6.13 on CARPK outperforms all training-based methods (e.g., LOCA at 9.97, BMNet+ at 5.76) and is close to TFCAC (4.39). This is a genuinely impressive result for a training-free method.
- **Thorough ablation studies.** Tables 3, 4, and 5 systematically evaluate the verification metric, iteration/prompt configurations, and output token vs. image embedding spaces. Table 4 in particular gives practitioners useful guidance on the accuracy-efficiency tradeoff across different iteration/prompt budgets.
- **Clean, practical approach.** The method requires no training, no additional models, and no architectural modifications to SAM. The probabilistic prompt generation with iterative refinement and probability distribution updates (Figure 3) is well-designed.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Unsubstantiated speed claim vs. TFCAC.** The paper claims inference time "more than twice as fast" as TFCAC (line 230), but reports only its own timing (1.69s/image on FSC-147). No TFCAC timing is provided — no speed comparison table, no measurement on shared hardware, no methodology. Since the speed claim is used to justify competitiveness against the higher-accuracy TFCAC, this gap weakens a key argument in the paper's positioning.
- **Large RMSE gap with TFCAC on FSC-147.** On FSC-147, TFCAC achieves RMSE 56.33 while TokenCount achieves RMSE 133.11 — more than 2× worse. The paper's SAM-only vs. SAM+α framing is reasonable, but this RMSE gap indicates large outlier errors that limit practical applicability on diverse-category benchmarks. The paper does not analyze or diagnose these failure cases.

### Minor
- **TS-SS improvement over cosine similarity is marginal.** Table 3 shows TS-SS achieves MAE 16.25 vs. 16.48 for cosine similarity (gap of 0.23, ~1.4%). The paper's interpretive claim that non-hybrid metrics have "reached a performance plateau" and TS-SS "breaks through this barrier" (line 275) is overstated given the small difference. The metric shows a consistent but incremental improvement; the real contribution is the output token space, not the specific metric.
- **Imprecise narrative around similarity.** The paper criticizes similarity-based methods and claims to "bypass the uncertainty of similarity" (line 28), yet the probabilistic prompt generation stage (Eq. 1) explicitly computes cosine similarity between exemplar and query image embeddings. While the verification stage does use output tokens rather than similarity for the final decision, the rhetorical framing conflates the two stages and the distinction is never explicitly discussed.
- **Underspecified hyperparameters.** The temperature parameter τ in Eq. 1 is never given a value. The token verification threshold of 300 (line 180) is stated without explaining what it operates on or how it was tuned. These gaps hamper reproducibility.

### Trivial
- No variance or error bars reported in any table, making it difficult to assess whether small differences (e.g., TS-SS vs. cosine similarity in Table 3, output token vs. image embedding in Table 5) are statistically meaningful.
- The Discussion section (Section 5) is brief and contains a confusing final sentence that seems to contradict the earlier efficiency claim ("This limitation affects scalability for real-time applications..."), likely a drafting artifact.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Failure case analysis for high-RMSE images on FSC-147 would help readers understand the method's limitations and build trust.
- Evaluation on additional counting benchmarks beyond FSC-147 and CARPK would strengthen the generality claim.
- The PCA visualization (Figure 5) could be strengthened with quantitative cluster separation metrics (e.g., silhouette score) beyond the two illustrative examples.

## Novel Insights
The paper's core insight — that SAM's decoder output tokens, which integrate semantic and positional information through bidirectional attention, provide a more discriminative feature space for instance verification than image embeddings — is genuinely novel in the object counting literature. The PCA visualizations (Figure 5) and the image embedding vs. output token comparison (Table 5) provide concrete evidence. This insight may have broader applicability beyond counting to other tasks that need to distinguish instances using foundation model features without additional training.

## Suggestions
- Either substantiate the speed comparison with TFCAC by running both methods on identical hardware and reporting wall-clock time, or remove the unsubstantiated "more than twice as fast" claim and simply report your own timing.
- Analyze and diagnose the high RMSE on FSC-147 (133.11). Which types of images cause large errors? This would strengthen the paper and honestly communicate limitations.
- Specify the value of τ and explain what the token verification threshold of 300 operates on and how it was tuned.
- Tone down the "bypass uncertainty of similarity" language to more precisely reflect that similarity is used for prompt sampling while output tokens handle verification.
