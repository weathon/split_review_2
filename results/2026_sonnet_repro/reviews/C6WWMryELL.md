## Summary

This paper investigates output volatility in long-form LLM generation through a three-stage approach: (1) VOLTBench, a multi-dimensional benchmark explicitly measuring output instability via multiple sampling, covering both structured and unstructured tasks up to 100k words; (2) an attention-trace analysis identifying two failure signatures—Attention Collapse and Attention Instability—as correlates of premature termination and section skipping; and (3) SELB (Structural Enforcement via Logits Boosting), a training-free decoding method that suppresses EOS tokens until a required number of sections are produced and boosts section-title logits at per-section length limits.

---

## Strengths

- **Multi-dimensional benchmark design with stability as a first-class metric.** VOLTBench is the first benchmark to embed multiple sampling (N=5) as a core evaluation protocol and to introduce Length Variation Coefficient (LVC) and Length Standard Deviation (LSD) alongside quality metrics. It covers eight task types, two languages, three instruction complexity levels, and generation lengths up to 100k words (Table 1). The contrast with all prior benchmarks—none of which include stability evaluation or multiple sampling—is genuine and clearly documented.

- **Automated quality evaluation for unstructured tasks.** By embedding verifiable fine-grained constraints (character-level pattern, keyword presence, specified theme) directly into prompts (Section 4.2), the paper enables objective quality assessment for narrative tasks that traditionally resist automation. This is a practical design contribution that makes the benchmark's UCA metric interpretable.

- **Empirical documentation of severe and universal volatility.** Table 2 and Figure 3 document that all mainstream models fail on tasks exceeding ~50 sections, with some (Mamba-7B, LongWriter-8B) exhibiting LVC above 45%. The result that constraint adherence universally collapses beyond 100 sections—with models producing *fewer* correct sections at 500 than at 200 (Section 4.3.1)—is a striking, actionable finding for the community.

- **Section-level volatility analysis.** Figure 3(d) shows that structured tasks produce more stable section counts than unstructured ones, providing data-driven support for the hypothesis that format constraints guide generation. This sub-result is independently useful beyond the main method.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None. The benchmark contribution is genuine, and the underlying method (EOS suppression + logit boosting) is functional.

### Major

- **Headline comparison obscures within-model improvement and ignores stronger same-model baseline.** The paper's headline claim—"69% reduction in LVC"—is computed as SELB on Qwen2.5-7B (14.02%) vs. LongWriter-8B (45.4%), which are *different base models* with different training. This renders the figure misleading as a measure of SELB's contribution. Table 2 already reports four decoding baselines applied to the same Qwen2.5-7B base: notably, Lookahead Decoding achieves LVC of 9.3%, which is *better than* SELB's 14.02%. SELB is never placed in Table 2, so readers cannot see this direct comparison. The correct framing is that SELB offers dramatically superior Mean Length Accuracy (78.25% vs. Lookahead's 14.4%) at the cost of slightly higher LVC—a real and meaningful trade-off that the current presentation conceals by switching base models for the headline metric.

- **SELB's length improvements are partially mechanical, not purely empirical.** Equation 3 explicitly sets the EOS logit to −∞ until p < P_total sections are generated, guaranteeing the model cannot terminate early. Equation 2 makes section-title token generation "nearly certain" at each section boundary. Together, these mechanics ensure approximately P_total × τ_max tokens are generated by construction. The MLA improvement is therefore partly a consequence of the method's design, not solely evidence that SELB achieves better generation. The paper presents the improvement as a purely empirical discovery without acknowledging this structural component. Related to this: the 30% UCA advantage over LongWriter-8B is compared against a model producing 6,320 words while SELB produces 15,651 words; LLM-as-a-judge is known to exhibit length preference bias, so this quality difference may be partially artifactual.

- **The attention analysis is insufficiently systematic to support causal claims.** The identification of "Attention Collapse" and "Attention Instability" as "common internal patterns" (abstract, Section 5) rests on exactly two trace examples—Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-3B on a single diary task (Figure 4). The paper does not quantify how frequently these patterns appear in failure cases, whether they appear in success cases (which would undermine the causal framing), or whether they generalize across task types or model families. Without this, "Attention Collapse" and "Attention Instability" are illustrative labels applied to two traces, not established phenomena.

### Minor

- **The framing "targeting the identified internal patterns" overstates the mechanistic link.** SELB addresses behavioral symptoms (premature termination via EOS suppression, section skipping via logit boosting) identified through attention analysis—it does not intervene in attention dynamics. The phrase "targeting the identified internal patterns" in the abstract, Section 1, and Section 7 implies a direct mechanistic connection that does not exist. The honest framing is that SELB suppresses *behavioral failure modes* identified through attention observation, which is already a meaningful contribution.

- **SCA = 100% is partly guaranteed by construction.** Equation 2 makes section-title token selection nearly certain at each section boundary; this inflates SCA compared to baselines where structural skeleton emerges naturally. Presenting SCA = 100% as an independent quality finding without acknowledging the boosting mechanism's structural role is imprecise.

- **Benchmark scale not reported in the main text.** The total number of prompts, instances per task, and per-dimension breakdowns in VOLTBench are not stated in the main text. This makes it difficult to assess statistical power and reproducibility from the paper alone.

### Trivial

- N=5 sampling for LVC estimation is low for highly volatile models; the paper does not discuss whether this sample size is sufficient for stable estimates of LVC, particularly for high-variance models like Mamba-7B (LVC 55.5%).

## Nice-to-Haves

- A single unified table comparing SELB side-by-side with all Table 2 decoding baselines (Repetition Penalty, Entropy-Stopping, Length Constraint, Lookahead Decoding) on the same base model (Qwen2.5-7B) would substantially clarify the actual incremental contribution of SELB. This would make visible the MLA/LVC trade-off that SELB makes relative to Lookahead Decoding.

- An ablation separating the EOS-suppression component (Eq. 3) from the section-boosting component (Eq. 2) would sharpen the contribution: it would clarify whether the MLA gain is driven primarily by EOS suppression or by structural enforcement.

- A length-controlled UCA evaluation (e.g., outputs from SELB and LongWriter-8B truncated to the same length before judging) would help disentangle length-preference bias from genuine quality gains.

- An analysis of content coherence or semantic consistency in later sections of SELB-generated outputs would be valuable; a model forced to continue past its natural stopping point may exhibit semantic drift or padding.

- Systematic attention traces across multiple models, task types, and success/failure conditions would transform the attention analysis from illustrative to rigorous.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most genuinely novel contribution, independent of any individual weakness, is the systematic demonstration that output length volatility is not a minor or incidental issue but a *structural* and universal failure mode in mainstream LLMs: all models collapse beyond 50–100 sections, and the collapse is monotone and predictable rather than random. The specific finding that constraint adherence at 500 sections is *worse* than at 200 (models actively un-learn structure at extreme lengths) suggests that this is not simply a context-length limitation but a failure of attention maintenance that deserves dedicated study. The attention trace framework—while underspecified for causal inference—is a useful starting lens for understanding *when* during generation the behavioral failure is already observable internally, which could inform future early-stopping or intervention strategies.

## Suggestions

1. Add SELB (and its ablations—EOS suppression alone vs. full SELB) to Table 2 alongside the other Qwen2.5-7B baselines. Report MLA and LVC on the same axis. This is the minimal change needed to give readers a fair view of SELB's contribution.

2. Change the headline from "69% reduction in LVC" (vs. LongWriter-8B) to the within-model comparison against Qwen2.5-7B, and reframe the primary result around MLA (where SELB genuinely dominates all baselines at 78.25%) rather than LVC.

3. Replace the two attention traces with a systematic analysis: report what fraction of failure runs show each failure pattern vs. success runs, across at least three models and two task types.

4. Explicitly acknowledge the EOS suppression constraint as a mechanical contributor to MLA improvement and frame the contribution accordingly: SELB is a practical enforcement mechanism, and its real added value is achieving structural quality (SCA, UCA, lexical diversity) *without sacrificing coherence* while generating at length—this is a meaningful claim that doesn't require overstating the mechanistic connection to attention.

5. Report the total benchmark size (number of prompts per task × dimension) in the main text.
