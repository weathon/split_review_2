## Summary
This paper proposes a unified transformer-based Seq2Seq framework that jointly models expressive piano performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT) via disentangled score-content and performance-style latent representations. A secondary diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module generates stylistically appropriate style embeddings from score content alone, enabling alignment-free and annotation-free expressive rendering. Both objective metrics (Tables 1–3) and a subjective listening study with 11 trained participants (Figure 2) are reported.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel joint modeling of two inverse tasks with competitive results on both.** The paper is among the first to explicitly treat EPR and APT as a unified Seq2Seq problem without note-level alignment, achieving the best MUSTER E_avg (12.48) on APT (Table 1) and the best KL/MAE for velocity (1.76 / 10.33) on EPR among non-trivial baselines (Table 2), all with statistical significance (Wilcoxon, p<0.01).

- **Effective content-style disentanglement backed by quantitative and qualitative evidence.** The style representation z_s yields 77.46% composer classification accuracy vs. 29.99% from the content representation (Table 4). Clear composer and performer clusters emerge in Figure 3, and the style-transfer listening test (Figure 5) shows that transferred styles increase similarity without degrading quality — evidence of a smooth, interpretable style space.

- **Well-structured evaluation combining objective, subjective, and representational analyses.** The subjective test spans five composers and four perceptual dimensions (dynamics, tempo, style, overall) with 11 trained participants. Ours (Target) achieves the highest ratings across all attributes (Figure 2a). This multi-pronged evaluation design is notably stronger than is typical for the MIR subfield.

- **Alignment-free training scaling to large unpaired data.** The masked reconstruction objective (Eq. 4) enables training on 75,913 MuseScore scores and a YouTube-transcribed MIDI corpus without paired annotations, demonstrating practical scalability beyond the small ASAP dataset.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Core "mutual benefit" claim is evidentially confounded.** The central contribution (#1 in §1) is that joint EPR+APT modeling is mutually beneficial. The ablation compares EPR-Only (trained solely on ASAP) against Ours (Target) (trained on ASAP + large unpaired data + APT loss). As the paper itself states in §5.1: "the consistent improvement of Ours-Target over the EPR-Only variant indicates that joint modeling, *together with training on additional unpaired data*, leads to better EPR performance." Two variables change simultaneously — the APT objective and the unpaired data volume (75,913 scores + YouTube MIDI). Without a control that trains EPR-only on the same full data mixture but without the APT loss, it is impossible to attribute the observed improvement to joint training rather than simply more data. The claim that "mutual supervision" between EPR and APT is beneficial is the paper's top contribution but remains unproven by the experiments as designed.

- **PSR's core claim — style-score *compatibility* — is evaluated only through indirect proxies.** The PSR module's stated goal is to generate styles "compatible with the score's content," mimicking a pianist who selects an appropriate style for a given piece. The evidence is: (a) Figure 4, a 2D visual comparison showing era clustering is similar between PSR-generated and real performance embeddings; and (b) the overall listening test ratings in Figure 2. Neither proxy directly tests whether PSR styles are appropriate *for the specific score*: a romantic score rendered with baroque-style dynamics could still score acceptably on "overall quality" if the variance is natural. The era clusters in Figure 4 are assigned via GPT-4o mini metadata parsing (§5.3), not a held-out evaluation targeting style-content compatibility. The module's core proposition is thus asserted rather than directly verified.

### Minor

- **The pitch error E_p framing is misleading.** The paper states results are "comparable to the state-of-the-art APT system" (§5.1), but for pitch error E_p — the most fundamental APT metric — the model scores 3.08 vs. 2.02 for Liu et al. (2022) and 2.09 for Shibata et al. (2021), a 50–52% relative increase. The paper attributes this to its alignment-free formulation and this is a plausible explanation, but the framing as "competitive" without noting the E_p gap more directly is slightly overstated.

- **Blank footnote 2 omits the provenance of the unpaired performance dataset.** Section 4.1 states the YouTube-transcribed unpaired MIDI corpus was created with "a state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" (footnote 2 is blank in the submission). Since training with this data is part of the confounded comparison, the quality and size of this corpus matter. The model identity should be stated.

- **Table 4 disentanglement results lack chance-level baselines.** Style→Perf achieves 42.07% accuracy. Whether this is strong or weak depends on the number of performers and class distribution in ATEPP (49 pianists, §4.1). Reporting chance level (1/49 ≈ 2% for uniform) would make the result immediately interpretable without requiring external knowledge of the dataset structure.

- **Tang et al. (2023) is absent from comparative tables.** Tang et al. (2023) is cited in §2.1 and §4.3 as a contemporary transformer-based EPR system with comparable scope, but it does not appear in Table 2 or Table 3 with no stated reason for its exclusion.

### Trivial

- VirtuosoNet appears in Table 2 but is absent from Table 3 (alignment/insertion/missing metrics) without explanation. A brief note on why it was excluded from Table 3 would avoid confusion.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A clean ablation isolating the APT objective from additional unpaired data (e.g., training EPR-only with the same full data mixture but no APT loss) would directly address the main evidential gap and substantially strengthen the paper's top contribution.
- For PSR, a quantitative style-content compatibility test — such as measuring inter-era KL divergence between PSR-generated and real performance style embeddings, or asking participants whether the expressed style feels appropriate for the score genre — would turn the current indirect evidence into a direct test.
- Adding a genre-matching condition to the listening study ("does the style feel appropriate for this score?") rather than just overall quality would directly probe the PSR's core claim with minimal additional effort.

## Novel Insights

The observation that the style representation z_s achieves 77.46% composer classification accuracy (vs. 29.99% for content-based) suggests that professional pianists encode compositional identity into their performance style — not just personal style — to a much greater degree than typically assumed. If this pattern generalizes beyond ATEPP, it has implications for how we define and measure "performer identity" vs. "composer compatibility" in MIR: the two are substantially entangled in the learned style space, and the PSR hypothesis (that appropriate styles can be inferred from scores) may partly work because composers strongly constrain acceptable style envelopes.

## Suggestions

1. **Ablation fix:** Add a single EPR-only baseline trained on the full data mixture (ASAP + unpaired data, masked reconstruction only, no APT loss). This is the missing control and would either validate or qualify the joint-training claim.
2. **PSR evaluation:** Report cluster purity or inter-era KL divergence between PSR-generated and real performance embeddings in Figure 4 to quantify visual similarity, and add one subjective question targeting style-score compatibility directly.
3. **Table 4 baselines:** Report chance-level accuracy for both performer (1/49) and composer (1/25) to make identification results self-contained.
4. **E_p framing:** Acknowledge the 50% relative pitch error gap more directly while maintaining the alignment-free motivation; the current framing of "comparable" is potentially misleading to readers not scanning all sub-metrics.
