## Summary

TokenCount proposes a training-free framework for class-agnostic object counting that repurposes SAM without architectural modification. It contributes two components: (1) a probabilistic prompt generation stage that iteratively samples from a ReLU-softmax similarity map and suppresses already-explored regions, and (2) an output token-based verification stage that uses SAM's decoder output tokens (which fuse semantic and positional information) together with the TS-SS geometric similarity metric to classify candidate instances as positive or negative. The method is evaluated on FSC-147 and CARPK, achieving the best MAE among SAM-only training-free methods on FSC-147 and strong CARPK results.

---

## Strengths

- **Output token verification is a genuinely novel and mechanistically motivated contribution.** The paper correctly identifies that SAM's decoder output tokens—formed through bidirectional attention between prompt embeddings and image features—encode richer, fused semantic-and-positional representations than raw image embeddings. The ablation in Table 5 confirms this: output-token-space verification (MAE 16.25) outperforms image-embedding-space verification (MAE 16.94). The PCA visualizations in Figure 5 corroborate the claim qualitatively, showing cleaner positive/negative cluster separation in output-token space.

- **Competitive FSC-147 MAE among SAM-only training-free methods with comprehensive ablations.** Table 1 shows MAE of 16.25, compared to TFOC's 19.95 and C-Anything's 27.97 — a substantial improvement of 3.7 MAE points over the nearest SAM-only competitor without any auxiliary model. Tables 3–5 provide ablations over the verification metric, iteration/prompt count, and token space, giving the reader a reasonable understanding of each design choice's effect.

- **Probabilistic prompt generation is well-described and empirically validated via Table 4.** The iterative distribution update (zeroing out confirmed positives, down-weighting negatives) is a principled coverage strategy. Table 4 shows that MAE improves consistently as iterations and prompts increase, from 29.10 at (8 iter, 32 prompts) to 16.25 at (16 iter, 128 prompts), demonstrating the mechanism works as intended.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **RMSE does not improve and is worse than simpler SAM-only baselines.** Verified from Table 1: Ours RMSE = 133.11; TFOC RMSE = 132.16; C-Anything RMSE = 131.24. The paper bolds all metrics including RMSE, implying parity or improvement, yet the method's RMSE is strictly worse than both TFOC and C-Anything, two baselines the method is supposed to outperform. This indicates the method reduces average error (MAE) while leaving the high-error tail essentially unchanged. RMSE captures the failure tail and is not a minor metric—a method that does not improve RMSE over the simpler SAM-only baselines is not delivering "superior accuracy" across the board. The Discussion (Section 5) acknowledges degradation in "dense distributions of very small instances" but does not connect this limitation explicitly to the RMSE failure or quantify how large a fraction of the test set this affects.

- **CARPK exemplar protocol is non-standard and not disclosed in the comparison table.** Section 4.1 states: "we randomly selected 12 objects from the training set to use as exemplars." Standard few-shot counting protocol provides 3 bounding-box exemplars from within the test image itself. The method is given 4× as many exemplars from a different distribution (training-set objects). FamNet, BMNet+, SAFECOUNT, and other supervised baselines in Table 1 all follow the 3-in-image exemplar protocol. Since the method's CARPK result (MAE 4.68, beating all supervised methods) is its strongest empirical claim, the protocol mismatch is a material fairness concern that is not flagged anywhere in the table or caption. If the comparison is unfair due to more/better exemplars, the main CARPK conclusion collapses.

- **The abstract materially overclaims accuracy.** The abstract states: "outperforming existing training-based and training-free counting methods." Table 1 shows the method is outperformed on FSC-147 by LOCA (10.79 MAE), TFCAC (12.26), SAFECOUNT (14.32), BMNet+ (14.62), and PseCo (13.05) — all existing methods listed in the same table. The method only beats a subset of training-based methods (FamNet, C-DETR, CFOCNet+). The abstract should accurately limit this claim to "best among SAM-only training-free methods."

### Minor

- **Individual contributions of the two components are not cleanly isolated.** Tables 3 and 5 ablate the verification metric and token space, respectively, but there is no experiment comparing (a) grid/uniform prompts + TS-SS output-token verification vs. (b) probabilistic prompts + TS-SS output-token verification. Without this, it is impossible to quantify how much of the MAE improvement over TFOC is attributable to probabilistic prompt generation versus output-token verification alone. The paper claims both components contribute, and the iterative ablation in Table 4 suggests the prompt count matters, but the interaction is not isolated.

- **The TS-SS improvement over cosine similarity is marginal, and the theoretical justification is thin.** Table 3 shows cosine similarity achieves MAE 16.48 while TS-SS achieves 16.25, a difference of 0.23 MAE. The argument in Section 4.4 that "non-hybrid metrics have reached a performance plateau" is an interpretive leap from two data points that happen to be close. The TS-SS metric originates from a 2016 document-clustering paper; its suitability for SAM decoder tokens is not rigorously argued beyond noting that output tokens have both angular and magnitude components.

- **Critical hyperparameter (threshold 300) has no ablation.** Section 4.1 states "we set the token verification threshold to 300" and references "validation experiments" but provides no validation curve or sensitivity analysis. This threshold directly determines what gets counted, making it a key hyperparameter whose behavior should be characterized.

These are different operations at different precision requirements. The critique conflates them and is not supported by a specific paper claim.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation in this work is that SAM's decoder output tokens, which are traditionally treated as an internal representation for mask generation, carry richer discriminative information about object identity than the image encoder's embeddings. This is mechanistically sensible: output tokens accumulate both semantic content and prompt-conditioned positional context through bidirectional attention, making them more instance-specific. The insight that these tokens can be repurposed as exemplar-matching signatures for counting — without any additional training — represents a meaningful contribution to the toolbox of training-free methods. The remaining components (probabilistic prompt generation, TS-SS metric) are supportive engineering choices rather than primary innovations.

## Suggestions

1. **Clarify or standardize the CARPK protocol.** Report whether the paper uses 3 in-image exemplars (standard) or 12 training-set exemplars, and add a comparison under the standard protocol to make the CARPK result directly comparable to other methods.
2. **Report per-difficulty or per-density RMSE stratification** on FSC-147 to explain why MAE improves while RMSE does not, and where the method fails.
3. **Add a direct ablation isolating probabilistic prompts:** run grid prompts + TS-SS output-token verification vs. probabilistic prompts + TS-SS output-token verification at the same total prompt budget.
4. **Fix the abstract:** narrow the claim to "best MAE among SAM-only training-free methods on FSC-147" rather than "outperforms existing training-based and training-free counting methods."
5. **Include a sensitivity analysis for the threshold (300)** and temperature (τ = 0.066) to confirm these choices are not tightly overfitted to the validation set.
