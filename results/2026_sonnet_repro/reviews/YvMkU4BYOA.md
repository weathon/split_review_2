## Summary

XBIC is a score-based method for causal structure learning on purely discrete data that augments BIC's complexity penalty with edge-specific directional evidence derived from aggregated Shapley values of per-node XGBoost classifiers. For each node $X_i$, a classifier $f_i(X_{\setminus i})$ is trained and mean Shapley attributions $\bar{\phi}_{j\to i}$ are computed over confidently predicted instances; the XBIC score soft-divides BIC's penalty by $\exp(w \cdot \text{SHAP}(G))$, reducing penalty for edges with strong directional attribution support. Evaluated on 700 runs across 10 bnlearn benchmark networks (6–76 nodes) and 7 sample-size regimes, XBIC reports aggregate oriented-edge $F_1$ improvements of +5.6% over BIC-HC, +9.6% over GES, and +20.9% over PC.

---

## Strengths

- **Novel integration of local Shapley attributions into score-based causal discovery for discrete data.** As stated in Section 2.3, no prior work has used local feature attributions as an edge-specific, directional modulation of a score-based objective (BIC) for purely discrete data; this distinguishes XBIC from work that uses known causal graphs to interpret SHAP values (Frye et al., 2020; Heskes et al., 2020).

- **Large and systematic empirical study.** The evaluation covers 10 benchmark discrete networks (6–76 nodes), 7 sample-size regimes, and 10 repetitions per cell, totaling 700 runs. Table 2 provides per-network, per-sample-size F₁ deltas, and Table 4 aggregates them, offering a thorough empirical picture rather than cherry-picked results.

- **Graceful degradation to standard BIC.** Equation (2) and the consistency remark in Section 3 prove that when SHAP(G) = 0 or w = 0, XBIC reduces exactly to BIC and the penalty still grows as O(log N). This is confirmed empirically: on low-signal settings (e.g., Asia and Survey at smallest sample sizes), Table 2 shows zero deltas, meaning XBIC does not hurt performance when attributions are unreliable.

- **Confidence-threshold robustness demonstrated.** Section 4.1 reports that varying the confidence threshold τ from 0.7 to 0.95 changes downstream F₁ by less than 1% on average, establishing that this hyperparameter does not require careful tuning.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

**1. Theoretical grounding for the directional asymmetry is absent.** The core claim of XBIC is that $|\bar{\phi}_{j\to i}| \gg |\bar{\phi}_{i\to j}|$ provides directional evidence that $X_j \to X_i$ (Section 3.2: "Intuitively, if $|\bar{\phi}_{1\to 2}| \gg |\bar{\phi}_{2\to 1}|$, the edge $X_1 \to X_2$ has stronger directional support"). But both $X_j$ and $X_i$ are predictive of each other in any observational distribution generated by a discrete BN—there is no a priori reason why a true parent's Shapley value in the child's model should dominate the child's Shapley value in the parent's model. The paper acknowledges in the Limitations that "formal analysis of the weighting mechanism… is an important direction" (Section, Limitations (iv)), but still presents the method as principled. Without a direct empirical validation of the asymmetry itself—e.g., measuring the fraction of true-direction pairs where $|\bar{\phi}_{j\to i}| > |\bar{\phi}_{i\to j}|$—the method remains an unvalidated heuristic whose directional interpretation is a conjecture. This is the question the entire method rests on.

**2. Random PDAG completion artificially inflates the advantage over PC.** Section 4.1 states: "For baselines that return a PDAG, we complete it to a DAG by randomly orienting undirected edges (while preserving acyclicity) before computing directed-edge metrics." For edges that are genuinely unresolvable by CI evidence alone (i.e., equivalence-class–ambiguous), random orientation gives roughly 50% precision on that subset. This artificially depresses PC's oriented-edge F₁, making the reported 20.9% advantage over PC partly a methodological artifact rather than a pure reflection of XBIC's directional recovery power. Principled PDAG completion (e.g., by BIC scoring), or separate reporting of skeleton F₁ vs. orientation F₁, would give a cleaner picture.

**3. "Consistent gains" overstated for the two largest and most practically relevant networks.** The abstract and introduction claim "consistent gains" across networks. Table 2 (vs BIC column) shows Win95pts (76 nodes) at: 0.0, 0.0, +0.02, +0.07, +0.07, 0.0, **−0.09** across sample sizes; Hepar2 (70 nodes) at: +0.01, +0.01, +0.01, 0.0, 0.0, **−0.02**, 0.0. XBIC is worse than plain BIC at large samples on Win95pts and provides essentially no benefit on Hepar2 throughout. These are the largest networks, the hardest test cases, and the ones the paper positions as most relevant (healthcare, insurance—Section Conclusions). The stated explanation—"few confident instances"—is speculative and unsupported by any analysis of $|S_i|$ sizes on these networks.

### Minor

**4. Hyperparameter w has no prospective selection procedure.** The paper evaluates w ∈ {1, 2, 3} and identifies w = 2 as the best setting from aggregate F₁ over all 700 runs (Table 4). No held-out or cross-validated procedure for choosing w is described, meaning a practitioner deploying XBIC has no principled way to select it. The sensitivity of the precision–recall trade-off to w (Figure 2) makes this non-trivial.

**5. Consistency remark does not establish DAG recovery consistency.** The remark in Section 3 proves only that the penalty preserves O(log N) growth, which is a necessary but not sufficient condition for consistent DAG recovery. The relevant question—whether XBIC returns the correct DAG in probability as N → ∞—is not addressed. This is acknowledged in Limitations (iv) but the remark is framed as preserving "large-sample consistency," which is misleadingly strong.

**6. Absolute Shapley values used without justification.** Equation (3) uses $|\bar{\phi}_{j\to i}|$ rather than signed values or another aggregation. SHAP values can be negative (a feature decreases the predicted-class probability), and the rationale for discarding sign information is not explained. This is a nontrivial design choice affecting what the directional signal measures.

### Trivial

None identified beyond the above.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Validate the directional asymmetry directly.** Compute $|\bar{\phi}_{j\to i}|$ and $|\bar{\phi}_{i\to j}|$ for all edges in the ground-truth DAG across benchmark networks and report the fraction of pairs where the true direction has the larger value. This would either confirm the heuristic's reliability or expose its conditions of applicability, and would substantially strengthen the paper's motivation.

- **Disaggregate skeleton F₁ and orientation F₁.** Reporting these separately would clarify whether XBIC's gains come from better skeleton recovery, better orientation of equivalence-class–ambiguous edges, or both—directly testing the stated contribution.

- **Investigate why XBIC degrades on Win95pts and Hepar2.** Reporting $|S_i|$ sizes and Shapley value magnitudes on those networks would replace the speculative "few confident instances" explanation with direct evidence.

- **MMHC comparison.** MMHC is a standard hybrid baseline for discrete BN learning and is applicable to several benchmark networks (Alarm, Win95pts, Hepar2). Including it would contextualize XBIC against a broader set of methods.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation across both reviewers is that the empirical gains from XBIC appear primarily on medium-to-large networks with moderate-to-large samples—exactly the regime where the XGBoost classifiers have enough data to produce confident predictions ($\kappa^{(n)} \geq \tau$) and thus non-zero SHAP signals. The inverse—failure on the two largest networks (Win95pts, Hepar2)—suggests a U-shaped reliability curve: too-small networks/samples → no attributional signal → BIC parity; medium networks → attributions informative → gains; very large/dense networks → either overfitting in classifiers, non-informative asymmetries, or SHAP aggregation noise → degradation. This observation, if validated empirically, would both explain the mixed results and provide actionable guidance on where XBIC should be deployed.

## Suggestions

1. Add a direct empirical test of the directional asymmetry assumption: for each ground-truth edge $j \to i$, compute whether $|\bar{\phi}_{j\to i}| > |\bar{\phi}_{i\to j}|$ and report this accuracy across networks and sample sizes.
2. Replace random PDAG completion for PC with BIC-based completion, or separately report skeleton F₁ and orientation F₁, to disentangle methodological artifact from genuine directional improvement.
3. For Win95pts and Hepar2, report $|S_i|$ statistics and mean $|\bar{\phi}_{j\to i}|$ magnitudes to empirically support or refute the "few confident instances" explanation for degradation.
4. Describe a cross-validated procedure for selecting w (e.g., via a small held-out split within each run), so practitioners can use the method without test-set inspection.
