## Summary

RedSage is a comprehensive open-source cybersecurity LLM system paper that contributes: (1) CyberFineWeb, an 11.7B-token continual pretraining corpus built via ModernBERT-based web filtering; (2) RedSage-Seed, 28.6K curated high-quality cybersecurity documents; (3) RedSage-Conv, 266K multi-turn SFT conversations generated by a two-stage Planner-Augmenter pipeline; (4) RedSage-Bench, a 30K MCQ + 240 open-ended QA benchmark spanning knowledge, skills, and tool proficiency; and (5) the RedSage-8B model trained through continued pretraining, SFT, and DPO, achieving a consistent +3–5.59 point improvement over Qwen3-8B on external cybersecurity benchmarks while preserving general capability.

---

## Strengths

- **Strong, consistent performance on five independent external cybersecurity benchmarks (Table 5).** RedSage-8B-DPO achieves a mean of 81.10% across CTI-Bench, CyberMetric, SECURE, SecBench, and SecEval — surpassing Qwen3-8B-Instruct (75.71%) by +5.39 points and all prior cybersecurity-tuned 8B models. The improvement holds across diverse task types (MCQ, Root-Cause Mapping, CVSS prediction). This is the paper's primary evidence of genuine generalization and is not contaminated by overlap with the training sources.

- **Per-stage ablation demonstrates each component contributes independently (Table 5).** RedSage-8B-CFW lifts the mean to 82.66, Seed to 84.45, and the combined Base to 84.56, showing CyberFineWeb and the curated seed provide complementary strengths. The full pipeline (Base → Ins → DPO) continues to improve on instruct benchmarks, confirming all stages matter.

- **General-knowledge replay effectively prevents catastrophic forgetting (Table 6).** RedSage base models maintain competitive scores on MMLU (78.63 vs. 78.73 for Qwen3-8B-Base) and ARC-C (66.72 vs. 68.09) while substantially improving cybersecurity coverage, validating the 30% FineWeb-Edu replay strategy described in Section 3.1.

- **Fully open release of all artifacts.** The paper releases the model, pretraining corpus, SFT data, benchmark, and code — a significant contrast with Foundation-Sec (closed data), PRIMUS (limited SFT), and DeepHat (no pipeline release), and a genuine community resource.

- **RedSage-Bench expands evaluation coverage.** As shown in Table 1, all prior cybersecurity benchmarks omit tool proficiency assessment and open-ended qualitative scoring. RedSage-Bench's inclusion of CLI and Kali Linux tool tasks and the LLM-as-judge rubric fills a documented gap, confirmed by the violin plots in Figure 6 that reveal tool-use as the primary difficulty dimension.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Train-benchmark contamination on RedSage-Bench (Section 3.3).** Both the SFT training corpus (RedSage-Conv) and the evaluation benchmark (RedSage-Bench) are generated from the same source documents — RedSage-Seed. The decontamination step described in Section 3.3 removes only surface near-duplicates with cosine similarity > 0.9 at the query level ("This eliminates 2.96% of data relative to the benchmark size"). This is not a knowledge-contamination audit. A model trained on CAPEC attack patterns, MITRE ATT&CK entries, and HackTricks write-ups will outperform models that have not seen these documents *because it has been trained on the same material the benchmark was generated from*, independent of whether any query string is duplicated. The paper's own MCQ analysis confirms this mechanism: "RedSage-8B-Seed achieves the best base result (85.21), demonstrating better alignment with the curated Seed data" (Section 4.1). The external benchmark results in Table 5 are not subject to this concern, and those results are the paper's primary credible evidence. However, the RedSage-Bench results in Table 4 and Figure 6 should not be presented as held-out evidence of generalization — the paper currently frames them as primary evaluation without adequate caveat.

### Minor

- **Suspicious Qwen3-8B HellaSwag score on general benchmarks (Table 6).** The paper reports Qwen3-8B (non-reasoning mode) achieving 56.70 on HellaSwag, against 79.62 for Qwen3-8B-Base. This 23-point drop is anomalous: HellaSwag uses log-likelihood completion, and instruction templates for hybrid models can interfere with this evaluation mode. If this is an evaluation artifact, RedSage's headline "+5.05 points on Open LLM Leaderboard tasks" (which compares against Foundation-Sec-8B-Instruct at 69.28, not Qwen3-8B) is numerically accurate, but the surrounding narrative that RedSage surpasses Qwen3-8B on general benchmarks is muddied. The evaluation setup should explicitly verify and document how hybrid model instruction templates interact with log-likelihood evaluation to ensure comparisons are fair.

- **LLM-as-judge circularity for open-ended QA (Section 3.3, Figure 6).** The 240-item open-ended QA benchmark uses Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct as judges (footnote 2), the same models that generated the reference answers and the SFT training data. Models fine-tuned to mimic these teachers' output distributions will tend to score higher on stylistic quality metrics independent of factual accuracy. Figure 6 shows a notable inconsistency: RedSage-8B-DPO achieves the highest correctness (0.73) but lower quality score (7.07) than both Qwen3-8B (7.50) and RedSage-8B-Ins (7.43), which is unexplained and consistent with this stylistic bias concern.

- **Abstract does not name the baseline for "+5.05 points" (Abstract).** The claim "+5.05 points on Open LLM Leaderboard tasks" refers to the gap over Foundation-Sec-8B-Instruct (74.33 − 69.28 = 5.05), the best prior cybersecurity-tuned model. This is a fair and meaningful comparison. But the abstract does not specify the baseline, and a reader would naturally assume a matched general-purpose model (Qwen3-8B, which actually scores 65.92 in the same table). The baseline should be named explicitly.

- **Ethical use treatment is insufficient for a model trained on offensive content (Section 5).** The model is explicitly trained on CAPEC attack patterns, penetration testing write-ups, HackTricks techniques, and Kali Linux tooling. Section 5 devotes a single sentence to dual-use risks: "we emphasize the importance of responsible application and good security practices to promote ethical use." For a fully open-released model and training corpus with this content profile, more substantive treatment — including what content filtering (if any) is incorporated, and any use-case guidance — is warranted.

### Trivial

- **240 open-ended benchmark items is small for category-level breakdown (Section 4.1).** If items are split evenly across four categories (knowledge_general, knowledge_frameworks, skill_offensive, tools_kali), each cell in the category-level analysis represents approximately 60 examples. The violin plots in Figure 6 show wide distributions, making mean comparisons without confidence intervals or standard deviations hard to interpret. Even approximate error bars would strengthen the comparison.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Ablation comparing the agentic augmentation pipeline against a simple QA reformatting baseline.** The paper shows the full pipeline improves over the base model but does not isolate whether the Planner-Augmenter framework specifically adds value beyond reformatting seed items as simple single-turn Q&A. An ablation of "seed-as-QA vs. seed-as-agentic-conv" would make the pipeline contribution concrete.

- **Held-out source split for RedSage-Bench.** Reserving a subset of seed sources entirely from both training and benchmark generation would allow reporting benchmark results on data with no shared source, providing unambiguous evidence of generalization. This would substantially strengthen the benchmark's credibility.

- **Extended human verification on MCQs.** Auditing ~500–1000 randomly sampled MCQs with expert reviewers would allow the authors to report human agreement rates with the LLM quality scorer (Llama-3.3-70B), substantiating the quality-scoring column in Table 1.

## Novel Insights

The paper surfaces a practical tension inherent in open-domain cybersecurity LLM evaluation: when the same source corpus (RedSage-Seed) is used both to train the model and to generate the benchmark, semantic decontamination at the query level is insufficient — the model may simply benefit from source coverage rather than generalization. The paper's own observation that "RedSage-8B-Seed achieves the best base result...demonstrating better alignment with the curated Seed data" inadvertently confirms this mechanism. This motivates a methodological recommendation that future cybersecurity benchmark papers treat source-level train/test exclusion (not just query deduplication) as a first-class design requirement.

## Suggestions

1. **Foreground external benchmark results as the primary evidence of cybersecurity capability** in the abstract and Section 4, with an explicit statement that RedSage-Bench results should be interpreted as in-distribution evidence rather than held-out generalization.
2. **Verify and report the Qwen3-8B HellaSwag evaluation configuration**: confirm whether the log-likelihood evaluation mode is applied consistently across all models, and document how hybrid model templates interact with completion-based evaluation.
3. **Name the "+5.05 points" baseline in the abstract** (Foundation-Sec-8B-Instruct) to avoid reader confusion about which model class is surpassed on general benchmarks.
4. **Expand Section 5** with concrete statements about what content-safety measures (if any) are embedded in the released model, and what the intended use-case boundaries are for the offensive-capability materials.
5. **Add error bounds** (standard deviation or 95% CI) to the open-ended QA category-level comparisons in Figure 6.
