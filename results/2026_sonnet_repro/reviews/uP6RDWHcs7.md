## Summary

Marginal Flow defines a density model by marginalizing out latent parameters $\mathbf{w}$ drawn from a learnable distribution $q_\theta(\mathbf{w})$, which is generated by pushing base samples through an unconstrained neural network. The resulting model, defined by a finite Monte Carlo sum (Eq. 2), is evaluated by forward passes alone (no Jacobians, ODE solvers, or invertibility constraints), yielding fast training and inference. The framework is demonstrated on 2D synthetic benchmarks, simulation-based inference, distributions over positive-definite (Wishart) matrices, and manifold learning in VAE latent spaces.

---

## Strengths

1. **Efficient runtime** (Figure 3): Marginal Flow is orders of magnitude faster than Normalizing Flows, Flow Matching, and Free-form Flows for both sampling and density evaluation across dimensions up to $10^5$, with NF and FM hitting OOM at high dimensions. This is a concrete, reproducible advantage.

2. **Marginalization prevents collapse to a finite GMM** (Figure 1, Section 2.1): The paper makes a clear and well-illustrated argument that *resampling* $\mathbf{w}$ from $q_\theta(\mathbf{w})$ at each iteration forces the network to learn a generative model for mixture components rather than optimizing a fixed finite set — producing smooth learned densities even with modest $N_c$.

3. **Plug-and-play kernel flexibility** (Section 4.3): Swapping the Gaussian $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$ for a Wishart allows the same framework to learn distributions on positive-definite matrices, including $100\times100$ SPD matrices where NFs are computationally infeasible. Figure 9 shows a factor of ~100× better test KL than NF on the $10\times10$ setting while simultaneously reconstructing the 1D Wishart manifold.

4. **Fast convergence** (Figure 7): On five 2D benchmarks, Marginal Flow reaches higher test log-likelihood in substantially less wall-clock time than NF, FM, and FFF — offering a practical advantage beyond just inference speed.

5. **Manifold learning built in** (Figure 4): By choosing a lower-dimensional base distribution ($m < d$), the framework learns a manifold simultaneously with the density, recovering the correct 1D spiral manifold where competitors fail or produce incorrect manifolds.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The "exact likelihood" claim in Table 1 and the abstract is misleading** — this is the paper's primary differentiator and it does not hold as stated. The model is formally defined in Eq. 1 as a marginal integral $q_\theta(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{w}\sim q_\theta(\mathbf{w})}[q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})]$, which is not closed-form. Eq. 2 approximates it via a finite MC sum of freshly resampled $\{w_{\theta,i}\}$. The paper itself states: "the parameters $w_{\theta,i}$ are not fixed themselves but rather *resampled* from $q_\theta(\mathbf{w})$ at each iteration." Consequently, evaluating $q_\theta(\mathbf{x})$ at the same point $\mathbf{x}$ on two separate calls yields two different numbers; the estimate has variance $O(1/N_c)$. In contrast, a trained NF gives a deterministically exact, reproducible density via the change-of-variables formula. Listing both in the same "Efficient exact likelihood ✓" row of Table 1 conflates very different senses of "exact." This matters practically: training dynamics depend on $N_c$ in ways the paper never analyzes, and practitioners using the model for likelihood-ratio tests or posterior estimation need to know the approximation error. The method is better described as providing *efficient approximate likelihood* via a learned continuous mixture model — a genuinely useful but different property.

2. **No ablation or analysis of $N_c$** — $N_c$ is the single hyperparameter that controls the cost/accuracy tradeoff of the model (it directly scales both runtime and density estimate variance), yet there are zero ablations on $N_c$ in the paper, no discussion of how to choose it, and the value used in Figure 3's runtime comparison is not stated in the main text (deferred to the appendix). Without this analysis, the central comparison to NF in Figure 3 is uninterpretable for practitioners (a large $N_c$ could make Marginal Flow slower than NF for the same density quality), and the paper's core claim cannot be fairly evaluated.

3. **State-of-the-art SBI results are entirely in the appendix** (Section 4.2): The paper devotes substantial main-text space to qualitative 2D visualizations (Figures 6, 8) and image-latent experiments (Figures 10, 11) while claiming "Marginal Flow achieves state-of-the-art results" for SBI — a quantitative claim substantiated only in Figure 14 of the appendix "due to space constraints." This inversion of priorities makes the most concrete performance claim unverifiable from the main paper and suggests the strongest result is being deprioritized relative to more illustrative but qualitative findings.

### Minor

1. **Universality assertion is unqualified** (Section 2.1): The claim that "the resulting marginal $q(\mathbf{x})$ is universal for many families of distributions $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$, e.g. if $q(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{w})$ is a kernel (Micchelli et al., 2006)" is stated without any conditions on $q_\theta(\mathbf{w})$. Universality of the class of learnable distributions also requires that $f_\theta$ can represent any distribution on $\mathbf{w}$, which depends on network architecture and is not guaranteed by the Micchelli reference alone. This claim should be either properly qualified or given a proof sketch.

2. **Multi-modal comparison uses only 150 training points** (Figure 5): The demonstration that Marginal Flow captures all 5 modes while FM/NF/FFF fail is compelling but relies on an unusually small training set. The caption notes "for a fair comparison, all models use a uniform base distribution," but a uniform base is well-suited to Marginal Flow (treating base samples as seeds for mixture components) and disadvantageous for flow-based models (which must map a unimodal base through a smooth bijection to cover multiple modes). The comparison as-is may reflect base distribution choice rather than a fundamental architectural advantage.

3. **Wishart underfitting claim is unquantified** (Section 4.3, Figure 9): The conclusion that NF "underfits" on $10\times10$ Wishart mixtures is shown only via a bar chart, without specifying the NF architecture, training time, or hyperparameter tuning effort. It is unclear whether the gap reflects a principled architectural limitation (plausible) or insufficient tuning.

4. **MNIST/JAFFE results are entirely qualitative** (Section 4.4): The manifold learning in image latent spaces is demonstrated visually but not quantitatively. Claims of "disentanglement" in Figure 10 and smooth interpolation in Figure 11 are subjective without at least a nearest-neighbor or interpolation quality metric.

## Novel Insights

The most genuinely novel observation from the combined reviews is that the method's "exactness" of likelihood is relative to the finite-sample definition in Eq. 2, not to the true marginal in Eq. 1. This creates an interesting design space question: what is the minimum $N_c$ needed for density estimates to be accurate enough for a given downstream task (posterior inference, test log-likelihood ranking)? The paper implicitly treats $N_c$ as a nuisance hyperparameter, but in principle the variance-$N_c$ curve could be used to derive a principled cost/accuracy tradeoff that would distinguish Marginal Flow from both exact NFs (zero variance, high cost-per-evaluation) and score-based models (no density at all). Formalizing this tradeoff would turn the paper's biggest current weakness into a strength.
