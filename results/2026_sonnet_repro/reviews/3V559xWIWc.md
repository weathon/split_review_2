## Summary

The paper proposes two complementary improvements to tree-based speculative decoding (SpD): TALF (tree-aware loss function), which aggregates cross-entropy loss across all dynamic tree nodes generated by the target LLM rather than only the most probable token at each position, and SALF (stopping at low further gains), a principled early-stopping criterion for tree construction backed by a provable monotonicity guarantee (Theorem 1). Together, SALF & TALF achieve 15.6–39.4% and 6.5–24.4% wall-clock speedups over EAGLE-2 and HASS across three Llama-based target models, five benchmarks, and both greedy and non-greedy sampling.

---

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated diagnostic for the core claim.** Figure 2(b) shows that EAGLE and HASS produce draft models with degrading top-1 accuracy and rising ECE when conditioned on lower-ranked tokens (ranked 2nd–5th), while TALF recovers roughly 5% accuracy and 0.05 ECE at those ranks. Figure 2(a) establishes that such lower-ranked tokens account for ~45% of tree nodes, making this degradation nontrivial. This decomposition cleanly supports the case for a tree-aware loss.

- **TALF improves τ consistently and independently of tree construction method.** Table 2 shows TALF boosts τ over HASS by 7.2%, 7.3%, and 3.5% under beam search, optimal tree search, and SALF, respectively, across all five benchmarks for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B. This cross-method consistency is strong evidence that the benefit comes from improved draft model calibration rather than interaction with any particular drafting strategy.

- **SALF with provable stopping guarantee.** Theorem 1 proves that the probability sum of candidate nodes decreases monotonically across drafting iterations, justifying the threshold-based early-stopping rule. Table 4 confirms the expected τ–speedup tradeoff curve, with th=0.5–0.6 yielding the best end-to-end performance while reducing unnecessary drafting.

- **Robust end-to-end speedups.** Table 1 shows consistent improvements across all three models, five tasks, and both temperature settings: 15.6–39.4% over EAGLE-2 and 6.5–24.4% over HASS. The improvements are larger for stronger models (Llama3-8B, DeepSeek), which aligns with the paper's stated intuition that stronger targets are harder for misaligned draft models to approximate.

- **Drop-in upgrade.** SALF & TALF use the identical draft model architecture as EAGLE, requiring no structural changes — only a new training objective and drafting algorithm. This substantially lowers the adoption barrier and isolates the gains to the proposed components.

- **Table 3 (k ablation) systematically supports the k=4 default.** τ increases monotonically as top-k is widened from 1 to 4 across all three tested benchmarks, and TALF with k=1 is nearly equivalent to HASS, confirming that the improvement is driven by tree breadth in training.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Component ablation (Table 2) is limited to a single model at a single temperature.** The decomposition of SALF vs. TALF contributions is shown only for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B at T=1. The end-to-end gains in Table 1 vary substantially across models (e.g., Llama3-8B gains 35–39% over EAGLE-2 vs. 28% for DeepSeek at T=0), suggesting that the relative contribution of TALF and SALF may shift with model and temperature. Without Table-2-equivalent data for at least one additional model, the general claim that "TALF and SALF each independently contribute" is supported by only one data point.

- **Training protocol introduces an epoch-count confound for Llama models.** For Llama2-7B and Llama3-8B, EAGLE is trained for 10 epochs, and then TALF trains for an additional 3 epochs on top of that checkpoint (§4.1). The paper justifies this by framing HASS/TALF as replacement objectives, and the DeepSeek experiment uses equal wall-clock time, which is more principled. However, there is no ablation for the Llama models showing that simply training EAGLE for 13 total epochs does not recover a significant fraction of the TALF gain. This gap makes the magnitude of the Llama results less attributable to the proposed objective change.

### Minor

- **Removal of the regression loss L_reg lacks ablation support.** Section 3.2 states: "Unlike EAGLE and HASS, TALF does not use a regression loss for feature alignment. In our experiments, training solely on the token probability distributions across multiple nodes was sufficient for the model to learn to use features in an autoregressive manner, yielding better performance." No quantitative comparison (e.g., TALF with vs. without L_reg) is given. Given that EAGLE specifically motivates L_reg for hidden-state alignment, its removal without ablation leaves the method underspecified.

- **The SALF default threshold (th=0.6) is weakly justified.** Table 4 shows th=0.5 strictly dominates th=0.6 for DeepSeek across all three reported benchmarks (2.62× vs. 2.59× mean speedup). The paper's justification is "more consistent performance improvements for the tested target LLMs," but Table 4's sensitivity analysis covers only one model. Showing threshold sensitivity across all three models, or acknowledging that th=0.5 may be preferable for similar models, would better support the default choice.

- **No run-to-run variance reported.** All speedup numbers in Table 1 are point estimates. On a PCIe A100, timing can vary with memory pressure and interconnect utilization. The improvements are large enough that significance is likely, but at least a brief characterization of variance would strengthen the empirical section.

### Trivial

- **Figure 2(a) caption ambiguity.** The caption says the proportion chart is "for EAGLE, HASS, and TALF," but the figure description shows a single set of bars. Since token rank proportions are determined by the tree construction algorithm at inference (not the loss function), it is unclear whether bars represent an aggregate or separate traces. A clarification would help readers interpret the motivating analysis.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Report TALF preprocessing time.** The paper notes that the target model precomputes the tree structure once before training and that it is reused across epochs (§3.2). However, no absolute or relative preprocessing time is reported. For practitioners deciding between TALF and EAGLE/HASS, this is a central practical quantity. A single table comparing preprocessing time per training sample would complete the picture.

- **Quantitative link between per-rank accuracy gains and τ.** Figure 2(b) shows TALF achieves ~5% accuracy improvement for ranks 2–5, but the paper does not connect this to the τ gains in Table 2. A back-of-envelope model (proportion at each rank × accuracy gain at that rank → expected τ improvement) would directly validate the causal story.

- **Evaluation on non-Llama architectures.** All three models are Llama-based (Llama-2, Llama-3, and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, which uses a Llama-3 architecture). Broader evaluation on Mistral, Qwen, or Gemma-family models would strengthen generality claims, though the paper is honest about its Llama focus.

- **70B-class experiments.** SpD's benefit grows as the target model scales. Demonstrating SALF & TALF on a 70B model would establish practical relevance at the scale most impactful commercially.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most distinctive analytical contribution is the diagnostic decomposition in Figure 2(b): it cleanly separates HASS's failure mode (marginal or negative gains for lower-ranked tokens) from TALF's fix. This reveals a second axis of training-inference misalignment in tree-based SpD that is orthogonal to what HASS addressed — while HASS corrects the *sequential* misalignment (using target features in training vs. draft features at inference), TALF corrects the *structural* misalignment (training on linear sequences vs. inferring over trees). The observation that ~45% of tree nodes are lower-ranked tokens, combined with the ECE/accuracy evidence, provides a crisp mechanistic explanation for why TALF works where HASS saturates. This diagnostic framework — evaluating draft model quality stratified by token rank — could be a reusable evaluation tool for future SpD work.
