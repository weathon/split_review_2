## Summary

PELICAN is a two-stage adaptive tutoring framework that (1) diagnoses a student's knowledge state through a "successor-first" hierarchical probing strategy with an expert-assistant-verifier pipeline, and (2) provides personalized tutoring by selecting instructional strategies via a fast/slow dual-system mechanism that simulates future dialogue paths. Experiments on 184 Gaokao exam questions include both an LLM-simulated evaluation and a real-student study with 169 high schoolers.

---

## Strengths

- **Strong cognitive diagnosis performance**: Table 1 shows PELICAN achieves F1 of 94.31, Precision 94.93, Recall 94.29, in just 5.83 average dialogue rounds — meaningfully superior to all baselines (best competitor, No-Pipeline, achieves 93.08 F1 at 5.84 rounds, while Free-Prompt reaches only 74.18 F1). This is the paper's cleanest empirical result.

- **Real-student evaluation includes meaningful R_coverage gain**: Table 6 shows PELICAN achieving R_coverage of 70.04 in the human study, versus 63.91 for the best baseline (Socratic), representing a ~9.6% relative improvement. This gap is substantive and provides real-world grounding beyond the simulated evaluation. The human Appropriateness (4.23), Inspiration (4.33), and Sentiment (4.42) scores also clearly exceed all baselines.

- **Inclusion of 169 real students**: In a field where most comparable papers rely entirely on LLM-simulated evaluations, conducting a human study at all is a genuine differentiator.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **Abstract headline claims (+18.7% critical thinking stimulation; +22.4% task completion) cannot be traced to any reported result.** Searching Tables 1–6: the closest match for "critical thinking stimulation" is Inspiration in Table 2 (PELICAN=4.21 vs. Socratic=3.99, ~5.5% relative gain; vs. Cot-Bridge=3.26, ~29% relative gain). The closest match for "task completion" from success rates in Table 6 is PELICAN 86.8% vs. Stepwise 86.5% (0.3pp) or vs. Socratic 80.3% (6.5pp). Neither comparison yields 18.7% or 22.4%. The selection of baselines for these percentages is nowhere disclosed. These two numbers appear as the primary evidence in the abstract and introduction and do not correspond to transparently reported comparisons. This directly undermines the paper's lead credibility.

- **Unexplained numerical discrepancy between Table 2 (main results) and Tables 3–4 (ablation/backbone).** PELICAN's R_coverage in Table 2 is 72.36; in Table 3 it is 54.84 (−17.5pp). F_frequency similarly drops from 72.06 to 61.47. Table 4 (backbone ablation) also shows 54.84, confirming the ablation uses a different configuration or subset than the main experiment, yet the paper provides no explanation. Since the ablation's purpose is to quantify the contribution of each module within the same evaluation framework, the magnitude of the module contributions (e.g., R_coverage gaining 10.9pp from 43.94 to 54.84 in Table 3) cannot be compared to the main results and has no grounding in the numbers that constitute the paper's headline claim. Without an explanation (e.g., a smaller random subset), the ablation conclusions are uninterpretable.

- **Primary evaluation in Table 2 is a closed LLM-to-LLM loop.** The teacher is GPT-4o, student responses are simulated (Appendix G), and the GPT-based quality scores (Suitability, Logic, Inspiration, Reliability, Overall) are generated by GPT-4o judging GPT-4o-generated dialogues. The "hard" metrics R_coverage and F_frequency measure whether the *teacher* addressed the correct knowledge points — they are properties of teacher output, not student learning. The paper presents Table 2 as its primary comparative result, but a model in this evaluation loop evaluating its own family's outputs cannot serve as reliable evidence of real student benefit.

### Minor

- **Human study success rate gap is tiny and statistically untested in the main text.** Table 6 shows PELICAN 86.8% vs. Stepwise 86.5% (0.3pp) and Free-Prompt 85.2% (1.6pp). The paper states ANOVA analysis is in Appendix I, but neither p-values, confidence intervals, nor effect sizes appear in the main text. For a dataset of 169 students and 6 conditions, whether PELICAN's success rate is statistically distinguishable from the nearest baseline is a basic question; the paper's claim that results "confirm the effectiveness of the tutoring approach" on the basis of 0.3pp is not well-supported by the reported numbers alone.

- **Strategy distribution contradicts the personalization claim.** Figure 4 / Section 4.4 data: six of nine strategies (Suggestion 2/2/2%, Confirmation 5/5/5%, Correction 8/8/8%, Open Question 5/5/5%, Closed Question 5/5/5%, Simplification 10/10/10%, Decomposition 12/12/12%) are *identical* across Low, Medium, and High cognitive levels. Only Analogies (22/18/15%) and, marginally, Explanation (32/33/30%) vary. The paper's claim that "higher-level students use more questioning strategies" is not borne out by the numbers (Closed Question and Open Question are both flat at 5%). This inconsistency between the claim and the reported distribution weakens the evidence for meaningful personalization of strategy selection.

- **Slow-thinking mechanism is shallower than its framing implies.** With M=1 (slow thinking activates after the *first* unresolved round), k=2 iterations, and m=2 candidates per node, the Simulated Teaching Tree evaluates at most 4 leaf paths total (depth 2, breadth 2). The scoring function in Equation 5 — score = 1 − λ×(d−1) — reduces to a binary {1, 1−λ} since d ∈ {1, 2}, which is equivalent to choosing the strategy that succeeds in fewer simulation steps. The MCTS-style framing is disproportionate to the actual computation, and the paper provides no ablation varying k or m to demonstrate that this specific tree configuration outperforms simply prompting for the single best strategy.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most actionable observation — implicit rather than foregrounded — is that the R_coverage gap between PELICAN and baselines in the human study (70.04 vs. 63.91 for Socratic) is larger and more credible than the success-rate gap (86.8% vs. 86.5%). This suggests that the primary benefit of cognitive-diagnosis-driven tutoring is not in pushing students past the "success threshold" but in *which* knowledge gaps are addressed during the tutoring session. The field has converged on success/failure as the headline metric; the coverage metric arguably captures the mechanism of benefit more directly and deserves more attention in future work.
