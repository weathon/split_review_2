## Summary

This paper proposes TokenCount, a training-free framework for class-agnostic object counting that works within SAM's existing architecture. It combines two components: (1) probabilistic prompt generation that samples prompts from a multinomial distribution defined by similarity to an exemplar, with iterative suppression of verified regions; and (2) output token-based verification that uses SAM's decoder output tokens (rather than image embeddings) to distinguish positive from negative instances, with TS-SS as the similarity metric. On FSC-147, the method achieves the best MAE (16.25) among SAM-only training-free methods; on CARPK, it achieves MAE 4.68, beating all training-based methods.

## Strengths

- **The core idea — using SAM's decoder output tokens as verification criteria for counting — is creative and well-motivated.** Unlike prior work that computes similarity in the image embedding space, this approach works within SAM's existing architecture without requiring a secondary encoder or embedding projection. The PCA visualization in Figure 5 provides evidence that output tokens yield better positive/negative separation than image embeddings.

- **The probabilistic prompt generation with suppression is a principled and sensible alternative to uniform grid or superpixel prompting.** Sampling from a multinomial distribution defined by similarity to the exemplar, then suppressing probability mass at verified positive/negative mask regions, addresses the real problem that deterministic prompting wastes computation on background and can miss distant objects.

- **The CARPK result (MAE 4.68) is genuinely strong.** It beats all training-based methods (best: SAFECOUNT at 5.33) and all training-free methods except TFCAC (4.39). For a method that uses only SAM with no fine-tuning and no auxiliary model, this is a notable practical result.

- **On FSC-147, the method achieves the best MAE (16.25) among SAM-only training-free methods**, outperforming TFOC (19.95), C-Anything (27.97), and plain SAM (42.48) by clear margins, demonstrating the value of the proposed components.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The abstract makes a demonstrably false claim.** The abstract states: "Experimental results show our method achieves superior accuracy with an MAE of 16.25, outperforming existing training-based and training-free counting methods." This is directly contradicted by the paper's own Table 1, where TFCAC (12.26, training-free with SAM+α), LOCA (10.79, training-based), PseCo (13.05), SAFECOUNT (14.32), and BMNet+ (14.62) all achieve lower (better) MAE on FSC-147. The method is best among SAM-only training-free methods and competitive overall — the abstract must be corrected to reflect this rather than claiming general superiority.

- **The TS-SS verification metric, heavily positioned as a key contribution (Section 3.2), provides only a marginal and unsubstantiated benefit.** Table 3 shows TS-SS improves over cosine similarity by only 0.23 MAE (16.48 → 16.25, roughly 1.4% relative improvement). No error bars, confidence intervals, or significance tests are reported. Given that the prompt sampling is stochastic (multinomial draws), this difference could easily fall within run-to-run noise. The paper devotes substantial space to motivating TS-SS and claims it "breaks through the plateau" of non-hybrid metrics, but the evidence does not support this conclusion. If this finding is not robust, the paper's framing of its contributions is significantly weakened.

- **No statistical rigor anywhere in the paper, despite inherently stochastic methodology.** The prompt generation stage samples from a multinomial distribution, making results inherently random. Yet no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or indication of how many random seeds were run are reported for any experiment (Tables 1–5). Without this, the reader cannot assess whether the reported MAE values are a single favorable draw or reliable estimates. This is a critical omission for a method whose output depends on random sampling.

### Minor

- **The speed claim against TFCAC is unsubstantiated.** The paper asserts "inference time that is more than twice as fast" as TFCAC but provides no timing data for TFCAC or any other baseline. Only the method's own average processing time (1.69 seconds per image) is reported. Given that the method uses up to ~1,438 mean prompts (16×128 setting, Table 4), running SAM's decoder many times, a proper timing breakdown and baseline comparisons are needed.

- **Several method details are underspecified, harming reproducibility.** (1) The verification threshold of 300 is stated without explanation — TS-SS is a product with unusual dimensionality (length^4) and no normalization is discussed, so a threshold of 300 has no clear interpretation. (2) The paper never explicitly states which iteration/prompt configuration was used for the main Table 1 results (though 16 iterations × 128 prompts from Table 4 produces the matching 16.25 MAE). (3) The probability update mechanism is ambiguous: "the probability distribution at the corresponding coordinate is set to 0" — it is unclear whether this refers to a single 64×64 spatial coordinate or the entire mask region, and whether re-normalization is performed.

- **The one-shot counting results (Table 2, MAE 18.57) are weaker than TFCAC (15.15, another training-free method) and several training-based methods.** The paper frames this as "competitive performance," which is reasonable, but it further underscores that the method does not achieve the general superiority claimed in the abstract.
