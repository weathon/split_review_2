## Summary

The paper proposes **IncentRL**, a framework that augments standard RL with a KL-divergence penalty between a predicted outcome distribution $p(o|s,a)$ and a preferred outcome distribution $q(o|s)$, weighted by a trade-off parameter $\beta$. The paper's central claimed novelty is treating $\beta$ as a Bayesian random variable adapted online. Experiments are conducted on a 2-state toy MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid.

## Strengths

- **Conceptually interesting framing.** The idea of aligning predicted outcome distributions with preferred ones via KL divergence (Sections 3.2–3.3) has intuitive appeal and connects to the Free Energy Principle, active inference, and dopamine RPE. The separation between fixed preferences and adaptive behavior is a clean distinction from active inference.

- **The toy 2-state experiment cleanly validates the basic mechanism.** Section 5.2 uses known transition probabilities for $p(o|s,a)$ and a hand-specified $q$, with results averaged over 7 seeds showing that all tested $\beta>0$ improve learning speed and reduce variance (69–74% improvement, Table 1). This is the only experiment where $p$ and $q$ are fully specified.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

- **The Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation mechanism — presented as the paper's central novelty — is never concretely specified.** The abstract states: "The central novelty is treating the incentive weight $\beta$ as a Bayesian random variable, updated online." Yet the paper never states: what prior is placed on $\beta$, what likelihood function is used, what data the posterior conditions on, how the posterior is updated (conjugate? variational? MCMC?), or how the sampled $\beta$ is deployed in the RL loop. Section 5.1 describes a 5-step algorithm with no Bayesian computation. Section 3 (Methods) describes only the KL shaping term. The Bayesian adaptation is mentioned only in passing (lines 230–247) with no mathematical description. Figures 3–4 and Table 3 show posterior summaries, but the procedure that produced them is a black box. **A reader cannot evaluate, implement, or reproduce the claimed main contribution.** This alone undermines the paper's core claim.

### Major

- **The empirical evidence about Bayesian $\beta$ contradicts the paper's own claims.** Table 3 reports the posterior mean of $\beta$ converging from 0.1057 (Round 1) to **0.0173** (Round 4). Yet the paper text (lines 230, 239, 245) repeatedly claims concentration "near the effective region ($\beta \approx 0.1$)." The dashed red line in Figures 3–4 at $\beta=0.1$ does not match the actual posterior mean at convergence ($\approx 0.02$). The paper's own data contradict its central empirical claim about posterior concentration.

- **$p(o|s,a)$ and $q(o|s)$ are never concretely specified for the non-toy experiments,** despite being the core computation of the method. For MountainCar (Section 5.3), $q$ is described only as "assigning all probability to the goal ($x \geq 0.5$)" — a continuous region, not a single outcome — with no specification of what outcome space $O$ is, how $p$ is represented or learned, or what architecture is used. For MiniGrid (Section 5.4), neither $p$ nor $q$ is described at all. This severely undermines reproducibility.

- **No comparisons to any intrinsic motivation baselines**, despite positioning against them. Section 2.1 discusses curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., Burda et al.), empowerment, and information gain methods as prior work whose limitations IncentRL addresses. Yet every experiment compares only to $\beta=0$ (standard RL). Without comparisons to ICM, RND, VIME, or similar, the paper cannot substantiate its claim of overcoming limitations of intrinsic motivation methods.

### Minor

- **Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation is evaluated only on posterior statistics, not task performance.** The paper claims Bayesian adaptation "achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" in "additional runs (not shown)" (line 228), offering no actual learning curves, success rates, or task-performance metrics for the Bayesian agent. Figures 3–4 only show $\beta$ posterior convergence, not whether the resulting agent actually performs well. Strikingly, Section 6.4 lists "Deeper evaluation of Bayesian $\beta$" as a *future direction* — this is inconsistent with presenting it as a current contribution.

- **Thin empirical evaluation overall.** MountainCar uses only 3 seeds; MiniGrid uses only 3 seeds and tests only $\beta=0.01$ (no sweep to establish the best fixed $\beta$ for that environment). No statistical significance tests are reported. The MiniGrid $\beta=0.01$ value is never justified.

- **Extreme sensitivity to $\beta$ in MountainCar** (Table 2: $\beta=0.1 \rightarrow +18\%$, $\beta=0.3 \rightarrow -53\%$, $\beta=1.0 \rightarrow -75\%$) indicates a very narrow window of effective values. This brittleness is acknowledged (Section 6.2) but undercuts the claim that Bayesian adaptation eliminates tuning difficulty, especially since the Bayesian $\beta$ converges to 0.02, not 0.1.

- **The theoretical analysis (Propositions 1 and 2) is basic.** Proposition 1 states that for sufficiently small $\beta$, the optimal policy is unchanged (a continuity argument with no bound on $\epsilon$). Proposition 2 states that as $\beta \to \infty$, the KL term dominates (definitional). These provide no practical bounds, convergence rates, or suboptimality characterizations, and Proposition 1 is weaker than existing potential-based shaping theory (Ng et al., 1999).

### Trivial

- **Figure 3 caption explicitly states the posterior mean "decreases to about 0.02 by Round 4,"** while the body text claims concentration "near $\beta \approx 0.1$." These are inconsistent descriptions of the same data.

## Nice-to-Haves

- Ablation studies separating the benefit of the KL shaping term alone from Bayesian $\beta$ adaptation would clarify which component drives improvements.
- A $\beta$ sweep on MiniGrid would establish the best fixed $\beta$ for fair comparison.
- Comparisons to meta-gradient or annealing approaches for $\beta$ adaptation would strengthen the adaptation claim.
- Reporting individual seed results and confidence intervals would help assess statistical reliability.

## Novel Insights
