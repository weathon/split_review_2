## Summary

This paper proposes *Conjugate Learning Theory*, a framework based on convex conjugate duality that aims to unify the analysis of trainability and generalization in deep neural networks. The central argument is that practical learnability requires exponential family distributions (via the Pitman–Darmois–Koopmans theorem), which in turn forces Fenchel–Young losses as the natural loss family for machine learning. The framework integrates prior knowledge as convex constraints, uses conjugate dual predictions, and claims to deliver convergence guarantees via a "structure matrix" and generalization bounds via generalized conditional entropy.

## Strengths

- **Principled softmax/cross-entropy derivation (Theorem 8, Eqs. 19–21):** The paper derives the softmax function and cross-entropy loss as the natural consequence of choosing negative entropy as the generating function with the probability simplex as the constraint set. This is a clean, self-contained result that gives a non-ad-hoc justification for these ubiquitous deep learning components.

- **Relaxed i.i.d. assumption with explicit prior knowledge integration:** The framework (Definition 1) explicitly accommodates non-i.i.d. data (e.g., Mixup, sequential dependencies) and encodes domain-specific prior knowledge as a convex constraint set C. This is a genuine extension beyond standard ERM formulations and is well-motivated with concrete examples (the probability simplex for classification).

- **Mathematical coherence of the presented framework:** The derivations linking exponential families, Fenchel–Young losses, and conjugate duality (Eqs. 12–14, Lemma 3, Lemma 4) are technically correct and internally consistent. The notation is clean and the conceptual flow from exponential families to conjugate learning is logically structured.

- **Ambitious scope:** The paper aims to unify trainability and generalization under a single framework—a genuinely important open problem. The claimed results (structure matrix for trainability, generalized conditional entropy bounds for generalization) would, if substantiated, represent a meaningful contribution.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None identified from the evaluable portions of the paper.

### Major

- **The "practical learnability" claim (Proposition 7) rests on a philosophical leap that conflates distinct notions of learnability.** The paper argues that because the PDK theorem states only exponential families admit bounded-dimensional sufficient statistics, *only* exponential family distributions are practically learnable from finite samples. This reasoning conflates (i) full conditional distribution estimation requiring sufficient statistics in the strict statistical sense with (ii) the ability to learn a predictor via empirical risk minimization. Many successful ML algorithms learn predictors without explicitly estimating the full conditional distribution as an exponential family, and ERM does not require the data-generating process to belong to an exponential family. The paper implicitly concedes this tension by noting that any bounded-domain distribution can be approximated by a multinomial (which is exponential family), effectively making the "necessity" claim a modeling choice rather than a mathematical constraint. Proposition 7 is presented as a theorem but functions as a philosophical stance; the argument does not rigorously establish *necessity* in the sense claimed. This weakens the foundation on which the entire framework is built.

### Minor

- **The finite feature-space assumption (|X| finite) is limiting and its justification is incomplete.** The paper asserts this is justified by discretization of continuous features in practice, and argues that without effective discretization, learning is fundamentally impossible. While the philosophical argument has merit, no bounds on discretization error or discussion of how the theory carries over to continuous-input regimes (images, audio, text embeddings) are provided. This limits confidence that the framework meaningfully addresses practical deep learning with high-dimensional continuous inputs.

- **Novelty relative to existing Fenchel–Young loss literature is not clearly demarcated.** The connection between exponential family MLE and Fenchel–Young losses is known (Blondel et al., 2020, cited as [8]). Section 3 presents this connection as part of the framework but does not explicitly state what the conjugate learning formulation adds beyond the existing Fenchel–Young setup, beyond the PDK-based "necessity" argument and the prior-knowledge constraint C. The value of the framework hinges on whether sections 4–5 deliver genuinely new analytical tools that could not be obtained from directly analyzing existing formulations.

- **The critique of existing theories (NTK, flat minima, IB) in Section 1 is too brief to be substantive.** The paper dismisses these frameworks in a few sentences without engaging deeply with their strengths or demonstrating that the proposed framework avoids their limitations. A more careful comparative analysis would strengthen the motivation.

## Novel Insights

The paper's most interesting insight is not the PDK-based necessity argument but rather the observation that softmax and cross-entropy—arguably the most ubiquitous components in modern classification networks—emerge *identically* from the conjugate dual of negative entropy constrained to the probability simplex, with no tuning or ad-hoc design choices. This is a crisp, clean result that genuinely connects convex analysis to deep learning practice. Whether this insight can be extended to produce the claimed trainability and generalization results remains to be seen in the full paper.

## Suggestions

- **Soften Proposition 7:** Reframe "practical learnability" as a modeling principle rather than a mathematical necessity. Acknowledge explicitly that many successful learning algorithms do not model the full conditional distribution as an exponential family, and clarify that the exponential family restriction is adopted as a *sufficient* condition that enables the conjugate duality machinery, not a *necessary* condition for all forms of learning.
- **Add a dedicated paragraph comparing with Fenchel–Young losses (Blondel et al.):** Explicitly state what the conjugate learning framework adds—e.g., the structure matrix, the gradient correlation factor, the generalized conditional entropy bounds—and why these could not be obtained from the standard Fenchel–Young setup alone.
- **Discuss the finite-X assumption more carefully:** Even if full discretization error bounds are deferred to future work, a brief discussion of how the theory might extend to continuous inputs (e.g., via covering arguments or limiting procedures) would significantly strengthen the paper's relevance claims.
