## Summary

This paper proposes a benchmark for evaluating text-to-image models on generating images for WordNet taxonomy concepts. It includes three datasets (easy concepts, random WordNet split, LLM predictions), evaluates 12 T2I models using 9 metrics (taxonomy-specific CLIP similarities, preference-based ELO from humans and GPT-4, FID, IS), and includes human evaluation with 4 expert annotators. The benchmark finds that Playground-v2 and FLUX perform best on preference-based metrics, while SDXL-turbo dominates CLIP-based similarity metrics.

## Strengths

1. **First large-scale evaluation of T2I models on WordNet taxonomy concepts.** The paper evaluates 12 publicly available models across 3 datasets spanning ~3,370 synsets, significantly extending prior work (Baryshnikov & Ryabinin 2023) that only considered a smaller subset of models and metrics. This provides a useful resource for the community.

2. **Novel taxonomy-structured similarity metrics validated against human judgments.** The Lemma, Hypernym, and Cohyponym similarities (Equations 1–3) leverage WordNet's hierarchical structure and are validated against human rank correlations (ρ≈0.91 for Hypernym, p≤0.00004). The Specificity metric (ratio of hypernym to cohyponym similarity) provides a measure of concept discrimination not present in prior T2I evaluation. These metrics generalize the prior In-Subtree Probability beyond ImageNet classes to any taxonomy node.

3. **Pairwise GPT-4 evaluation for T2I with explicit bias analysis.** The paper pioneers the use of Bradley-Terry ELO from GPT-4 pairwise comparisons for T2I, provides detailed alignment analysis with human preferences (Spearman ρ=0.92 with definitions, ρ=0.73 without), and identifies GPT-4's first-option bias (Figure 5), offering practical guidance for future automated evaluation pipelines.

4. **Human evaluation component.** The benchmark includes human ELO evaluation on 3,370 pairwise comparisons by 4 expert annotators, with inter-annotator agreement reported (Spearman ρ=0.8, p≤0.05) — a substantial effort that strengthens the credibility of the preference-based results.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Dataset construction description is internally inconsistent (§2.2).** The Random Split description states: "the probabilities of occurrence in the test set differ between cases: for Hypernymy is set very low at 1×10⁻⁵, higher for Hyponymy at 0.05, and highest for Synset Mixing at 0.1." Yet the reported test set composition is 828 Hypernymy (69%), 170 Mix, and 204 Hyponymy — directly contradicting the stated intention to keep Hypernymy occurrences "very low." The reader cannot determine whether the sampling description is wrong, the probabilities are wrong, or the counts are wrong. Since the entire evaluation depends on the validity of this dataset, this must be resolved for the benchmark to be credible.

2. **Unsubstantiated claim about different rankings from standard T2I benchmarks (§1, Abstract).** The paper repeatedly asserts that the proposed task "yields different rankings for models compared to those in text-to-image benchmarks" (citing Jiang et al. 2024a) and that results "differ significantly from standard T2I tasks." However, no direct comparison table or analysis is provided — no ranking from GenAI Arena, MS-COCO FID, or any other standard T2I leaderboard is shown alongside the benchmark's rankings. This headline finding is asserted rather than demonstrated. The claim should either be substantiated with evidence or rephrased as a more modest observation.

3. **Overclaimed "fully covers WordNet-3.0" contribution (§1, line 83).** The contribution list states: "We publish the dataset of the images generated by the best Text-to-Image approach from the benchmark that fully covers WordNet-3.0 extending the ImageNet dataset." The paper evaluates models on only ~3,370 synsets across three subsets, while WordNet-3.0 contains ~80,000 synsets. No evidence, methodology description, or validation is provided for a full-coverage dataset. This statement appears to claim a massive separate undertaking that is neither described nor validated in the paper, and should be removed or precisely qualified.

### Minor

1. **GPT-4 position bias acknowledged but not fully addressed (§4.1, §5).** The paper notes that GPT-4 exhibits a strong first-option bias (Figure 5) and zero correlation with humans at the individual-battle level, yet still reports GPT-4 ELO as a primary metric. The high ranking correlation (ρ=0.92 with definitions) suggests the bias may not distort overall rankings, but the paper does not demonstrate this explicitly (e.g., by measuring consistency under swapped order). The analysis would be strengthened by addressing how the bias is handled in the ELO computation.

2. **LLM Predictions evaluation pipeline is under-specified in the main text (§2.3).** The paper says "To match the original WordNet synsets, we generate definitions for every generated node with GPT4, described in Appendix C." It is unclear from the main text whether the 1,685 LLM-generated predictions correspond to existing WordNet synsets or are novel labels, and if novel, how taxonomy-dependent metrics (Hypernym/Cohyponym Similarity) are computed. This may be clarified in the stripped appendix, but the main text should be self-contained.

3. **Retrieval model details are insufficient for reproducibility (§3).** The Wikimedia Commons retrieval model is referenced only by citations (Ferrada et al. 2018; Jones & Oyen 2022). The actual query construction method, ranking function, and retrieval pipeline are not described, making it impossible to reproduce this baseline.

4. **FID computed using retrieved images as the "real" distribution is an unusual proxy (§4.3).** The paper acknowledges this limitation, but the interpretation of FID in this setting — measuring similarity to retrieved images rather than canonical "real images" — is unclear and could mislead readers who skim the results.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting finding is the sharp divergence between different metric families: SDXL-turbo dominates all taxonomy-structured CLIP similarities (Lemma, Hypernym, Cohyponym) while scoring poorly on human and GPT-4 preference rankings, whereas Playground-v2 and FLUX lead on preferences but not on CLIP similarities. This reveals a substantive gap between text-image alignment as measured by CLIP and human-judged appropriateness for taxonomy-concept visualization. Additionally, the strong validation of hypernym similarity against human rank correlations (ρ≈0.91) suggests these taxonomy-structured metrics capture something meaningful about human perception of concept-to-image mappings, beyond what standard text-image alignment metrics measure.
