## Summary

This paper presents VisualPRM400K, a large-scale (400K samples, ~2M steps) multimodal process supervision dataset constructed via an automatic Monte Carlo pipeline, along with VisualPRM, an 8B multimodal process reward model trained on this dataset. The authors also contribute VisualProcessBench, a human-annotated benchmark (2,866 samples, 26,950 steps) for step-level error detection in multimodal reasoning. The key empirical finding is that VisualPRM, used for Best-of-N (BoN) test-time scaling, improves reasoning performance across four MLLM families (MiniCPM-V2.6, Qwen2.5-VL, InternVL2.5 at 8B–78B) and seven multimodal benchmarks, with gains of +3.7 to +8.9 overall points, and that PRM consistently outperforms Outcome Reward Models and Self-Consistency.

## Strengths

1. **First large-scale multimodal PRM dataset filling a clear gap.** VisualPRM400K (~400K samples, ~2M step-level correctness labels) is the first dataset of its kind for multimodal process supervision. It directly adapts the established MathShepherd Monte Carlo pipeline to the multimodal setting, providing a useful resource for the community. The paper commits to releasing model, data, and benchmark.

2. **Consistent and substantiated BoN gains across diverse settings.** Table 2 reports improvements of +8.0 (MiniCPM-V2.6), +3.7 (Qwen2.5-VL-7B), +8.4 (InternVL2.5-8B), +8.9 (InternVL2.5-26B), +6.3 (InternVL2.5-38B), and +5.9 (InternVL2.5-78B) overall points, covering 4 model families and 3–4 scales. The gains are not cherry-picked — they appear on nearly every individual benchmark.

3. **PRM > ORM > SC with widening gap at larger N.** Figure 4 shows that process-level supervision scales better than outcome-level: for InternVL2.5-8B, PRM outperforms ORM by 1.5 points at N=8 and 4.3 at N=128. This is the core theoretical claim of the paper and is convincingly demonstrated.

4. **VisualProcessBench is a high-quality benchmark.** With 2,866 samples, 26,950 human-annotated steps, full-error detection (not just first error), and diverse solution sources (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5, Gemini-2.0-Flash, QvQ, InternVL2.5-78B), this is a rigorous benchmark. It cleanly shows that open-source MLLMs score near-random (48.0–52.6 F1 vs. 50.0 random baseline), while VisualPRM achieves 62.0 — outperforming GPT-4o (60.3) and matching Gemini-2.0-Flash (62.3).

5. **Efficient single-forward-pass inference design.** VisualPRM scores all steps in one forward pass using the "+" token's probability, unlike MLLM judges that require autoregressive per-step generation. This is a practical advantage for BoN evaluation.

6. **Useful ablation studies.** Table 4 compares value-based vs. advantage-based PRMs, early-stop vs. full-step supervision, and aggregation methods (min/max/avg). The finding that advantage-based PRMs underperform is honestly attributed to noise in the data pipeline — a transparent discussion of limitations.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Dataset label quality is not directly validated.** The step-level correctness annotations in VisualPRM400K are generated by an automatic Monte Carlo pipeline (16 continuations per step, mc_i > 0 threshold). The paper provides no human evaluation of label accuracy, no comparison to a hand-annotated subset, and no analysis of labeling noise (e.g., distribution of mc_i values). The paper itself acknowledges (Section 4.3) that "inherent noise in our training data" may explain why advantage-based PRMs underperform, and that raising the correctness threshold *hurts* downstream performance (Appendix B) — suggesting the labels are indeed fragile. While indirect validation exists (trained PRM achieves strong results on VisualProcessBench and in BoN evaluations), a direct human validation of a subset of the training labels would significantly strengthen the paper's central claim that VisualPRM400K is an "effective dataset." This is a significant gap in the evidence chain, though it does not invalidate the empirical results.

### Minor

- **Text-only evaluation input format is underspecified.** Table 5 reports that VisualPRM improves text-only reasoning (GSM8K, MATH-500, GPQA-Diamond) for both LLMs (Qwen2.5 series) and MLLMs (InternVL2.5 series). However, the paper never describes how VisualPRM — a multimodal model trained with image inputs — processes purely textual inputs. The model architecture (presumably InternVL2.5-based) can handle text-only inputs, but the specific input format (e.g., blank/placeholder image, or text-only without any image token) is not described. This is needed for reproducibility and to rule out degenerate scoring behavior.

- **BoN results lack variance estimates.** All BoN results (Tables 2, 4, 5; Figure 4) are reported as single-point accuracies without confidence intervals or standard deviations across multiple sampling seeds (temperature 0.7 is used). While some individual gains are large (+16.9 on MathVerse-VO for MiniCPM-V2.6), others are small (+1.3 on MathVision, +1.4 on DynaMath for the same model) and could fall within sampling noise. Reporting error bars or multiple-run statistics would strengthen the reliability of the claims, especially for the small-margin comparisons.

- **Figure 4 caption is garbled.** Due to a likely formatting artifact, the caption labels both the "VisualPRM-8B" (blue) and what should presumably be "ORM" (red) lines as "VisualPRM-8B," making the figure legend ambiguous. This should be corrected.

### Trivial

- None beyond the formatting issues noted above.

## Novel Insights

The harsh critic's central concern about label validation is valid and important, but it sits within a broader observation: the paper follows exactly the same validation paradigm as the text-only PRM literature it builds on (MathShepherd, OmegaPRM) — automatic labels → train PRM → evaluate on downstream tasks. The paper is transparent about noise and limitations. What distinguishes this work is the multimodal extension, which is non-trivial due to the challenges of visual reasoning evaluation, and the high-quality VisualProcessBench benchmark that provides a clearer, more direct evaluation of step-level critic ability than BoN accuracy alone. The combination of a large automatically-generated training set with a carefully human-annotated evaluation benchmark creates a useful ecosystem for future work, even if the training labels themselves admit noise.

## Suggestions

1. **Validate a subset of VisualPRM400K labels.** This is the single highest-impact improvement. Sample 200–500 instances and have human annotators evaluate step correctness (following the VisualProcessBench protocol). Report agreement between automatic labels and human labels. If agreement is high, this resolves the main concern. If moderate, analyze where errors concentrate.

2. **Describe the text-only evaluation input format explicitly.** State whether a blank image, a placeholder token, or an entirely image-free input is used, and verify that the model does not exhibit degenerate scoring behavior on such inputs.

3. **Report BoN results with confidence intervals or multiple-seed standard deviations.** Even 3 random seeds with error bars would significantly increase confidence in the results, especially for the smaller-margin comparisons.
