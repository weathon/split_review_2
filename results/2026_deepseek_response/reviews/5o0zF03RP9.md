## Summary

The paper proposes **IncentRL**, a framework that augments standard RL with a KL divergence term between predicted and preferred outcome distributions. The central claimed novelty is a Bayesian scheme for online adaptation of the incentive weight β. Experiments are conducted on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8.

## Strengths

1. **Clear and well-motivated formulation**: The KL-based preference-prediction shaping term (§3) is clearly defined, easy to understand, and the connection to cognitive concepts (dopamine RPE, Free Energy Principle) is gracefully drawn without overstating the link.

2. **MiniGrid quantitative results with fixed β**: Figure 2 shows that a small fixed β=0.01 achieves 98% success rate vs. 90.5% for β=0, with substantially shorter episode lengths (29 vs. 75 steps), averaged over 3 seeds. This demonstrates that the shaping idea has practical value in a moderately challenging sparse-reward domain.

3. **Toy MDP ablation is informative**: Table 1 shows that all β>0 values produce 69–74% improvement over β=0 with dramatically reduced variance (std ~6 vs. 11.30), suggesting the KL shaping is robust to β choice in simple settings.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Bayesian adaptation of β — the paper's primary claimed contribution — is never described.** The Abstract, Introduction, and Conclusion all highlight Bayesian adaptation as the "central novelty" and key contribution. Yet Section 3 (Methods) and Section 5.1 (Algorithm) describe only the fixed-β KL shaping term. There is no prior specification, likelihood model, inference procedure, or update rule for β anywhere in the paper. Figures 3 and 4 show posterior statistics without explaining how they were computed, and what a "round" constitutes is never defined. This means the paper's core algorithmic novelty cannot be evaluated or reproduced. A paper can list multiple contributions and fall short on some, but the contribution claimed as the primary novelty must be present.

2. **Experimental evidence for Bayesian adaptation is incomplete and contains a contradiction.** The only mention of Bayesian β performance is the sentence: "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (§5.4). These runs are not shown, and no learning curves or final-task-performance numbers for the adaptive agent are provided. Furthermore, Figure 3 shows a dashed line labeled "target effective β = 0.1" but the posterior mean converges to ~0.02 by Round 4 (confirmed by Table 3: mean β=0.0173 in Round 4), while the text claims the posterior "quickly concentrates near the effective region (β≈0.1)." Even if the Bayesian adaptation works, this inconsistency undermines the presented evidence.

3. **No comparison against existing intrinsic motivation methods.** The baselines consist only of standard RL (β=0). No comparisons are made to curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al. 2017; Burda et al. 2018), ICM, RND, count-based exploration, or any other method that handles sparse rewards. Since the related work section (§2) discusses these methods, their absence from the experimental comparison makes it impossible to assess whether the KL-based shaping offers any advantage over established approaches. The paper's claim of "improved sample efficiency over standard RL and fixed-regularization baselines" is accurate but weak because "standard RL" (β=0) is a minimal bar.

### Minor

4. **The theoretical analysis (Propositions 1 and 2) is standard and not specific to IncentRL.** These propositions state that for small β the optimal policy is unchanged, and for large β the KL term dominates. These are general continuity arguments that hold for any additive reward shaping term of the form `-β·f(s,a)`. They provide no insight into why KL divergence over outcome distributions is a good choice, and they do not involve or inform the Bayesian adaptation mechanism.

5. **MountainCar results show β sensitivity that undermines the "eliminates manual tuning" claim.** Table 2 shows β=0.1 helps (+18%) but β=0.3 and β=1.0 hurt substantially (-53% and -75%), a clear non-monotonic pattern. The paper's framing claims Bayesian adaptation eliminates tuning, but the fixed-β experiments themselves show that β selection matters a great deal, and the Bayesian results that would substantiate the claim are not provided as performance curves.

None.
