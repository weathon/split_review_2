## Summary
VideoJudge introduces a bootstrapping framework for training small (3B, 7B) MLLM-based evaluators for video understanding. The method uses a generator-evaluator pipeline to create >100K training examples with fine-grained 1–5 ratings without human annotation, and also develops a variant (VideoJudgeR) that generates instance-specific rubrics at test time. The paper reports that their small models match or surpass models 10× larger across multiple benchmarks.

## Strengths
1. **Creative bootstrapping methodology.** The generator-evaluator pipeline (Section 3.1, Equations 1–4) produces responses conditioned on target ratings, validates them, and iteratively refines mismatches. This is a well-designed approach for creating training data without human annotation at a substantial scale (103,825 examples across 20,765 unique video-instruction pairs).

2. **Instance-specific rubric generation (VideoJudgeR).** Training the model to generate task-specific rubrics at test time (Table 2, Figure 3) is a genuinely novel contribution. VideoJudgeR-3B achieves Pearson correlation of 73.96, competitive with Qwen2.5-VL-72B (78.10) while being substantially smaller. The human evaluation of rubric quality (unanimous annotation, three annotators) provides stronger signal than LLM-as-Judge alone.

3. **Diagnostic ablations.** The frame count analysis (Section 6.2 — diminishing returns after ~240 frames for training, ~120 for evaluation) and decoding temperature robustness study (Figure 4 — VideoJudge maintains Spearman 0.73 at T=1.0 while base model degrades to 0.42) provide actionable practical guidance for practitioners deploying video judge models.

4. **Open resources.** The paper releases bootstrapped datasets, meta-evaluation benchmarks, and trained models, supporting reproducible research in video understanding evaluation.

## Weaknesses

### Major

1. **Closed-loop evaluation structure undermines the central claim.** Two of the four pointwise benchmarks (VideoJudgeLLaVA-MetaEval and VideoJudgeVCG-MetaEval) use ground-truth ratings generated by the same bootstrapping pipeline that produced the training data (Section 4.2: "generating additional responses via our bootstrapping pipeline (Algorithm 1)"). VideoJudge performs strongest on precisely these closed-loop benchmarks. On the independently human-annotated benchmarks — VateX-Eval (PSUP: VideoJudge-3B 0.61, VideoJudge-7B 0.66 vs. Qwen2.5-VL-32B 0.73 and 72B 0.71) and LongVideoBench (PSUP: VideoJudge-3B 0.61, VideoJudge-7B 0.66 vs. Qwen2.5-VL-32B 0.73) — the results are more modest and do not support the claim of matching or surpassing models 10× larger. The paper acknowledges this as a "partial closed-loop effect" in Limitations (Section 7), but this framing understates the severity: the headline claim rests most heavily on benchmarks where the model may have learned to replicate pipeline labeling patterns rather than acquiring generalizable human-aligned evaluation criteria.

2. **Severe overestimation bias limits practical utility.** The paper's own error analysis (Section 6.2) reveals that the model overestimates scores by ≥2 points in 14.8% of cases vs. only 1.5% underestimation. Only 36.9% of rating-3 responses receive the correct score, with 46.6% inflated to 5, and 81.3% of rating-4 responses incorrectly rated as 5. The model collapses distinctions between mediocre, good, and excellent responses — a fundamental limitation for a fine-grained evaluation tool. The paper mentions this as future work, but it should qualify all reported results and is central to assessing the model's practical usefulness.

3. **Human validation of data validates pipeline consistency, not final model alignment with human judgment.** The human evaluation (Section 5.2, 250 pairs of ratings 2 vs. 3) shows >92% annotator agreement with the "gold preference" — but this gold preference is simply the higher pipeline-generated rating (Section 5.2: "choose the higher-rated one as preferred"). This validates that the pipeline produces reasonable labels, not that the *trained VideoJudge model* produces evaluations aligned with independent human judgment. The pairwise evaluation on VideoAutoArena (which has human ground truth) partially addresses this, but for pointwise evaluation — where the closed-loop concern is greatest — the evidence for human alignment is substantially weaker than the paper's presentation suggests.

### Minor

1. **Rubric quality comparison against strongest baseline is near-chance.** In human evaluation (Figure 3), VideoJudgeR-3B wins against GPT-4o-mini at only 53.4%. The paper's claim of "substantially higher-quality rubrics" is supported against smaller baselines (3B, 7B, 32B) but overstated when assessed against the strongest competitor.

2. **Missing baseline: the pipeline evaluator model (E).** The paper never reports the performance of the evaluator model used in the bootstrapping pipeline to validate generated responses. If E is a strong model (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B), readers need to know how E performs on the evaluation benchmarks to understand what the bootstrapping process adds beyond distilling E's judgments.

3. **The acceptance threshold α for training data is unspecified.** Section 3.1 defines α as the acceptance criterion but only specifies its value (0) for the meta-evaluation benchmarks (Section 4.2). The α used during training data generation is not reported, making it impossible to assess potential noise in the training labels.

4. **No human validation of pointwise absolute ratings.** The human evaluation covers only pairwise preferences in a narrow range (2 vs. 3). There is no human validation of the 1–5 pointwise absolute ratings that the model is trained to predict — which is the setting where the overestimation bias is most problematic.

5. **The conclusion overclaims.** The conclusion states "VideoJudge-7B consistently outperforms larger video-language models across multiple benchmarks," but on independent human-annotated benchmarks (VATEx PSUP 0.66 vs. Qwen2.5-VL-72B 0.71; LongVidB PSUP 0.66 vs. Qwen2.5-VL-32B 0.73), the largest baselines outperform VideoJudge-7B. This claim is accurate only for the closed-loop benchmarks and the pairwise VJ/VJ-H settings where the comparison is more favorable.

## Novel Insights
The harsh critic's observation that the closed-loop evaluation is not merely "partial" but **structural** to how the paper's strongest evidence is constructed is worth noting. Even though the paper acknowledges the issue, the severity is understated: the best-performing results for VideoJudge on pointwise benchmarks come from the two benchmarks sharing the same pipeline as training, while the independent human-annotated benchmarks show the model underperforming the largest baselines. This means readers cannot distinguish whether the model learned generalizable evaluation skills or simply learned to predict the pipeline's labeling patterns. The critic's cross-tabulation of Table 1 data makes this visible in a way the paper's narrative does not.

## Suggestions
1. Report the pipeline evaluator (E) as a baseline to clarify what bootstrapping adds beyond distillation.
2. Annotate 500–1000 pointwise examples with human raters to break the closed loop and provide direct evidence for the paper's central claim of human-aligned evaluation.
3. Address the overestimation bias through either data-level interventions (hard negatives near the top of the scale) or post-hoc calibration, and report calibration metrics alongside correlation metrics.
4. Tone down the claim that small models "match or surpass" 10× larger models, or clearly qualify it to specify which benchmarks support this claim and which do not.
