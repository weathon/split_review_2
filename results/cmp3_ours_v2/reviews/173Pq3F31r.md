## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework for joint expressive performance rendering (EPR) and automatic piano transcription (APT), built around disentangled representations of note-level score content and global performance style. The key ideas are: (1) jointly modeling the inverse mapping between symbolic scores and expressive performances; (2) formulating EPR as a Seq2Seq task without expensive note-level alignment; and (3) a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone. The framework is evaluated on the ASAP and ATEPP datasets using both objective metrics and subjective listening tests.

## Strengths

1. **Well-motivated task formulation (Section 1, Figure 1).** The duality between EPR and APT as inverse mappings — analogous to TTS/ASR in speech — is genuinely novel in music MIR. The paper uses this framing to motivate the architectural design (shared content space, separate style space), and this conceptual contribution is worth publishing on its own terms.

2. **Seq2Seq EPR without note-level alignment (Section 3, Table 3).** The paper demonstrates that EPR can be formulated as a Seq2Seq task that does not require note-level alignment preprocessing, a genuine practical bottleneck for existing EPR systems. Table 3 shows a 92.27% alignment rate for Ours-PSR, validating the viability of this approach.

3. **The PSR module as a self-contained contribution (Section 3.4, Figure 4).** Generating style embeddings from score content alone — without requiring performer/composer labels or manual expressive controls — is practically useful. The diffusion-based generation suits the one-to-many mapping from score to plausible styles. The qualitative analysis in Figure 4 — where PSR-generated styles mirror the era-based clustering of actual performance styles — provides real evidence that the module captures meaningful stylistic structure.

4. **Broad evaluation scope.** The paper includes objective metrics (Tables 1–3), subjective listening tests (Figure 2), disentanglement verification through classification (Table 4), style transfer evaluation (Figure 5), and latent space visualization (Figures 3, 4). Whether each component is individually convincing is a separate question, but the breadth itself demonstrates thoroughness.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **No explicit alignment mechanism between z_x and z_y, despite claiming a "shared latent content space" (Section 3.2, Figure 1 caption).** The paper states that content features "z_x and z_y ... are encouraged to align" (line 71) and that "Both domains share a latent content space Z_c" (line 79). However, the loss formulation (Equations 1–6) contains no term, architectural constraint, or regularization that pushes the two content representations toward each other. The content encoders f_c,X and f_c,Y are separate networks (Section 3.5) trained on entirely different inputs (score vs. performance). The losses — L_EPR (uses z_x for rendering), L_APT (uses z_y for transcription), L_rec,X and L_rec,Y (within-domain reconstruction), and L_KL (on z_s) — all operate independently. Two independent encoders trained with separate decoding losses do not produce "aligned" or "shared" representations by default; any rotation or scaling of z_x that the score decoder can invert would be equally valid regardless of alignment with z_y. This gap is central because the paper's core conceptual framing — a unified framework with disentangled content and style supporting mutual supervision — depends on the content space being shared. Without explicit alignment, the architecture is better described as two separate encoder-decoder pipelines with a shared style encoder.

2. **APT results are overstated relative to the primary metric (Table 1, Section 5.1).** The paper claims (line 207) "performance comparable to the state-of-the-art APT system." On the MUSTER E_p (pitch edit distance) — arguably the most important single metric for transcription accuracy — the proposed model scores **3.08**, substantially worse than Liu et al. (2022) at **2.02** and Shibata et al. (2021) at **2.09–2.11** (~50% worse). The model also has higher E_miss (8.43 vs. 6.38/6.47). The model does achieve best results on several secondary metrics (E_extra, E_onset, E_offset, E_pitch in ScoreSimilarity), but the claim of broadly "comparable" APT performance is not supported by the primary metric. The paper should acknowledge this limitation rather than stating "comparable" without qualification.

3. **EPR-Only ablation confounds joint modeling with data quantity (Section 5.1).** The EPR-Only variant retains only the Score Encoder, Style Encoder, and Performance Decoder, and is "trained solely on the ASAP dataset" (line 209). The full model (Ours-Target) is trained on ASAP plus 75,913 unpaired scores and a YouTube-derived performance dataset. The paper attributes improvement to "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data" (line 235), conflating two variables simultaneously: (a) joint modeling vs. EPR-only architecture, and (b) training data scale. A proper ablation would compare against a variant with access to the same unpaired data but trained without the APT objective, isolating whether joint modeling itself benefits EPR.

### Minor

4. **Score baseline outperforms the model on note accuracy (Table 3) without discussion.** The Score baseline (mechanically playing score notes) achieves 93.52% alignment rate vs. Ours-PSR 92.27%, and 2.91% miss rate vs. Ours-PSR 3.96%. While the goal of EPR is expressiveness rather than note accuracy, the paper does not acknowledge or discuss that the model introduces note-level artifacts beyond what the score prescribes.

5. **Style transfer evaluation relies on only 3 samples with no listener count specified (Section 5.2, Figure 5).** The subjective evaluation of style transfer uses only 3 samples. The number of listeners is not stated. Sample 2 shows the Target condition having *lower* style similarity than the Mean condition (~2.4 vs. ~3.0), which is inconsistent with successful transfer and is not discussed or explained.

6. **The unpaired performance dataset uses an unnamed transcription model with no quality documentation (Section 4.1).** The unpaired performance dataset is assembled using "a state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" selected based on a "pilot study." No details are given about the model, its accuracy, genre coverage, or what the pilot study showed. Since transcription errors in this data propagate into reconstruction training and style encoding, the lack of documentation makes this portion of the results partially unverifiable.

7. **Era labels for PSR evaluation assigned by GPT-4o mini without validation (Section 5.3).** The historical era labels (Baroque, Classical, Romantic, Modern) used in Figure 4 are assigned by GPT-4o mini based on title and composer metadata. This is an unconventional and potentially noisy labeling approach that is not validated or discussed as a limitation.

### Trivial
None.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Report confidence intervals or effect sizes for the main listening test (11 participants, 5 pieces).
- Provide quantitative evaluation of PSR-generated styles beyond visualization (e.g., Fréchet distance between real and generated style distributions).
- Document the specific audio-to-MIDI model used for unpaired performance data and its transcription accuracy.
- Include a proper ablation (Ours-NoAPT variant) that has access to unpaired data but is trained without the APT loss.

## Novel Insights
The key insight from the reviews is the gap between the paper's architectural description and its claimed conceptual framework. The paper advertises a "shared latent content space" with aligned representations, but the loss formulation contains no explicit mechanism for cross-modal content alignment — only separate task-specific losses. This observation sharpens the paper's main weakness: the conceptual contribution (joint modeling via shared representations) is stronger than what the current architecture actually realizes. A second useful insight is that the ablation design confounds two variables (joint modeling and data scale), making it impossible to attribute EPR improvements specifically to joint training. The PSR module stands on firmer ground and could be the paper's strongest independent contribution.

## Suggestions
1. **Add an explicit alignment loss between z_x and z_y** (e.g., cosine similarity, MSE, or a cycle-consistency constraint), or alternatively drop the claim of a "shared" content space and honestly describe the architecture as two separate content encoders with a shared style component.
2. **Run a proper ablation** comparing the full model against a variant with access to the same unpaired data but trained without the APT loss, to isolate the benefit of joint modeling.
3. **Calibrate the APT claim** — acknowledge that pitch accuracy (E_p) is below SOTA and discuss whether this is a fundamental limitation of the Seq2Seq approach or something that could be improved.
4. **Document the unpaired performance data pipeline** — name the transcription model, report its accuracy on held-out data, and discuss potential failure modes.
