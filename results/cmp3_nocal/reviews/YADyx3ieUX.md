## Summary

This is a philosophical position paper that challenges a foundational assumption underlying the "black box" characterization of neural networks: the assumption that causal continuity across a system implies correlative continuity (i.e., that if a distal cause produces an effect, there must exist individuable intermediate features that correlate with both). The paper argues this assumption is not a conceptual necessity, using a clay potter's wheel counterexample where causal continuity holds but no fine-grained intermediate features can be individuated. It then applies this insight to a recent "subliminal learning" study (Cloud et al., 2025) and discusses consequences for explainability discourse.

## Strengths

- **A genuine and well-articulated conceptual distinction.** The paper clearly distinguishes epistemic opacity (features exist but are hard to find) from ontological absence (no such intermediate features exist). This is a real contribution to the conceptual foundations of XAI, and the paper articulates it with precision (Section 1.1, Section 2.3). The Maxwell's demon footnote (12) crisply captures the ontological claim.

- **The clay potter's wheel counterexample is effective on its own terms.** The example (Section 2.2) genuinely illustrates a case where causal continuity runs through a system without any individuable intermediate feature that serves as a causal correlate of the target effect. This provides a clear intuition-pump for the distinction the paper draws, and the paper is careful to note that it is "something of a special case" (Section 2.3).

- **The paper is internally well-structured and philosophically sophisticated.** The organization — establishing the opacity problem, providing a counterexample from a non-neural system, then applying the lesson — is logical. The writing is precise, and the paper handles philosophical subtleties (causation vs. explanation, high-level vs. low-level causation) with appropriate care.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **Equivocation on what counts as a "feature" in the owls case.** The paper claims the owls dataset has "no discernible features that could correlate to" an owl disposition (Section 3.1) and "there is no feature of the set that 'means' 'owl'" (Section 3.1). But the training dataset, generated by an owl-disposed teacher model, almost certainly has *statistical* features — distributional properties, digit co-occurrence patterns, n-gram statistics — that carry the causal signal. The paper moves from "no semantically interpretable owl-related features" to "no correlative features at all" without adequately defending this transition. If statistical features *do* count as features that could be individuated and correlated, then the owls case may actually exhibit correlative continuity after all — just not at a semantically interpretable level. The paper needs to clarify what kinds of features it means "features" to exclude and why. This equivocation is the most consequential flaw, as it directly undermines the primary application of the argument.

- **No engagement with the mechanistic interpretability literature.** The paper makes claims about intermediate features in neural networks but never engages with the extensive body of work that has successfully identified such features — feature visualization (Olah et al., 2020), circuits analysis (Wang et al., 2022), induction heads (Nanda et al., 2023), sparse autoencoders, and superposition (Elhage et al., 2022). Engaging with this literature would not refute the paper's core conceptual claim (that the assumption of universal correlative continuity is false). However, it would force the paper to address an important question: if neural network features *can* routinely be individuated, how often does the "ontological absence" scenario actually arise? The paper's current silence on this literature makes its claim harder to evaluate and weakens its relevance to ML audiences. Acknowledging that many intermediate features *are* findable but arguing that the assumption of universal findability remains unwarranted would be a stronger, more nuanced position.

### Minor

- **The strong framing outstrips what the argument supports.** The paper is titled "The Myth of the Box" and concludes that "this ubiquitous box is mere myth" (Section 3.3). But the argument only establishes that correlative continuity is not a *conceptual necessity* — i.e., we cannot assume hidden features must exist. This is importantly different from showing that the black box *is* a myth. Even if the conceptual point is accepted, the "black box" characterization could still be an accurate description of an *epistemic* opacity problem (features exist but are practically impossible to find), which the paper does not refute. The title and "myth" framing claim more than the argument delivers. The paper's own hedging — "nothing in the above argumentation guarantees that this is the *correct* explanation" (Section 3.1) — is at odds with its framing.

- **Practical significance for ML research is unclear, and the paper concedes this.** Section 3.2 acknowledges that the reframing "may make no ultimate difference" to trust considerations. Section 3.3 says the "elision of 'opacity' from the language... in no way undermines" existing research efforts. The paper's concrete deliverable amounts to a terminological recommendation (stop calling neural networks black boxes) with no demonstrated effect on experimental design, XAI methodology, or trust judgments. For a paper submitted to a machine learning conference, the lack of actionable consequences is a significant limitation — even if the conceptual point is sound, the paper does not show why ML researchers should care beyond linguistic reform.
