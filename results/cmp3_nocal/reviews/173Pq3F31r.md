## Summary

This paper proposes a unified transformer-based framework that jointly models expressive performance rendering (EPR, score→performance) and automatic piano transcription (APT, performance→score) by disentangling note-level score content from global performance style. It additionally introduces a diffusion-based Performance Style Recommendation (PSR) module that generates style embeddings from score content alone, enabling automated style-aware rendering without performer labels. The model uses a Seq2Seq formulation that avoids expensive note-level alignment preprocessing, and the paper reports competitive results on both tasks along with evidence of content-style disentanglement.

---

## Strengths

1. **Bridging two inverse tasks is well-motivated and the analogy to ASR/TTS joint modeling is apt.** The paper correctly identifies the duality of EPR and APT and frames using their inverse relationship for mutual supervision, which is a genuinely novel framing for piano music (Section 1, lines 15–17).

2. **Seq2Seq EPR without note-level alignment is a practical advance.** Existing EPR systems (VirtuosoNet, DExter) require expensive alignment preprocessing that breaks down for ornaments and rubato passages. The paper shows competitive alignment rates (92.27%, Table 3, Ours-PSR) without this preprocessing, reducing engineering burden (lines 42–43, 209).

3. **The PSR module is a clever and practical addition.** Generating style embeddings from score content alone (Section 3.4) addresses a real limitation of prior work that requires manual control or performer labels. The diffusion formulation using a frozen pre-trained style encoder as the ground-truth source is clean, and the era-conditional visualization (Figure 4) provides qualitative support.

4. **The disentanglement analysis is informative and concrete.** The performer/composer identification experiments (Table 4) provide specific evidence that style representations encode performer/composer information while content representations do not (Style→Comp 77.46% vs. Cont→Comp 29.99%). The t-SNE visualizations (Figure 3) add complementary support.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None.

### Major

1. **The claimed content-space alignment between score and performance domains has no enforcement mechanism in the loss function.** The paper states that score content features z_x and z_y "are encouraged to align" (Figure 1 caption, line 71) and that "both domains share a latent content space Z_c" (line 79). However, the total loss (Equation 6, line 109) contains no explicit alignment term — no contrastive loss, cosine similarity loss, MSE, or cycle-consistency loss between z_x and z_y. The two content encoders f_{c,X} and f_{c,Y} are separate transformer components with no weight sharing (Section 3.5), receive different input modalities with different attribute sets (k_x=8 vs k_y=4, line 73), and are never directly compared in any loss term. The only pressure toward alignment is indirect and weak: z_x must combine with z_s to reconstruct performances (EPR loss), and z_y must reconstruct scores (APT loss). Nothing prevents these representations from drifting into entirely different latent manifolds. The experiments also do not test whether the two content spaces are actually aligned — the performer/composer identification (Table 4) probes style-vs-content separation within each domain but does not test cross-domain alignment. This gap between the architectural claim and the implemented loss undermines one of the paper's central framing points.

2. **A factual claim about velocity variance is contradicted by the paper's own data.** Section 5.1 (line 235) states: "our models exhibit duration and velocity variances that more closely match those of human performances compared with other baselines." Table 2 shows σ²(V) = 241.04 for Human, 238.86 for DExter (diff=2.18), and 151.03 for Ours-Target (diff=90.01). For velocity variance, DExter is dramatically closer to human — roughly 40× closer — than either Ours variant. The sentence groups duration and velocity together, but the velocity claim is simply false when checked against the table. This is a factual error in the paper's own reporting that needs correction, not a matter of interpretation.

### Minor

1. **The EPR-Only ablation is confounded with data quantity.** The EPR-Only variant is "trained solely on the ASAP dataset" (line 209), while Ours-Target uses both paired ASAP data and the large unpaired datasets (75,913 MuseScore files plus YouTube transcriptions). The improvement is attributed to "joint modeling, together with training on additional unpaired data" (line 235), but these two factors are completely confounded — the improvement could come entirely from more data. A proper ablation would require an EPR-Only variant trained on the full dataset or a joint model trained only on paired data.

2. **The subjective evaluation is thin.** The main listening test uses 11 participants evaluating 5 pieces (lines 149, 201). The style transfer evaluation uses 3 samples (Figure 5). These sample sizes are small enough that piece-specific effects or individual biases could dominate. No inter-rater reliability metric (e.g., Fleiss' kappa) is reported, and the numerical values for Figure 2 are presented only as an image rather than in a table or text.

3. **Several experimental details are underspecified.** The "state-of-the-art audio-to-MIDI transcription model" used to create the unpaired performance dataset (line 139) is not named, making it impossible to assess the transcription quality that feeds into training. The KL penalty weight (beta) in the VAE-style style regularization (Equation 5) is not reported despite being a sensitive hyperparameter. The era labels for PSR evaluation (Section 5.3, line 265) are assigned by GPT-4o mini with no validation of label accuracy.

4. **The unpaired performance dataset quality is not assessed.** Transcribing YouTube audio to MIDI for expressive piano performances and then using those transcriptions as EPR training targets could introduce systematic artifacts. The paper mentions a pilot study (line 139) but provides no quantitative validation of transcription accuracy on the types of expressive playing in the target domain.

5. **No discussion of failure cases or limitations.** The paper presents uniformly positive results throughout. There is no analysis of where the model struggles (e.g., extreme tempo changes, dense polyphony, unfamiliar composers), and the error propagation from the joint model's style encoder to the PSR module is not discussed as a limitation.

### Trivial

- The KL divergence weight (beta) for style regularization is not reported (Equation 5).

## Nice-to-Haves

- The broadcasted addition of the global style vector to each time step (Section 3.2, Equation 2) is a strong architectural assumption — style effects in real piano performance are context-dependent and interact with local note content. An ablation or discussion of alternative conditioning mechanisms would strengthen the paper.
- A comparison of inference cost (latency, FLOPs) relative to alignment-based baselines would be informative, since Seq2Seq models typically have different computational profiles.
- The additive style conditioning (z_x ⊕ z_s) assumes style affects all notes equally — this could be discussed as a design choice with potential limitations.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The reviews surface a clear architectural-level gap (content alignment claim without enforcement) and a factual reporting error, but do not reveal any deeper conceptual insight about the paper that the paper itself does not already provide.

## Suggestions

1. **Add an explicit alignment mechanism** between z_x and z_y — a contrastive loss, cycle-consistency loss, or at minimum an MSE/cosine-similarity loss — and verify cross-domain content alignment experimentally.
2. **Correct the velocity variance claim** in Section 5.1 to accurately reflect Table 2. The sentence should separate duration (where Ours is closer to human) from velocity (where DExter is closer), or retract the velocity component of the claim.
3. **Disentangle the ablation** by training an EPR-Only variant on the full unpaired dataset (using the masked reconstruction objective) to isolate the benefit of joint modeling from the benefit of additional data.
4. **Expand the subjective evaluation** — even adding participants through crowdsourcing with a music-training qualification would substantially strengthen the evidence.
