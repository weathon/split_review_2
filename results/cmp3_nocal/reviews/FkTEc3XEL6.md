## Summary

MOCHA is presented as a curated resource of multi-subject spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT) datasets with expert pathologist annotations, intended to support the development and evaluation of multi-sample spatial clustering methods. The paper catalogs 10 publicly available cohorts spanning multiple tissue types, disease contexts, and technology platforms (10x Visium and ST). However, the submission as written is an incomplete dataset paper: it provides no access mechanism for the data, no characterization or validation of its claimed core contribution (expert annotations), no experimental benchmarks demonstrating utility, and devotes roughly half its content to generic methodology review rather than describing the dataset itself.

## Strengths

- **Genuine gap identified.** The paper correctly identifies that multi-subject SRT datasets with expert spatial annotations are scarce, which constrains method development for multi-sample integration (Section 1, paragraph 3). This bottleneck is real and a well-curated resource would be valuable to the community.
- **Reasonable cohort diversity.** The 10 cohorts (Table 1) span breast, colorectal, kidney, lung, and brain tissues, include both human and mouse studies, and cover two technology platforms (10x Visium and ST). BC.TNBC.ST (94 subjects) and RCC.TLS_10x (23 subjects) provide substantial multi-subject scale, while DLPFC_10x and MOB.ST offer benchmark-friendly datasets with known anatomical structures.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **No data access mechanism provided.** The paper states that MOCHA "is released in formats readily usable with Python and R and distributed for integration into existing pipelines" (Section 1, final paragraph), but it gives no URL, DOI, repository name, or download instruction. A dataset paper without a way to obtain the dataset cannot be evaluated as a released resource, and the core contribution is unreviewable.

- **The expert annotations — the paper's central differentiator — are uncharacterized and unvalidated in the main text.** The paper claims "domain annotations from expert pathologists" (abstract, line 10) as its primary value-add, yet provides: (a) no information about the pathologists (how many, qualifications, single or multiple annotators); (b) no description of the annotation protocol (spot-by-spot, region-level, or tissue-level; guidelines; software); (c) no quality metrics (inter-annotator agreement, consistency checks, or validation against any reference); and (d) no descriptive statistics (number of labeled spots per domain class per cohort, annotation distribution, or which cohorts map to which categories). The four broad categories (immune, stroma, tumor, normal) are named in Section 4 (line 90) but with zero characterization. For a paper whose central contribution is "human annotations," this gap undermines the core claim.

- **No experimental validation or benchmarks.** The paper lists three multi-sample spatial clustering methods (BayeSMART, BASS, STAGATE) in Table 2 and describes them in Section 4, but never runs any of them on MOCHA data. There are no benchmark results, no clustering accuracy metrics (ARI, NMI, or similar), no comparison tables, and no demonstration that the annotations are usable as ground truth. A dataset paper should demonstrate that its resource is usable and useful; this paper contains zero experimental results. The only two figures are a box-plot summary of molecular characteristics (Figure 1) and a diagram of a generic preprocessing pipeline (Figure 2) — neither validates MOCHA's contribution.

- **No description of what curation added beyond re-listing public datasets.** Section 2 states the datasets were "curated from publicly available SRT datasets" (line 28) and searched from existing repositories, but it never specifies what MOCHA contributed beyond aggregation. Was gene nomenclature standardized? Were coordinate systems unified? Was metadata harmonized? Were the pathologist annotations generated by the MOCHA authors or sourced from the original publications? The paper does not clarify what curation labor was performed, making it impossible to assess whether MOCHA is a value-added resource or simply a re-index of existing data.

- **Sections 3 and 4 are generic methodology review that does not advance the dataset contribution.** Section 3 (Pre-processing and Batch Effect Correction) describes standard tools (scater, scran, Seurat, scanpy, SPARK-X, Harmony, Crescendo) without any MOCHA-specific content — no recommended MOCHA pipeline, no analysis of batch effects across MOCHA cohorts, no MOCHA preprocessing code. Section 4 (Multi-sample Spatial Clustering Methods) describes three existing methods in a survey-like manner. Together these sections occupy roughly half the paper's content but contribute nothing specific to characterizing MOCHA. A dataset paper should describe its own resource, not provide a general tutorial on other people's tools.

- **No differentiation from existing SRT resources.** The Introduction (Section 1, paragraph 2) lists SORC, Aquila, SODB, STOmicsDB, and SpatialDB but never explicitly states what annotation gap these resources leave that MOCHA fills. Many of these also contain domain labels or annotations. Without a clear statement of what MOCHA provides that existing resources do not, the reader cannot assess whether it genuinely fills a gap.

### Minor

- **Several included cohorts are very small for multi-sample integration.** KC.TLS_10x has 3 samples, LC.TLS_10x has 5 samples, and MOB.ST (1 subject, 12 samples) is from a single animal — limiting the cross-subject variability the paper claims to address. The paper does not discuss how these small cohorts contribute to the stated goal of multi-sample method evaluation.
- **No data format specification.** The abstract promises "efficient storage formats for large-scale processing," but the paper never states what file formats are used (h5ad? HDF5? Seurat objects? CSV matrices?). This makes it impossible to assess practical usability.
- **No license information.** The paper does not state under what terms MOCHA is distributed, which is a standard expectation for a released resource.
- **Figure 1 is referenced without textual summary.** The paper notes molecular characteristics are "presented in Figure 1" (line 47) but provides no analysis or interpretation of what the box plots reveal about the data.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A code repository with loading scripts, preprocessing wrappers, and example usage would substantially improve usability.
- A per-cohort breakdown of annotation categories (domain names, spot counts per category) would help users select appropriate cohorts for their tasks.
- A datasheet or data card following community standards would systematize the documentation.

## Novel Insights

None beyond the paper's own contributions. The harsh critic's observations are standard evaluation criteria for a dataset paper (access, validation, characterization, differentiation) rather than novel insights.

## Suggestions

1. Provide a download URL/DOI for MOCHA and specify the file formats used. Without this, the dataset cannot be reviewed as a released resource.
2. Characterize the annotations with descriptive statistics per cohort (number of spots per domain class, domain maps, representative H&E overlays) and, if multiple annotators were involved, inter-annotator agreement metrics.
3. Run at least one existing method (e.g., BASS or STAGATE) on 2–3 MOCHA cohorts and report domain identification accuracy against the pathologist annotations. Even a single table with ARI values would demonstrate the dataset's benchmarking utility.
4. Replace the generic methodology review in Sections 3 and 4 with MOCHA-specific documentation: data schema, standardization procedures, per-cohort annotation taxonomies, and guidance on appropriate use cases.
5. Explicitly state what MOCHA adds beyond existing resources (SORC, Aquila, SODB, etc.) and clarify whether the pathologist annotations were generated for this project or sourced from original publications.
