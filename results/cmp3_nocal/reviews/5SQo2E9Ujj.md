## Summary

This paper proposes reframing curriculum learning in goal-conditioned RL as "selective data acquisition" — a mechanism that reshapes the training distribution rather than merely an exploration heuristic. Using UVFAs trained via supervised regression on fixed datasets collected in a GridWorld environment, the authors compare uniform goal sampling against hand-crafted edge-weighted curricula. The results show modest improvements in edge-goal success rates under curriculum-biased sampling.

## Strengths

- **Conceptual reframing is genuinely interesting.** The idea of treating curriculum as a data-distribution mechanism rather than purely an exploration aid (Section 1, lines 15–17) provides a fresh lens for thinking about curriculum design. This connects to broader questions about inductive bias in function approximation that are often under-discussed in the curriculum learning literature.

- **Well-positioned in the literature.** The paper clearly situates itself relative to UVFAs (Schaul et al., 2015), PBRS (Ng et al., 1999), prior curriculum work (Florensa, Held, Portelas, Matiisen, Racanière), and open-ended learning (Hughes et al., 2024; Clune, 2019). The motivation is coherent and the paper knows which conversation it joins.

- **Limitations are honestly acknowledged.** Section 4.1 (lines 160–174) is explicit about the small GridWorld setting, manually specified curricula, and modest/inconsistent gains. This candor is commendable.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

**1. Claim about "reducing approximation error" is made but never measured or reported.** The abstract (line 9) states curricula "reduce approximation error," the introduction (line 23) repeats this, and the conclusion (line 180–181) claims curricula "improve value approximation." However, the paper reports **only success rates** — no approximation error metric (e.g., value-prediction MSE on held-out state-goal pairs) is ever computed or presented. "Approximation error" is a distinct technical quantity from downstream policy success, and the paper provides zero evidence for this specific claim. This is a direct discrepancy between what the paper asserts and what it demonstrates.

**2. Inconsistent baseline performance across experimental conditions, unexplained.** The NoCurr (uniform sampling) condition produces very different results at the same horizon H=16 in different parts of the paper. In the "Baseline" panel of Figure 2, NoCurr achieves edge success ~0.19 (overall ~0.37). In the "Weighted" panel of the same figure, NoCurr achieves edge success ~0.05 (overall ~0.28) — a roughly 4× difference on the edge metric. Table 1 (lines 133–137) confirms the latter numbers (0.060±0.055 edge). The paper never explains why the same uniform-sampling strategy yields radically different performance across these conditions. If these are different environments, grid sizes, or configurations, the paper must say so explicitly. As presented, this discrepancy undermines the reliability of cross-condition comparisons and makes the results difficult to interpret.

**3. The training protocol strips away the central challenges of GCRL.** The agent collects a fixed dataset via greedy action selection under a hand-crafted PBRS shaping reward (Section 2.5, lines 80–81). The UVFA is then trained via supervised regression on this static dataset. There is no RL loop, no policy learning through interaction, no exploration/exploitation tradeoff, and no temporal-difference learning. The paper's framing and title position it in GCRL, but the actual experiments are closer to a supervised learning problem on data generated by a heuristic. This limits the applicability of the paper's claims to actual GCRL settings, where the interplay between exploration, credit assignment, and function approximation is the core challenge.

**4. Statistical evidence is weak for the paper's quantitative claims.** Results are reported over only 3 seeds. The key comparison in the baseline condition (Figure 1) shows edge success improving from 0.183±0.131 to 0.217±0.125 — a difference of 0.034, well within one standard deviation of either group. In the weighted condition (Table 1), edge improves from 0.060±0.055 to 0.143±0.107, again with substantially overlapping error bars. No statistical tests (t-test, bootstrap, confidence intervals, effect sizes) are reported anywhere. With N=3 and overlapping error bars, the paper's core quantitative claims are not robustly supported.

### Minor

**5. No comparison against existing curriculum learning methods.** The paper cites a substantial body of curriculum learning work (Florensa et al., 2017; Held et al., 2018; Portelas et al., 2020; Matiisen et al., 2019; Racanière et al., 2020) but compares only against uniform sampling. While the paper is a conceptual proof-of-concept rather than a new method, comparing against even simple existing approaches (e.g., reverse curriculum generation) would help the reader assess whether the data-selection framing leads to different or better outcomes.

**6. The experiments do not cleanly isolate the proposed mechanism from alternative explanations.** The paper's central thesis is that curriculum should be understood as "selective data acquisition" rather than merely an "exploration heuristic." However, the paper never explicitly argues how its experimental design discriminates between these views. The non-RL setup (fixed dataset, supervised regression) does remove exploration dynamics, making it a reasonable test of data-selection effects — but this argument is never made. A cleaner experiment would compare: (a) re-weighting only (data selection without sequencing) vs. (b) easy-to-hard ordering (traditional curriculum), to show whether ordering adds anything beyond re-weighting. This is acknowledged in the "Strengthening" section of this review as a nice-to-have but remains a gap in the current paper.

**7. Missing experimental details that undermine reproducibility.** The paper does not specify: the GridWorld size (how many cells?); the action space (presumably 4 cardinal directions, but not stated); the UVFA architecture depth (only "hidden dimension 64" is given, not the number of layers); the exact sampling proportions distinguishing the "baseline curriculum" from the "weighted curriculum." These are basic reproducibility requirements.

### Trivial

**8. Reference list contains a placeholder entry.** Line 255 reads: "First Wang and Others. Title placeholder for wang et al. 2024." This appears to be a placeholder left in error.

**9.** The connection to open-ended learning (Hughes et al., 2024) is stated in the introduction and conclusion but never tested or substantiated in the experiments. A GridWorld navigation task with hand-crafted curricula does not bridge to open-ended learning claims.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Report approximation error directly** (e.g., MSE between predicted and true values on held-out state-goal pairs), since this is claimed in the abstract.
- **Compare a pure re-weighting condition (no sequencing) against an easy-to-hard sequencing condition** to empirically distinguish whether data selection alone explains the benefit, or whether ordering matters.
- **Add statistical significance tests** (bootstrap, confidence intervals, or at minimum more seeds) to support the quantitative claims.
- **Provide concrete reproducibility details**: grid size, action space, number of hidden layers in the UVFA, and exact sampling proportions for each curriculum variant.
