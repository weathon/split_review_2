## Summary

The paper proposes IncentRL, a framework that augments standard RL rewards with a KL-based penalty aligning predicted outcome distributions p(o|s,a) with preferred outcome distributions q(o|s), weighted by a parameter β. The headline contribution is treating β as a Bayesian random variable adapted online to avoid manual tuning. Experiments on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8×8 show improvements over β=0 (standard RL).

## Strengths

- **Well-motivated problem.** Manual tuning of the trade-off between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards is a practical bottleneck for intrinsic motivation methods, and the paper correctly identifies this as a limitation worth addressing (Section 2.1).

- **Clean conceptual framing via outcome distributions.** The formulation of the shaping term as KL between predicted and preferred outcome distributions (Section 3.2) provides a unified language for describing what an internal incentive signal does. The connections drawn to the Free Energy Principle and dopamine-based reward prediction error (Sections 2.3–2.4) are conceptually coherent.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal

None. The issues below are major but fixable.

### Major

- **KL(p∥q) is mathematically undefined in the paper's own toy example.** The toy experiment (Section 5.2, line 166) specifies p(o|s₀,a₁) = {s₁:0.3, s₀:0.7} and q(o|s₀) = {s₁:1.0, s₀:0.0}. Because q(s₀)=0 while p(s₀)>0, KL(p∥q) = 0.3·log(0.3/1.0) + 0.7·log(0.7/0.0) = ∞. The paper neither acknowledges this issue nor describes any smoothing, regularization, or alternative computation (e.g., KL(q∥p), Jensen-Shannon divergence, or adding a small ε to q). The reported results (cumulative reward ≈28 for β>0 in Table 1) cannot be explained by the stated equation, because an infinite penalty would make action a₁ never selected. The same issue potentially affects MountainCar (line 199: q "assigning all probability to the goal" in a continuous state space without specifying how it is represented as a distribution) and MiniGrid (where neither p nor q is described). This is a mathematical error in the paper's core computation *as written*, and must be resolved before the method can be considered valid.

- **Bayesian β adaptation mechanism is not specified.** The paper lists Bayesian β adaptation as its central novelty (lines 9, 21, 289) and claims it "eliminates manual trade-off tuning." Yet the mechanism is never described: no prior is stated, no likelihood function is given, the inference method (exact update? variational? MCMC? moment matching?) is absent, the data the posterior conditions on (episode return? per-step TD error?) is not disclosed, and the update schedule (per episode? per round? per step?) is unspecified. The only description is "we tracked the posterior evolution of β using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" (line 230). A reader cannot reconstruct what was done, evaluate whether the scheme is sound, or reproduce the results. For a claimed central contribution, this is a structural gap.

- **Contradiction between text/figures and Table 3 on β convergence.** The paper repeatedly states that the Bayesian posterior for β concentrates "near β ≈ 0.1" (lines 230, 239, 245, 247; Figures 3–4 captions show a dashed "target effective β = 0.1" reference line). However, Table 3 (lines 318–321) reports mean β values of 0.1057 → 0.0454 → 0.0194 → 0.0173 across rounds—converging to ≈0.02, not 0.1. The figure descriptions themselves acknowledge the actual data goes to "about 0.02" (line 237), while the body text and captions claim 0.1. This internal inconsistency between the paper's quantitative claim about Bayesian adaptation and the evidence it presents needs resolution.

- **Insufficient baselines for the paper's strongest claims.** The experiments compare only against β=0 (standard RL). There is no comparison against existing intrinsic motivation methods (curiosity/ICM, RND, empowerment, VIME) or other adaptive β schemes (meta-gradient RL, scheduled annealing). The abstract claims improvement "over standard RL and fixed-regularization baselines," but the only "fixed-regularization baseline" shown is β=0. The claim that Bayesian β matches the best fixed β is relegated to "additional runs (not shown)" (line 228). Without proper baselines, the paper's central claim that Bayesian adaptation "removes the need for manual trade-off tuning" cannot be evaluated.

### Minor

- **p(o|s,a) and q(o|s) are underspecified in non-toy environments.** For MountainCar (continuous state space), q is described as "assigning all probability to the goal (x ≥ 0.5)" (line 199), but how this is represented as a probability distribution (Dirac delta? Gaussian? uniform over the goal region?) is not explained. For MiniGrid, neither p nor q is described. The paper states these "may be obtained from a forward model or from environment dynamics" (line 63) without saying what was actually done, hindering reproducibility.

- **Limited statistical power.** MountainCar and MiniGrid experiments use only 3 seeds. The MountainCar improvement (β=0.1: 4136±535 vs β=0: 3502±472, Table 2) has high variance relative to the effect size, making the reported 18% improvement statistically uncertain.

- **No analysis of computational cost.** If p(o|s,a) requires a learned forward model, the overhead is not discussed. Similarly, the cost of the Bayesian β update (whatever it is) is not quantified.

- **No ablation of the KL divergence.** The paper does not test alternative divergences (reverse KL, Jensen-Shannon, α-divergence) or non-probabilistic distance measures, so it is unclear what role the specific choice of KL(p∥q) plays versus any prediction-preference alignment signal.
