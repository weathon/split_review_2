## Summary

This paper proposes the Fast and Slow Effect (FSE) framework and the Class Representation Index (CRI) metric to automatically evaluate whether concept annotations generated by LLMs/VLMs provide sufficient semantic coverage for concept-based XAI. The framework incrementally gathers concept annotations across five hierarchical stages, tracks classification accuracy from visual-only (fast mode) through text-only (slow mode), and quantifies annotation sufficiency via CRI. Experiments across six models and five datasets reveal that slow-mode (text-only) classification underperforms fast-mode (visual) classification by approximately 25% on fine-grained datasets, and that fused (image+text) evaluation can mask this insufficiency.

## Strengths

1. **The utility-as-proxy critique (Table 4, Section 6) is a clean and practically important finding.** The paper demonstrates that fused-mode CRI (~90%) far exceeds slow-mode CRI (~50%) under identical conditions, providing direct empirical evidence that strong downstream task performance does not guarantee that the underlying textual concepts are semantically sufficient. This finding is less affected by the main confound discussed below because it compares two text-involving conditions (fused vs. slow) and directly challenges a widespread evaluation practice in the literature.

2. **The five-stage hierarchical annotation design is well-motivated and grounded in prior work.** Extending existing 1–3 level schemes to a structured five-stage process (Background → Superclass → Salient Features → Detailed Features → Auxiliary Features) is a sensible operationalization of coarse-to-fine refinement, and it enables tracking how CRI evolves as concepts accumulate.

3. **Broad model coverage.** The evaluation covers six models spanning three families (GPT, Llama, Qwen) and two size tiers each, providing reasonable breadth for a study of this nature.

4. **The reversal on coarse-grained datasets (Table 3) is an informative sanity check.** The fact that slow mode outperforms fast mode on CIFAR-100 and Caltech-101 (CRI > 90%) shows that text-only reasoning is not inherently inferior to visual reasoning, which partially bounds the scope of the paper's main confound.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

1. **The CRI metric conflates annotation sufficiency with the model's text-only reasoning ability, weakening the central claim.** The paper defines annotation sufficiency as "the generated concepts alone are expressive, clear, and precise enough to enable accurate inference" (Definition 3.1). CRI operationalizes this by asking the *same model that generated the concepts* to classify from text alone at t>0. If the model fails, the paper interprets this as annotation insufficiency. But failure could equally arise from the model being worse at text-only classification from short concept lists than at direct visual recognition. This confound is not a minor ambiguity — it directly affects the interpretation of the paper's headline result (the ~25% CRI gap).

   **Mitigating factors present in the paper**: The coarse-grained dataset results (Table 3) show that slow mode *outperforms* fast mode on CIFAR-100 (94.07% vs. 84.84%) and Caltech-101 (93.77% vs. 91.48%). If text-only reasoning were inherently and uniformly worse, this reversal would not occur. This suggests the issue is specific to fine-grained domains, which is consistent with the paper's framing. Also, the utility-as-proxy finding (Table 4) is less affected by this confound because the fused-vs-slow comparison is between two conditions that both involve textual concepts.

   **Why it remains a major weakness**: The paper never fully decouples the two factors. Without controls (e.g., oracle concept baselines, cross-model evaluation), the extent to which the observed gap reflects annotation quality vs. reasoning modality remains uncertain. The authors should address this to make the central claim fully rigorous.

2. **No oracle or human baseline for calibration.** The paper criticizes human evaluation as costly and then proposes a fully automatic alternative, but never establishes what CRI values would look like under *known-good* annotations. Without this calibration, it is unclear whether a CRI of ~50–60% (slow mode at t=5) means "annotations are genuinely insufficient" or "classification from textual concept lists is inherently harder than visual recognition even for good concepts." A small oracle validation (e.g., manually curated discriminative concept sets for a subset of classes) would substantially strengthen the paper's interpretation.

### Minor

3. **Same-model evaluation risks correlated errors.** The paper uses the same model to generate concepts, classify in fast mode, and classify in slow mode. Systematic biases in the model — toward generating generic rather than discriminative concepts, or toward being overconfident in certain reasoning regimes — could affect both generation and evaluation in correlated ways. The use of six different models, all showing consistent patterns, partially mitigates this concern, but cross-model evaluation (Model A generates, Model B evaluates) would be a stronger design.

4. **Sample sizes for the main CRI experiments are not reported.** The distractor selection experiment (Table 1) explicitly states "randomly sampling 100 images from each dataset." The main CRI experiments (Figure 3, Table 2) do not specify how many images or test cases were used, making it difficult to assess statistical reliability.

5. **The CRI-Gap analysis averages across models with different behaviors.** QwenVL2-7b shows *positive* CRI-Gap on CUB-Bird (7.50%) and Flower (3.24%), while GPT-4o shows large negative gaps (Table 2). Averaging across these heterogeneous results obscures the fact that the claimed phenomenon does not hold for all models.

### Trivial

6. **Equation (2) has an index notation issue.** The CRI formula reads `1/t * sum_{i=1}^t`, but `t` is the annotation step (0–5) and `l` (defined on line 115) is the number of test cases. The intended meaning (accuracy proportion) is clear, but the equation is technically incorrect as printed.

7. **The abstract does not clearly restrict the fast/slow comparison to visual-grounded annotation.** The paper correctly states this restriction in Section 4.1 (line 139: "The post-hoc scenario inherently requires explicit conceptual annotations and thus is not suitable for this mode"), but the abstract presents the 25% drop as a general finding without noting that it applies only to the visual-grounded scenario.

## Nice-to-Haves

- **Cross-model evaluation**: Having Model A generate concepts and Models B–D evaluate CRI would help decouple annotation quality from idiosyncratic reasoning patterns.
- **Oracle concept control**: A small-scale validation with expert-curated discriminative concept sets would calibrate the CRI scale.
- **Content analysis of generated concepts**: Systematically analyzing whether LLMs generate generic vs. discriminative concepts (e.g., "has wings" vs. "red facial skin") would make the findings more actionable and connect to the motivating example (Figure 1).

## Novel Insights

The most noteworthy observation to emerge from this review is the tension between the paper's main confound and the coarse-grained dataset results. The confound (text-only reasoning vs. visual reasoning) would predict uniformly worse slow-mode performance across all datasets, but the reversal on CIFAR-100/Caltech-101 (slow mode > fast mode) shows that text-only reasoning can be highly effective. This actually strengthens the paper's domain-specific conclusion: the insufficiency appears to be annotation-quality-driven rather than purely reasoning-driven in fine-grained settings. However, the paper does not exploit this tension to sharpen its claims, instead leaving both the confound and the reversal underexplored. The utility-as-proxy finding (Table 4) emerges as the paper's cleanest result and may be its most impactful contribution independently of the confound debate.

## Suggestions

1. Add an oracle concept control: for 5–10 classes from CUB-Bird, replace LLM-generated concepts with expert-written discriminative descriptions and measure slow-mode CRI. If oracle concepts achieve significantly higher CRI, the annotation-insufficiency interpretation is strongly supported. If not, the bottleneck is the text-only reasoning modality.
2. Report sample sizes for all main experiments and clarify the number of test cases used in Figure 3.
3. Disaggregate the CRI-Gap results (Table 2) per-model rather than only reporting averages, and discuss why some models (e.g., QwenVL2-7b) show positive gaps.
4. Restrict the abstract's wording of the 25% drop claim to the visual-grounded scenario where it was measured, or explicitly note that it does not apply to post-hoc annotation.
