## Summary
This paper proposes DTERM (Dynamic Task-Embedded Reward Machine), a hypernetwork-based framework that dynamically weights reward components (compilation, test passing, BLEU, style, efficiency) conditioned on task embeddings for RL-based code generation. The core idea replaces static linear reward combinations with task-conditioned softmax weights generated by a hypernetwork, augmented with FiLM modulation and a cross-attention-based prototype mechanism for zero-shot generalization. Experiments across five benchmarks (summarization, translation, completion, repair, competitive programming) show consistent improvements over Uniform, Expert-Tuned, and GradNorm baselines.

## Strengths
- **Clear problem framing with formal contrast (Section 3.2, Eqs 2, 5-6):** The paper explicitly formalizes the limitation of static linear reward weighting (Eq 2) and replaces it with task-conditioned dynamic weights (Eqs 5-6), making the gap between prior work and the proposed method precise and falsifiable.
- **Cross-task prototype mechanism (Section 4.3, Eqs 8-9):** The hierarchical adaptation via learned prototype vectors and cross-attention provides a structured inductive bias for handling unseen tasks by composing known weighting patterns rather than extrapolating from scratch.
- **Empirical gains across five benchmarks (Table 1):** DTERM improves over all three baselines on all five task types, with the largest gains on translation (+7.7 BLEU-4 over the best baseline) and repair (+10.5% fix rate). The diversity of tasks and metrics strengthens the claim that dynamic weighting generalizes across code-related RL scenarios.
- **Ablation study isolates key components (Table 2):** Removing the hypernetwork causes the largest drop (−4.6 Pass@1), confirming dynamic weight generation as the primary driver. Replacing CodeBERT with bag-of-words causes a 15% drop, supporting the importance of rich task representations.
- **Interpretable learned weight distributions (Figure 3):** The hypernetwork assigns higher compilation weight to repair (0.22) and completion (0.21) vs. translation (0.09), and higher efficiency weight to completion (0.28) vs. problems (0.05) — patterns that align with task semantics.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
- **Policy model not specified (Section 5.1):** The paper describes the hypernetwork architecture, CodeBERT embeddings, and PPO hyperparameters but never identifies what code generation model is being trained as the policy. Without knowing whether the policy is a fine-tuned CodeBERT, a CodeLLM, or another architecture, the experimental results cannot be reproduced or contextualized against prior work.
- **No variance reported:** Despite stating experiments use 3 random seeds, Tables 1 and 2 present only point estimates with no standard deviations, confidence intervals, or statistical tests. The claimed improvements (e.g., +12.7% BLEU on translation, +18.4% fix rate on repair) are unverifiable — the reader cannot assess whether these differences are statistically meaningful or within noise.
- **Garbled, non-functional conclusion (Section 6):** The conclusion opens with hallucinated LLM text about an unrelated architecture ("The Dual Selfular-Acting Machine (DSAM.Mouth Rachel)...") and provides no summary, no limitations, no discussion of failure modes, and no contextualization of results. This reflects insufficient quality control in preparation.
- **Unevaluated framework components:** Section 4.4 (multi-modal fusion with CLIP) and Section 4.6 (RLHF integration) are described as part of the framework but never appear in any experiment or ablation. The cross-attention prototype mechanism (Section 4.3) is not individually ablated — "Static Prototypes Only" (Table 2) conflates removing cross-attention with removing prototypes entirely, leaving the contribution of the attention mechanism untested.

### Minor
- **Weak baseline selection:** Uniform, Expert-Tuned, and GradNorm are all static or semi-static weighting methods. GradNorm (Chen et al., 2018b) was designed for multi-task supervised learning loss balancing, not RL reward weighting. No comparison is made to any method that also learns to adapt reward weights dynamically.
- **Figure 2 (zero-shot generalization) lacks explanation:** The x-axis shows 10 unseen tasks where all methods improve monotonically. The paper does not explain task ordering or why performance increases across tasks, making the zero-shot claim difficult to assess.
- **Title promises "Reward Machines" the method does not use:** Section 3.5 explicitly states the method differs from reward machines as finite-state automata and only borrows the insight of modular decomposition. The title is misleading relative to the actual contribution.

### Trivial
- A "visualization" task type appears in Figure 3 and its data table but is never described in the experimental setup (Section 5.1) and has no corresponding results in Table 1.
- "Meta-training loss" in Figure 4 is never formally defined in the method or experiment sections.
- Minor grammatical issues in Section 4.6 (line 162: "Bat var 'Learning from choice of model"; line 168: "certainly there is get dynamic weights") and Section 3.4 (line 98: "The Word xog").
