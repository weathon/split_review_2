## Summary
This paper introduces the Open Proof Corpus (OPC), a dataset of 5,062 human-evaluated mathematical proofs generated by six state-of-the-art LLMs across over 1,000 competition problems. Each proof receives binary correctness judgments from IMO-level judges with ~10% double-grading yielding 90.4% inter-judge agreement. Using the OPC, the authors analyze (1) the informal-vs-formal proof generation gap (~4× on PutnamBench), (2) the relationship between final-answer accuracy and proof correctness (revealing model-specific differential drops), (3) best-of-n selection strategies, and (4) LLM-as-judge performance. They also release a fine-tuned 8B judge model (OPC-R1-8B) that matches Gemini-2.5-Pro at 88.1% accuracy.

## Strengths

- **Large-scale, rigorously-validated dataset**: 5,062 proofs across 1,010 problems from prestigious competitions (IMO, USAMO, Putnam, etc.), evaluated by 13 IMO-level judges with a pilot phase (~300 proofs, 35% double-graded), ~10% double-grading overall, and a dedicated coordinator for consistency (§3, §4). This scale and rigor fill a genuine gap in proof-generation research.
- **Quantified informal-vs-formal proof gap**: §5.3 provides a direct same-benchmark comparison — Gemini-2.5-Pro at 82.7% vs. Goedel-Prover-V2 at <19% on PutnamBench, demonstrating approximately 4× advantage for informal proofs. The controlled setup (appending final answers to problem statements) makes this comparison meaningful.
- **Final-answer accuracy is a poor proxy for proof quality — with model-specific effects**: §5.4 reveals that o3 drops from 87.6% final-answer to 59.5% proof correctness, while Gemini-2.5-Pro drops only from 84.9% to 77.6%. The differential nature of this gap (not merely its existence) is the novel finding, enabled by OPC's joint collection of final-answer and proof judgments.
- **Best-of-n with pairwise ranking outperforms and continues to scale**: §5.5 shows Rank (Swiss) improving proof accuracy from 22.7% to 40.0%, while discrete/continuous methods plateau near 35%. Ranking methods continue improving with n beyond n=5 whereas scoring methods saturate.
- **Fine-tuned 8B judge model matches frontier models**: OPC-R1-8B achieves 88.1% majority-vote accuracy (Table 2), a 17-point improvement over its base model (R1-Qwen3-8B, 71.3%), approaching GPT-5 (90.8%). This demonstrates the OPC's training utility.
- **Self-evaluation deficit across models**: Table 3 reveals that all major models except Qwen3 perform worst when judging their own proofs (e.g., o3 drops from 83.1–91.6% on others' to 76.9% on its own) — a non-obvious finding with implications for self-improvement methods.
- **Reluctance to acknowledge failure**: Only 114 out of 1,700+ incorrect proofs acknowledge inability, with 109 from a single model (o3). This quantified behavioral pattern is striking and relevant to LLM trustworthiness (§5.1).
- **Clever contamination sanity check**: §5.6 tests whether providing ground-truth solutions to judge models changes accuracy; small/non-significant deltas (e.g., GPT-5: −0.3%) support that judging results are not meaningfully inflated by contamination.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **"Human-level judging" claim conflates inter-judge agreement with accuracy**: The paper headlines that GPT-5 achieves "90.8% accuracy in judging proof correctness, which is on-par with human performance" (abstract, §1), where the 90.4% human baseline is derived from inter-judge agreement on double-graded proofs (§4). Inter-judge agreement measures consistency, not accuracy — two judges can agree while both being wrong. The paper estimates individual judge error at 5% using 0.904 = (1−p)² + p², which assumes independent, uniformly distributed errors. Judges with shared training backgrounds may share systematic blind spots, in which case agreement would overestimate true accuracy. The finding that GPT-5 achieves 90.8% majority-vote accuracy against human majority labels is strong on its own, but the framing as "on-par with human performance" overstates the evidence.

- **Best-of-n selection uses a self-evaluating judge known to be biased**: In §5.5, all four best-of-n selection methods use O4-MINI as the judge/selector for O4-MINI-generated proofs. Yet Table 3 (§5.2) explicitly shows O4-MINI's accuracy drops from ~87% (judging others' proofs) to 81.3% (judging its own proofs). The paper identifies self-evaluation bias as a "critical limitation" in §5.2 but does not acknowledge this contradiction in §5.5. The best-of-n selection quality numbers are likely underestimated, and the relative ranking of methods could shift under an unbiased judge.

### Minor

- **LLM-generated issue summaries introduced mid-annotation create potential bias**: After "several hundred graded proofs" (§3.2), O4-MINI-generated summaries flagging potential issues were introduced. The validation test — checking whether O4-MINI-as-judge agreement with humans changed — tests for divergence between model and human judgments, not whether summaries biased both in the same direction. The risk is subtle but real. The paper does not report what fraction of proofs were graded before vs. after summaries were introduced.

- **Formal-vs-informal comparison confounded by evaluation strictness**: The "4×" claim (§5.3) compares formal verification (binary, unforgiving) against human judgment (guidelines explicitly permitting minor omissions, §3.2). The paper acknowledges the formal advantage of automatic verifiability but the framing that informal "significantly outperforms" conflates model capability with evaluation leniency.

- **Best-of-n subset only has 60 fully-judged problems**: The best-of-n subset has 152 problems, but only 60 have human judgments for all 8 generations (§4). The remaining 92 have judgments only for selected generations, limiting downstream utility for computing pass@n or evaluating alternative strategies.

- **MathArena analysis conditions on correct final answers, narrowing the framing**: The abstract and §1 frame the analysis as "how final-answer accuracy relates to full proof correctness." What was actually measured (§5.4) is: conditional on a correct final answer, how often is the proof valid? This excludes cases with wrong answers — a large and informative part of the distribution. The finding remains valuable but the framing overstates its generality.

### Trivial
- The paper would benefit from clearer statements about dataset hosting, license, and format, though these are presumably in supplementary material.

## Nice-to-Haves
- Finer-grained annotations (minor error vs. fatal flaw, or scoring on a scale) would increase utility for training judge models beyond binary labels.
- A dedicated memorization analysis (checking whether model outputs match known solutions) would strengthen the contamination section.
- Reporting the fraction of proofs graded before vs. after LLM summaries were introduced would help readers assess potential bias exposure.

## Novel Insights
The paper's most genuinely novel insight is the differential nature of the final-answer-to-proof-correctness gap across models: o3 and Gemini-2.5-Pro achieve nearly identical final-answer accuracy (~87%) but diverge dramatically in proof correctness (59.5% vs. 77.6%). This is not merely "final answers are an imperfect proxy" — it reveals that some models are substantially better at answer-finding than proof-construction relative to others, a finding with implications for how reasoning benchmarks should be designed and interpreted.

## Suggestions
- Reframe the "human-level judging" claim to "matches human inter-judge consistency" or similar. The 90.8% majority-vote result is strong on its own without overclaiming equivalence to human judgment capability.
- Either add a discussion of how the Table 3 self-evaluation bias affects interpretation of Fig. 6, or re-run the best-of-n selection with a held-out judge model.
- Report the fraction of proofs graded before vs. after LLM summaries were introduced, and consider a small re-grading experiment to measure any systematic shift.
- Temper the formal-informal comparison by explicitly acknowledging evaluation-mechanism differences and framing the "4×" as an observation about the current state of both paradigms rather than a controlled experiment.
