# Review of ../review_agent/paper.md

## Summary

This paper exposes a fundamental gap between pixel-level privacy metrics (PSNR, SSIM) and actual identity-level privacy in frequency-domain privacy-preserving face recognition (PPFR) systems. The authors present FaceLinkGen, a simple distillation-style attack that extracts identity embeddings from protected templates and uses Arc2Face for face regeneration — without ever recovering original pixels. The attack achieves ≥98.5% matching accuracy and ≥96% regeneration success on three recent frequency-domain PPFR systems (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace), and a constrained variant using only a high-pass proxy and 30 paired samples maintains ≥92% matching. The paper also demonstrates the attack transfers to adversarial de-identification (TIP-IM) and non-frequency PPFR (CanFG), and documents soft-biometric leakage from protected templates.

## Strengths

1. **Clear, well-supported central result**: The paper convincingly shows that existing pixel-level metrics overestimate privacy. Table 2 reports near-perfect regeneration success across three PPFR methods and three datasets (97.9–100% Success@5), and Table 6 starkly contrasts FracFace's claimed protection (68–100% channel disruption) with actual identity leakage (0.2–1.5% protection rate under identity-centric evaluation). This directly supports the paper's core argument that current evaluation paradigms are insufficient.

2. **Strongest attack variant requires almost no system knowledge**: Section 6 demonstrates that a single student model, trained on a Gaussian-blur-based high-pass filter proxy with only 30 validation pairs and no knowledge of PPFR internals, achieves >92% 1-to-1 matching and >94% regeneration success across all three methods. This generalizes the vulnerability beyond oracle-access settings and shows it is structural to the representation itself.

3. **Generalization beyond frequency-domain PPFR**: Section 8 shows the same distillation pipeline extracts identity from TIP-IM (adversarial de-identification, 0.997 linkage accuracy) and CanFG (non-frequency PPFR, 0.825 cross-image linkage), broadening the paper's scope and showing the problem is not limited to one family of methods.

4. **Minimal-resource attack demonstrates fundamental weakness**: Section 5.1 reports that training on just 256 images (≈50 seconds) still yields 98.7% linkage accuracy and 90.5% regeneration success, conclusively showing that identity information is preserved in a trivially extractable form.

5. **Soft-biometric leakage analysis**: Table 9 shows protected templates leak race, gender, and age nearly as accurately as original images (e.g., gender accuracy 0.925 vs. original 0.949 for FracFace), revealing privacy risks that extend beyond identity re-identification.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major
None. The paper's core claims are well-supported by evidence, and the limitations are appropriately scoped.

### Minor

1. **All three frequency-domain PPFR methods originate from the same research group** (PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace all by Mi et al.). The paper acknowledges this and notes these are the only open-sourced methods of this class, but the claim that the vulnerability is inherent to "frequency-domain PPFR" would be stronger with at least one method from a different lineage. The constrained attack partially addresses this by showing a shared high-pass structure exists across all three, and Section 8 tests non-frequency methods from other groups, but the paper's central claim about the frequency-domain family rests on three sibling systems.

2. **Regeneration evaluation relies primarily on black-box commercial APIs** (Face++, Amazon Rekognition) whose internal models, training data, and failure modes are unknown. While the paper mitigates this by reporting cosine similarity in ArcFace embedding space (Section 7) and 1-to-1 verification accuracy (Table 3), a systematic evaluation on a transparent, open-source FR backbone would strengthen reproducibility and help rule out API-specific biases.

### Trivial

- The paper's narrative places the oracle-access attack (Section 5) first and the more general constrained attack (Section 6) later. A reader might initially underestimate the attack's deployability, though the paper does present both variants clearly.

## Nice-to-Haves

- A Fourier power spectrum comparison between protected templates and original images would provide quantitative evidence for why the high-pass proxy works, directly confirming the visual observation reported in Section 6.
- A direct comparison with existing reconstruction attacks (e.g., U-Net, StyleGAN) on the same methods and evaluation protocol (Face++ thresholds) would make the paper's central point about the failure of pixel-level metrics irrefutable by head-to-head comparison.

## Novel Insights

The most interesting synthesis from these reviews is the observation that the paper's strongest evidence is actually its *least* system-dependent experiment: the constrained attack (Section 6) uses a generic high-pass filter with no knowledge of the PPFR conversion and still succeeds. This shifts the narrative from "the attacker has oracle access" to "the vulnerability is baked into the structure of frequency-domain representations." The paper could restructure to present this variant first, which would make the subsequent oracle-access results feel like strengthening an already-strong point rather than the primary case. Additionally, the interplay between the paper's three evaluation prongs — commercial API verification, ArcFace embedding similarity, and soft-biometric prediction — creates a triangulation argument for identity leakage that is stronger than any single metric.

## Suggestions

1. **Explicitly scope claims to the evaluated family** in the abstract and conclusion: replace "frequency-domain PPFR systems" with "the three evaluated frequency-domain PPFR systems (all from the channel-splitting family)" to preempt generalization concerns.
2. **Give the constrained attack (Section 6) more prominent placement** — consider moving it earlier or explicitly stating in the abstract that a zero-knowledge variant achieves similar results.
3. **Add a Fourier power spectrum analysis** comparing protected templates to original images to provide quantitative evidence for the high-pass proxy intuition in Section 6.
4. **Add an open-source FR backbone** (e.g., a different ResNet-based model) to the regeneration evaluation for transparency, alongside the existing commercial APIs.
