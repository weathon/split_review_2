## Summary

IncentRL proposes augmenting RL rewards with a KL divergence between predicted and preferred outcome distributions (preference-prediction shaping), with the incentive weight β treated as a Bayesian random variable adapted online. The paper reports empirical gains over β=0 baselines on a toy 2-state MDP, MountainCar, and MiniGrid Doorkey 8x8, alongside a brief theoretical analysis. The central claimed novelty is Bayesian β adaptation, but this mechanism is never described in the available paper body, and no task-performance evidence is shown for it.

## Strengths

- **Consistent empirical improvement from the KL shaping term across three sparse-reward benchmarks.** The fixed-β experiments show clear and consistent gains: 69–74% improvement in the toy 2-state MDP (Table 1, 7 seeds), 18% on MountainCar at β=0.1 (Table 2, 3 seeds), and 98% vs 90.5% success rate on MiniGrid at β=0.01 (Figure 2, 3 seeds). These span tabular, continuous-state, and grid-world settings.

- **The KL-based preference-prediction objective is clearly formulated.** Equation 1 and the exposition in Section 3 provide a clean, interpretable framework for combining external rewards with a divergence-based internal incentive. The connection to distributional alignment (as opposed to scalar reward prediction error) is conceptually interesting and well-motivated.

## Weaknesses

### Major

- **The central claimed contribution — Bayesian β adaptation — is never described in the available paper body.** The abstract (line 9) and contributions list (line 21) present Bayesian β adaptation as the *primary novelty*. However, Section 3 (Methods) describes only the shaped reward objective. The algorithm pseudocode in Section 5.1 lists five steps (Predict Outcomes, Specify Preferences, Compute Incentive, Shape Reward, Update Policy), none involving β updates. Section 5.4 states "we tracked the posterior evolution of β using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" (line 230) without specifying the prior, likelihood, posterior update rule, or what constitutes a "round." The paper says "using our Bayesian adaptation scheme" as if the reader should already know what it is. A reader cannot understand, implement, or evaluate the central claimed contribution from this paper as presented.

- **No task-performance evidence is shown for Bayesian β adaptation.** The paper states "In additional runs (not shown), the Bayesian adaptation of β achieved performance comparable to the best fixed value" (line 228). This is a central claim backed only by a parenthetical "not shown." Figures 3–4 and Table 3 show posterior statistics (mean, std, range) of β but no task rewards, success rates, or learning curves for the Bayesian agent. Showing that a parameter concentrates is not evidence that it concentrates on a good value — task performance must be reported.

- **No comparison against any existing intrinsic motivation method.** The paper motivates itself against curiosity-driven exploration (Pathak et al., Burda et al.), empowerment, and information-gain methods (Section 2.1), identifying "fixed trade-offs between extrinsic and intrinsic rewards" as their limitation. Yet the experiments compare only against the paper's own β=0 baseline. Without comparisons to ICM, RND, or other published intrinsic-motivation approaches on the same benchmarks, the claim that IncentRL addresses their limitations is unsupported.

### Minor

- **The theoretical analysis (Propositions 1 and 2) is quite basic.** Proposition 1 states that a sufficiently small perturbation does not change the optimal policy — a generic continuity argument that would apply to any bounded reward perturbation, not specific to IncentRL. Proposition 2 observes that an infinitely large β makes the external reward negligible — essentially definitional. Neither proposition addresses the Bayesian adaptation scheme.

- **The posterior statistics contradict the paper's own narrative about β≈0.1.** Table 3 and Figure 3 show the posterior mean decreasing from 0.106 (Round 1) to 0.017 (Round 4), moving *away* from the dashed "target effective β = 0.1" line. The caption claims the posterior "concentrates near the effective region (β ≈ 0.1)," but the numbers show it concentrating near 0.02 — which aligns with the MiniGrid best fixed β of 0.01. The figure labels and text are inconsistent with the reported data.

- **MountainCar results show β sensitivity that undercuts claims about easy tuning.** β=0.3 and β=1.0 *degrade* performance by 53% and 75% (Table 2). While the paper acknowledges this, the shift from β=0.1 (best for MountainCar) to β=0.01 (best for MiniGrid) further demonstrates substantial cross-task variation in the optimal β, raising questions about how one Bayesian scheme can handle both without manual adjustment of the prior.

- **Limited experimental scale.** Only 3 seeds are used for both MountainCar and MiniGrid, with no statistical significance testing. Standard deviations are large (e.g., MountainCar β=0.3: 1654.67 ± 908.94). The 98% vs 90.5% MiniGrid result lacks error bars on the final numbers.

- **The forward model p(o|s,a) is underspecified for non-toy experiments.** For MountainCar (continuous state) and MiniGrid, the paper does not describe how p(o|s,a) is obtained, what architecture is used, or how the KL divergence is computed tractably.
