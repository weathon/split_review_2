## Summary

The paper argues that LLMs' over-cautious responses to health queries constitute a distinct safety harm—not just a helpfulness problem—especially for vulnerable users with OCD and health anxiety. It defines over-cautiousness as combining objective risk overstatement and subjectively alarming tone, introduces OCD-Eval (225 OCD-triggered health queries evaluated across 22 LLMs), and finds a 43.9% average over-cautious rate on low-risk scenarios. The paper's central normative claim is that "over-alignment" with safety values, combined with legal/PR pressures, causes LLMs to over-escalate health risks, reinforcing anxiety-driven reassurance-seeking behavior in a way that is itself harmful.

## Strengths

- **Clear, discussable position with a novel reframing**: The paper reframes over-cautiousness as a *safety* problem rather than merely a helpfulness deficit, which is a genuine conceptual contribution that opens productive debate at NeurIPS. The "over-alignment" analogy to overfitting (lines 34-35) and the virtue-theoretic argument that maximizing safety in isolation can cause harm (lines 152-162) give the position theoretical grounding beyond mere assertion.

- **Original empirical evidence supporting the position**: The OCD-Eval benchmark targets a real gap left by HealthBench—emotionally loaded reassurance-seeking queries vs. general health inquiries—and the 43.9% OCR finding provides concrete, measurable evidence that the problem is widespread across 22 LLMs, not just anecdotal. The annotation reliability validation (Gwet's AC2 of 0.93, MAE of 0.3, upper-bound noise analysis at OCR > 0.16) adds methodological credibility.

- **The rabies vaccine counterargument (lines 362-381) is the paper's strongest argumentative move**: It decomposes the "OCD patients shouldn't seek reassurance" objection into two distinct claims (whether patients should consult LLMs vs. how LLMs should respond), and the rabies example shows that both over-cautious and well-calibrated responses provide reassurance—the difference is that over-cautious responses redirect the reassurance loop toward more costly compulsive actions. This is a genuinely novel and productive reframing.

- **Section 4.3 finds an important tension**: Providing explicit OCD context reduces some models' emergency recognition (Gemma 3 27B drops to 68.0%) without reliably reducing over-caution (GPT 5.5 Thinking still at 44.2% OCR). This complicates naive solutions and productively challenges the paper's own position, inviting disagreement about personalization vs. safety.

## Weaknesses

### Fatal
None.

### Major

- **The title and framing claim more generality than the evidence and argument support.** The title states "LLMs Are Too Cautious About Health," but the evidence specifically concerns OCD-related health-anxiety queries, and the argument's harm mechanism (anxiety reinforcement, compulsive behavior) applies most directly to that population. The broader claim about general health caution is supported only by the abstract argumentation in Section 3 (legal/PR pressures, over-alignment) without population-specific evidence. The paper would be substantially stronger if it scoped its claim to what it demonstrates—"LLMs are too cautious about health for vulnerable users with anxiety disorders"—and treated the generalization as a plausible extension rather than the headline.

- **The engagement with the "asymmetric costs" counterargument (lines 409-423) is the paper's thinnest section and its most important objection to address.** The response is primarily moral condemnation ("inhuman and unfair") plus prevalence data that 30-50% of health searchers have anxiety—relevant but insufficient to engage the decision-theoretic argument that even modest under-caution could cause rare but catastrophic outcomes (missed emergencies, liability). The Figure 2 evidence that some models simultaneously achieve high emergency recognition and low OCR (lines 421-423) partially addresses this, but the paper misses the chance to make a systematic argument about the shape of the error-cost function. For a position paper whose central claim challenges a widely held default about health safety, this counterargument deserves the most rigorous treatment, not the least.

### Minor

- **The gap between the two-component definition of over-cautiousness (objective risk overstatement + subjectively alarming tone, lines 30-32) and the empirical operationalization is partially acknowledged but should be stated more explicitly.** OCR primarily measures objective risk overstatement; the tag analysis (symptom checking, reassurance undermining) partially captures tone, but the two components are never separately quantified in a way that clarifies their relative contributions. This doesn't undermine the main finding but makes it harder to know what recalibration should target.

- **The claim about LLMs having "knowledge and expertise on par with clinical professionals" (lines 396-404) is stronger than needed and opens an unnecessary empirical side-debate.** The paper's core position—that over-caution is harmful regardless of whether models are expert-level—does not require this claim. Including it is a minor distraction that opponents could attack independently of the central argument.

- **The solutions section (Section 5) is somewhat generic.** While "more professionals in alignment" and "industry standards" follow naturally from the argument, the paper does not articulate a concrete target policy for model behavior in OCD/anxiety-related health interactions. Given that Section 4.3 already identifies the tension between reducing over-caution and maintaining emergency recognition, a more specific proposal for navigating this tradeoff would strengthen the paper.
